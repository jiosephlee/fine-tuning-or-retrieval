Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Forgetting to Breathe: Ondine's Curse and the Voluntary-Automatic Breathing Divide

## The Case That Should Make You Rethink "Anxiety"

A woman in her early 40s walks into an airport, sits down to wait for her flight, and suddenly feels dizzy and nauseous. Medical personnel check her oxygen saturation: 78%. She's denied boarding. They give her supplemental oxygen, and within minutes, she's completely fine.

Here's the thing—she wasn't short of breath. She didn't feel air hunger. She just got dizzy.

Multiple emergency department visits attributed her symptoms to anxiety. Her home pulse oximeter, purchased during the COVID pandemic, kept showing saturations dipping into the 70-80% range. She felt nothing.

This went on for 14 years.

The diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent compressing her brainstem. And the physiology behind it teaches us something profound about how we breathe—and what happens when that system fails.

## The Voluntary-Automatic Breathing Divide

Here's what makes this case so instructive: our patient could breathe perfectly well when she thought about it. Her voluntary breathing pathways were intact. But automatic breathing—the kind that runs in the background while you're reading this, while you're distracted, while you're asleep—was failing.

Voluntary breathing travels via corticospinal tracts from the motor cortex directly to respiratory motor neurons. Automatic breathing, however, originates in the medulla—specifically the pre-Bötzinger complex and other respiratory centers that generate the rhythmic drive to breathe. This automatic pathway is what keeps us breathing during sleep, during distraction, during moments when we're not consciously thinking about respiration.

In Chiari malformation type I, the cerebellar tonsils herniate downward through the foramen magnum. When that herniation is significant, it compresses the medulla. And when you compress the medulla, you can selectively disrupt automatic respiratory control while leaving voluntary pathways untouched.

The result? A patient who breathes normally when they think about it—but "forgets to breathe" when distracted or asleep.

## Why No Dyspnea? The Brainstem Hypothesis

The most alarming feature in this case wasn't just the desaturations—it was the complete absence of air hunger despite profound hypoxemia. Saturations in the 70s without any sensation of breathlessness.

The authors propose a compelling mechanism: brainstem compression may interrupt afferent pathways carrying chemoreceptor and mechanoreceptor signals to the cortex before they can be processed into conscious breathlessness.

Think about that for a moment. The sensors detecting hypoxia are firing. But those signals never reach the insular cortex and other interoceptive centers because the brainstem compression severs the connection. The patient's cortex literally doesn't know it's hypoxic.

This is textbook "Ondine's curse"—the acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome. Unlike congenital central hypoventilation syndrome with PHOX2B mutations, this is structural, potentially reversible, and should prompt immediate imaging of the posterior fossa.

## The Choking That Wasn't GERD

For seven years, this woman had choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies. All normal. Her swallowing symptoms were attributed to reflux, globus, anxiety.

But here's the key clinical pearl: dysphagia affecting all consistencies equally is a red flag for neurogenic etiology. Mechanical obstruction typically shows food-specific difficulty. When your patient chokes on thin liquids just as readily as solids, think neurological.

In Chiari, lower cranial nerve compression produces oropharyngeal dysfunction. Studies show dysphagia in up to 36% of CMI patients, with neurogenic patterns predominating. The case series literature even reports CMI prevalence of 3.3% in children with persistent dysphagia—significantly higher than general population estimates.

## The CT That Had the Answer All Along

Perhaps the most humbling part of this case: a CT scan performed 14 years earlier, after her motor vehicle accident, showed a crowded foramen magnum. It was reported as normal. The finding sat in her electronic medical record for over a decade.

When the authors re-reviewed that scan, they found cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion with loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction. The diagnosis was literally there from day one.

This raises an important question about our current imaging review practices. How often do we look at the foramen magnum on routine head CTs? In patients with unexplained neurologic symptoms—particularly headaches, respiratory complaints, or swallowing difficulties—specifically assessing for tonsillar position and CSF space at the craniocervical junction should be routine.

## When Central Events Dominate the Sleep Study

The patient's sleep study showed a surprisingly mild apnea-hypopnea index of 6.0. But 72% of events were central.

In obstructive sleep apnea, you see effort against a closed airway. In central apnea, the drive to breathe itself ceases. No effort, no airflow. When central events predominate—especially in a patient without heart failure or opioid use—you need to think about structural brainstem pathology.

The relatively low AHI in this case likely underestimated true severity. Type III sleep studies lack EEG monitoring, so AHI gets calculated using total recording time rather than actual sleep time. The authors acknowledge this methodological limitation and arranged for full polysomnography with EEG.

## The Practical Takeaway

Posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms in Chiari malformation. This patient was offered surgical intervention given her symptomatic progression, central sleep apnea predominance, and significant tonsillar descent.

She opted for conservative management with close monitoring—monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. But the key point remains: recognizing this entity opens up therapeutic possibilities that wouldn't exist if her symptoms remained attributed to anxiety.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Asymptomatic hypoxemia is never anxiety** — Profound desaturation without dyspnea suggests interruption of afferent pathways, not hyperventilation syndrome
- **Equal dysphagia for solids and liquids is neurogenic until proven otherwise** — Mechanical causes are consistency-dependent; neurogenic causes affect all textures
- **Central-predominant sleep apnea demands structural evaluation** — When central events dominate, image the brainstem
- **Look at the foramen magnum on every head CT** — Chiari malformation type I is present in approximately 1% of adults on imaging; symptomatic presentations occur in 0.1%
- **Voluntary breathing can mask automatic failure** — Patients may breathe normally during examination yet have life-threatening hypoventilation during sleep or distraction

## The Bottom Line

When a patient has unexplained respiratory dysfunction with preserved voluntary breathing and absent dyspnea despite hypoxemia, think brainstem. Think Ondine's curse. And definitely think about looking at that foramen magnum.

The voluntary-automatic breathing divide isn't just physiology textbook material—it's a clinical clue that can unlock diagnoses hidden in plain sight for years.



# When the Scope is Normal: A Neurogenic Approach to Unexplained Dysphagia

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her early 40s. Seven years of choking episodes. Three laryngoscopies, two upper GI endoscopies—all normal. She's avoiding restaurants, withdrawing socially, and being told her symptoms are anxiety. Then COVID-19 home monitoring catches something terrifying: oxygen saturations dipping into the 70–80% range without any respiratory distress. She literally doesn't notice she's hypoxing.

The unifying diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, causing brainstem compression and acquired central hypoventilation.

This case is a masterclass in what happens when we silo our diagnostic thinking. The patient was worked up repeatedly from gastroenterology and ENT perspectives, but nobody stepped back to ask: what if the problem isn't structural at the level we're looking?

## The Mechanical vs. Neurogenic Distinction

Here's the key pearl from this case, and one worth tattooing into your clinical reasoning: **dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally is a red flag for neurogenic etiology.**

Mechanical dysphagia—the kind you see with strictures, tumors, or Schatzki rings—typically follows a predictable pattern. Solids first. Patients can often work around it by chewing more carefully, taking smaller bites, washing food down with liquids. The problem is physical obstruction, and liquids flow around obstructions.

Neurogenic dysphagia is different. When the problem is neuromuscular coordination—whether from bulbar cranial nerve involvement, myasthenia gravis, or brainstem compression from Chiari—the swallow mechanism itself is compromised. The striated muscle of the pharynx and upper esophagus doesn't care about bolus consistency. It's equally dysfunctional for everything.

This patient reported her choking episodes affected solids and liquids unpredictably. That's your clue. If your dysphagia patient tells you water is just as problematic as steak, start thinking neurologically.

## The Normal Scope Isn't the End—It's the Beginning

This woman underwent five endoscopic procedures over seven years. All normal. And each normal result seemed to reinforce the conclusion that her symptoms were functional or anxiety-related.

But here's the trap: a normal laryngoscopy tells you the anatomy looks fine. It says nothing about whether the neurological control of that anatomy is intact. The lower cranial nerve nuclei that coordinate swallowing sit in the medulla—precisely where cerebellar tonsillar herniation in Chiari malformation causes compression.

The prevalence of CMI in pediatric patients with persistent dysphagia is 3.3%—significantly higher than the 0.1–0.77% prevalence in the general population. That number should give us pause. We're missing this diagnosis because we're not looking for it.

## Chiari and Respiratory Dysfunction: An Underrecognized Connection

The respiratory manifestations of CMI deserve particular attention. In prospective studies, 50% of adults with CMI were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Central apnea with an index ≥5 was observed in 6.7% of patients. Pure central sleep apnea is rarer still, but when it occurs, it reflects direct brainstem compression of respiratory control centers.

This patient's presentation is striking: she maintained voluntary breathing but lost automatic respiratory drive during distraction and sleep. During an airport episode, momentary distraction led to oxygen saturation of 78% without any sensation of dyspnea. She literally forgot to breathe.

The proposed mechanism is elegant: brainstem compression interrupts afferent pathways that normally signal hypoxia and hypercapnia to the cortex. Without that input reaching conscious awareness, there's no air hunger, no distress—just silent hypoxia. That's acquired Ondine's curse, and it's life-threatening.

## Clinical Pearls from This Case

The 14-year diagnostic delay in this case happened for predictable reasons. Occipital headaches were attributed to nummular headache. Choking episodes were worked up from a GI and ENT perspective. Hypoxic episodes were labeled anxiety. Each specialist saw their piece of the puzzle but nobody assembled the full picture.

When you see unexplained bulbar symptoms—dysphagia, choking, voice changes—particularly in combination with occipital or vertex headaches, think brainstem. And when you think brainstem, think about what could be compressing it.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally suggests neurogenic etiology—pursue neurological workup when structural studies are normal
- In Chiari malformation type I, oropharyngeal dysfunction occurs in 15–26% of patients due to lower cranial nerve involvement
- CMI can present with acquired central hypoventilation—patients may have severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnea
- A normal laryngoscopy or endoscopy does not exclude neurogenic dysphagia; it should prompt consideration of brainstem pathology
- Occipital headaches (especially with Valsalva exacerbation) combined with bulbar symptoms should raise concern for Chiari malformation
- Sleep study in CMI patients may show predominantly central events, even with relatively mild overall apnea-hypopnea index

The bottom line: when the scope is normal and the dysphagia persists, stop looking at the pipes and start looking at the wiring. The brainstem controls the swallow, and structural compression there can turn a routine choking complaint into a case of silent, life-threatening hypoventilation.



# Look Again: The Crowded Foramen Magnum Hiding in Plain Sight

## The Case That Stopped Me In My Tracks

A woman in her early 40s. Seven years of terrifying choking episodes—both during meals and while sleeping. Fourteen years of chronic occipital headaches. And here's the kicker: during the COVID-19 pandemic, she casually documented her own oxygen saturations dropping to 70–80% while sitting at home, completely asymptomatic. No dyspnea. No distress. She just happened to check her pulse oximeter and found herself severely hypoxic.

Multiple emergency department visits. Three flexible laryngoscopies. Two upper GI endoscopies. All normal. Her symptoms were attributed to anxiety.

But here's what makes this case extraordinary: a CT scan performed 14 years earlier—reported at the time as normal—actually showed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction. The finding was there all along. It was simply... missed.

## The 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

Let that sink in. Fourteen years. That's how long this patient struggled with progressive symptoms before someone thought to pull up her old imaging and actually look at it themselves.

When the clinical team finally retrieved and re-reviewed that decade-old CT, they found the answer hiding in plain sight. Subsequent MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study revealed predominantly central apneas (72% of events), and her clinical picture finally made sense: brainstem compression causing both dysphagia and an acquired form of central hypoventilation.

This wasn't anxiety. This was "Ondine's curse"—the patient literally forgetting to breathe when distracted or asleep because her automatic respiratory drive was being compressed at the brainstem.

## The Radiology Report Is Not the Final Word

Here's the uncomfortable truth this case forces us to confront: radiology reports are interpretations, not verdicts. And sometimes, those interpretations are wrong or incomplete.

In this case, the original CT was reported as normal following what was presumably a trauma workup. The radiologist was likely looking for acute hemorrhage, fractures, or mass effect. A crowded foramen magnum? That may not have been on their radar. It wasn't the clinical question being asked.

But when you're facing a patient with unexplained symptoms that span years—especially neurological symptoms that don't fit a clear pattern—you owe it to them to look at the imaging yourself. Pull up the slices. Scroll through the study. Train your eye on the craniocervical junction.

## What to Look For: The Crowded Foramen Magnum

So what exactly should you be looking for? Here's a practical approach:

The foramen magnum is your window into the posterior fossa. In a normal study, you should see a clear cushion of cerebrospinal fluid surrounding the cerebellar tonsils and medulla at the cranio-cervical junction. The tonsils should sit above the foramen magnum or descend no more than 5 mm below it.

When you're reviewing a CT head, specifically examine the foramen magnum on axial and sagittal reconstructions. Look for effacement of the CSF spaces. Look for cerebellar tissue crowding the opening. Look for whether you can see clear fluid separation between the neural structures and the bone.

In this patient's case, the CT showed cerebellar tissue extending to the opisthion (the posterior margin of the foramen magnum) with loss of CSF space. That's the telltale sign of a crowded posterior fossa—the anatomical substrate of Chiari malformation.

## Why This Matters

The clinical implications are profound. Chiari malformation type I isn't just a radiographic curiosity—it can cause:

- Brainstem compression leading to central respiratory dysfunction
- Lower cranial nerve dysfunction causing dysphagia and choking
- Syrinx formation with potential myelopathy
- Life-threatening apneic episodes

And here's the most important point: surgical posterior fossa decompression can reverse these symptoms. This patient has a treatable condition. But only if you find it.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Personally review prior imaging when facing diagnostic uncertainty.** Don't rely solely on the radiology report, especially for studies performed years ago for different clinical questions.
- **Train your eye on the craniocervical junction.** Look for CSF space effacement, tonsillar descent, and crowding at the foramen magnum.
- **Chiari malformation can present primarily with respiratory and bulbar symptoms.** Central sleep apnea, unexplained hypoxia, and dysphagia may be the presenting features—not just headaches.
- **The absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is itself a red flag.** When patients have profound desaturations without air hunger, think about central hypoventilation and brainstem pathology.
- **Normal endoscopies don't exclude neurogenic dysphagia.** Equal difficulty with solids and liquids suggests a neurologic origin rather than mechanical obstruction.

## The Bottom Line

This patient's diagnosis was literally sitting in her electronic medical record for 14 years. The imaging finding was there. It just needed someone to look.

The next time you're evaluating a patient with a constellation of unexplained symptoms and a stack of "normal" workups, go back to the source material. Pull up the old studies. Scroll through the slices yourself. Sometimes the answer has been hiding in plain sight the entire time—and it's waiting for you to look again.



# Beyond AHI: Why Central Apnea Matters More Than You Think

## The Numbers Lied

Here's a case that should make you pause the next time you review a sleep study report. A woman in her early 40s. AHI of 6.0. That's "mild" sleep apnea, right? Most of us wouldn't lose sleep over an AHI of 6.

Except this patient was documenting oxygen saturations dropping into the 70–80% range at home. She had an incident at an airport where medical personnel recorded an SpO2 of 78% — and was denied boarding. She was having nocturnal choking episodes three to four times per week for seven years.

The disconnect between the "mild" numbers and the clinical reality nearly killed her. Multiple emergency department visits attributed her symptoms to anxiety. It took 14 years to reach the correct diagnosis: Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent causing central hypoventilation.

## When "Mild" Is Anything But

The sleep study showed an AHI of 6.0 — technically mild sleep apnea. But dig deeper and the pattern becomes alarming: 72% of the events were central apneas. The predominant apnea type matters enormously here.

Central events indicate the brainstem is failing to drive respiration. In Chiari malformation, cerebellar tonsillar descent compresses the medulla, disrupting the respiratory control centers that maintain automatic breathing. This patient essentially had acquired central hypoventilation syndrome — "Ondine's curse" — where automatic respiratory drive is lost while voluntary breathing remains intact.

The clinical implications are stark. This patient could breathe normally when consciously attending to it. But when distracted or asleep, she simply stopped breathing. At the airport, momentary distraction from dizziness led to profound desaturation without any sensation of air hunger. She literally forgot to breathe.

## Why the AHI Underestimated Severity

The reported AHI of 6.0 almost certainly underestimated the true burden of disease. The study used a type III sleep monitor, which has a critical limitation: no EEG. Without brain wave data, you cannot accurately determine total sleep time.

Here's the problem. AHI is calculated as events per hour of sleep. Without EEG, the denominator becomes total recording time rather than total sleep time. If the patient lies awake in the lab for two hours before falling asleep, those awake hours dilute the AHI calculation. The respiratory events during actual sleep get averaged across a longer period, artificially lowering the index.

In this case, the predominance of central events (72%), the documented desaturations to 70–80% without awareness, and the anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression all pointed toward serious pathology — despite the reassuring AHI number.

## The Silent Hypoxia Problem

Perhaps the most dangerous feature of central hypoventilation is the absence of dyspnea. This patient had oxygen saturations in the 70s without feeling short of breath. Multiple clinicians attributed her symptoms to anxiety because she wasn't "acting hypoxic."

The proposed mechanism is elegant and terrifying: brainstem compression interrupts afferent neural pathways carrying respiratory sensation to the cortex. The interoceptive processing that normally generates the feeling of air hunger — involving the anterior insular cortex — never receives its input signal. The patient's brain remains unaware that anything is wrong.

This is not hypoxia the patient can report. This is hypoxia that happens silently, often during sleep or distraction, discovered only by chance or catastrophe.

## What This Means for Your Practice

When you see a sleep study with a "mild" AHI, look at the event composition. A predominance of central apneas changes the differential diagnosis entirely. Think structural brainstem pathology, not just upper airway obstruction.

Central events on polysomnography warrant neurological evaluation. In this case, the diagnosis was Chiari malformation, but the differential includes stroke, tumor, demyelination, and other brainstem pathologies.

When clinical severity seems to outstrip the numbers, trust the patient. This woman had documented desaturations and years of disabling symptoms. The normal laryngoscopies, endoscopies, and "mild" sleep study created a false sense of reassurance that delayed diagnosis for over a decade.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **AHI is a calculated metric with limitations.** Type III sleep studies lack EEG and may underestimate true disease severity by using recording time instead of sleep time as the denominator.

- **Central apneas are not obstructive apneas.** A predominance of central events should trigger evaluation for brainstem pathology — Chiari malformation, stroke, tumor, or demyelination.

- **Silent hypoxia is real in central hypoventilation.** Brainstem compression can interrupt afferent pathways, preventing the sensation of air hunger even with severe hypoxemia.

- **Clinical correlation trumps numbers.** When the patient's reported symptoms and documented desaturations don't match the "mild" AHI, investigate further. The numbers can lie.

- **Dysphagia without structural cause needs neurological workup.** Equal difficulty with solids and liquids suggests neurogenic rather than mechanical etiology. In this case, it was lower cranial nerve compression from Chiari malformation.

This case is a reminder that our diagnostic metrics are tools, not verdicts. The patient in front of you may have pathology that the numbers don't capture. Look at the pattern, not just the summary statistic. Sometimes the most important findings are hiding in plain sight — you just need to know where to look.



# The Vertex Allodynia Enigma: Scalp Hypersensitivity in Chiari Malformation

## When Hair Weight Becomes Agony

Here's a symptom that stopped me in my tracks: a patient who cut her hair short because the mere weight of it against her scalp caused pain. Not a fashion choice—a survival strategy. This woman had been living with vertex allodynia so severe that even her hair triggered discomfort, reducing her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10 when she finally made the cut. The cultural cost was significant, but the relief was real.

This wasn't a primary headache disorder. This was Chiari malformation type I.

## The Case That Made Us Look Twice

A woman in her early 40s presented with a constellation of symptoms that had stumped multiple specialists over 14 years. The vertex allodynia was just one piece of a puzzle that included recurrent choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally, nocturnal desaturations documented on home pulse oximetry (70–80% without any respiratory distress), and chronic occipital headaches. Multiple laryngoscopies, endoscopies, and emergency department visits had yielded normal results. Her symptoms were repeatedly attributed to anxiety.

The turning point came when a 14-year-old CT scan, originally performed after a minor motor vehicle accident, was re-reviewed. What had been reported as normal showed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. MRI confirmed it: 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent consistent with Chiari malformation type I.

## Vertex Allodynia: A Clinical Observation Worth Exploring

The scalp hypersensitivity in this case deserves special attention. The patient described marked allodynia at the vertex, where even innocuous stimulation triggered discomfort. She ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short provided meaningful relief—a finding that's both fascinating and underreported in the literature.

The mechanism here isn't entirely clear, but several possibilities emerge:

**Occipital neuralgia extension.** The greater occipital nerve (C2) and lesser occipital nerve (C2-C3) provide sensory innervation to the posterior scalp. While classic occipital neuralgia presents with pain in the occipital region, the sensory distribution can extend superiorly toward the vertex. Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia specifically include "dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair." In the context of CMI, compression or irritation at the craniocervical junction could theoretically affect these nerve pathways, though robust data establishing this causation remains elusive.

**Burning vertex syndrome.** This recently described clinical entity presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that doesn't fit neatly into established primary headache classifications like migraine, tension-type, or nummular headache. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fiber pathways originating in the scalp itself. Patients show variable responses to greater occipital nerve blocks, suggesting neural mechanisms may be at play. Whether this represents a distinct entity or an atypical presentation of occipital neuralgia remains debated.

**Brainstem connectivity disruption.** Here's where things get interesting from a neuroanatomical perspective. CMI causes compression of brainstem structures, potentially disrupting afferent sensory pathways ascending to the cortex. The experience of pain and allodynia depends on processing of afferent signals by key brain regions, including the anterior insular cortex. If CMI disrupts these signals during their transit through the brainstem, it could theoretically produce unusual patterns of cutaneous hypersensitivity.

## Why This Matters Clinically

The systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations is notably absent from the literature. Prior to the development of the Chiari Symptom Profile, there was no published instrument specifically designed to assess these symptoms in CMI patients. This case suggests we should be asking about scalp hypersensitivity more deliberately.

Consider this: occipital neuralgia in association with CMI is rarely reported, yet this patient's presentation suggests it may be more common than we recognize. The vertex symptoms could represent occipital neuralgia related to CMI, burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally, or an entirely separate primary headache disorder. Without systematic study, we're left with interesting clinical observations rather than definitive answers.

## The Diagnostic Lesson

This patient's vertex allodynia was initially diagnosed as nummular headache. She underwent multiple local scalp injections that provided only transient relief. The real diagnosis emerged only when her scalp symptoms were considered alongside her other complaints—choking episodes, unexplained hypoxia, and occipital headaches.

When a patient presents with cutaneous allodynia of the scalp, particularly if it's accompanied by other neurological symptoms, the differential diagnosis should extend beyond primary headache disorders. Structural abnormalities at the craniocervical junction deserve consideration, especially if standard headache treatments fail.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Vertex allodynia can be a real symptom, not anxiety.** This patient's hair weight causing pain wasn't psychogenic—it was neuropathic, likely related to CMI affecting upper cervical nerve pathways or brainstem connectivity.

- **Scalp allodynia deserves systematic inquiry.** When evaluating patients with suspected CMI or unexplained headaches, specifically ask about cutaneous hypersensitivity and what relieves it. The answer might surprise you.

- **Treatment resistance should prompt diagnostic reconsideration.** Multiple failed local injections for "nummular headache" should have triggered a broader workup earlier.

- **Occipital neuralgia criteria include hair-triggered allodynia.** The International Headache Society diagnostic criteria specifically mention this finding—knowing this can help frame the symptom appropriately.

- **The Chiari Symptom Profile is a new tool worth knowing.** Developed to address the gap in systematic symptom assessment for CMI patients, it may help capture findings like this more consistently.

The relationship between CMI and cutaneous allodynia remains an area crying out for further research. For now, this case stands as a compelling reminder that sometimes the most unusual symptoms—in this case, hair weight triggering scalp pain—can point toward a unifying structural diagnosis that explains everything else.
