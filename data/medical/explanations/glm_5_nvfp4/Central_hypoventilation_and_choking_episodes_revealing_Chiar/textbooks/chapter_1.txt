Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient, a woman in her early 40s, presented to a family medicine clinic with a chief complaint of recurrent choking episodes that had persisted for seven years. These episodes occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affecting solids and liquids equally—a pattern that would later prove diagnostically significant. The episodes were described as sudden and distressing, often interrupting meals or awakening her from sleep with violent coughing and gasping, though notably never resulting in complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times weekly, occasionally twice in a single night.

A careful reconstruction of the clinical timeline revealed that her symptoms had begun 14 years earlier, following a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness. Shortly thereafter, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches described as constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. Unlike the classic Chiari malformation headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, her headaches lacked this feature, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty. The presence of marked allodynia at the vertex—where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort—led to a diagnosis of nummular headache. She ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, though this practical solution conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress.

The initial diagnostic workup focused almost exclusively on the choking episodes from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective. Over seven years, the patient underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all reported as normal. This sequence illustrates a common diagnostic pitfall: when symptoms persist despite negative targeted investigations, clinicians should broaden the differential diagnosis rather than repeat the same tests. The normal findings on laryngoscopy and endoscopy effectively excluded structural lesions of the upper aerodigestive tract but could not identify neurological dysfunction originating at the brainstem level. Each specialty evaluated the patient through its own lens, missing the unifying neurological diagnosis.

A critical turning point occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the patient began self-monitoring her oxygen saturation using a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she repeatedly documented asymptomatic episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80%. These episodes were often unrecognized until she checked her device, highlighting a dangerous disconnect between physiological hypoxemia and subjective awareness. Multiple emergency department evaluations attributed her symptoms to anxiety—an attribution that delayed diagnosis and illustrates how psychological explanations can be incorrectly assigned when initial investigations are unrevealing.

The most dramatic manifestation occurred at an international airport, where the patient experienced acute dizziness and nausea while awaiting her flight. On-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%, and she was denied boarding. Administration of supplemental oxygen led to complete symptom resolution within minutes. This incident demonstrated two important clinical features: first, the profound hypoxemia that could occur without preceding exertion or obvious precipitant; and second, the rapid reversibility with supplemental oxygen, suggesting a problem with respiratory drive rather than intrinsic pulmonary pathology. Subsequent pulmonary evaluation, including chest imaging and pulmonary function testing, was normal—effectively excluding primary lung disease.

The symptom burden significantly impacted the patient's quality of life. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings due to fear of public choking episodes, leading to progressive social withdrawal. The need to cut her hair short for headache relief created cultural and emotional conflict. These psychosocial consequences underscore how chronic undiagnosed medical conditions can compound physical suffering with psychological distress, and how symptoms dismissed as "anxiety" may actually reflect genuine physiological dysfunction that has not yet been identified.

This initial presentation phase of the case demonstrates several important principles. First, symptoms that affect multiple body systems—headaches, swallowing difficulties, and respiratory dysfunction—should prompt consideration of a unifying anatomical diagnosis rather than separate evaluations by multiple specialists. Second, normal findings on targeted investigations should prompt expansion rather than narrowing of the differential diagnosis. Third, objective physiological data, such as the oxygen saturation measurements documented by the patient, must be taken seriously even when they seem incongruent with the clinical context. The eventual recognition that her 14-year-old CT scan showed a crowded foramen magnum illustrates how reviewing prior imaging with fresh clinical questions can reveal previously overlooked findings.
