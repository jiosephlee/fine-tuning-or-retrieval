Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient, a woman in her early 40s, presented to a family medicine clinic with a chief complaint of recurrent choking episodes that had persisted for seven years. These episodes occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affecting solids and liquids equally—a pattern that would later prove diagnostically significant. The episodes were described as sudden and distressing, often interrupting meals or awakening her from sleep with violent coughing and gasping, though notably never resulting in complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times weekly, occasionally twice in a single night.

A careful reconstruction of the clinical timeline revealed that her symptoms had begun 14 years earlier, following a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness. Shortly thereafter, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches described as constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. Unlike the classic Chiari malformation headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, her headaches lacked this feature, contributing to diagnostic uncertainty. The presence of marked allodynia at the vertex—where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort—led to a diagnosis of nummular headache. She ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, though this practical solution conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress.

The initial diagnostic workup focused almost exclusively on the choking episodes from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective. Over seven years, the patient underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all reported as normal. This sequence illustrates a common diagnostic pitfall: when symptoms persist despite negative targeted investigations, clinicians should broaden the differential diagnosis rather than repeat the same tests. The normal findings on laryngoscopy and endoscopy effectively excluded structural lesions of the upper aerodigestive tract but could not identify neurological dysfunction originating at the brainstem level. Each specialty evaluated the patient through its own lens, missing the unifying neurological diagnosis.

A critical turning point occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the patient began self-monitoring her oxygen saturation using a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she repeatedly documented asymptomatic episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80%. These episodes were often unrecognized until she checked her device, highlighting a dangerous disconnect between physiological hypoxemia and subjective awareness. Multiple emergency department evaluations attributed her symptoms to anxiety—an attribution that delayed diagnosis and illustrates how psychological explanations can be incorrectly assigned when initial investigations are unrevealing.

The most dramatic manifestation occurred at an international airport, where the patient experienced acute dizziness and nausea while awaiting her flight. On-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%, and she was denied boarding. Administration of supplemental oxygen led to complete symptom resolution within minutes. This incident demonstrated two important clinical features: first, the profound hypoxemia that could occur without preceding exertion or obvious precipitant; and second, the rapid reversibility with supplemental oxygen, suggesting a problem with respiratory drive rather than intrinsic pulmonary pathology. Subsequent pulmonary evaluation, including chest imaging and pulmonary function testing, was normal—effectively excluding primary lung disease.

The symptom burden significantly impacted the patient's quality of life. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings due to fear of public choking episodes, leading to progressive social withdrawal. The need to cut her hair short for headache relief created cultural and emotional conflict. These psychosocial consequences underscore how chronic undiagnosed medical conditions can compound physical suffering with psychological distress, and how symptoms dismissed as "anxiety" may actually reflect genuine physiological dysfunction that has not yet been identified.

This initial presentation phase of the case demonstrates several important principles. First, symptoms that affect multiple body systems—headaches, swallowing difficulties, and respiratory dysfunction—should prompt consideration of a unifying anatomical diagnosis rather than separate evaluations by multiple specialists. Second, normal findings on targeted investigations should prompt expansion rather than narrowing of the differential diagnosis. Third, objective physiological data, such as the oxygen saturation measurements documented by the patient, must be taken seriously even when they seem incongruent with the clinical context. The eventual recognition that her 14-year-old CT scan showed a crowded foramen magnum illustrates how reviewing prior imaging with fresh clinical questions can reveal previously overlooked findings.

## Chiari Malformation Type I: Pathophysiology and Clinical Spectrum

Understanding why this patient's diagnosis was delayed for 14 years requires a firm grasp of Chiari malformation type I (CMI)—its definition, pathophysiology, and remarkably variable clinical spectrum. The central challenge in this case stems from the fact that CMI can present with symptoms that seem purely respiratory or gastrointestinal, misleading clinicians away from the underlying neurological disorder.

CMI is defined radiologically by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. This anatomical threshold, while straightforward, masks considerable clinical complexity. Contemporary imaging studies reveal CMI in approximately 1% of the adult population, yet only about 0.1% develop symptomatic disease. This striking discrepancy between anatomical prevalence and clinical manifestation is central to understanding why CMI can be overlooked: many individuals harbor the malformation asymptomatically, and its mere presence on imaging does not establish it as the cause of a patient's symptoms. The condition demonstrates a modest female predominance of 1.3:1 and typically presents during the third decade of life—the age range of our patient when her symptoms began.

The pathophysiology centres on a developmentally small posterior fossa, creating a volumetric mismatch between neural structures and available space. The posterior fossa, bounded by the occipital bone and upper cervical vertebrae, contains the cerebellum, pons, medulla, and fourth ventricle. When this compartment is underdeveloped, the cerebellar tonsils are forced downward through the foramen magnum. This herniation obstructs cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow at the craniocervical junction and compresses the medulla and upper cervical cord. In our patient, the historical CT scan from 14 years prior already demonstrated a "crowded foramen magnum"—the imaging hallmark of this volumetric mismatch—though its significance was not recognised at the time.

Contemporary evidence challenges the traditional view of CMI as universally pathological, proposing instead a spectrum from normal anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations. Volumetric studies demonstrate substantial demographic variability in posterior fossa measurements without consistent symptom correlation. This evolution in understanding necessitates individualised, symptom-based clinical decision-making. The mere finding of tonsillar descent does not establish causality; clinicians must determine whether the patient's symptoms are physiologically consistent with CMI and whether they warrant intervention.

The classic presentation of symptomatic CMI is a paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres—coughing, sneezing, straining, or laughing—present in over 80% of cases. The headache results from transient obstruction of CSF flow during increased intracranial pressure, causing pressure gradients that stress the herniated tonsils and surrounding pain-sensitive structures. Interestingly, our patient's occipital headaches were not typically Valsalva-induced, an atypical feature that may have contributed to delayed diagnosis. However, she did exhibit marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort—a phenomenon potentially linked to occipital neuralgia associated with CMI.

Lower cranial nerve involvement occurs in 15%–26% of patients with symptomatic CMI, presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. This neurological involvement explains our patient's most distressing symptom: recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally. The equal difficulty with all consistencies is a crucial diagnostic clue distinguishing neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical causes, where solid food dysphagia typically predominates. Despite multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies, the neurological origin was not initially considered—a pattern that delayed diagnosis for years.

Respiratory manifestations represent an increasingly recognised complication of CMI, occurring through several interrelated mechanisms. Brainstem compression directly affects the respiratory control centres located in the medulla, including the dorsal respiratory group (which generates the basic respiratory rhythm) and the ventral respiratory group (which coordinates voluntary and automatic breathing). CSF flow obstruction at the foramen magnum disrupts the normal dynamics that cushion these structures. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders, with a central apnoea index ≥5 observed in 6.7% of patients. Pure central sleep apnoea is uncommon but particularly significant, reflecting direct brainstem compression.

The respiratory dysfunction in CMI represents an acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes termed "Ondine's curse"—a reference to mythological figures cursed to remember to breathe consciously. This is distinct from congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, which results from PHOX2B mutations. The critical distinction in CMI is between voluntary and automatic breathing: voluntary breathing, mediated by cortical pathways, typically remains intact, while automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres and essential during sleep, becomes compromised. Our patient vividly illustrated this dissociation—she maintained normal ventilation when awake and attentive, yet experienced profound desaturation during sleep or distraction. The airport episode, where acute dizziness and nausea accompanied an oxygen saturation of 78%, exemplifies this selective failure of automatic control: she literally "forgot to breathe."

Understanding these mechanisms explains why routine pulmonary evaluation was normal in our patient. Chest imaging, pulmonary function tests, and arterial blood gas during wakefulness cannot capture the intermittent, sleep-related respiratory dysfunction characteristic of CMI. The diagnosis required polysomnography, which demonstrated predominantly central apnoeic events (72% of respiratory events), and MRI, which confirmed 11 mm tonsillar descent. This constellation of findings—bulbar symptoms, central sleep apnoea, and anatomical evidence of brainstem compression—established CMI as the unifying diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case exemplifies a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when multiple specialized evaluations fail to identify a cause for persistent symptoms, clinicians must step back and consider whether a unifying neurological diagnosis has been overlooked. The patient presented with three apparently distinct problems—chronic headaches, recurrent choking episodes, and unexplained hypoxia—that had been evaluated separately by different specialists over many years. The breakthrough came not from ordering new tests, but from systematically revisiting existing data with fresh clinical perspective.

The differential diagnosis was constructed around the three cardinal symptom domains. For the choking episodes, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially considered given the nature of the symptoms. However, normal pH studies, normal upper endoscopies, and lack of response to maximal acid suppression effectively excluded this diagnosis. Myasthenia gravis was investigated due to the bulbar distribution of symptoms; negative acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibody testing, combined with the absence of fatigability or diurnal variation, made this diagnosis unlikely. Primary pulmonary pathology was thoroughly investigated after the hypoxic episodes were documented, but normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests ruled out intrinsic lung disease. Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome—acquired Ondine's curse—emerged as a strong consideration given the characteristic pattern of preserved voluntary breathing with impaired automatic control during distraction or sleep.

The pivotal diagnostic breakthrough came from re-examining historical imaging. A CT scan performed 14 years earlier following the motor vehicle accident had been reported as normal at the time. Upon systematic review, however, it revealed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. This finding illustrates an important lesson: imaging findings may be present but unrecognized when they do not fit the clinical question being asked at the time. The original scan was performed to evaluate acute trauma, not to investigate chronic neurological symptoms, and the crowded foramen magnum was not flagged as relevant.

MRI of the brain and cervical spine confirmed the diagnosis, demonstrating 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum—the defining anatomical criterion for Chiari malformation type I, which requires at least 5 mm descent on sagittal imaging. The study also revealed crowding at the craniocervical junction with effacement of CSF spaces, providing the anatomical substrate for brainstem compression. The degree of tonsillar descent correlated with the severity of clinical manifestations and supported the pathophysiological model of brainstem compression affecting both respiratory control centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei.

Sleep study findings provided critical physiological correlation with the anatomical abnormality. Type III (four-channel) sleep study demonstrated an apnoea-hypopnoea index of 6.0 events per hour, technically classifying as mild sleep apnoea. However, the distribution of events was highly significant: 72% were central apnoeas rather than obstructive events. This predominance of central events points toward dysfunction of the brainstem respiratory control centres rather than upper airway obstruction, distinguishing Chiari-related sleep-disordered breathing from typical obstructive sleep apnoea. The finding of predominantly central apnoeas in the context of documented structural brainstem compression strongly supported the diagnosis of acquired central hypoventilation.

The sleep study methodology warrants careful consideration. Type III studies monitor respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate, but lack electroencephalographic monitoring. Without EEG data, total sleep time cannot be accurately calculated, and the apnoea-hypopnoea index is derived using total recording time rather than actual sleep time. This methodological limitation likely resulted in underestimation of the true AHI, as recording time includes periods of wakefulness when respiratory events are less likely to occur. The authors acknowledge that the documented AHI of 6.0 may underestimate the severity of sleep-disordered breathing, and they have arranged for Type I polysomnography with full EEG monitoring to provide more definitive characterization.

Videofluoroscopic swallowing study (modified barium swallow) provided objective evidence of oropharyngeal dysfunction. The study demonstrated neurogenic dysphagia—characterized by equal difficulty with all consistencies, distinguishing it from mechanical obstruction where liquids typically pass more easily than solids. This pattern of neurogenic dysphagia correlates with lower cranial nerve dysfunction from brainstem compression, specifically affecting the nucleus ambiguus which controls pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles. The swallowing study findings provided physiological evidence that complemented the anatomical MRI findings and helped explain the patient's most distressing symptom.

Laboratory investigations served primarily to exclude alternative diagnoses. A comprehensive metabolic panel, thyroid function tests, vitamin B12 levels, and inflammatory markers were normal. Crucially, acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative, excluding myasthenia gravis. Arterial blood gas on room air showed normal pH, PaCO₂, and PaO₂, indicating that the patient was not hypoxaemic at rest during the examination—underscoring the episodic nature of her respiratory dysfunction.

The synthesis of these findings led to the unifying diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I with secondary central hypoventilation and neurogenic dysphagia. The anatomical evidence (11 mm tonsillar descent, craniocervical junction crowding), physiological evidence (predominantly central sleep apnoea, neurogenic dysphagia pattern), and clinical history (chronic occipital headaches, progressive bulbar symptoms, episodic hypoxia without dyspnoea) all converged on this single diagnosis. The case demonstrates that CMI can present primarily with respiratory and bulbar symptoms rather than the classic Valsalva-induced occipital headache, and that structural brainstem abnormalities should be considered in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction even when initial specialized evaluations are normal.

## Central Hypoventilation in Structural Brainstem Disease

The patient's presentation—with oxygen saturations plummeting to 70–80% without any sensation of breathlessness—illustrates one of the most striking and dangerous phenomena in neurological medicine: acquired central hypoventilation. This condition, historically termed "Ondine's curse," represents a selective failure of automatic respiratory control while voluntary breathing remains intact. Understanding why this patient could breathe normally when consciously attending to her respiration, yet "forgot to breathe" during distraction or sleep, requires an appreciation of the dual neural pathways that govern human breathing.

Central hypoventilation syndrome exists in both congenital and acquired forms. The congenital form, true Ondine's curse, results from mutations in the PHOX2B gene and presents in infancy with absent respiratory drive during sleep. The acquired form, as demonstrated in this case, occurs when structural pathology disrupts the brainstem respiratory centers. While the congenital form is a developmental disorder of autonomic nervous system maturation, acquired central hypoventilation can result from any process affecting the medulla: brainstem stroke, tumor, hemorrhage, traumatic injury, or—as in this case—compression from cerebellar tonsillar herniation. The distinction matters because acquired forms may be reversible if the underlying structural cause is addressed, offering hope for patients like this one.

The neural control of respiration follows two distinct pathways that anatomically and functionally separate voluntary from automatic breathing. Voluntary breathing originates in the cerebral cortex, primarily the motor cortex, with descending pathways that travel through the corticospinal tracts to innervate the phrenic and intercostal motor neurons directly. This pathway allows us to hold our breath, speak, sing, or voluntarily modify our breathing pattern at will. Automatic breathing, by contrast, depends entirely on brainstem nuclei located in the medulla and pons. The pre-Bötzinger complex within the ventral medulla generates the intrinsic respiratory rhythm, while dorsal respiratory groups process chemoreceptor and mechanoreceptor inputs to modulate breathing rate and depth. These automatic centers operate continuously and unconsciously, ensuring that ventilation matches metabolic demands during sleep, distraction, and all states when cortical attention is directed elsewhere.

Brainstem respiratory centers occupy a compact but vulnerable anatomical region. The medullary respiratory groups lie in the ventrolateral medulla, near the surface, where they are susceptible to external compression. In Chiari malformation type I, the cerebellar tonsils herniate downward through the foramen magnum, creating a piston-like effect that compresses the posterior medulla and cervicomedullary junction. This patient's 11 mm tonsillar descent positioned cerebellar tissue directly against the dorsal medulla, where respiratory nuclei reside. The compression disrupts not only the rhythm-generating capacity of these centers but also their ability to receive and process afferent signals from peripheral chemoreceptors and lung stretch receptors.

The mechanism by which voluntary breathing is preserved while automatic control fails reflects the anatomical separation of these pathways. Cortical voluntary commands descend via the corticospinal tracts, which travel more ventrally in the brainstem and spinal cord, bypassing the compressed dorsal medullary regions. The patient could thus breathe normally when consciously deciding to do so—during her neurological examination, while speaking with physicians, or whenever she focused on her breathing. However, once her attention shifted elsewhere, the automatic brainstem centers failed to maintain the respiratory rhythm, and ventilation would progressively decline. The airport episode exemplifies this phenomenon: while distracted, waiting for her flight, she developed profound hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 78%) without any awareness of respiratory distress. She literally forgot to breathe.

Perhaps the most dangerous aspect of this condition is the patient's complete lack of dyspnea awareness despite severe hypoxemia. Under normal circumstances, hypoxia and hypercapnia trigger powerful sensations of air hunger through afferent signals from peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) and central chemoreceptors that ascend to the cortex via brainstem relay stations. The experience of breathlessness—a critical protective interoceptive signal—depends on these afferent inputs reaching the anterior insular cortex and other cortical regions that generate conscious awareness of physiological distress. In this patient, brainstem compression likely interrupted these ascending pathways during their transit through the medulla. The cortex never received the signals that would normally produce the sensation of breathlessness, even as oxygen saturation fell to life-threatening levels. This disconnect between physiological derangement and subjective awareness renders patients vulnerable to sudden, unheralded respiratory failure.

This case demonstrates that significant hypoxemia can occur entirely without patient awareness, a fact with profound clinical implications. The patient's repeated emergency department evaluations attributed her symptoms to anxiety precisely because she appeared calm and reported no dyspnea despite documented hypoxemia. Emergency personnel expected that anyone with oxygen saturation below 80% would appear distressed, gasping, and anxious. The absence of these expected signs led clinicians to discount the objective finding. This case teaches that in structural brainstem disease, the expected coupling between hypoxemia and symptomatic distress can be completely severed. Objective monitoring—pulse oximetry, capnography, polysomnography—becomes essential, as subjective reports and clinical observation alone may fail to detect dangerous respiratory compromise.

The clinical implications for patient safety are substantial. This patient was denied boarding at an airport because her hypoxemia was documented, but she might easily have flown and experienced severe decompensation at altitude. Her nocturnal episodes—occurring three to four times weekly—placed her at risk for complications of chronic intermittent hypoxemia, including pulmonary hypertension, cognitive impairment, and sudden death. The implementation of home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic proved life-saving, revealing what history and examination could not. For any patient with known or suspected brainstem pathology, sleep-disordered breathing should be actively investigated with polysomnography, and patients should be counseled about the risks of sedation, high-altitude travel, and any situation where respiratory drive might be further suppressed. Recognition that posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse these respiratory manifestations underscores the importance of identifying CMI in patients presenting with unexplained central hypoventilation.

## Management Decisions and Clinical Lessons

The management of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) requires careful integration of clinical findings, patient preferences, and risk-benefit considerations. In this case, the treatment decision centered on whether to proceed with posterior fossa decompression surgery or pursue conservative management with close surveillance. This decision exemplifies the broader challenges in managing CMI, where the boundary between anatomical variant and pathological entity is not always clear-cut.

**Treatment Options and Surgical Indications**

Posterior fossa decompression represents the definitive surgical treatment for symptomatic CMI. The procedure involves suboccipital craniectomy with or without duraplasty to enlarge the posterior fossa volume, relieve brainstem compression, and restore normal cerebrospinal fluid dynamics. The theoretical benefit in this patient would be direct decompression of the medullary respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei, potentially reversing both the central hypoventilation and oropharyngeal dysfunction.

Surgical intervention was recommended in this case based on several compelling indications: severe symptomatic burden with significant quality-of-life impairment, objective evidence of brainstem compression demonstrated by 11 mm tonsillar descent, documented central sleep apnea with oxygen desaturations to life-threatening levels (70–80%), and the risk of further neurological deterioration. Contemporary guidelines support surgical intervention when CMI is associated with syringomyelia, progressive neurological deficits, or debilitating symptoms attributable to brainstem compression.

Conservative management, alternatively, involves symptom-directed treatment and close monitoring without surgical intervention. This approach may be appropriate for patients with mild symptoms, those who decline surgery, or when surgical risks outweigh potential benefits. Conservative strategies in this patient included lifestyle modifications—dietary adjustments to reduce choking risk and cutting her hair short for headache relief—along with structured clinical surveillance.

**Shared Decision-Making and Patient Counseling**

The treatment decision in CMI exemplifies the importance of shared decision-making, particularly when the condition exists on a spectrum from incidental finding to severe pathology. The patient was informed that while surgery could potentially reverse her symptoms by addressing the underlying compression, it carries risks including cerebrospinal fluid leakage, infection, and neurological complications. She was also counseled that symptom improvement following surgery is not guaranteed, and some patients experience persistent or recurrent symptoms.

The patient ultimately opted for conservative management while contemplating surgical options. This decision highlights the legitimate role of patient autonomy in neurological conditions that are not immediately life-threatening. The choice to delay surgery allowed her time to process the diagnosis, consider the implications of surgical intervention, and implement coping strategies while maintaining the option for future surgery should her condition deteriorate.

**Monitoring Strategies**

When conservative management is pursued, structured surveillance becomes essential to detect clinical deterioration. In this case, a monitoring plan was implemented comprising monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. Overnight pulse oximetry provides a practical method for tracking the frequency and severity of nocturnal desaturation episodes. MRI surveillance allows detection of progressive tonsillar descent, development of syringomyelia, or other structural changes that might prompt reconsideration of surgical intervention.

The patient has remained under close observation for eight months without clinical deterioration, though the long-term natural history of untreated CMI with central hypoventilation remains incompletely characterized. This monitoring approach balances respect for patient autonomy with the clinical imperative to intervene promptly if neurological decline occurs.

**Diagnostic Delays: Root Causes**

The 14-year delay in diagnosis represents a striking but unfortunately common pattern in CMI. Several factors contributed to this delay. First, the patient's symptoms were investigated in isolation rather than as an integrated clinical picture. Choking episodes were repeatedly evaluated from gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspectives, with three laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies—all normal. The respiratory symptoms were attributed to anxiety during multiple emergency department visits. The occipital headaches were diagnosed and treated as nummular headache without investigation of an underlying structural cause.

A critical missed opportunity occurred when the initial CT scan, performed following the motor vehicle accident, was interpreted as normal. Upon re-review, this scan clearly demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction—findings suggestive of CMI that went unrecognized. This oversight underscores the importance of systematic review of posterior fossa anatomy when clinical suspicion warrants it, even on studies performed for unrelated indications.

**Clinical Lessons and Diagnostic Principles**

Several clinical lessons emerge from this case that apply broadly to diagnostic reasoning:

*Dysphagia without structural cause warrants neurological evaluation.* When swallowing dysfunction affects solids and liquids equally and repeated endoscopic evaluations are normal, the evaluation should shift from structural to neurological etiologies. Neurogenic dysphagia results from impaired motor control rather than mechanical obstruction, producing this characteristic pattern of equal difficulty across consistencies. In CMI, compression of the nucleus ambiguus and lower cranial nerve nuclei produces oropharyngeal dysfunction that cannot be detected on standard endoscopic examination performed during wakefulness.

*Unexplained respiratory dysfunction requires consideration of structural brain abnormalities.* The combination of central sleep apnea, oxygen desaturation without dyspnea, and preserved voluntary breathing should prompt evaluation for brainstem pathology. The phenomenon of "forgetting to breathe"—maintaining voluntary respiratory control while losing automatic drive—represents a red flag for brainstem compression rather than primary pulmonary disease.

*Integrating symptoms across organ systems enables pattern recognition.* The occipital headaches, choking episodes, and unexplained hypoxia were initially evaluated as separate problems by different specialists. Only when these findings were considered together did the unifying diagnosis of CMI emerge. This case illustrates how fragmented care can obscure diagnoses that span multiple organ systems, and how a comprehensive history that links seemingly disparate symptoms can reveal underlying pathology.

*Atypical presentations require heightened clinical suspicion.* While Valsalva-exacerbated occipital headache is the classic presentation of CMI, this patient's headaches lacked this feature and were instead characterized by vertex allodynia relieved by hair cutting. Similarly, while CMI typically presents with headache as the dominant symptom, this patient's most distressing symptoms were bulbar and respiratory. Atypical presentations do not exclude the diagnosis and may, in fact, signal more extensive involvement requiring prompt evaluation.

The clinical lesson is clear: when standard evaluations for common symptoms yield normal results, the diagnostic net must be cast wider to include less common but treatable structural causes. For patients with unexplained bulbar dysfunction or respiratory dysregulation, CMI should be considered even in the absence of classic headache patterns.
