Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Beyond AHI: Why Central Apnea Matters More Than You Think

## The Numbers Lied

Here's a case that should make you pause the next time you review a sleep study report. A woman in her early 40s. AHI of 6.0. That's "mild" sleep apnea, right? Most of us wouldn't lose sleep over an AHI of 6.

Except this patient was documenting oxygen saturations dropping into the 70–80% range at home. She had an incident at an airport where medical personnel recorded an SpO2 of 78% — and was denied boarding. She was having nocturnal choking episodes three to four times per week for seven years.

The disconnect between the "mild" numbers and the clinical reality nearly killed her. Multiple emergency department visits attributed her symptoms to anxiety. It took 14 years to reach the correct diagnosis: Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent causing central hypoventilation.

## When "Mild" Is Anything But

The sleep study showed an AHI of 6.0 — technically mild sleep apnea. But dig deeper and the pattern becomes alarming: 72% of the events were central apneas. The predominant apnea type matters enormously here.

Central events indicate the brainstem is failing to drive respiration. In Chiari malformation, cerebellar tonsillar descent compresses the medulla, disrupting the respiratory control centers that maintain automatic breathing. This patient essentially had acquired central hypoventilation syndrome — "Ondine's curse" — where automatic respiratory drive is lost while voluntary breathing remains intact.

The clinical implications are stark. This patient could breathe normally when consciously attending to it. But when distracted or asleep, she simply stopped breathing. At the airport, momentary distraction from dizziness led to profound desaturation without any sensation of air hunger. She literally forgot to breathe.

## Why the AHI Underestimated Severity

The reported AHI of 6.0 almost certainly underestimated the true burden of disease. The study used a type III sleep monitor, which has a critical limitation: no EEG. Without brain wave data, you cannot accurately determine total sleep time.

Here's the problem. AHI is calculated as events per hour of sleep. Without EEG, the denominator becomes total recording time rather than total sleep time. If the patient lies awake in the lab for two hours before falling asleep, those awake hours dilute the AHI calculation. The respiratory events during actual sleep get averaged across a longer period, artificially lowering the index.

In this case, the predominance of central events (72%), the documented desaturations to 70–80% without awareness, and the anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression all pointed toward serious pathology — despite the reassuring AHI number.

## The Silent Hypoxia Problem

Perhaps the most dangerous feature of central hypoventilation is the absence of dyspnea. This patient had oxygen saturations in the 70s without feeling short of breath. Multiple clinicians attributed her symptoms to anxiety because she wasn't "acting hypoxic."

The proposed mechanism is elegant and terrifying: brainstem compression interrupts afferent neural pathways carrying respiratory sensation to the cortex. The interoceptive processing that normally generates the feeling of air hunger — involving the anterior insular cortex — never receives its input signal. The patient's brain remains unaware that anything is wrong.

This is not hypoxia the patient can report. This is hypoxia that happens silently, often during sleep or distraction, discovered only by chance or catastrophe.

## What This Means for Your Practice

When you see a sleep study with a "mild" AHI, look at the event composition. A predominance of central apneas changes the differential diagnosis entirely. Think structural brainstem pathology, not just upper airway obstruction.

Central events on polysomnography warrant neurological evaluation. In this case, the diagnosis was Chiari malformation, but the differential includes stroke, tumor, demyelination, and other brainstem pathologies.

When clinical severity seems to outstrip the numbers, trust the patient. This woman had documented desaturations and years of disabling symptoms. The normal laryngoscopies, endoscopies, and "mild" sleep study created a false sense of reassurance that delayed diagnosis for over a decade.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **AHI is a calculated metric with limitations.** Type III sleep studies lack EEG and may underestimate true disease severity by using recording time instead of sleep time as the denominator.

- **Central apneas are not obstructive apneas.** A predominance of central events should trigger evaluation for brainstem pathology — Chiari malformation, stroke, tumor, or demyelination.

- **Silent hypoxia is real in central hypoventilation.** Brainstem compression can interrupt afferent pathways, preventing the sensation of air hunger even with severe hypoxemia.

- **Clinical correlation trumps numbers.** When the patient's reported symptoms and documented desaturations don't match the "mild" AHI, investigate further. The numbers can lie.

- **Dysphagia without structural cause needs neurological workup.** Equal difficulty with solids and liquids suggests neurogenic rather than mechanical etiology. In this case, it was lower cranial nerve compression from Chiari malformation.

This case is a reminder that our diagnostic metrics are tools, not verdicts. The patient in front of you may have pathology that the numbers don't capture. Look at the pattern, not just the summary statistic. Sometimes the most important findings are hiding in plain sight — you just need to know where to look.
