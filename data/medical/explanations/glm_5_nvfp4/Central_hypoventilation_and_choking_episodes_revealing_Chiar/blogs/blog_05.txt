Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Vertex Allodynia Enigma: Scalp Hypersensitivity in Chiari Malformation

## When Hair Weight Becomes Agony

Here's a symptom that stopped me in my tracks: a patient who cut her hair short because the mere weight of it against her scalp caused pain. Not a fashion choice—a survival strategy. This woman had been living with vertex allodynia so severe that even her hair triggered discomfort, reducing her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10 when she finally made the cut. The cultural cost was significant, but the relief was real.

This wasn't a primary headache disorder. This was Chiari malformation type I.

## The Case That Made Us Look Twice

A woman in her early 40s presented with a constellation of symptoms that had stumped multiple specialists over 14 years. The vertex allodynia was just one piece of a puzzle that included recurrent choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally, nocturnal desaturations documented on home pulse oximetry (70–80% without any respiratory distress), and chronic occipital headaches. Multiple laryngoscopies, endoscopies, and emergency department visits had yielded normal results. Her symptoms were repeatedly attributed to anxiety.

The turning point came when a 14-year-old CT scan, originally performed after a minor motor vehicle accident, was re-reviewed. What had been reported as normal showed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. MRI confirmed it: 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent consistent with Chiari malformation type I.

## Vertex Allodynia: A Clinical Observation Worth Exploring

The scalp hypersensitivity in this case deserves special attention. The patient described marked allodynia at the vertex, where even innocuous stimulation triggered discomfort. She ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short provided meaningful relief—a finding that's both fascinating and underreported in the literature.

The mechanism here isn't entirely clear, but several possibilities emerge:

**Occipital neuralgia extension.** The greater occipital nerve (C2) and lesser occipital nerve (C2-C3) provide sensory innervation to the posterior scalp. While classic occipital neuralgia presents with pain in the occipital region, the sensory distribution can extend superiorly toward the vertex. Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia specifically include "dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair." In the context of CMI, compression or irritation at the craniocervical junction could theoretically affect these nerve pathways, though robust data establishing this causation remains elusive.

**Burning vertex syndrome.** This recently described clinical entity presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that doesn't fit neatly into established primary headache classifications like migraine, tension-type, or nummular headache. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fiber pathways originating in the scalp itself. Patients show variable responses to greater occipital nerve blocks, suggesting neural mechanisms may be at play. Whether this represents a distinct entity or an atypical presentation of occipital neuralgia remains debated.

**Brainstem connectivity disruption.** Here's where things get interesting from a neuroanatomical perspective. CMI causes compression of brainstem structures, potentially disrupting afferent sensory pathways ascending to the cortex. The experience of pain and allodynia depends on processing of afferent signals by key brain regions, including the anterior insular cortex. If CMI disrupts these signals during their transit through the brainstem, it could theoretically produce unusual patterns of cutaneous hypersensitivity.

## Why This Matters Clinically

The systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations is notably absent from the literature. Prior to the development of the Chiari Symptom Profile, there was no published instrument specifically designed to assess these symptoms in CMI patients. This case suggests we should be asking about scalp hypersensitivity more deliberately.

Consider this: occipital neuralgia in association with CMI is rarely reported, yet this patient's presentation suggests it may be more common than we recognize. The vertex symptoms could represent occipital neuralgia related to CMI, burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally, or an entirely separate primary headache disorder. Without systematic study, we're left with interesting clinical observations rather than definitive answers.

## The Diagnostic Lesson

This patient's vertex allodynia was initially diagnosed as nummular headache. She underwent multiple local scalp injections that provided only transient relief. The real diagnosis emerged only when her scalp symptoms were considered alongside her other complaints—choking episodes, unexplained hypoxia, and occipital headaches.

When a patient presents with cutaneous allodynia of the scalp, particularly if it's accompanied by other neurological symptoms, the differential diagnosis should extend beyond primary headache disorders. Structural abnormalities at the craniocervical junction deserve consideration, especially if standard headache treatments fail.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Vertex allodynia can be a real symptom, not anxiety.** This patient's hair weight causing pain wasn't psychogenic—it was neuropathic, likely related to CMI affecting upper cervical nerve pathways or brainstem connectivity.

- **Scalp allodynia deserves systematic inquiry.** When evaluating patients with suspected CMI or unexplained headaches, specifically ask about cutaneous hypersensitivity and what relieves it. The answer might surprise you.

- **Treatment resistance should prompt diagnostic reconsideration.** Multiple failed local injections for "nummular headache" should have triggered a broader workup earlier.

- **Occipital neuralgia criteria include hair-triggered allodynia.** The International Headache Society diagnostic criteria specifically mention this finding—knowing this can help frame the symptom appropriately.

- **The Chiari Symptom Profile is a new tool worth knowing.** Developed to address the gap in systematic symptom assessment for CMI patients, it may help capture findings like this more consistently.

The relationship between CMI and cutaneous allodynia remains an area crying out for further research. For now, this case stands as a compelling reminder that sometimes the most unusual symptoms—in this case, hair weight triggering scalp pain—can point toward a unifying structural diagnosis that explains everything else.
