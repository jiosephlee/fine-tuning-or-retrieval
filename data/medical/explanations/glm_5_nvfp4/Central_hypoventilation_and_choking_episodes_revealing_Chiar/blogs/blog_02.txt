Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# When the Scope is Normal: A Neurogenic Approach to Unexplained Dysphagia

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her early 40s. Seven years of choking episodes. Three laryngoscopies, two upper GI endoscopies—all normal. She's avoiding restaurants, withdrawing socially, and being told her symptoms are anxiety. Then COVID-19 home monitoring catches something terrifying: oxygen saturations dipping into the 70–80% range without any respiratory distress. She literally doesn't notice she's hypoxing.

The unifying diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, causing brainstem compression and acquired central hypoventilation.

This case is a masterclass in what happens when we silo our diagnostic thinking. The patient was worked up repeatedly from gastroenterology and ENT perspectives, but nobody stepped back to ask: what if the problem isn't structural at the level we're looking?

## The Mechanical vs. Neurogenic Distinction

Here's the key pearl from this case, and one worth tattooing into your clinical reasoning: **dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally is a red flag for neurogenic etiology.**

Mechanical dysphagia—the kind you see with strictures, tumors, or Schatzki rings—typically follows a predictable pattern. Solids first. Patients can often work around it by chewing more carefully, taking smaller bites, washing food down with liquids. The problem is physical obstruction, and liquids flow around obstructions.

Neurogenic dysphagia is different. When the problem is neuromuscular coordination—whether from bulbar cranial nerve involvement, myasthenia gravis, or brainstem compression from Chiari—the swallow mechanism itself is compromised. The striated muscle of the pharynx and upper esophagus doesn't care about bolus consistency. It's equally dysfunctional for everything.

This patient reported her choking episodes affected solids and liquids unpredictably. That's your clue. If your dysphagia patient tells you water is just as problematic as steak, start thinking neurologically.

## The Normal Scope Isn't the End—It's the Beginning

This woman underwent five endoscopic procedures over seven years. All normal. And each normal result seemed to reinforce the conclusion that her symptoms were functional or anxiety-related.

But here's the trap: a normal laryngoscopy tells you the anatomy looks fine. It says nothing about whether the neurological control of that anatomy is intact. The lower cranial nerve nuclei that coordinate swallowing sit in the medulla—precisely where cerebellar tonsillar herniation in Chiari malformation causes compression.

The prevalence of CMI in pediatric patients with persistent dysphagia is 3.3%—significantly higher than the 0.1–0.77% prevalence in the general population. That number should give us pause. We're missing this diagnosis because we're not looking for it.

## Chiari and Respiratory Dysfunction: An Underrecognized Connection

The respiratory manifestations of CMI deserve particular attention. In prospective studies, 50% of adults with CMI were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Central apnea with an index ≥5 was observed in 6.7% of patients. Pure central sleep apnea is rarer still, but when it occurs, it reflects direct brainstem compression of respiratory control centers.

This patient's presentation is striking: she maintained voluntary breathing but lost automatic respiratory drive during distraction and sleep. During an airport episode, momentary distraction led to oxygen saturation of 78% without any sensation of dyspnea. She literally forgot to breathe.

The proposed mechanism is elegant: brainstem compression interrupts afferent pathways that normally signal hypoxia and hypercapnia to the cortex. Without that input reaching conscious awareness, there's no air hunger, no distress—just silent hypoxia. That's acquired Ondine's curse, and it's life-threatening.

## Clinical Pearls from This Case

The 14-year diagnostic delay in this case happened for predictable reasons. Occipital headaches were attributed to nummular headache. Choking episodes were worked up from a GI and ENT perspective. Hypoxic episodes were labeled anxiety. Each specialist saw their piece of the puzzle but nobody assembled the full picture.

When you see unexplained bulbar symptoms—dysphagia, choking, voice changes—particularly in combination with occipital or vertex headaches, think brainstem. And when you think brainstem, think about what could be compressing it.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally suggests neurogenic etiology—pursue neurological workup when structural studies are normal
- In Chiari malformation type I, oropharyngeal dysfunction occurs in 15–26% of patients due to lower cranial nerve involvement
- CMI can present with acquired central hypoventilation—patients may have severe oxygen desaturation without dyspnea
- A normal laryngoscopy or endoscopy does not exclude neurogenic dysphagia; it should prompt consideration of brainstem pathology
- Occipital headaches (especially with Valsalva exacerbation) combined with bulbar symptoms should raise concern for Chiari malformation
- Sleep study in CMI patients may show predominantly central events, even with relatively mild overall apnea-hypopnea index

The bottom line: when the scope is normal and the dysphagia persists, stop looking at the pipes and start looking at the wiring. The brainstem controls the swallow, and structural compression there can turn a routine choking complaint into a case of silent, life-threatening hypoventilation.
