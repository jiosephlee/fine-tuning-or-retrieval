Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Look Again: The Crowded Foramen Magnum Hiding in Plain Sight

## The Case That Stopped Me In My Tracks

A woman in her early 40s. Seven years of terrifying choking episodes—both during meals and while sleeping. Fourteen years of chronic occipital headaches. And here's the kicker: during the COVID-19 pandemic, she casually documented her own oxygen saturations dropping to 70–80% while sitting at home, completely asymptomatic. No dyspnea. No distress. She just happened to check her pulse oximeter and found herself severely hypoxic.

Multiple emergency department visits. Three flexible laryngoscopies. Two upper GI endoscopies. All normal. Her symptoms were attributed to anxiety.

But here's what makes this case extraordinary: a CT scan performed 14 years earlier—reported at the time as normal—actually showed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction. The finding was there all along. It was simply... missed.

## The 14-Year Diagnostic Odyssey

Let that sink in. Fourteen years. That's how long this patient struggled with progressive symptoms before someone thought to pull up her old imaging and actually look at it themselves.

When the clinical team finally retrieved and re-reviewed that decade-old CT, they found the answer hiding in plain sight. Subsequent MRI confirmed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent. A sleep study revealed predominantly central apneas (72% of events), and her clinical picture finally made sense: brainstem compression causing both dysphagia and an acquired form of central hypoventilation.

This wasn't anxiety. This was "Ondine's curse"—the patient literally forgetting to breathe when distracted or asleep because her automatic respiratory drive was being compressed at the brainstem.

## The Radiology Report Is Not the Final Word

Here's the uncomfortable truth this case forces us to confront: radiology reports are interpretations, not verdicts. And sometimes, those interpretations are wrong or incomplete.

In this case, the original CT was reported as normal following what was presumably a trauma workup. The radiologist was likely looking for acute hemorrhage, fractures, or mass effect. A crowded foramen magnum? That may not have been on their radar. It wasn't the clinical question being asked.

But when you're facing a patient with unexplained symptoms that span years—especially neurological symptoms that don't fit a clear pattern—you owe it to them to look at the imaging yourself. Pull up the slices. Scroll through the study. Train your eye on the craniocervical junction.

## What to Look For: The Crowded Foramen Magnum

So what exactly should you be looking for? Here's a practical approach:

The foramen magnum is your window into the posterior fossa. In a normal study, you should see a clear cushion of cerebrospinal fluid surrounding the cerebellar tonsils and medulla at the cranio-cervical junction. The tonsils should sit above the foramen magnum or descend no more than 5 mm below it.

When you're reviewing a CT head, specifically examine the foramen magnum on axial and sagittal reconstructions. Look for effacement of the CSF spaces. Look for cerebellar tissue crowding the opening. Look for whether you can see clear fluid separation between the neural structures and the bone.

In this patient's case, the CT showed cerebellar tissue extending to the opisthion (the posterior margin of the foramen magnum) with loss of CSF space. That's the telltale sign of a crowded posterior fossa—the anatomical substrate of Chiari malformation.

## Why This Matters

The clinical implications are profound. Chiari malformation type I isn't just a radiographic curiosity—it can cause:

- Brainstem compression leading to central respiratory dysfunction
- Lower cranial nerve dysfunction causing dysphagia and choking
- Syrinx formation with potential myelopathy
- Life-threatening apneic episodes

And here's the most important point: surgical posterior fossa decompression can reverse these symptoms. This patient has a treatable condition. But only if you find it.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Personally review prior imaging when facing diagnostic uncertainty.** Don't rely solely on the radiology report, especially for studies performed years ago for different clinical questions.
- **Train your eye on the craniocervical junction.** Look for CSF space effacement, tonsillar descent, and crowding at the foramen magnum.
- **Chiari malformation can present primarily with respiratory and bulbar symptoms.** Central sleep apnea, unexplained hypoxia, and dysphagia may be the presenting features—not just headaches.
- **The absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is itself a red flag.** When patients have profound desaturations without air hunger, think about central hypoventilation and brainstem pathology.
- **Normal endoscopies don't exclude neurogenic dysphagia.** Equal difficulty with solids and liquids suggests a neurologic origin rather than mechanical obstruction.

## The Bottom Line

This patient's diagnosis was literally sitting in her electronic medical record for 14 years. The imaging finding was there. It just needed someone to look.

The next time you're evaluating a patient with a constellation of unexplained symptoms and a stack of "normal" workups, go back to the source material. Pull up the old studies. Scroll through the slices yourself. Sometimes the answer has been hiding in plain sight the entire time—and it's waiting for you to look again.
