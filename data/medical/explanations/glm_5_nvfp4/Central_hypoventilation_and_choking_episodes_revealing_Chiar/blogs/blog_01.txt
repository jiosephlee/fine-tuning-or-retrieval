Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Forgetting to Breathe: Ondine's Curse and the Voluntary-Automatic Breathing Divide

## The Case That Should Make You Rethink "Anxiety"

A woman in her early 40s walks into an airport, sits down to wait for her flight, and suddenly feels dizzy and nauseous. Medical personnel check her oxygen saturation: 78%. She's denied boarding. They give her supplemental oxygen, and within minutes, she's completely fine.

Here's the thing—she wasn't short of breath. She didn't feel air hunger. She just got dizzy.

Multiple emergency department visits attributed her symptoms to anxiety. Her home pulse oximeter, purchased during the COVID pandemic, kept showing saturations dipping into the 70-80% range. She felt nothing.

This went on for 14 years.

The diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I, with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent compressing her brainstem. And the physiology behind it teaches us something profound about how we breathe—and what happens when that system fails.

## The Voluntary-Automatic Breathing Divide

Here's what makes this case so instructive: our patient could breathe perfectly well when she thought about it. Her voluntary breathing pathways were intact. But automatic breathing—the kind that runs in the background while you're reading this, while you're distracted, while you're asleep—was failing.

Voluntary breathing travels via corticospinal tracts from the motor cortex directly to respiratory motor neurons. Automatic breathing, however, originates in the medulla—specifically the pre-Bötzinger complex and other respiratory centers that generate the rhythmic drive to breathe. This automatic pathway is what keeps us breathing during sleep, during distraction, during moments when we're not consciously thinking about respiration.

In Chiari malformation type I, the cerebellar tonsils herniate downward through the foramen magnum. When that herniation is significant, it compresses the medulla. And when you compress the medulla, you can selectively disrupt automatic respiratory control while leaving voluntary pathways untouched.

The result? A patient who breathes normally when they think about it—but "forgets to breathe" when distracted or asleep.

## Why No Dyspnea? The Brainstem Hypothesis

The most alarming feature in this case wasn't just the desaturations—it was the complete absence of air hunger despite profound hypoxemia. Saturations in the 70s without any sensation of breathlessness.

The authors propose a compelling mechanism: brainstem compression may interrupt afferent pathways carrying chemoreceptor and mechanoreceptor signals to the cortex before they can be processed into conscious breathlessness.

Think about that for a moment. The sensors detecting hypoxia are firing. But those signals never reach the insular cortex and other interoceptive centers because the brainstem compression severs the connection. The patient's cortex literally doesn't know it's hypoxic.

This is textbook "Ondine's curse"—the acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome. Unlike congenital central hypoventilation syndrome with PHOX2B mutations, this is structural, potentially reversible, and should prompt immediate imaging of the posterior fossa.

## The Choking That Wasn't GERD

For seven years, this woman had choking episodes affecting solids and liquids equally. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies. All normal. Her swallowing symptoms were attributed to reflux, globus, anxiety.

But here's the key clinical pearl: dysphagia affecting all consistencies equally is a red flag for neurogenic etiology. Mechanical obstruction typically shows food-specific difficulty. When your patient chokes on thin liquids just as readily as solids, think neurological.

In Chiari, lower cranial nerve compression produces oropharyngeal dysfunction. Studies show dysphagia in up to 36% of CMI patients, with neurogenic patterns predominating. The case series literature even reports CMI prevalence of 3.3% in children with persistent dysphagia—significantly higher than general population estimates.

## The CT That Had the Answer All Along

Perhaps the most humbling part of this case: a CT scan performed 14 years earlier, after her motor vehicle accident, showed a crowded foramen magnum. It was reported as normal. The finding sat in her electronic medical record for over a decade.

When the authors re-reviewed that scan, they found cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion with loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction. The diagnosis was literally there from day one.

This raises an important question about our current imaging review practices. How often do we look at the foramen magnum on routine head CTs? In patients with unexplained neurologic symptoms—particularly headaches, respiratory complaints, or swallowing difficulties—specifically assessing for tonsillar position and CSF space at the craniocervical junction should be routine.

## When Central Events Dominate the Sleep Study

The patient's sleep study showed a surprisingly mild apnea-hypopnea index of 6.0. But 72% of events were central.

In obstructive sleep apnea, you see effort against a closed airway. In central apnea, the drive to breathe itself ceases. No effort, no airflow. When central events predominate—especially in a patient without heart failure or opioid use—you need to think about structural brainstem pathology.

The relatively low AHI in this case likely underestimated true severity. Type III sleep studies lack EEG monitoring, so AHI gets calculated using total recording time rather than actual sleep time. The authors acknowledge this methodological limitation and arranged for full polysomnography with EEG.

## The Practical Takeaway

Posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms in Chiari malformation. This patient was offered surgical intervention given her symptomatic progression, central sleep apnea predominance, and significant tonsillar descent.

She opted for conservative management with close monitoring—monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. But the key point remains: recognizing this entity opens up therapeutic possibilities that wouldn't exist if her symptoms remained attributed to anxiety.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Asymptomatic hypoxemia is never anxiety** — Profound desaturation without dyspnea suggests interruption of afferent pathways, not hyperventilation syndrome
- **Equal dysphagia for solids and liquids is neurogenic until proven otherwise** — Mechanical causes are consistency-dependent; neurogenic causes affect all textures
- **Central-predominant sleep apnea demands structural evaluation** — When central events dominate, image the brainstem
- **Look at the foramen magnum on every head CT** — Chiari malformation type I is present in approximately 1% of adults on imaging; symptomatic presentations occur in 0.1%
- **Voluntary breathing can mask automatic failure** — Patients may breathe normally during examination yet have life-threatening hypoventilation during sleep or distraction

## The Bottom Line

When a patient has unexplained respiratory dysfunction with preserved voluntary breathing and absent dyspnea despite hypoxemia, think brainstem. Think Ondine's curse. And definitely think about looking at that foramen magnum.

The voluntary-automatic breathing divide isn't just physiology textbook material—it's a clinical clue that can unlock diagnoses hidden in plain sight for years.
