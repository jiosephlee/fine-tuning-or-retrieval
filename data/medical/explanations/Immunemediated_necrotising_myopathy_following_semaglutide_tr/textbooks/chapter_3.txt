Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Chapter 3: ## IMNM Essentials: Pathophysiology, Serologies, and Histopathology

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies that classically presents with relatively rapid onset, severe proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK), and frequent bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia). It is an uncommon disorder, most often seen in middle‑aged or older adults, and is distinguished from other myopathies by its histopathologic pattern: prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with only sparse lymphocytic infiltrates on muscle biopsy.

Serologic classification of IMNM is clinically useful because defined autoantibody subsets associate with different clinical contexts and prognosis. Two well‑recognized antibodies are anti‑HMGCR (anti‑3‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase) and anti‑SRP (signal recognition particle). Anti‑HMGCR IMNM is strongly linked to prior statin exposure in many patients and often presents with subacute severe weakness and high CK; anti‑SRP IMNM typically associates with very severe weakness, more frequent treatment‑resistance, and a higher risk of extramuscular involvement (including cardiac involvement) in some series. Importantly, about one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative for these defined antibodies; seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis and may reflect as‑yet unidentified autoantibodies or non‑antibody–mediated triggers.

Pathophysiologically, IMNM is characterized by immune‑mediated myofibre injury that is not primarily driven by dense endomysial T‑cell infiltration (as in polymyositis). A key feature is complement activation with deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of affected fibres, implicating antibody‑ or complement‑dependent cytotoxic mechanisms. Macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis is also a hallmark: activated macrophages (CD68+) clear necrotic fibres and show increased lysosomal activity on special stains such as acid phosphatase. By contrast, only limited numbers of CD3+ T lymphocytes are typically present, often scattered and perivascular rather than forming dense endomysial infiltrates.

The muscle biopsy in this patient demonstrates the canonical features of IMNM and helps explain why clinicians considered an immune cause rather than pure toxic rhabdomyolysis. The biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating myofibres, prominent CD68+ macrophages with evidence of myophagocytosis, and increased acid phosphatase activity consistent with phagolysosomal activation. Complement activation was documented by membrane attack complex deposition on necrotic and some non‑necrotic fibres, supporting a complement‑mediated myocytotoxic process. Only rare CD3+ T cells and some perivascular inflammation were present, which fits the expected paucity of lymphocytes in IMNM. Taken together, these histologic findings are characteristic of necrotising autoimmune myopathy and help distinguish it from other entities.

Distinguishing IMNM from polymyositis, dermatomyositis, and toxic rhabdomyolysis depends on clinicopathologic correlation. Polymyositis typically shows prominent endomysial CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates invading non‑necrotic fibres; dermatomyositis shows perifascicular atrophy, perimysial/perivascular inflammation, and complement deposition in capillaries. Toxic rhabdomyolysis or purely metabolic muscle injury can produce myofibre necrosis and high CK, but generally lacks the immune signatures seen in IMNM — namely MAC deposition on fibre membranes and macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis with persistent weakness that improves with immunotherapy rather than simply with withdrawal of the offending agent and supportive care.

Clinically relevant triggers for IMNM have been identified, most notably statin exposure in association with anti‑HMGCR positivity. Other drugs, infections, and malignancy have been implicated in subsets of patients. The existence of a seronegative IMNM subgroup underscores that additional autoantibodies and triggers likely remain to be described. In the present case, the patient was seronegative for HMGCR and SRP but had biopsy‑proven IMNM and a temporal relationship to semaglutide exposure; this constellation fits a seronegative, likely drug‑associated IMNM phenotype—one in which muscle biopsy, not serology alone, is decisive for diagnosis and for guiding immunosuppressive therapy.
