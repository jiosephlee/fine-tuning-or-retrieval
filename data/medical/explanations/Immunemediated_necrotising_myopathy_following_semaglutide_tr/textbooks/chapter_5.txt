Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Chapter 5: ## Management, Follow-up, and Practical Lessons

Initial management of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is driven by severity of weakness (especially bulbar or respiratory involvement), rapidity of progression, and objective markers such as creatine kinase (CK). In this case the patient had progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms and a CK that rose into the thousands, which appropriately prompted aggressive initial immunotherapy. High‑dose corticosteroids remain the first‑line immediate therapy: an intravenous pulse (methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days) is commonly used when rapid control is needed, followed by oral prednisone at approximately 1 mg/kg/day. This approach treats ongoing immune‑mediated muscle injury and stabilises patients while additional therapies take effect.

IVIG has an established role in IMNM, particularly when there are severe features (bulbar dysfunction, marked weakness, rapidly rising CK) or when a rapid clinical response is required. Typical dosing is 2 g/kg given over 2–5 days (often split over 3–5 infusions). In the presented patient, IVIG was added because of persistent severe weakness and bulbar involvement; the combination of pulse steroids and IVIG frequently produces a relatively fast biochemical and clinical improvement in drug‑triggered or antibody‑positive IMNM.

Decisions about adding steroid‑sparing agents depend on the presumed cause and clinical trajectory. Idiopathic or antibody‑positive IMNM (for example anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) often requires prolonged multi‑agent immunosuppression: methotrexate or mycophenolate are common steroid‑sparing choices started early to allow steroid tapering, and rituximab is used for refractory disease or certain antibody profiles. By contrast, when a drug trigger is suspected and the patient shows a clear clinical and CK response after withdrawal and a short course of immunotherapy (as in this case), prolonged immunosuppression may not be necessary. Practically, reserve addition of steroid‑sparing agents for patients who fail to improve within several weeks, whose CK remains elevated, who relapse during steroid taper, or who have steroid‑related toxicity.

Monitoring during treatment and follow‑up should be structured and multidisciplinary. Serial CK measurements are the most accessible biochemical marker of disease activity; interpret CK alongside AST/ALT (which may be elevated from muscle as well as liver). Early and repeated quantitative strength assessments (e.g., manual muscle testing or timed functional measures) document recovery. Bulbar symptoms warrant formal swallow evaluation and otolaryngology or speech–language pathology input; respiratory muscle involvement should prompt baseline and serial spirometry with supine and upright forced vital capacity and, if available, maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures or overnight oximetry. MRI of affected muscle groups is useful to document active inflammatory edema and to guide biopsy when needed; MRI changes also help track response. Cardiac surveillance is indicated when troponin/pro‑BNP are abnormal or when symptoms/signs suggest myocarditis: ECG, transthoracic echocardiography, and cardiac MRI are the preferred modalities and were appropriately obtained in this patient because of elevated cardiac biomarkers and MRI evidence of myocardial edema. Repeat imaging or biomarker reassessment should follow until findings stabilise or normalise.

Tapering corticosteroids is individualized. In drug‑associated IMNM with a robust early response (clinical improvement and CK normalisation), an accelerated taper can be reasonable to minimise steroid toxicity—many clinicians begin taper after clear improvement and aim to stop or reach a low maintenance dose over weeks to a few months while monitoring closely. In idiopathic or antibody‑positive IMNM, a slower taper over many months is generally required to avoid relapse. Relapse vigilance is essential after discontinuation: new or recurrent weakness, worsening dysphagia, or any CK rise should prompt re‑evaluation. Practical follow‑up frequency is typically every 1–4 weeks initially (with CK and clinical exam), then lengthened to every 2–3 months once stable.

Preventing and managing complications of treatment and of the disease is a critical part of care. High‑dose steroids and adjunctive immunotherapy increase infection risk; inpatient periods of high‑dose therapy warrant attention to catheter care, urine monitoring, and early treatment of infections (this patient developed a pseudomonal UTI and septic shock). Consider Pneumocystis prophylaxis (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) when prolonged corticosteroid therapy (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) or additional immunosuppression is anticipated. Vaccinate against influenza, pneumococcus, COVID‑19 and other indicated non‑live vaccines before starting immunosuppression when feasible; live vaccines should be avoided during significant immunosuppression. IVIG carries specific risks including volume overload, renal dysfunction, infusion reactions, and thromboembolic events; assess renal function and thrombotic risk before and during infusions and adjust infusion rate as needed. Bone health should be addressed early with calcium, vitamin D, lifestyle measures, and consideration of bisphosphonate therapy for those at sustained high steroid exposure. Immobility, inflammation and steroids increase venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk — prophylactic anticoagulation during hospitalisation and active disease should be considered, especially in patients with prior VTE.

Rehabilitation and nutrition are central to recovery. Early, supervised physical and occupational therapy focused on graded strengthening and prevention of contractures improves functional outcomes; avoid overly aggressive exercise in the inflammatory phase. For bulbar dysfunction, swallow therapy and dietary modification reduce aspiration risk; temporary enteral feeding should be considered for severe dysphagia. Nutritional strategies include adequate protein intake and caloric support to preserve or rebuild lean muscle mass, and attention to vitamin and micronutrient status.

Long‑term planning after an episode of presumed drug‑triggered IMNM includes avoidance of the suspected offending agent(s) — in this case, GLP‑1 receptor agonists — and careful documentation of the adverse drug reaction in the medical record. Counsel the patient about alternative weight‑management strategies (lifestyle modification, referral to obesity medicine or bariatric care, and discussion of other pharmacologic agents with a different mechanism) using shared decision‑making that weighs metabolic benefits against potential immune risks. Report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance authorities to contribute to post‑marketing safety data. Follow‑up visits should continue at regular intervals (initially frequent, then spacing as stability is demonstrated) with CK checks and clinical assessment; re‑evaluate sooner for any new symptoms suggesting relapse.

Key practical lessons from this case
- IMNM can be seronegative; absence of myositis‑specific antibodies does not exclude the disease and muscle biopsy remains diagnostic when clinical suspicion is high.  
- Differentiate immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy from toxic rhabdomyolysis by time course (persistently rising CK and progressive weakness over weeks–months suggest immune mediation) and by biopsy showing necrosis with inflammatory markers rather than solely acute necrosis.  
- Consider recently introduced drugs as potential triggers for IMNM; prompt withdrawal plus early immunotherapy (steroids ± IVIG) can be effective when a drug etiology is suspected.  
- Tailor immunosuppression intensity and duration to response: drug‑associated cases may remit after a short early course, whereas idiopathic/antibody‑positive IMNM often requires prolonged and multi‑agent therapy.  
- Monitor for and proactively mitigate complications of disease and therapy (infection, VTE, bone loss, swallowing/respiratory compromise), and coordinate rehabilitation and nutrition to optimise functional recovery.

In summary, aggressive early treatment (as used for this patient: pulse steroids followed by high‑dose prednisone and a one‑time course of IVIG) is appropriate for severe IMNM with bulbar involvement. Close monitoring, prevention of treatment complications, dedicated rehabilitation, and avoidance of the suspected trigger are the pillars of management and of achieving durable recovery.
