Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Morning Report] In a patient with subacute progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and creatine kinase around 4000 U/L without prior statin exposure, what specific studies would you prioritize to confirm immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and exclude mimics (e.g., toxic myopathy, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis), and in what sequence would you obtain serologies, MRI, EMG, and muscle biopsy?

Bottom line
- In a patient with subacute, severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, and CK ~4,000 U/L the priority is to (1) rapidly distinguish immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from toxic/rhabdomyolysis and other inflammatory myopathies and (2) obtain tissue confirmation if the diagnosis will change therapy. Send myositis serologies and basic labs immediately, obtain muscle MRI early to localize active muscle, perform EMG for supportive electrodiagnostic data, and obtain a targeted muscle biopsy (the diagnostic standard) before prolonged immunosuppression if clinically feasible. If the patient is deteriorating (airway, severe dysphagia, respiratory failure), start therapy while arranging diagnostic testing — do not delay treatment for biopsy.

Recommended studies and rationale (what to order and why)
1) Immediate blood studies (send at presentation)
  - CK (trend), CMP, AST/ALT, TSH, CBC, ESR/CRP — to quantify muscle injury and screen metabolic/toxic mimics.
  - Myositis serology panel focused on IMNM: anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (highly associated with IMNM). Also send extended myositis panel (Jo‑1 and other antisynthetase Abs, Mi‑2, MDA5, TIF1‑γ, NXP2) and cN1A (helpful for IBM but imperfect specificity).
  - Viral serologies as indicated (HIV, HCV), pregnancy test if appropriate, and a careful medication/supplement/toxin screen (statins, red yeast rice, supplements) — toxic causes are common mimics.
  - Cardiac troponin, ECG, BNP/echocardiography if concern for myocarditis (common in some autoimmune myopathies).

Rationale: serologies are noninvasive, can point to a specific IMNM subtype (HMGCR or SRP) and are available quickly; but up to ~30% of IMNM are seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude disease.

2) Muscle MRI (early, concurrently with labs)
  - Obtain MRI of proximal muscles (thighs/pelvis) to demonstrate edema (active inflammation/necrosis), document distribution (symmetric proximal pattern favors IMNM), and to select the optimal biopsy site (area of greatest edema but avoiding end‑stage fatty replacement).
Rationale: MRI is noninvasive, guides biopsy sampling to improve yield, and helps exclude chronic fatty atrophy patterns (IBM) or focal lesions.

3) Electromyography (EMG)/nerve conduction studies
  - Perform EMG to document a myopathic pattern (short‑duration low‑amplitude motor unit potentials, early recruitment, spontaneous fibrillations/positive sharp waves) and to exclude neurogenic or motor‑neuron causes.
Rationale: EMG supports a myopathic process, identifies irritability that may correlate with active necrosis, and can help choose biopsy site if MRI is not available.

4) Muscle biopsy (diagnostic standard)
  - Perform an open or core biopsy targeted to an MRI‑abnormal but not end‑stage muscle.
  - Request histology and immunohistochemistry for necrosis/regeneration, inflammatory cell profile (macrophages vs dense CD8+ endomysial infiltrates), membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition, MHC‑I expression, and special stains for rimmed vacuoles.
Rationale: IMNM classically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with abundant macrophages/myophagocytosis, minimal lymphocytic invasion, and complement (MAC) deposition on myofibres. Polymyositis shows endomysial CD8 T‑cells invading non‑necrotic fibers; IBM shows rimmed vacuoles, congophilic inclusions, and mixed inflammatory and neurogenic features. Toxic myopathies and acute rhabdomyolysis lack the immune features (MAC, persistent regenerating fibers with macrophages).

Sequence and practical plan
- Day 0 (initial evaluation): clinical assessment, CK and urgent labs, obtain myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) and viral serologies; review medications/supplements; get troponin/echo if cardiac symptoms.
- Day 0–1 (concurrent): schedule and obtain muscle MRI of thighs/pelvis (to map edema) and arrange EMG (can be same day or within 48 h).
- Day 1–3: perform EMG and use MRI + EMG to choose biopsy site; schedule muscle biopsy as soon as reasonable. If biopsy must be delayed >48–72 hours, proceed with serologies and MRI but remember seronegative IMNM is common — biopsy remains crucial.
- Timing of immunosuppression: if patient has life‑threatening features (severe dysphagia, respiratory compromise) begin high‑dose steroids/IVIG without waiting for biopsy results; document that treatment may blunt inflammatory infiltrates but necrotic/regenerating fibers and complement deposition often persist and biopsy can still be diagnostic (as in the presented case, where biopsy after pulse steroids showed classic IMNM features).

How the published case illustrates these principles
- In this report the team obtained an extensive serologic panel (negative for HMGCR and SRP — reminding us that ~1/3 of IMNM are seronegative), performed MRI of the thighs that showed symmetric proximal muscle edema, and obtained a targeted right vastus lateralis biopsy. The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition — histologic hallmarks of IMNM that confirmed the diagnosis despite negative serologies. High‑dose steroids and IVIG were started for severe disease; clinical and CK improvement followed, and biopsy remained diagnostic as predicted.

Key diagnostic pearls (teaching points)
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are highly useful but not fully sensitive — a negative panel does not exclude IMNM.
- Muscle MRI should be obtained early to localize active disease and maximize biopsy yield.
- EMG is supportive and helps exclude neurogenic mimics; it does not replace biopsy.
- Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic standard: IMNM = necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation, scarce lymphocytes, and complement deposition.
- Do not delay life‑saving immunotherapy for biopsy if the patient has severe deficits (airway, swallowing, respiratory failure); biopsy often remains informative after short courses of steroids/IVIG as in this case.
- Always screen for statins, supplements, toxins and for cardiac involvement; consider malignancy screening when appropriate.

In short: send serologies immediately, obtain MRI early to localize disease, do EMG for supportive data, and perform a targeted muscle biopsy (gold standard) — ideally before prolonged immunosuppression but do not withhold urgent treatment for severe disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] After exposure to a new medication, which clinical and biochemical time-course features most reliably distinguish toxic rhabdomyolysis from immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (e.g., peak CK level and trajectory after drug cessation, symptom evolution, and response to hydration versus immunosuppression)?

Short answer
- Toxic (direct) rhabdomyolysis classically presents acutely with very large CK rises during or immediately after exposure, systemic myalgias/tea‑coloured urine and/or AKI, and CK falls rapidly over days after drug withdrawal and aggressive hydration.  
- Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) presents subacutely to chronically with progressive proximal weakness (often dysphagia/dysphonia), CK in the high hundreds to low‑tens of thousands that may persist or increase after drug cessation, characteristic biopsy findings (necrotic fibres, macrophages, membrane attack complex) and clinical/biochemical improvement only after immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents).  
Use the time course and treatment response more than a single CK value to distinguish the two.

Teaching points with supporting clinical reasoning and the case

1) Timing of onset and symptom evolution
- Toxic rhabdo: symptoms occur during or shortly after exposure (hours–days) and are typically dominated by myalgia, swelling, dark urine and systemic features of muscle breakdown or volume depletion.  
- IMNM: weakness is subacute to chronic (weeks–months) and is dominated by progressive symmetric proximal weakness, sometimes bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia) and functional decline rather than primarily myalgias or dark urine.  
- Case: the patient had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion reaction within hours of semaglutide but then developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia over 4 months — a pattern more consistent with IMNM than an isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis.

2) CK magnitude and trajectory after drug cessation
- Toxic rhabdo: CK often very high (frequently >10,000 U/L, commonly much higher), peaks early, and with cessation of the offending agent plus fluids/bicarbonate it usually falls quickly over days to a few weeks. The tirzepatide example in the discussion had CK ≈57,000 with rapid improvement after stopping the drug and hydration.  
- IMNM: CK can be markedly elevated but often in the low‑to‑mid thousands and importantly may persist or even worsen after drug withdrawal if the immune process continues. Improvement in CK is typically seen after initiation of immunosuppression/IVIG rather than after hydration alone.  
- Case: initial CK was only 337 U/L during the acute hospitalization but rose progressively to 3,992 U/L over 4 months after stopping semaglutide — a delayed, progressive rise inconsistent with the rapid resolution expected for toxic rhabdo and more supportive of IMNM.

3) Response to therapy (hydration vs immunosuppression)
- Toxic rhabdo: mainstays are aggressive IV fluids (and supportive care); CK and symptoms usually respond without immunosuppressive therapy.  
- IMNM: requires immunosuppression (high‑dose corticosteroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) for clinical and biochemical improvement; hydration alone will not reverse ongoing immune‑mediated muscle necrosis.  
- Case: the patient’s CK and weakness improved markedly after pulse steroids and IVIG and remained normal off immunosuppression — consistent with an immune‑mediated process rather than a toxin that would have recovered with hydration.

4) Histology and immunologic testing
- Toxic rhabdo shows muscle necrosis but typically lacks the specific immune features of IMNM.  
- IMNM shows necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage predominant infiltrate, membrane attack complex deposition, and sometimes autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP), though up to ~1/3 can be seronegative. Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic standard when the clinical course is subacute/persistent.  
- Case: thigh muscle biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition and rare T cells — classic for IMNM despite negative myositis serologies.

Practical diagnostic approach (summary algorithm)
- If acute severe CK elevation with myalgias, dark urine and/or AKI at the time of exposure → treat as toxic rhabdo: stop drug, aggressive IV fluids, monitor renal function and CK; expect rapid CK fall over days.  
- If subacute progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, CK that is persistent or rising despite drug withdrawal and supportive care → investigate for IMNM: check myositis autoantibodies, MRI of muscle, and proceed to muscle biopsy. If biopsy or clinical severity supports IMNM, treat with immunotherapy (steroids ± IVIG ± steroid‑sparing agents).  
- Always remember overlap/exception: some drugs can trigger autoimmune myopathy (drug‑triggered IMNM), so a temporal relationship to a medication does not automatically mean toxic rhabdo.

Bottom line anchored in the case
- The decisive clues favoring IMNM in this report were the delayed progressive proximal weakness and dysphagia over 4 months, rising CK despite stopping the drug and initial supportive care, biopsy showing immune necrotising features, and prompt clinical/biochemical recovery only after corticosteroids and IVIG — a pattern that is the opposite of the rapid rise–and‑rapid‑fall pattern you expect with direct toxic rhabdomyolysis.

### [Pathophysiology] Which muscle-biopsy histopathologic and immunohistochemical findings are characteristic of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and help differentiate it from polymyositis or dermatomyositis (e.g., necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, scant lymphocytes, membrane attack complex deposition)?

Answer:

The diagnostic muscle‑biopsy signature of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is dominated by myofibre necrosis and regeneration with a macrophage‑predominant inflammatory response and only scant T‑lymphocytes — in contrast to the lymphocyte‑driven patterns seen in polymyositis (PM) or dermatomyositis (DM).

Key histopathologic and IHC features that point to IMNM and help distinguish it from PM or DM

- Prominent scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres as the predominant lesion (rather than perifascicular atrophy or dense endomysial inflammation).
- Macrophage‑rich myophagocytosis: abundant CD68+ macrophages engulfing necrotic fibres; acid phosphatase positivity reflecting lysosomal activity. (Seen in this case: dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibres with CD68 and acid phosphatase staining and myophagocytosis.)
- Relative paucity of lymphocytes: only rare CD3+ T cells on IHC, and lack of the CD8+ endomysial cytotoxic T‑cell invasion of non‑necrotic fibres that defines PM. (This case showed rare CD3+ cells.)
- Deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9, “MAC”) on the sarcolemma of necrotic and non‑necrotic myofibres — a marker of complement‑mediated myofibre injury commonly seen in IMNM. (This case demonstrated MAC positivity on necrotising and non‑necrotising fibres.)
- Diffuse upregulation of MHC‑class I on fibres is often present in IMNM (not specific, but supportive).
- Absence of features typical of dermatomyositis: lack of prominent perifascicular atrophy, absence of perivascular CD4+/B‑cell–predominant infiltrates and endothelial capillary loss; DM often shows complement deposition in endomysial capillaries and MxA expression in perifascicular fibres (not the pattern seen here).

Clinical correlation: IMNM often associates with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies but up to ~30% are seronegative — so the biopsy pattern is essential for diagnosis and to guide immunotherapy. In this patient, the combination of necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage‑rich myophagocytosis, scant lymphocytes and sarcolemmal MAC deposition was diagnostic of IMNM and helped distinguish it from PM or DM.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which autoantibodies are most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (HMGCR, SRP), how does their presence or absence influence diagnostic confidence and expected treatment course, and how should a seronegative panel be interpreted in the context of supportive histology?

Direct answer
- The two autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Their detection substantially increases diagnostic confidence that the clinical syndrome and necrotising muscle biopsy represent autoimmune IMNM rather than another myopathy.
- Presence of a specific antibody influences prognosis and usual treatment intensity: SRP‑positive disease is classically severe, frequently refractory to steroids alone and often requires early multi‑agent immunotherapy (IVIG, rituximab, cyclophosphamide). HMGCR‑positive disease is strongly linked to prior statin exposure, can be persistent despite stopping the statin, and often responds well to IVIG (frequently requires sustained immunosuppression).  
- Absence of HMGCR and SRP (and a negative myositis panel) does not exclude IMNM. About one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative. When biopsy shows the characteristic picture (scattered necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition) and the clinical phenotype (acute/subacute severe proximal weakness, high CK, dysphagia/cardiac/respiratory risk), you should manage as IMNM while searching for triggers (drugs, statins, malignancy, infection) and monitoring response.

Clinical reasoning and application to the presented case
- The patient’s myositis panel was negative for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP yet her muscle biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition—histology that is diagnostic of IMNM. This exactly illustrates the key teaching point: seronegativity does not invalidate the histologic diagnosis.
- The clinical course—temporal relationship to a drug exposure (semaglutide), progressive weakness and high CK followed by prompt improvement after drug cessation and a short course of high‑dose steroids plus a single IVIG cycle, and durable remission off maintenance immunosuppression—fits a drug‑associated IMNM phenotype rather than classic antibody‑positive idiopathic IMNM. Antibody‑positive IMNM more often requires prolonged and sometimes escalating immunosuppression to maintain remission.

Practical management implications
- Workup: test for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (and an extended myositis panel), obtain MRI/EMG as needed, and perform muscle biopsy when serology is negative or uncertain. Evaluate for triggers (statin use, new drugs, cancer screening tailored to age and risk).
- Treatment principles: start high‑dose glucocorticoids for severe weakness; add IVIG early for severe disease or when HMGCR positivity is suspected (IVIG has good evidence in HMGCR IMNM) or for poor steroid response. For SRP‑positive or refractory cases, escalate to steroid‑sparing agents (rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) sooner. In drug‑triggered cases, stop the offending agent; if the patient improves rapidly and sustains remission (as in this case), prolonged immunosuppression may not be required.
- Follow‑up: monitor strength, CK, swallowing/respiratory status and cardiac signs; recheck serology only if it would change management (some antibodies are persistent, but lab sensitivity varies). If disease relapses or fails to improve promptly, treat as antibody‑positive IMNM with escalation.

Takeaway clinical pearl
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are highly specific markers that increase diagnostic certainty and predict a more refractory course that often needs aggressive, prolonged immunotherapy. However, a negative serology does not rule out IMNM: biopsy‑proven necrotising myopathy with compatible clinical features warrants treatment as IMNM and a search for triggers. The case illustrates a seronegative, likely drug‑associated IMNM that improved rapidly after drug withdrawal and brief immunotherapy—an expected, more favorable trajectory than typical antibody‑positive IMNM.

### [Management] What is your initial treatment strategy for severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy with dysphagia, including steroid dosing (pulse methylprednisolone and transition to oral prednisone), indications for IVIG, and early monitoring for respiratory, swallowing, and cardiac complications?

Bottom line
For severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with bulbar involvement (dysphagia) treat promptly with high‑dose IV corticosteroids, add IVIG early for severe/bulbar or rapidly progressive disease, and institute immediate focused monitoring for respiratory failure, aspiration risk and cardiac involvement. In the presented case this approach (methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily ×5 → oral prednisone 1 mg/kg and IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days) resulted in rapid CK fall and clinical recovery — a practical template for initial management.

Initial immunotherapy
- Pulse steroids: give IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily (typical range 500–1000 mg) for 3–5 days in life‑ or function‑threatening myositis (marked proximal weakness, bulbar dysfunction, rapidly rising CK). The team in this case used 1 g daily ×5.
- Transition to oral steroids: after pulses, convert to oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (usual practical max ~60–80 mg) and reassess frequently. Plan a taper guided by clinical strength, swallowing, and CK trend rather than fixed calendar once improving.
- IVIG indications and dosing: add IVIG early (total 2 g/kg, commonly given over 2–5 days) when there is bulbar involvement/dysphagia, severe weakness, respiratory compromise, very high CK, or when rapid effect is desired or when antibodies (anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR) predict IVIG responsiveness. In this case IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days was given because of persistent weakness and severe features and likely hastened improvement.
- When to add steroid‑sparing agents: if incomplete response after 2–4 weeks, steroid dependence, or relapsing disease, add a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant (eg, methotrexate, azathioprine) or consider rituximab/cyclophosphamide in refractory or life‑threatening cases. (The reported patient improved and did not require prolonged agents.)

Early and ongoing monitoring (prioritize)
1) Respiratory
- Baseline and serial measurements: bedside forced vital capacity (FVC) sitting and supine, negative inspiratory force (NIF/MIP), and pulse oximetry. Obtain arterial blood gas if hypercapnia suspected.
- Thresholds for escalation: FVC <50% predicted, a fall in FVC >20% when supine, or NIF less negative than about −30 cm H2O warrant ICU monitoring and low threshold for elective intubation to protect ventilation/airway.
- Monitor frequently (daily to several times daily) in the acute phase if bulbar weakness or declining FVC.

2) Swallowing and nutrition
- Otolaryngology/speech–language pathology bedside swallow evaluation before oral intake. Keep patient NPO until swallowing is judged safe if there is dysphagia or aspiration concern.
- Early enteral access (nasogastric or percutaneous gastrostomy if prolonged) should be considered to maintain nutrition and prevent aspiration. In practice, bulbar IMNM is an accepted indication to add IVIG early because of high aspiration risk.

3) Cardiac
- Baseline ECG, troponin (high‑sensitivity), BNP, and transthoracic echocardiography if any abnormalities or symptoms. Consider cardiology consult and cardiac MRI if myocarditis suspected.
- Continuous telemetry if troponin/BNP elevated, arrhythmia history, or cardiac MRI/echo abnormalities. In this case the patient had mild hs‑troponin elevation, markedly elevated proBNP, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema — all arguing for close cardiac monitoring and cardiology input.

4) Laboratory and infection surveillance
- Daily or near‑daily CK and basic metabolic panel early (to track muscle injury, renal function, and steroid side effects). Monitor glucose, CBC, electrolytes, and liver tests.
- VTE prophylaxis while immobilized.
- Be vigilant for infection: high‑dose steroids ± IVIG increase infection risk (the case patient subsequently developed pseudomonal urosepsis). If steroids are to be continued at immunosuppressive doses >3–4 weeks, consider Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis per institutional practice.

Supportive care and disposition
- Admit to monitored bed (step‑down/ICU) if bulbar dysfunction, declining FVC, or cardiac involvement.
- Early physical and occupational therapy when safe; swallow therapy.
- Coordinate rheumatology/neuromuscular, neurology, cardiology, speech–language pathology, and nutrition services.

How this case supports the approach
- The team used 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily ×5 then oral prednisone 1 mg/kg with IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days because of severe proximal weakness and dysphagia. CK fell markedly over 2 weeks and the patient regained baseline function off immunosuppression over months. Cardiac markers and MRI abnormalities prompted cardiac imaging and monitoring, and the patient later developed bacterial sepsis — underscoring infection risk with aggressive immunosuppression and the need for vigilance.

Clinical pearl
Treat severe IMNM early and aggressively when bulbar or respiratory or cardiac involvement is present: pulse steroids → transition to prednisone 1 mg/kg, add IVIG (2 g/kg) promptly for bulbar disease or rapidly progressive weakness, and institute frequent respiratory, swallow and cardiac monitoring to anticipate airway, ventilatory, or cardiac complications.

### [Clinical Pearls] Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, which elements in this case support a “probable” relationship between semaglutide and the myopathy (time relationship, alternative causes unlikely, response to withdrawal), and why is rechallenge generally inappropriate?

Short answer: The case meets the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre “probable” category because (1) the temporal sequence is compatible with a drug‑triggered immune myopathy, (2) competing explanations are unlikely based on history, serology and biopsy, and (3) there was a clinically meaningful response after drug withdrawal and immunotherapy. Rechallenge is generally inappropriate because of the high risk of provoking a recurrent — potentially more severe or life‑threatening — immune reaction and because a deliberate re‑exposure would be unethical when safer alternatives exist.

Why this is “probable” (WHO‑UMC reasoning tied to the case)
- Time relationship: the single semaglutide dose preceded the patient’s immediate intolerance and systemic illness (nausea, vomiting, AKI) within hours to days, and the progressive proximal weakness with a marked CK rise evolved over the expected interval for an immune‑mediated adverse event (weeks to months). The chronology (exposure → symptom onset → progressive myopathy) fulfills the required reasonable temporal association.
- Alternative causes unlikely: the patient denied statin or other known myotoxic exposures; myositis autoantibody panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) was negative; there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of a systemic connective‑tissue disease to explain the picture; no family history or other obvious metabolic/toxic cause was identified. Muscle biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage infiltration, membrane attack complex deposition and scant lymphocytes — histology consistent with IMNM and not simply metabolic myopathy or pure toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Response to withdrawal (dechallenge) and treatment: semaglutide was not continued, and after high‑dose steroids + IVIG the patient’s CK fell rapidly and her strength and function recovered to baseline. Importantly, she remained off immunosuppression and off GLP‑1 receptor agonists for many months with sustained clinical and biochemical remission — a pattern more typical of a drug‑triggered IMNM that resolves after removal of the trigger and a finite course of immunotherapy than of idiopathic IMNM, which frequently requires prolonged immunosuppression.

Important caveats that keep the label at “probable” rather than “certain”
- No pre‑exposure baseline CK is available, so a pre‑existing smouldering myopathy cannot be excluded definitively.
- Immunosuppression (steroids + IVIG) is a confounder for dechallenge interpretation — the improvement may reflect effective treatment rather than withdrawal alone. The durable remission off therapy and drug does, however, support a trigger relationship.
- Lack of a disease‑specific autoantibody (seronegative IMNM) limits mechanistic proof; about one‑third of IMNM cases are seronegative.

Why rechallenge is generally inappropriate in this context
- Safety/ethics: deliberate re‑exposure risks recurrence of the same immune process with unpredictable severity — dysphagia/respiratory muscle involvement, myocarditis, or fulminant necrotising myopathy can be life‑threatening. Exposing a recovered patient to that risk solely to prove causality is not ethically justified.
- Immunologic principle: immune‑mediated adverse reactions often become more rapid and severe on re‑exposure because of immunologic memory; a positive rechallenge therefore may cause a worse outcome than the index event.
- Clinical utility: a positive rechallenge would provide stronger causal proof but confers unacceptable patient risk, and a negative rechallenge could be misleading if immunomodulatory treatment or waning immune responsiveness confounds results.
- Alternatives to rechallenge: careful reporting to pharmacovigilance systems, case aggregation, and mechanistic studies (for example, searching for shared HLA associations or autoantibody discovery) are safer ways to establish causality at the population level.

Bottom line: The case fulfills the WHO‑UMC criteria for a “probable” adverse‑drug reaction — compatible timing, lack of plausible alternative explanations, and sustained clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide and treating the immune process — but rechallenge to prove causality would be inappropriate because it risks provoking a recurrent, potentially severe immune myopathy.

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with elevated transaminases and concomitant hyperCKemia, what clinical and laboratory clues help distinguish hepatic transaminitis from muscle-derived enzyme elevations (e.g., AST/ALT pattern, gamma-glutamyl transferase, bilirubin, imaging, and timing relative to CK), and how would you apply them in a patient with known NAFLD?

Takeaway: when AST and ALT are both elevated in a patient with concurrent CK elevation, ask first “liver or muscle?” Key discriminators are the pattern of AST vs ALT, liver-specific tests (GGT, bilirubin, synthetic function), temporal relationship to CK, prior baseline liver tests and imaging, and targeted muscle testing. Applying these systematically separates true hepatic transaminitis from muscle-derived transaminase release — a distinction that mattered in this patient with known NAFLD.

Why this works (pathophysiology, briefly)
- AST is present in both liver and skeletal muscle (cytosolic and mitochondrial isoforms); ALT is far more liver‑enriched. Significant muscle injury therefore raises AST proportionally more than ALT.  
- GGT and bilirubin are produced in the liver/biliary system and are not elevated from skeletal muscle injury. Synthetic markers (albumin, INR, platelets) reflect chronic hepatic function, not muscle breakdown.  
- Enzyme timing: CK rises rapidly with muscle injury and falls over days (CK half‑life ≈ 36 h). AST and ALT have their own half‑lives (AST shorter than ALT), so when transaminases are muscle‑derived they should move in parallel with CK; persistent transaminase elevation after CK normalizes points to hepatic origin.

Practical clinical and laboratory clues
- AST/ALT pattern
  - Muscle source: AST often ≥ ALT (AST≫ALT or AST>ALT), and transaminase elevations generally correlate with CK magnitude.  
  - Hepatic source (including NAFLD): ALT commonly > AST (especially early/mild NAFLD); AST>ALT can occur with fibrosis/cirrhosis or alcoholic injury.
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase
  - GGT elevated → supports hepatic/biliary origin. GGT normal → favors muscle source when CK is high. Alkaline phosphatase is less specific but helps when cholestasis is suspected.
- Bilirubin and synthetic markers
  - Elevated bilirubin, low albumin, prolonged INR, thrombocytopenia → support hepatic dysfunction, not muscle injury.
- CK and timing
  - If CK is high and AST/ALT rise at the same time, suspect muscle contribution. Repeat tests after CK normalizes: if transaminases fall with CK, muscle was the major source; if transaminases persist, liver is likely responsible.
- Imaging and prior tests
  - Ultrasound/MRI showing steatosis or biopsy-proven NAFLD supports chronic hepatic source. Prior baseline transaminases are invaluable.
- Targeted muscle testing if suspicion remains
  - Aldolase, LDH, myoglobin, myositis serologies, muscle MRI, EMG, and ultimately muscle biopsy for definitive myopathy diagnosis.

Applying this to the presented case
- Baseline data: the patient had chronic mild transaminase elevation 3 years before semaglutide, with ALT>AST, normal GGT, normal bilirubin and synthetic indices, ultrasound showing hepatosteatosis and liver biopsy consistent with NASH. Those data argue for a chronic hepatic source (NAFLD) for her baseline transaminase abnormalities.  
- At myopathy presentation: CK became markedly elevated (peak ~3992 U/L) and AST (191) slightly exceeded ALT (165). That change (AST rising with CK and AST≥ALT) is compatible with a muscle contribution to the transaminase elevation during the acute myopathy.  
- Follow‑up behavior: after immunosuppression and recovery the CK normalized and the patient’s transaminases returned to the same borderline elevations seen previously (ALT>AST), consistent with chronic hepatic (NAFLD) rather than ongoing muscle injury. This temporal pattern — transaminases rising with CK during active myopathy but returning to baseline hepatic pattern once CK normalized — is the classic discriminator.

Bottom line practical approach at the bedside
1. Measure CK whenever transaminases are elevated without clear liver syndrome.  
2. Check GGT, bilirubin and basic synthetic function (INR, albumin, platelets).  
3. Compare AST vs ALT pattern and prior liver tests; review imaging/biopsy if available.  
4. Reassess after CK normalizes: a fall of AST/ALT with CK → muscle source; persistence → liver.  
5. If muscle source is suspected, pursue myositis workup (aldolase, myositis serologies, MRI/EMG, biopsy) rather than escalating hepatic workup.  
6. In patients with known NAFLD, don’t assume all transaminase elevations are hepatic — use the CK/GGT/timing strategy to avoid misattribution.

In this patient the preexisting NAFLD explained the chronic ALT>AST and the persistent mild transaminitis after CK normalized, while the transient AST predominance and marked CK elevation during her illness signaled a superimposed muscle source (IMNM) during the acute episode.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you evaluate and interpret possible cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies—specifically, how do high-sensitivity troponin, natriuretic peptides, echocardiography, and cardiac MRI showing edema without late gadolinium enhancement inform the differential between myocarditis and stress cardiomyopathy and guide management?

Short answer
Inflammatory myopathies can involve the heart in several ways (myocarditis, arrhythmia, heart‑failure, or stress cardiomyopathy). Interpretation relies on pattern and magnitude of biomarkers, imaging, and clinical course. In this case the combination of a very high pro‑BNP (4358 pg/mL), only a mild hs‑troponin rise (62 pg/mL), preserved LVEF by echocardiography, CMR showing myocardial edema but no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and rapid clinical and imaging recovery favors a transient, non‑necrotic process — most consistent with stress (takotsubo‑type) cardiomyopathy or very early/limited myocarditis rather than frank necrotizing myocarditis. The available data supported conservative management and monitoring rather than escalation to cardiac‑directed immunosuppression; subsequent normalisation of echo/CMR and resolution of arrhythmia confirm the transient nature.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Biomarkers
  - High‑sensitivity troponin: cardiac‑specific troponin elevation signals myocyte injury. The magnitude matters — myocarditis and MI commonly produce larger troponin rises than seen here. Importantly, in patients with severe skeletal myopathy troponin T can be spuriously elevated because of skeletal muscle expression of troponin T isoforms; troponin I is more specific for cardiac injury. A single, mild hs‑troponin rise (62 pg/mL; lab upper limit 53) is nondiagnostic and must be interpreted in the larger clinical and imaging context.
  - Natriuretic peptides: marked pro‑BNP elevation (4358 pg/mL) reflects myocardial wall stress and is commonly high in takotsubo/stress cardiomyopathy and in heart‑failure due to myocarditis. BNP is less specific for mechanism than troponin but is useful for gauging hemodynamic impact and for follow‑up.
- Echocardiography
  - Assesses global and regional systolic function. In myocarditis you may see reduced LVEF or regional wall‑motion abnormalities; in stress cardiomyopathy you classically see regional wall‑motion abnormalities that do not follow a single coronary distribution and often transiently reduce EF. In this patient LVEF was preserved (65–70%) with only mild regional hypokinesis on CMR, arguing against significant global necrotic myocarditis or infarction.
- Cardiac MRI (CMR)
  - The 2018 Lake Louise update requires evidence of myocardial edema (T2 imaging/mapping) plus a T1‑based marker (LGE, abnormal T1 mapping or increased ECV) to make a confident noninvasive diagnosis of acute myocarditis. Edema without a T1‑based abnormality (no LGE and presumably normal T1/ECV) may indicate very early or mild inflammation without irreversible injury, or a noninflammatory process that causes edema (stress cardiomyopathy can cause edema).
  - LGE indicates focal necrosis or fibrosis. Absence of LGE lowers the likelihood of permanent myocyte necrosis and predicts a better prognosis; it also argues against fulminant necrotizing myocarditis.
- Pattern recognition
  - Takotsubo/stress cardiomyopathy: modest troponin increases, marked BNP elevations, regional wall‑motion abnormalities in noncoronary patterns, myocardial edema on CMR, and minimal or absent LGE; rapid recovery is typical.
  - Autoimmune myocarditis/IMNM cardiac involvement: may produce larger troponin rises, LGE in characteristic nonischemic patterns (often subepicardial/inferolateral), reduced EF, arrhythmias, and may require targeted cardiac immunosuppression if there is ongoing inflammation or dysfunction.

Application to this case
- The patient had: mild hs‑troponin elevation, very high pro‑BNP, preserved LVEF on echo, CMR with focal edema and mild hypokinesis but no LGE, and clinical/imaging normalization on follow‑up. These findings do not meet full CMR criteria for definitive myocarditis (edema without T1‑based abnormality) and are entirely compatible with stress cardiomyopathy or very early/limited myocarditis without necrosis.
- She also received systemic immunosuppression for IMNM (pulse steroids + IVIG), which could have treated any coexisting immune myocarditis. The lack of persistent cardiac dysfunction and the rapid recovery argue against ongoing necrotizing myocarditis that would mandate prolonged cardiac‑directed immunosuppression.

Practical management approach (how to act)
1. Treat the patient, not the single test:
   - Hemodynamically stable, preserved EF, and no high‑grade arrhythmia → conservative management, cardiac monitoring, guideline‑directed supportive care.
2. Short‑term measures:
   - Cardiac telemetry while in hospital for arrhythmia surveillance.
   - Serial hs‑troponin and BNP to assess trajectory (falling levels support transient injury).
   - Baseline and follow‑up ECGs.
   - Avoid intense exertion for 3 months if myocarditis cannot be confidently excluded.
3. Imaging follow‑up:
   - Repeat echocardiogram and consider repeat CMR in 4–12 weeks if symptoms or biomarkers persist; normalization supports transient process (as occurred here).
4. When to escalate:
   - New or worsening LV dysfunction, persistent or rising troponin/BNP, hemodynamic compromise, uncontrolled arrhythmia, or CMR showing progressive LGE → cardiology consultation and consideration of endomyocardial biopsy and targeted immunosuppression (in consultation with rheumatology and heart‑failure specialists).
5. Specific to inflammatory myopathy:
   - Because systemic immunosuppression was already indicated for IMNM, coordinate care with rheumatology. If cardiac inflammation is proven and progressive, intensification of immunotherapy may be warranted; if cardiac findings resolve, long‑term cardiac immunosuppression is usually unnecessary.

Take‑home pearls
- Edema on CMR without LGE indicates inflammation/edema without clear irreversible injury; it does not by itself meet formal noninvasive criteria for myocarditis.
- Small troponin rises in a patient with skeletal myopathy should be interpreted cautiously; troponin I is more cardiac‑specific than troponin T in this setting.
- A pattern of modest troponin elevation, high BNP, edema on CMR, absent LGE, preserved EF and rapid recovery favors stress cardiomyopathy or transient inflammation over necrotizing myocarditis.
- Management is driven by hemodynamics, arrhythmia risk, imaging evidence of necrosis/fibrosis, and clinical trajectory — serial monitoring and repeat imaging are essential; biopsy and aggressive cardiac immunosuppression are reserved for progressive or severe cases.

### [Management] What preventive measures would you implement to mitigate infection risk when initiating high-dose glucocorticoids and IVIG for inflammatory myopathy (e.g., vaccination review, PJP prophylaxis considerations, glucose monitoring, catheter stewardship), and how would you monitor for and manage infectious complications?

Direct answer
When you start high‑dose glucocorticoids (eg, IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by prednisone ≈1 mg/kg) and IVIG for inflammatory myopathy you must treat the patient as newly immunosuppressed and take concrete steps to reduce predictable infectious risks, to screen for latent infections that can reactivate, and to monitor closely so infections are recognized and treated early.

Practical checklist (what to do before or at initiation)
- Vaccination review
  - Give indicated inactivated vaccines if there is time before immunosuppression (ideally ≥2 weeks before). Priority: seasonal influenza, pneumococcal (per schedule), Tdap, hepatitis B if susceptible, SARS‑CoV‑2 booster per guidelines.
  - Do NOT give live vaccines once high‑dose steroids are planned (live vaccines should be given ≥4 weeks before immunosuppression).
  - Recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is non‑live and preferred over live zoster; give ideally before immunosuppression when feasible, but recognize response may be blunted if given after high‑dose steroids/IVIG.
- Screen for latent/reactivable infections
  - Hepatitis B serologies (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, anti‑HBs). If HBsAg+ or anti‑HBc+ (especially if HBsAg−/anti‑HBc+), consult Hepatology/ID and start antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir/tenofovir) when significant immunosuppression is expected.
  - TB screening (IGRA or PPD and CXR as indicated) in persons with risk factors; treat latent TB before prolonged immunosuppression when feasible.
  - HIV test (baseline).
  - Varicella zoster immune status if history unclear (to counsel about risk and therapy).
  - Check baseline CBC, CMP, creatinine, glucose/HbA1c (to guide prophylaxis dosing and steroid‑diabetes planning).
  - Consider testing for IgA deficiency before IVIG infusion (anaphylaxis risk) and be aware that recent IVIG will interfere with some serologies (eg, anti‑HBs).
- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis
  - Offer PJP prophylaxis if prednisone ≥20 mg/day (or equivalent) is expected for ≥4 weeks or if other significant immunosuppression is added (rituximab, high‑dose cyclophosphamide, etc.). First‑line: TMP‑SMX (single‑strength daily or DS 3×/week). Alternatives: atovaquone, inhaled pentamidine, or dapsone (check G6PD).
- Catheter and device stewardship
  - Avoid indwelling urinary catheters unless absolutely necessary; if placed, use strict aseptic insertion and daily review with prompt removal.
  - Follow central line insertion and maintenance bundles (chlorhexidine skin prep, maximal barrier precautions, sterile dressings, daily site assessment).
- Metabolic and glucose management
  - Start routine capillary glucose monitoring (eg, q4–6 hours while inpatient) and plan for early insulin management: steroids commonly cause marked hyperglycemia—use basal‑bolus or scheduled insulin rather than only PRN sliding‑scale if significant elevations are expected or patient has diabetes.
  - Check electrolytes, potassium and magnesium periodically (steroids and infections can alter them).
- IVIG precautions
  - Pre‑medicate for infusion reactions when appropriate (acetaminophen, antihistamine); hydrate to reduce renal risk, infuse slowly in renal insufficiency, monitor for hemolysis, thrombosis, and TRALI. Inform that IVIG may blunt vaccine responses and transiently affect serologies.

Monitoring while on therapy
- Low threshold for surveillance and early evaluation of fever or subtle signs of infection. Immunosuppressed patients may have atypical or muted inflammatory responses.
- Daily assessment of lines/catheters and prompt removal when no longer needed.
- Routine inpatient monitoring: vitals, focused exam, daily review of glucose logs, CBC and creatinine as clinically indicated.
- If new fever or clinical change: obtain directed cultures (blood, urine if urinary symptoms or catheter present, sputum if respiratory), CXR for pulmonary symptoms, and start empiric antibiotics based on source and local ecology—don’t delay cultures or source control.
- Specific for PJP suspicion: monitor oxygenation and consider early LDCT and empiric TMP‑SMX pending diagnostic testing in hypoxemic patients.

Management of infectious complications (principles)
- Early source control: remove infected catheters/foley, drain abscesses, debride if needed.
- Empiric antimicrobial choices should cover likely pathogens and local resistance patterns; for severe healthcare‑associated UTI/sepsis consider anti‑Pseudomonal beta‑lactam (eg, cefepime, piperacillin‑tazobactam) until cultures return—then de‑escalate.
- Balance immunosuppression: for life‑threatening sepsis, hold or reduce immunosuppressive agents in consultation with rheumatology and ID. Short pulses of steroids for inflammatory myopathy may be stopped or tapered during septic shock; however, physiologic stress‑dose steroids may be needed if adrenal insufficiency is a concern.
- Involve Infectious Diseases early for complex or opportunistic infections, HBV reactivation, persistent bacteremia, or multidrug‑resistant organisms.
- For viral reactivation (HBV, VZV, CMV) follow standard antiviral treatments and consider prophylaxis strategies for future immunosuppression.

How this applies to the presented case
- The patient received IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by prednisone ≈1 mg/kg and IVIG and subsequently developed septic shock from a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection about 12 days after discharge. Likely contributors: recent high‑dose steroid exposure, possible urinary catheter use during prolonged hospitalization, steroid‑related hyperglycemia, and immune modulation from IVIG/steroids.
- Measures that could have reduced that risk: strict avoidance/early removal of urinary catheters, daily catheter‑need reassessment and catheter‑care bundles, rigorous glucose monitoring and insulin management, early screening for and treatment of latent infections (HBV/TB/HIV) pre‑treatment, and clear discharge instructions with low threshold to seek care for fever or urinary symptoms.
- PJP prophylaxis may not have prevented her UTI but would have been indicated if prednisone >20 mg/day was expected for ≥4 weeks; in her case prednisone was tapered over ~2 months, so prophylaxis would have been reasonable.

Clinical pearl (one‑line takeaways)
- Before high‑dose steroids ±IVIG: update inactivated vaccines when possible, screen for HBV/TB/HIV/varicella, plan PJP prophylaxis if prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks, enforce catheter and line stewardship, and institute proactive glucose monitoring and management; monitor closely for fever or subtle signs of infection and involve ID early for severe or unusual infections.

### [Management] For a patient with likely drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy temporally associated with semaglutide, how would you counsel regarding future use of GLP-1 receptor agonists (class avoidance vs cautious reintroduction) and what alternative strategies would you consider for obesity and NAFLD management?

Bottom line
- In this patient I would recommend permanent avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists (and likely other agents with GLP‑1 activity such as tirzepatide). The myopathy was biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), temporally linked to a single semaglutide dose, required high‑dose steroids and IVIG, and then resolved off the drug. Given the severity of the immune‑mediated reaction and the ethical and clinical risk of provoking recurrence, rechallenge or class‑wide reintroduction is not advisable in routine practice.

Why avoid the class in this case
- The case meets criteria for “probable” drug causality (WHO‑UMC): clear temporal relationship, lack of alternative explanation, clinical improvement after drug withdrawal plus immunotherapy.  
- IMNM is an aggressive, immune‑mediated process rather than a transient toxic rhabdomyolysis. This patient had progressive proximal weakness, CK rise to ~4,000 U/L, and muscle biopsy with necrosis, macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex — all consistent with IMNM. She required IV methylprednisolone and IVIG to recover.  
- Re‑exposure to a drug that has triggered an immune‑mediated myopathy risks a more rapid and severe recurrence; rechallenge would therefore be unethical unless there were compelling reasons and very close specialist oversight. Case reports of muscle injury have also been described with other incretin agents (e.g., tirzepatide), so cross‑reactivity or a class effect cannot be excluded.

How to counsel the patient (practical script)
- “Your muscle disease was biopsy‑proven IMNM that began after semaglutide. Although we cannot prove causation beyond doubt, the timing and the severity make it likely the drug triggered an autoimmune myopathy. Because this was serious and required IVIG and steroids, I recommend you do not take semaglutide again and avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists in the future. We will document this clearly in your chart and report it to pharmacovigilance. If you are later offered another drug in this class, please bring it to our team first — rechallenge would only be considered in rare, carefully controlled circumstances with full informed consent and specialist oversight.”

Alternatives for treating obesity and NAFLD (practical, evidence‑based options)
1) Reinforce first‑line therapy: intensive lifestyle intervention
   - Structured, multidisciplinary programs targeting a 7–10% weight loss (calorie reduction, increased physical activity, behavioral therapy) remain the cornerstone for NAFLD improvement and are effective for many patients.
   - Refer to dietitians, exercise physiologists, and behavioral health for sustained programs.

2) Bariatric/metabolic surgery
   - If the patient meets guideline criteria (typically BMI ≥40, or ≥35 with obesity‑related comorbidities such as NAFLD/NASH), bariatric surgery offers the most durable weight loss and has been shown to improve steatosis, NASH, and fibrosis in many patients.
   - Discuss surgical risks and a formal surgical evaluation.

3) Non‑GLP‑1 pharmacotherapy for weight loss (choose based on comorbidities and contraindications)
   - Phentermine‑topiramate: high efficacy for weight loss; consider cardiovascular history and tolerability.
   - Naltrexone‑bupropion: alternative with different mechanism; watch for hypertension, psychiatric history, and seizure risk.
   - Orlistat: modest weight loss, acts in the gut; consider GI tolerability and lipid lowering effects.
   - These agents are less effective than GLP‑1 RAs on average but are reasonable alternatives when GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated.

4) Treating NAFLD/NASH specifically
   - Weight loss through lifestyle or surgery is the primary treatment and improves histology.  
   - Pioglitazone can improve steatohepatitis (particularly in biopsy‑proven NASH), including in non‑diabetics, but causes weight gain, edema and has other long‑term risks—use after shared decision‑making and cardiology/hepatology input given her cardiac history and proBNP elevation earlier.  
   - Vitamin E (800 IU/day) is an option in non‑diabetic adults with biopsy‑proven NASH (weigh benefits vs risks).  
   - SGLT2 inhibitors (if she has diabetes) and statins for cardiovascular risk reduction may have modest hepatic benefits; statins should be used when indicated despite prior muscle disease only after careful review (note: patient denied prior statin use and IMNM was seronegative for HMGCR).  
   - Refer to hepatology for consideration of clinical trials of emerging NASH therapies, fibrosis staging (transient elastography), and longitudinal monitoring.

5) Monitoring and documentation
   - Prominently document “severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy attributed to semaglutide — avoid GLP‑1 RAs” in problem list and drug allergy/alert systems.  
   - Report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance registries.  
   - Continue surveillance for NAFLD progression (liver labs, fibrosis assessment) and for recurrent myopathic symptoms; check CK only if symptoms suggest recurrence.

When (rare) cautious re‑exposure might be discussed
- Only in exceptional situations (e.g., patient refuses all other options and the clinical benefit is judged to outweigh risk) would re‑challenge be considered — and then only after multidisciplinary review (rheumatology, neurology, hepatology), with explicit informed consent, baseline CK and muscle testing, very close serial monitoring, and readiness to stop immediately if symptoms or CK rise. Given this patient’s course, that would be a high‑risk choice and is not recommended.

Bottom line for this case
- The safest, evidence‑based approach is class avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists given a biopsy‑proven, severe IMNM temporally linked to semaglutide. Focus management on intensive lifestyle change, consideration of bariatric surgery if eligible, alternative anti‑obesity medications, and hepatology‑directed therapies (weight loss, pioglitazone or vitamin E where appropriate, and clinical trials).
