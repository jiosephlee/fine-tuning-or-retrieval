### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a creatine kinase (CK) that rises over months after exposure to a new medication, what clinical and laboratory features should prompt suspicion for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than toxic rhabdomyolysis?

Think first about the tempo and the tissue response. Toxic rhabdomyolysis is usually an acute toxic or metabolic injury that produces a very rapid rise in CK with myoglobinuria/AKI and then improves promptly after the offending exposure is removed and supportive care is given. Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is an autoimmune, subacute to progressive disease that often continues or worsens despite stopping a putative trigger and requires immunotherapy.

Clinically, IMNM should be suspected when weakness is subacute–progressive over weeks to months (rather than abrupt), is symmetric and predominantly proximal (often with neck‑flexor weakness and bulbar symptoms such as dysphagia or dysphonia), and when the patient deteriorates or fails to recover after withdrawal of the new medication. Dysphagia or early respiratory or cardiac involvement (troponin/proBNP abnormalities, myocarditis features) points toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Laboratory and ancillary features that favour IMNM include a persistently elevated CK that remains high or rises over weeks–months rather than a single dramatic spike that rapidly falls with hydration; modest to marked elevations in ESR/CRP; myopathic changes on EMG with irritability; muscle MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle oedema; and, most definitively, muscle biopsy showing widespread myofibre necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition and relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. The presence of myositis‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) supports IMNM when positive, but up to one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude the diagnosis. By contrast, toxic rhabdomyolysis typically shows necrosis without the immune activation pattern (complement deposition, macrophage‑predominant inflammatory response) and resolves after stopping the toxin and supportive care.

Finally, the therapeutic response is informative: improvement with immunosuppression and IVIG after drug cessation supports an immune‑mediated process, whereas uncomplicated toxic rhabdomyolysis improves with withdrawal and supportive measures alone. In practice, when you see progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, persistent or rising CK over months after exposure to a new drug, inflammatory markers or cardiac involvement, and biopsy/MRI/EMG findings as above, treat and investigate for IMNM rather than attributing the syndrome to simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.

### [Morning Report] What are the core elements of the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) criteria that support a diagnosis of IMNM, and how does seronegativity for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies affect the diagnostic approach?

The ENMC diagnostic framework for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is fundamentally clinicopathologic: it requires a compatible clinical syndrome, supportive laboratory/imaging/EMG findings, a characteristic muscle biopsy, and exclusion of alternate explanations. Clinically the patient typically has subacute to rapidly progressive, symmetric proximal weakness often with dysphagia or respiratory involvement and markedly elevated serum CK. Ancillary testing that supports the diagnosis includes myopathic changes on EMG and muscle edema on MRI. The histopathologic hallmarks are numerous necrotic and regenerating myofibres with macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, scant lymphocytic infiltrates, sarcolemmal/microvascular membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition, and relative preservation of endomysial architecture compared with polymyositis. Demonstration of one of the disease‑associated autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) is highly supportive and helps phenotype prognosis and treatment response. Equally important in the ENMC approach is excluding other causes — toxin/drug myopathy, metabolic myopathies, hereditary myopathies, infection, and paraneoplastic syndromes.

Seronegativity for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP changes the weighting of the diagnostic workup but does not rule out IMNM. About one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so when antibodies are absent you rely more heavily on the clinical picture, imaging/EMG, and, above all, the muscle biopsy and exclusion of mimics. In practice that means: interpret a compatible history (rapid proximal weakness, very high CK), supportive MRI/EMG, and a biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage predominance and C5b‑9 deposition as sufficient to make the diagnosis of seronegative IMNM; search carefully for triggers (statins, other drugs, infections, cancer) and consider repeating autoantibody testing or broader panels if suspicion remains. Finally, an empirical therapeutic trial of immunotherapy (with careful monitoring) may both treat the disease and provide diagnostic confirmation when biopsy and other data point toward IMNM — as in this case, where characteristic biopsy findings plus dramatic clinical and CK improvement after immunosuppression and withdrawal of semaglutide support a diagnosis of IMNM despite negative HMGCR/SRP antibodies.

### [Pathophysiology] Which histopathologic findings on muscle biopsy distinguish IMNM from polymyositis and dermatomyositis?

The single most important distinguishing feature is the pattern and cellular composition of muscle injury: IMNM is defined by prominent myofibre necrosis with abundant macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and only minimal lymphocytic inflammation, whereas polymyositis and dermatomyositis show characteristic and different inflammatory patterns.

In practice this means that on biopsy IMNM shows widespread necrotic and regenerating fibres, frequent CD68+ macrophages engulfing debris, diffuse sarcolemmal up‑regulation of MHC class I, and often complement (membrane attack complex) deposition on necrotic fibres — but only sparse CD3+/CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates. By contrast polymyositis classically demonstrates an endomysial inflammatory infiltrate rich in CD8+ T cells that invade otherwise non‑necrotic, MHC‑I–expressing fibres (true fibre invasion), implicating direct T‑cell–mediated cytotoxicity. Dermatomyositis shows a perifascicular and perivascular pattern with capillary loss, complement (MAC) deposition in endomysial capillaries, perifascicular atrophy, and a more prominent perimysial/perivascular B‑cell and plasmacytoid dendritic cell–predominant inflammation (MxA staining often positive).

Thus, when necrosis with macrophage predominance and few lymphocytes is the dominant lesion, IMNM is favored; the presence of endomysial CD8+ fibre invasion points to polymyositis, and perifascicular atrophy with capillary/vascular complement deposition points to dermatomyositis. Correlation with serology (eg, anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in IMNM) and clinical phenotype completes the diagnosis.

### [Pathophysiology] What roles do anti–HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti–signal recognition particle (SRP) autoantibodies play in IMNM pathogenesis and phenotype, and how should their absence influence clinical decision-making?

Anti–HMG‑CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti–signal recognition particle (SRP) autoantibodies are the two best‑characterized serologic markers in immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and they help explain both disease biology and expected clinical behavior — but they are not the whole story.

Mechanistically, both autoantibodies identify muscle‑restricted antigens and are associated with immune‑mediated, complement‑ and macrophage‑driven myofibre necrosis rather than a primarily lymphocytic, MHC‑I–driven myositis. Pathology in antibody‑positive IMNM commonly shows myofibre necrosis with membrane attack complex deposition and prominent CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis, which supports a model in which humoral immunity (autoantibodies and complement) and innate effector cells mediate myofibre injury. Whether the circulating autoantibodies are directly pathogenic or serve as biomarkers of a broader autoreactive process remains incompletely settled, but their presence reliably identifies distinct clinical phenotypes and prognoses.

Clinically, anti‑HMGCR IMNM classically clusters with prior statin exposure, occurs more commonly in older adults, presents with markedly elevated CK and severe proximal weakness, and often requires prolonged, multi‑agent immunotherapy — many patients have a protracted course with frequent relapses if therapy is withdrawn too quickly. Anti‑SRP IMNM tends to present with very severe weakness, earlier functional decline, and a higher likelihood of extramuscular involvement (notably respiratory muscle and sometimes cardiac involvement); it is often more refractory to treatment and carries a worse short‑term prognosis.

Importantly, about a third of IMNM patients are seronegative for HMGCR and SRP. Seronegativity does not exclude IMNM: the diagnosis rests on the clinical picture (rapid proximal weakness, high CK), supportive MRI, and characteristic biopsy findings of necrosis with macrophage predominance and complement deposition. In seronegative cases the differential shifts: drug‑induced necrotising myopathy and other yet‑unidentified autoantibodies become more likely, and the clinical course may be more variable — some drug‑related cases improve after stopping the offending agent plus a limited immunosuppressive course, whereas occult idiopathic IMNM can still require prolonged therapy.

How this should influence clinical decision‑making: don’t wait for serologies to act if the patient is severe. Obtain a myositis panel and other relevant testing, but treat empirically based on severity, biopsy, and trajectory — high‑dose steroids and IVIG are appropriate for fulminant disease while you rule out alternate diagnoses. In a seronegative patient, intensify the search for triggers (recent drugs, supplements, statin exposure, cancer), recheck serologies if negative initially, and use biopsy and imaging to guide prognosis and intensity/duration of therapy. For suspected drug‑associated IMNM (as in this case following semaglutide), immediate withdrawal of the agent combined with a tailored, often shorter immunosuppressive course may be reasonable when the patient improves rapidly and remains stable off therapy; conversely, persistent weakness or recurrent CK rise warrants escalation and a treatment strategy similar to antibody‑positive IMNM.

In summary: anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP define biologically and clinically distinct, often more refractory forms of IMNM and help prognosticate and guide expectations about treatment duration. Their absence, however, does not rule out IMNM and should shift the clinician’s focus toward identifying reversible triggers and relying on histology and clinical course to determine the urgency and duration of immunosuppression. In the presented patient, negative SRP/HMGCR serologies support—but do not prove—a drug‑related, potentially self‑limited IMNM and justify the pragmatic approach taken: stop the suspected agent, give targeted immunotherapy for severe disease, and follow closely off immunosuppression.

### [Pathophysiology] How does complement-mediated membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition contribute to myofiber injury in IMNM?

Complement-mediated injury in IMNM is best understood as an antibody‑driven, non‑cell‑mediated attack on the myofibre membrane that culminates in necrosis rather than classic lymphocyte‑directed cytotoxicity. In patients with IMNM the sequence is typically: circulating autoantibodies (for example anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP when present) or other immune complexes bind antigens at or near the myofibre sarcolemma, this immune complex formation activates the classical complement pathway, and terminal complement components assemble as C5b‑9—the membrane attack complex (MAC)—on the sarcolemmal surface. Insertion of MAC pores increases sarcolemmal permeability, allowing uncontrolled calcium influx, disruption of excitation–contraction coupling, mitochondrial injury, activation of calcium‑dependent proteases and phospholipases, and finally irreversible myofibre necrosis.

Pathologically this explains several hallmark features of IMNM: necrotic and regenerating fibres with prominent macrophage myophagocytosis but only sparse T‑cell infiltrates, and positive C5b‑9 staining on necrotic (and sometimes adjacent non‑necrotic) fibres. MAC deposition therefore acts as an initiator of myofibre death and a stimulus for secondary innate inflammation (macrophage recruitment and phagocytosis), rather than a T‑cell–mediated process. Clinically, this mechanism accounts for the brisk CK elevations and the responsiveness of many cases to therapies that reduce antibody production or complement activity (corticosteroids, IVIG, complement inhibitors in experimental settings), because lowering antibody titers or interfering with complement assembly prevents further MAC formation and ongoing muscle injury.

### [Morning Report] What is the typical MRI pattern of thigh muscles in IMNM, and how can MRI guide both diagnosis and biopsy site selection?

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy produces a characteristic, fairly stereotyped MRI appearance that both supports the diagnosis of an active inflammatory myopathy and helps choose the best biopsy target.

On fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR or T2 with fat suppression) IMNM most commonly shows symmetric, proximal-predominant muscle edema — classically involving the thigh extensor compartment, especially the quadriceps (vastus lateralis and intermedius), and often the adductors. The edema may be diffuse or patchy but tends to be bilaterally symmetric. Early/active disease shows high T2/STIR signal without much fatty replacement; chronic disease shows muscle atrophy and T1 hyperintense fatty infiltration. Subcutaneous and fascial edema can also be present. By contrast, other myopathies have different distributions (eg, dermatomyositis with perifascial and patchy muscle involvement, inclusion body myositis with selective distal/atrophic patterns).

Why MRI matters clinically: first, the pattern — symmetric proximal thigh edema with relative quadriceps predominance — raises the pretest probability for an inflammatory necrotising process rather than pure toxic rhabdomyolysis or neurogenic atrophy. Second, and most practically, MRI directs biopsy to the most informative site. Target the muscle showing active T2/STIR hyperintensity (ongoing edema/necrosis) and avoid muscles that are end‑stage (marked fatty replacement or severe atrophy), because necrotic/regenerating fibres and inflammatory markers are most likely to be present in edematous, not end‑stage, muscle. Bilateral imaging is useful because involvement can be patchy and the contralateral same‑named muscle sometimes provides a better target. Finally, MRI can guide needle versus open biopsy, help interpret mildly elevated CK when biopsy is equivocal, and provide a baseline for monitoring treatment (edema resolves with successful immunotherapy; fatty replacement does not).

In short: look for symmetric proximal thigh (especially quadriceps) T2/STIR hyperintensity for IMNM; biopsy the area of maximal active edema rather than fatty‑replaced tissue to maximize diagnostic yield.

### [Morning Report] Outline a high-yield initial workup for suspected IMNM, including laboratory tests, imaging, and tissue studies.

When you suspect immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) the initial workup must establish the diagnosis, exclude mimics (toxic/rhabdomyolysis, metabolic, infectious, paraneoplastic), assess severity (including aspiration and cardiac risk), and obtain tests that will guide therapy and prognosis. In practice I look for three parallel streams of data at the bedside and in the first 48–72 hours: laboratory serology/metabolic testing, targeted imaging/electrophysiology, and a muscle biopsy directed by imaging.

First obtain focused laboratory studies. Measure serum creatine kinase (CK) and, if available, aldolase to quantify muscle injury and to follow response; check AST/ALT and recognize that modest transaminase elevations may be muscle‑derived. Obtain basic metabolic panel, calcium, phosphate, magnesium and creatinine to exclude metabolic causes and to guide hydration/IVIG dosing. Check inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and thyroid‑stimulating hormone because thyroid disease can mimic myopathy. Routinely screen for myositis‑specific and myositis‑associated autoantibodies early: at minimum anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (the two autoantibodies most strongly tied to IMNM), and a broader myositis panel (Jo‑1 and other anti‑tRNA synthetases, Mi‑2, MDA5, TIF1‑γ, NXP2) plus ANA and extractable nuclear antigen profile as clinically indicated. Also obtain viral serologies and HIV testing where relevant, and review medication/supplement exposure carefully (statins, red yeast rice, new drugs such as GLP‑1 agonists in this case). Because IMNM can be paraneoplastic, perform age‑ and sex‑appropriate malignancy screening (basic CT imaging or PET/CT if clinical concern persists).

Second, get targeted imaging and electrophysiology. Contrast or fluid‑sensitive MRI of affected muscles is high‑yield: it localises active inflammatory/edematous muscle, helps choose the biopsy site, and documents distribution (IMNM typically causes symmetric proximal edema). Obtain ECG, troponin and consider transthoracic echocardiogram and cardiac MRI when there are cardiac symptoms or troponin/proBNP elevations, because myocarditis or cardiac involvement can occur and changes management. High‑resolution chest imaging is indicated when interstitial lung disease is suspected. Electromyography with nerve conduction studies can support a myopathic process, detect irritable myopathy, and help exclude neuropathy; it is complementary but not a substitute for biopsy.

Third, pursue tissue diagnosis with a muscle biopsy guided by MRI. The biopsy should be full‑thickness (not needle only if possible), from an actively involved but not end‑stage muscle; include fresh frozen tissue for histochemistry and immunostains plus paraffin for immunohistochemistry. Expect to see the hallmark of IMNM: scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, complement (membrane attack complex) deposition, upregulation of MHC‑I, and relatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates. Send for special stains (acid phosphatase, MHC‑I), CD68/CD3 immunostaining, and C5b‑9 (MAC) when available. Correlate histology with serology: anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP positivity supports IMNM and predicts a more refractory course; seronegativity does not exclude IMNM and makes clinicopathologic correlation essential. Consider muscle culture and PCR if infection is a concern.

Finally, integrate the results quickly to triage therapy. Very high CK, bulbar involvement (dysphagia) or respiratory/cardiac compromise require prompt immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) even before autoantibody results return; biopsy and serologies then refine prognosis and duration of therapy. In every patient review exposures and discontinue suspect offending agents (statins historically; increasingly suspect drugs should be recorded — as in this semaglutide case), and institute supportive care (swallow evaluation, aspiration precautions, physical therapy, DVT prophylaxis, monitoring for treatment complications).

Taken together, these steps establish the diagnosis, exclude mimics, gauge disease severity, and provide the immunopathologic and serologic information that guides treatment intensity and expected recovery.

### [Clinical Pearls] How can you differentiate hepatic versus myopathic sources of transaminase elevation in a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy?

When you see transaminase elevations in a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy you must stop asking “liver or muscle?” and start asking “what other data tie the enzymes to muscle versus hepatocyte injury?” The distinction is clinical and biochemical — follow the pattern and the corroborating tests rather than any single number.

First, obtain a CK (and preferably aldolase and LDH) at the same time as AST/ALT. In muscle injury CK tracks with AST and often with ALT; a rising CK that parallels rises in AST (and to a lesser extent ALT) strongly points to a myopathic source. By contrast, isolated transaminase elevation with a normal CK argues for a hepatic source.

Second, examine other liver-specific tests. If alkaline phosphatase, GGT, bilirubin, albumin and INR are abnormal, or imaging shows hepatic inflammation/obstruction, a hepatic source is more likely. Normal GGT and normal cholestatic markers in the face of high AST/ALT make primary liver disease less likely and support muscle as the source.

Third, look at the pattern and context. Muscle injury classically produces AST > ALT or similar elevations of both; NAFLD and most hepatocellular processes more often give ALT ≥ AST (although this is not absolute). The time course matters: drug/toxic rhabdomyolysis usually causes very large CK elevations that fall quickly with withdrawal and hydration; immune myopathies produce persistently high CK over weeks–months and transaminases that follow the CK course. Correlate symptoms — proximal weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, MRI with muscle edema or EMG abnormalities point to muscle as the source.

Fourth, use targeted tests when uncertainty remains. An isolated small AST rise can come from cardiac muscle (check troponin), haemolysis, or macro‑AST. Hepatic imaging and serologies (viral, autoimmune) and, when indicated, liver biopsy define chronic liver disease. Conversely, muscle MRI, EMG and — if needed — muscle biopsy establish inflammatory myopathy.

Apply this to the case: she had pre‑existing mild ALT>AST with ultrasound and biopsy confirming NAFLD, so chronic transaminase elevation before semaglutide was hepatic. When she developed proximal weakness, her CK rose into the thousands and AST/ALT rose in parallel, then fell as CK normalized after immunotherapy — a pattern consistent with muscle as the acute source of the transient transaminase spike superimposed on chronic hepatic steatosis. The persistent low‑grade ALT after CK normalization therefore reflects her underlying NAFLD, not ongoing muscle injury.

In practice: always check CK (± aldolase/LDH) with any unexplained transaminase rise, compare the trajectories, assess GGT/ALP/bilirubin/INR, obtain muscle testing if clinical features suggest myopathy, and biopsy the organ that remains discordant after this workup. That algorithm prevents misattribution of transaminase elevations to liver when they are muscular, and vice versa.

### [Clinical Pearls] In inflammatory myopathies with possible cardiac involvement, which cardiac troponin assay is preferred and why?

Use cardiac troponin I (cTnI). High‑sensitivity troponin T (hs‑TnT) is frequently elevated in active skeletal muscle disease because regenerating or inflamed skeletal muscle can express T‑type epitopes or cross‑react with the assay, producing false‑positive signals for myocardial injury. cTnI assays do not have that same cross‑reactivity with diseased skeletal muscle, so an isolated rise in cTnI is much more specific for true myocardial injury (including myocarditis) in patients with inflammatory myopathies.

Clinically, measure cTnI (not hs‑TnT) when screening for cardiac involvement in myositis or IMNM; interpret any cTnI elevation alongside symptoms, ECG, CK, natriuretic peptide, and imaging. A true cTnI rise should prompt early cardiology involvement and further evaluation with echocardiography and cardiac MRI for myocarditis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Compare and contrast the time course, symptom profile, and CK trajectory of drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis with that of IMNM.

The two syndromes can look similar at a single timepoint because both cause muscle necrosis and CK elevation, but they differ in timing, associated symptoms, laboratory tempo, pathology and treatment response — distinctions that matter when you decide whether to stop a drug and start immunotherapy.

Drug‑induced toxic rhabdomyolysis is an acute toxic event. Symptoms typically begin within hours to days of exposure or of an identifiable stressor, with prominent myalgias, weakness, and often systemic features of muscle breakdown (dark urine, cola‑colored urine, frank myoglobinuria) and frequently acute kidney injury and metabolic derangements from myoglobinuria and hypovolemia. CK usually rises very rapidly to very high levels — often tens of thousands IU/L — and, if the offending drug or trigger is removed and supportive care (aggressive hydration, correcting electrolytes) instituted, CK falls quickly over days to weeks. Muscle biopsy, if performed early, shows myofiber necrosis without a dominant adaptive immune infiltrate; there is no consistent autoantibody signature and immunosuppression is not required in typical toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is subacute to chronic and immune‑driven. Symptoms evolve over weeks to months and are dominated by progressively disabling symmetric proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), with relatively less myalgia and less frequent frank myoglobinuria. Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and cardiomyopathy can occur and point toward an autoimmune myopathy. CK is elevated — often in the low thousands but sometimes higher — and importantly the elevation tends to be sustained or progressive despite withdrawal of a putative offending agent; spontaneous rapid normalization is uncommon. Muscle biopsy characteristically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and only sparse lymphocytes. A proportion of patients have disease‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP), but a substantial minority are seronegative. IMNM typically requires immunotherapy (corticosteroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) and CK and strength often improve only after immunosuppression.

Applying these principles to the case: the patient had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion illness with only modest CK (337 U/L) at first presentation — an expected picture for volume‑related muscle injury. The subsequent clinical course, however, was incompatible with simple toxic rhabdomyolysis: over four months she developed progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms and a delayed, sustained rise in CK to ~4,000 U/L rather than a single massive spike that resolved with hydration. Muscle biopsy showed dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition — the pathological pattern of IMNM — and her weakness and CK improved only after corticosteroids and IVIG. Those temporal features (subacute progression despite drug cessation), symptom profile (prominent proximal weakness and bulbar involvement with less pain), and CK trajectory (sustained elevation and fall with immunotherapy rather than rapid resolution after stopping drug) therefore favor IMNM over isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Clinically, when a patient on a new medication presents with CK elevation, acutely ill with myoglobinuria and renal failure suggests primary toxic rhabdomyolysis managed with supportive care and drug withdrawal. When weakness is progressive over weeks–months, bulbar features or cardiomyopathy are present, CK elevation is persistent or rising despite stopping the drug, or biopsy shows immune‑mediated necrosis, you should pursue myositis serologies and muscle biopsy and treat as IMNM because delay in immunotherapy risks irreversible weakness.

### [Morning Report] Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality framework, what factors support a 'probable' drug–adverse event relationship in a patient with suspected drug-induced IMNM?

In the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework a “probable/likely” causality assignment rests on a consistent temporal relationship, plausibility, lack of a more likely alternative explanation, and a clinically reasonable response to withdrawal — without requiring a positive rechallenge. Applying those principles to this case explains why the authors judged the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM as probable.

First, the temporal relationship is compatible: the patient had an acute systemic reaction within hours of a single semaglutide dose and then developed progressive proximal weakness with a rising CK over the ensuing weeks to months. Although the full myopathic phenotype evolved over months (as is typical for an immune‑mediated process), the onset followed exposure in a biologically plausible timeframe for a drug‑triggered autoimmune reaction.

Second, there is a clear dechallenge response. After semaglutide was discontinued and the patient received immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids and IVIG), her CK normalized and she returned to baseline functional status and remained well off immunosuppression for many months. That sustained improvement after removal of the suspected agent supports causality under the UMC criteria.

Third, competing causes were reasonably excluded. She had no history of statin use or other known pharmacologic or environmental triggers for IMNM, myositis serologies were negative (acknowledging seronegative IMNM is well described), and biopsy demonstrated necrotizing myopathy with inflammatory features consistent with IMNM rather than pure toxic rhabdomyolysis or an alternative neuromuscular diagnosis. Chronic liver enzyme abnormalities were present before exposure and liver biopsy confirmed NAFLD, reducing the likelihood those transaminase elevations reflected preexisting muscle disease.

Fourth, the clinical picture is biologically plausible. GLP‑1 receptor agonists have been associated with muscle mass loss and isolated reports of severe muscle injury with agents in this class; immune activation after a drug exposure is a recognized mechanism for IMNM. Although mechanistic data are lacking, plausibility strengthens the association.

Finally, the only major criterion absent for a “certain” classification is a positive rechallenge, which is neither ethical nor necessary for a probable designation. Limitations remain — notably the single‑dose exposure with delayed full expression of disease and the absence of a specific autoantibody — so “probable” appropriately reflects the balance of supportive evidence without overcalling certainty.

In short: a compatible time course, marked clinical and laboratory improvement after withdrawal and immunotherapy, exclusion of more likely alternatives, supportive muscle biopsy, and biological plausibility together satisfy the key WHO‑UMC elements that define a “probable/likely” drug–adverse event relationship.

### [Clinical Pearls] When encountering new-onset myopathy after exposure to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, what clinical features and time course would increase concern for an autoimmune mechanism rather than direct drug toxicity?

When you see new‑onset myopathy after exposure to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, the key clinical distinction is whether the picture behaves like an acute toxic myonecrosis (rhabdomyolysis) that improves after drug withdrawal and supportive care, or like an immune‑mediated myopathy that progresses despite stopping the drug and requires immunotherapy. Features and time course that should raise concern for an autoimmune mechanism are the following.

First, a subacute to progressive course over weeks to months with worsening proximal weakness (often with bulbar involvement such as dysphagia or dysphonia) is more consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) than with direct toxin‑induced rhabdomyolysis, which is typically abrupt and improves rapidly after removal of the offending agent. In the case presented, weakness and disabling dysphagia evolved over four months after a single semaglutide dose — a pattern that argues against a transient direct toxic effect.

Second, a persistently elevated or rising CK after cessation of the agent (especially CK in the thousands that does not fall quickly with hydration and discontinuation) suggests ongoing immune injury rather than one‑time muscle breakdown. In contrast, drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis usually shows a sharp CK peak and then decline over days once the drug is stopped and the patient is supported.

Third, supportive laboratory and imaging findings that point to inflammation — modestly elevated ESR/CRP, symmetric muscle edema on MRI, and extramuscular inflammatory features (for example possible myocarditis) — favor an autoimmune process. The presence of systemic inflammatory markers or multisystem involvement increases the likelihood that the mechanism is immune‑mediated.

Fourth, muscle biopsy is decisive: necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition, and a relative paucity of classic lymphocytic infiltrates are characteristic of IMNM and distinguish it from pure toxic necrosis. Serologic assays for IMNM autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) can help when positive, but up to a third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative panel does not rule out autoimmune disease.

Finally, the clinical response to immunosuppression is informative. Sustained improvement in strength and normalization of CK with corticosteroids and IVIG — and the ability to taper off immunosuppression without relapse — supports a drug‑triggered immune myopathy. By contrast, toxin‑related injury typically recovers without immunomodulation.

Putting it together: a subacute, progressive proximal myopathy with bulbar signs, persistent or rising CK after drug discontinuation, MRI evidence of muscle edema, inflammatory biopsy features (macrophage infiltration, MAC deposition), systemic inflammatory markers or extramuscular involvement, and clinical response to immunosuppression all point to an autoimmune mechanism rather than direct drug toxicity.

### [Clinical Pearls] List common exposures and agents associated with IMNM, including medications and supplements, and describe how they inform history-taking.

Key clinical pearl: IMNM is most often drug- or immune‑triggered rather than a primary inherited myopathy, so an efficient history targeted at exposures often yields the diagnosis or a clue to mechanism (for example HMGCR‑positive disease after statin exposure). Ask not only about current prescriptions but about remote, over‑the‑counter, and complementary products — and about cancer and immune therapies — because triggers may precede symptom onset by weeks to months.

The common exposures and agents to consider, and why they matter for history‑taking:

- Statins (including intermittent or remote use). Statins are the best‑established trigger for HMGCR‑positive IMNM. The autoimmune process can begin while on drug or after stopping it; many patients report prior or even brief exposures. Always ask about any statin ever taken, duration, dose, and timing of cessation relative to symptom onset.

- Statin‑containing or statin‑like supplements (red yeast rice, some “natural” cholesterol products, certain mushrooms or traditional remedies). These contain lovastatin‑like compounds and can produce the same HMGCR immune response. Explicitly ask about OTC lipid supplements, herbal preparations, and traditional medicines — patients may not volunteer these unless asked.

- Immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD‑1, PD‑L1, CTLA‑4 agents). These cancer therapies are increasingly recognized causes of autoimmune myopathies including IMNM and may be associated with more fulminant disease and cardiac involvement. Ask about any cancer history and treatments, including recent infusions or clinical trials.

- Other medications reported in association with necrotising or autoimmune myopathies. While less frequent than statins and checkpoints, agents such as certain antivirals, antipsychotics, or other novel biologics have been implicated in case reports. When evaluating, review the full medication list (prescription, PRN, topical) including recent new starts.

- Weight‑loss agents/GLP‑1 receptor agonists (eg, semaglutide, tirzepatide). Emerging reports suggest possible links to muscle injury or necrotising myopathy in rare cases. Given expanding use, ask specifically about GLP‑1 agonist use and exact timing relative to symptom onset.

- Infections, vaccines, and systemic autoimmune disease. Viral infections and, less commonly, post‑infectious or post‑vaccination immune activation can precede IMNM. Ask about recent febrile illnesses, unusual infections, and new symptoms suggestive of connective‑tissue disease (rash, sicca, Raynaud’s, dysphagia).

- Malignancy and paraneoplastic associations. Some IMNM cases are paraneoplastic. Ask about constitutional symptoms, unexplained weight loss, recent cancer diagnoses, and family cancer history; consider age‑appropriate malignancy screening if clinical suspicion.

How this informs the bedside history
Begin with an exposure time‑line: date when weakness began, any new medications or supplements in the preceding weeks–months, and any transient adverse reactions (eg, nausea, AKI, hospitalization) that might have been the sentinel event. Explicitly ask “Have you ever taken a statin? Have you ever taken any natural cholesterol pills such as red yeast rice or mushroom extracts? Have you started any new weight‑loss or diabetes injections (eg, semaglutide, tirzepatide)?” Probe for cancer therapies or participation in trials. Ask about prior CK values (if available) and whether symptoms improved after stopping any agent. Ask about systemic autoimmune features (skin rashes, sicca, arthralgias) and cardiac or bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia) because IMNM can involve heart and swallowing and this affects urgency of workup.

Clinical reasoning tie‑in
Identifying an exposure narrows serologic testing (eg, HMGCR antibody when statin exposure) and guides management: drug‑triggered IMNM may improve after withdrawal plus short immunosuppression, whereas idiopathic/seropositive IMNM often requires prolonged multi‑agent therapy. A careful exposure history therefore directly affects diagnostic probability, urgency of cardiac evaluation, and counseling about drug avoidance and rechallenge.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain why some patients with anti-HMGCR myopathy have persistent disease activity despite statin withdrawal.

This is a question about the difference between a drug‑triggered toxic myopathy that remits when the drug is stopped and a drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy that becomes self‑sustaining. In anti‑HMGCR myopathy the inciting exposure (most commonly a statin) appears to break immune tolerance to HMG‑CoA reductase, but removing the drug does not necessarily extinguish the immune response.

There are three linked pathophysiologic reasons for persistence after statin withdrawal. First, statins increase HMGCR expression in muscle, especially in regenerating myofibres. That upregulated antigen expression during muscle injury provides the signal that initiates and amplifies an autoreactive B‑ and T‑cell response. Once anti‑HMGCR autoantibodies and autoreactive T cells are established, they can recognize HMGCR on regenerating fibres even in the absence of ongoing statin exposure.

Second, the adaptive immune system generates immunologic memory and long‑lived plasma cells. These cells continue producing pathogenic anti‑HMGCR antibodies for months to years independent of the original trigger. The antibodies fix complement and drive muscle fibre necrosis (membrane attack complex deposition is typical), and ongoing muscle injury provokes further regeneration and antigen expression — a self‑perpetuating cycle of antigen exposure and immune attack.

Third, genetic and immunologic susceptibility matters. Certain HLA alleles (for example HLA‑DRB1*11:01) are associated with anti‑HMGCR disease and favor a strong, sustained autoimmune response; epitope spreading and T‑cell help can further broaden and fix the autoimmunity so it no longer requires the drug to persist.

Clinically this explains why statin‑associated necrotising myopathy often fails to remit after stopping the statin and instead requires immunomodulatory therapy (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) to suppress antibody production and interrupt the complement‑mediated muscle injury. It also explains the key diagnostic distinction from toxic rhabdomyolysis: toxic myopathies improve rapidly with removal of the offending agent, whereas immune‑mediated myopathies show ongoing weakness, necrosis on biopsy with complement deposition, and improvement only with immunotherapy.

### [Management] What are the first-line immunomodulatory treatments for severe IMNM with dysphagia or profound weakness, and what is the rationale for adding IVIG early?

In severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with bulbar involvement (dysphagia) or rapidly progressive, profound proximal weakness, initial therapy should be aggressive and combination‑oriented because the disease produces rapid myofibre necrosis and risk of respiratory compromise or aspiration.

First‑line immediate therapy is high‑dose corticosteroids and early intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Practically this is methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days (pulse) followed by oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day, combined up front with IVIG 2 g/kg given over 2–5 days (often split over 3–4 days). In most centers a second immunomodulator is started early as a steroid‑sparing agent (methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate) or, for refractory or antibody‑positive (especially anti‑SRP) disease, rituximab or cyclophosphamide may be used. Plasmapheresis is considered in fulminant cases or severe bulbar/respiratory failure when rapid antibody removal is needed.

The rationale for adding IVIG early is both pathophysiologic and pragmatic. IMNM is frequently antibody‑mediated and shows complement activation and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition on myofibres; IVIG neutralises pathogenic autoantibodies, downregulates complement activation, modulates Fc receptor function on macrophages, and has broad immunomodulatory effects that can arrest ongoing necrosis faster than steroids alone. Clinically, early IVIG is associated with more rapid reductions in CK, faster improvement in strength and bulbar function, and a lower risk of irreversible muscle loss or aspiration that would follow delayed control of immune injury. In the presented case, prompt pulse steroids plus IVIG produced a rapid CK fall and recovery of function, which illustrates the benefit of early combined therapy.

Keep in mind the tradeoffs: IVIG is resource‑intensive and carries risks (volume overload, thromboembolism, infection transmission, renal dysfunction), so monitor closely and tailor subsequent steroid‑sparing therapy based on antibody status, clinical course, and infection risk.

### [Management] Which steroid-sparing agents are commonly used in IMNM, and in what clinical scenarios would you escalate to rituximab or other advanced therapies?

In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) you almost always start with high‑dose corticosteroids for rapid control, but long‑term steroid monotherapy is undesirable because of toxicity and because many IMNM patients need additional immunomodulation to achieve steroid‑sparing and durable remission. The common “next‑line” steroid‑sparing agents are methotrexate, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil — chosen because they are familiar, generally well tolerated, and have the best evidence across inflammatory myopathies for permitting prednisone taper. Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus or ciclosporin) are used when faster or stronger T‑cell suppression is needed or when mycophenolate/azathioprine are not tolerated. IVIG occupies a special role: for IMNM (particularly anti‑HMGCR or when there is severe weakness, dysphagia, or respiratory/cardiac involvement) IVIG is often used early and may be continued as maintenance; in many series IVIG produces rapid clinical and CK responses and is considered first‑line in combination with steroids for severe presentations.

Escalation to rituximab or other advanced therapies is driven by disease severity, autoantibody profile, and response to initial therapy. You should consider rituximab when a patient has refractory disease despite an adequate trial of steroids plus a conventional steroid‑sparing agent (or steroid + IVIG), or when the phenotype is severe from the outset (progressive profound weakness, persistent very high CK, bulbar involvement/dysphagia, respiratory failure, or cardiac involvement). Anti‑SRP–positive IMNM and some seronegative cases are more often refractory and are frequent scenarios in which teams escalate to rituximab. Cyclophosphamide is reserved for life‑threatening or organ‑threatening disease (severe myocarditis, rapidly progressive respiratory failure) or when rapid, broad immunosuppression is required. Plasma exchange and other biologics (e.g., abatacept, tocilizumab) have been used in small series or case reports for refractory disease but lack high‑quality evidence and are generally tertiary options after rituximab or cyclophosphamide fail or are contraindicated.

Clinically, apply these principles: if a patient improves promptly with steroids ± IVIG and can taper off steroids, no escalation is needed; if weakness or CK plateau or worsen after an adequate trial (weeks to a few months depending on severity) of combined therapy, escalate. Bulbar, respiratory, or cardiac involvement should prompt early aggressive therapy (steroids + IVIG and early addition of a steroid‑sparing agent, with low threshold for rituximab or cyclophosphamide). When choosing agents, account for comorbidities (liver disease, infection risk, fertility), screen appropriately (TB, hepatitis, TPMT for azathioprine), vaccinate before B‑cell depletion, and monitor CK, strength, and adverse effects closely.

### [Management] What nonpharmacologic measures are essential in the acute management of IMNM with bulbar involvement?

When immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy presents with bulbar signs (dysphagia, dysphonia, impaired secretion clearance), the immediate priorities are airway protection, nutrition and aspiration prevention, and prevention of respiratory failure and systemic complications — measures that are as important as starting immunotherapy.

Assess and monitor the airway and breathing continuously. Early involvement of ICU, ENT and speech‑language pathology is essential because bulbar dysfunction can rapidly progress to loss of airway protection. Perform a bedside swallowing screen and urgent instrumental evaluation (videofluoroscopic or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing) if available; keep the patient nil per os until the safety of oral intake is confirmed. Low thresholds for elective airway control are appropriate: progressive dysphagia with pooling/aspiration, ineffective cough, increasing drooling, or signs of fatigue/respiratory compromise should prompt consideration of definitive airway protection (endotracheal intubation) because emergent intubation in a weakened patient is higher risk.

Provide safe enteral nutrition and hydration while avoiding aspiration. If oral feeding is unsafe but the course is expected to be short, place a nasogastric tube; if prolonged dysphagia is likely, arrange percutaneous enteral access (PEG) once the patient is medically stable. Manage secretions actively with anticholinergic agents judiciously, frequent suctioning, and chest physiotherapy to reduce aspiration risk.

Monitor respiratory function objectively and frequently. Measure forced vital capacity and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures serially; large or rapid declines (FVC falling toward or below ~15–20 mL/kg, or rapid decline in FVC/MIP, rising CO2, hypoxaemia) indicate impending respiratory failure and need for ventilatory support. Use pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases as indicated, and consider prophylactic noninvasive ventilation for hypoventilation if appropriate.

Prevent systemic complications of severe myonecrosis. Aggressively hydrate and monitor renal function and urine output (and urinalysis for myoglobinuria) to prevent or mitigate acute tubular injury from muscle breakdown. Provide venous thromboembolism prophylaxis and pressure‑injury prevention in deconditioned patients. Early involvement of physical and occupational therapy for passive/active mobilization as tolerated reduces deconditioning and long‑term disability.

Coordinate multidisciplinary care and monitor for cardiac involvement. Because IMNM can be associated with myocarditis or arrhythmias (as in this case), obtain cardiac monitoring and cardiology consultation when troponin/proBNP are abnormal or if symptoms/signs suggest cardiac involvement.

In short, while initiating immunosuppression and IVIG, treat the patient with bulbar IMNM as you would any neuromuscular patient at high risk for aspiration and respiratory failure: protect the airway early, institute safe alternate nutrition, monitor respiratory mechanics closely with low thresholds for ventilatory support, manage secretions and prevent systemic complications, and engage a multidisciplinary team for swallowing, respiratory, nutritional and rehabilitative care.

### [Management] How do you structure monitoring of treatment response in IMNM, including clinical assessments and laboratory/imaging follow-up, and how do these inform steroid tapering?

In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy the single most important principle is to follow the patient’s clinical function and objective muscle injury markers together, and to let sustained clinical recovery plus durable normalization of biomarkers—not an arbitrary calendar—drive steroid tapering. In practice I structure follow‑up around a few paired elements: careful bedside strength and functional assessment, serial biochemical markers of muscle injury/inflammation, targeted imaging when needed, and surveillance for organ involvement and steroid toxicity.

On the clinical side you should document proximal limb strength (e.g., MRC grades), timed functional measures (rising from a chair, timed up‑and‑go, 6‑minute walk or distance walked in clinic), and bulbar/respiratory status at each visit. Dysphagia and respiratory compromise are high‑risk features that mandate slower immunosuppression changes and often IVIG/early escalation. In the case presented the prominent proximal weakness and dysphagia justified aggressive initial therapy and close clinical reassessment.

For laboratory monitoring check CK (and AST/ALT as adjuncts) frequently until there is a clear downward trend: daily to every other day while inpatient or during very active disease, then weekly after discharge until levels are falling and symptoms improving. Once CK is normal and the patient is improving clinically, extend intervals to every 2–4 weeks for the first 2–3 months, then monthly for the next 3–6 months, and every 3–6 months thereafter for the first year. Track inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR) as adjuncts—rising CRP/ESR without CK rise may prompt reassessment but are less specific. If myocarditis is suspected or troponin/BNP are elevated (as in this patient), obtain baseline echo and consider cardiac MRI; repeat imaging is guided by symptoms and biomarker trends and is appropriate after clinical recovery to confirm resolution.

Use MRI of affected muscle when the diagnosis is uncertain, to document active myofibre edema at baseline, or when there is discordance between symptoms and CK (for example persistent weakness with normal CK). MRI is also useful to localize biopsy sites and to detect relapse if clinical assessment is equivocal. Electromyography and repeat muscle biopsy are reserved for atypical courses or failure to respond.

How this informs steroid tapering: start with high‑dose glucocorticoids for severe IMNM (pulse IV methylprednisolone for fulminant cases, then 1 mg/kg oral prednisone commonly), but do not taper solely by time elapsed. Tapering should be individualized: once the patient has clinically improved (meaning objective gains in strength and function) and CK has decreased substantially and normalized, you can begin a gradual prednisone taper. In classic autoantibody‑positive IMNM (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) relapse is common and steroids are tapered slowly over months with early addition of steroid‑sparing agents (azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate, rituximab) or maintenance IVIG. By contrast, suspected drug‑induced IMNM that responds promptly to drug withdrawal and a short immunosuppressive course—as in this semaglutide case—may allow a faster taper once CK and function normalize and remain stable. In that situation a reasonable approach is to reduce prednisone by 10–20% every 1–2 weeks while monitoring strength and CK; if any clinical deterioration or CK rise occurs, pause the taper, increase steroids back to the last effective dose, and consider resuming or adding steroid‑sparing therapy or additional IVIG.

Be explicit about triggers for slower taper or escalation: persistent weakness despite CK improvement, recurrent CK elevations, new bulbar or respiratory symptoms, cardiac involvement, or documented autoantibody positivity. Also monitor and manage steroid complications (glucose, blood pressure, bone protection, infection surveillance)—these considerations often push toward earlier steroid‑sparing strategies.

In summary: pair frequent, structured clinical exams with serial CK (daily/weekly early, then spacing as stable) and targeted imaging for organ involvement. Use sustained clinical recovery plus durable CK normalization to guide decreases in prednisone; taper faster only when both clinical and laboratory remission are clear and the offending drug has been stopped. If either clinical or biochemical relapse occurs, resume or escalate immunotherapy rather than continuing the taper.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What features help distinguish steroid-induced myopathy from active IMNM during treatment?

This is a common and important clinical dilemma: a patient with proximal weakness who is receiving or has just received high‑dose corticosteroids — is the deterioration steroid myopathy, or ongoing/relapsing inflammatory necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that needs more immunotherapy? Distinguishing the two matters because management is opposite (reduce steroids for steroid myopathy versus escalate immunosuppression for active IMNM). The keys are timing, symptoms and distribution, objective testing (CK, MRI/EMG), histology, and the clinical course after steroid change.

Timing and clinical features. Steroid myopathy most commonly appears subacutely to chronically after days–weeks to months of high‑dose or repeated corticosteroids and typically produces painless, symmetric proximal weakness affecting hip flexors and shoulder abductors with relative preservation of bulbar and respiratory function. By contrast, IMNM usually presents with more rapid progressive weakness over weeks, often with myalgias, bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia), and systemic features; IMNM may also have cardiac involvement. In this case the prominent dysphagia/dysphonia and rapid progression over months before steroid exposure argue for IMNM rather than steroid myopathy.

Creatine kinase and other labs. CK is usually normal or only mildly elevated in steroid myopathy (often low or normal because steroid myopathy is a myofibrillar atrophy rather than necrosis), whereas IMNM typically produces marked CK elevations (often in the thousands). A falling CK after steroid initiation suggests that muscle necrosis/inflammation is responding; persistently high or rising CK despite corticosteroids suggests active IMNM or inadequate immunotherapy rather than steroid myopathy. In this patient the CK rose to nearly 4,000 prior to steroid pulse and then fell with IVIG and steroids — a pattern consistent with treated IMNM.

Electrodiagnostic testing and MRI. EMG in steroid myopathy shows myopathic motor unit potentials with early recruitment but lacks the inflammatory irritability (fibrillations) often seen in IMNM. Muscle MRI in IMNM shows muscle edema and contrast enhancement consistent with active inflammation/necrosis; steroid myopathy shows fatty replacement and atrophy without edema. Use these studies when the diagnosis is unclear.

Muscle biopsy — the clincher. Steroid myopathy is characterized by type II fiber atrophy and preservation of myofiber membranes without necrosis, macrophage invasion, complement deposition, or significant necrotising changes. IMNM shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, and variable lymphocytes — exactly the findings reported in this case. A biopsy showing necrosis and MAC deposition points to IMNM and not steroid myopathy.

Response to therapy and course. Steroid myopathy typically improves slowly after reducing steroid dose; it does not respond to immunotherapies directed at autoimmune myopathy. IMNM generally requires immunosuppression (steroids plus second‑line agents/IVIG), and CK and strength often improve with appropriate therapy. Rapid improvement in CK and strength after IVIG and steroids, and sustained remission off steroids as in this patient, supports drug‑triggered IMNM rather than steroid toxicity.

Practical approach at the bedside. First, review the temporal relationship: did weakness precede or follow steroid exposure? Check CK and repeat it after any steroid change. Obtain MRI/EMG if uncertain. If weakness is painless, insidious, with normal CK and prolonged high steroid exposure, favor steroid myopathy and reduce steroid dose. If weakness is rapidly progressive, associated with high CK, bulbar/respiratory or cardiac features, or biopsy/MRI show necrosis/edema/MAC, treat as active IMNM and escalate immunotherapy.

In short, steroid myopathy is an atrophic, low‑CK, painless proximal weakness that follows steroid exposure and improves with steroid reduction; IMNM is a necrotising, high‑CK, often symptomatic myositis (frequently with bulbar/cardiac involvement) that demonstrates inflammatory/necrotic changes on biopsy and responds to immunosuppression. The patient in this report had features — bulbar involvement, high CK, MRI edema and a necrotising biopsy with macrophages and MAC — that point to IMNM rather than steroid‑induced myopathy.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the estimated frequency of seronegative IMNM, and how should seronegativity influence decisions about muscle biopsy and treatment initiation?

Estimated frequency: roughly one-third of patients with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are seronegative for the two canonical autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP). In other words, absence of those antibodies does not exclude IMNM — seronegativity is common and likely reflects either as‑yet‑unidentified antibodies or non‑autoantibody–mediated triggers.

Clinical consequence for biopsy and treatment: seronegativity should not delay definitive evaluation or appropriate therapy when the clinical picture points to IMNM. When a patient has subacute severe proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, dysphagia/respiratory symptoms, characteristic MRI changes, or an otherwise unexplained necrotising pattern on muscle biopsy, the pretest probability for IMNM is high even with a negative myositis panel. Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic cornerstone in seronegative cases because the histopathology (widespread myofibre necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation, membrane‑attack complex deposition and relative paucity of lymphocytes) establishes the diagnosis and helps exclude mimics. In patients with severe disease (rapidly progressive weakness, bulbar or respiratory involvement, very high CK), start immunosuppressive therapy promptly — typically high‑dose corticosteroids with consideration of IVIG and early steroid‑sparing agents — rather than waiting for serologies or biopsy results, because treatment delay worsens outcomes. In milder, non‑threatening presentations you can reasonably pursue serologies and biopsy to confirm the diagnosis before escalating long‑term immunosuppression, but maintain close monitoring and a low threshold to treat if progression occurs.

### [Management] Discuss the known skeletal muscle adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists and how you would counsel patients about future use or rechallenge after suspected IMNM.

Clinical pearl — what we know about GLP‑1 receptor agonists and skeletal muscle, and how to counsel a patient after a suspected immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) are well‑established to cause gastrointestinal adverse effects and weight loss; loss of skeletal muscle mass has been reported as a physiologic consequence of weight loss and calorie restriction, and small observational studies and case reports have described sarcopenic changes after GLP‑1 RA use. Separately, rare case reports link GLP‑1 RAs (and related incretin agonists) to frank muscle injury: most commonly an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis phenotype (very high CK that improves promptly with drug withdrawal and supportive care), and very rarely a necrotising myopathy pattern. The literature remains sparse — there are isolated reports (including the present semaglutide case and a tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis report) but no large signal or mechanistic proof that GLP‑1 RAs provoke autoimmune myopathies.

How to think about mechanisms and diagnosis in practice. Distinguish three patterns because counseling differs by mechanism. First, physiologic muscle loss related to weight reduction or deconditioning — slow, proportional, not inflammatory, CK usually normal. Second, toxic rhabdomyolysis — abrupt, massive CK elevation tied temporally to drug exposure, rapid fall after withdrawal and hydration, no requirement for immunosuppression. Third, immune‑mediated myopathy (IMNM or other autoimmune myositis) — progressive proximal weakness, sustained CK elevation, muscle biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis and complement activation, and often need for immunosuppression. Autoantibodies (eg, HMGCR, SRP) can be present but a substantial minority are seronegative. In this case the time course (progressive weakness over months after one dose), biopsy showing necrosis with inflammatory features, and response to immunotherapy favor IMNM rather than acute toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Counseling about future use / rechallenge after suspected IMNM. For clinical decision‑making you must weigh the probability that the drug caused an immune‑mediated process, the severity of the event, and the availability of alternatives.

If you have high suspicion for immune‑mediated myopathy (biopsy‑proven IMNM, sustained CK elevation, clinical severity, and improvement only after immunosuppression and drug withdrawal): do not rechallenge with the offending agent and generally avoid the entire class unless there is compelling, unavoidable clinical need and the patient consents to risk. The known uncertainty of mechanism and the potential for recurrence or worsening with rechallenge make avoidance the prudent course. Document the reaction clearly, counsel the patient that re‑exposure risks recurrence of severe, potentially life‑threatening muscle disease, and consider alternative therapies for diabetes/weight management. Report the adverse event to local pharmacovigilance/regulatory bodies.

If the presentation is more consistent with toxic rhabdomyolysis (acute, very high CK with rapid resolution after discontinuation and no biopsy evidence of autoimmune inflammation), a cautious rechallenge could be considered in selected situations after full recovery, with informed consent and close monitoring (baseline CK, repeated CK during early weeks, symptom vigilance). Rechallenge should be avoided if other precipitants (statins, drug interactions, dehydration) cannot be corrected.

Practical steps when considering any re‑exposure
1) Convene multidisciplinary discussion (rheumatology/myositis specialist, neurology, endocrinology/diabetes or obesity medicine) and involve the patient in shared decision‑making.
2) Reassess for alternative causes (statins, supplements, infections, malignancy) and check myositis serologies, baseline CK, and consider repeat imaging or biopsy if uncertainty remains.
3) If a rechallenge is contemplated despite prior IMNM, obtain explicit informed consent explaining uncertain risk, institute close objective monitoring (CK twice weekly for several weeks initially, early clinic or phone checks for weakness/dysphagia/respiratory signs), and stop immediately at any new symptoms or CK rise — but even with that infrastructure, rechallenge is generally discouraged.
4) Consider alternative non‑GLP‑1 therapies for glycemic control or weight loss and engage nutrition/exercise/safety plans to preserve muscle mass.

In the presented case the lesion was severe, biopsy‑proven IMNM with dramatic clinical course and improvement only after immunotherapy and withdrawal of semaglutide; rechallenge would be inadvisable and ethically problematic. Counsel the patient that future use of semaglutide or closely related incretin agonists is not recommended, alternatives should be pursued, and the event should be reported so we can better define rare anti‑muscle adverse effects of these widely used drugs.

### [Morning Report] What is the approach to evaluating possible myocarditis in inflammatory myopathy, and what is the role of cardiac MRI?

When you see an inflammatory myopathy and worry about cardiac involvement, think first about two clinical questions: is there clinically important myocardial injury now that requires urgent management, and is there myocarditis that would change diagnosis or prompt immunotherapy beyond what you’re already doing for the skeletal myositis?

Begin with targeted bedside evaluation: ask about new chest pain, dyspnea out of proportion to respiratory findings, palpitations, syncope, or orthopnea; obtain vital signs and a 12‑lead ECG. Obtain cardiac biomarkers (high‑sensitivity troponin and natriuretic peptide) and a transthoracic echocardiogram to assess biventricular function, wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion and pulmonary pressures. These tests identify patients who need urgent therapy or invasive evaluation (heart failure, cardiogenic shock, life‑threatening arrhythmia, large effusion).

Cardiac MRI is the pivotal noninvasive test when you suspect myocarditis after that initial screen. Use MRI to look specifically for myocardial edema and injury using contemporary Lake Louise criteria: T2‑weighted or T2 mapping sequences for edema, native T1 and extracellular volume or late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) for necrosis/fibrosis and inflammation. A positive edema signal with concomitant parametric abnormalities or LGE in a nonischemic distribution gives good sensitivity and specificity for myocarditis. MRI also defines the extent and pattern of involvement, helps exclude ischemic infarction, and provides a baseline to follow recovery or progression.

But interpret MRI in context. Early or focal myocarditis may show edema without LGE, and lack of LGE does not completely exclude myocarditis; conversely, LGE denotes more established injury and predicts worse outcomes. MRI is most useful when the pretest probability is moderate and the result would change management. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the gold standard if the diagnosis is uncertain and the result would alter therapy — for example, fulminant myocarditis, rapidly progressive heart failure, refractory arrhythmia, or when giant‑cell or eosinophilic myocarditis is suspected and specific treatment differs.

In inflammatory myopathies the practical approach is to screen asymptomatic patients with ECG and troponin (and consider BNP) at baseline and with new symptoms, perform an echocardiogram if any abnormality, and obtain cardiac MRI when biochemical or echo findings, symptoms, or ECG suggest myocardial involvement. Reserve biopsy for high‑risk or treatment‑defining situations. Finally, serial MRI can document resolution of inflammation (as in this patient, where edema resolved and no scar was seen), which supports a reversible inflammatory process rather than irreversible infarction and helps inform prognosis and duration/intensity of immunosuppression.

### [Management] Outline an evidence-informed approach to infection risk mitigation in IMNM patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids and/or IVIG.

Teaching pearl:

When you give high‑dose corticosteroids and often IVIG for disorders such as immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy, the key to safe care is anticipatory risk‑reduction — identify latent infections, vaccinate when possible before immunosuppression, use targeted antimicrobial prophylaxis when indicated, minimize steroid exposure, and maintain close surveillance for early infection. Here is an evidence‑informed, practical approach tied to the case.

Before or at treatment initiation, perform a focused infection risk assessment: obtain history for travel/epidemiologic exposures (strongyloides endemic areas), prior hepatitis or tuberculosis, prior varicella/zoster, vaccination status, and recent infections. Baseline testing should include screening for hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti‑HBc total), an interferon‑gamma release assay (IGRA) or chest x‑ray for latent TB if epidemiologic risk, and consider Strongyloides serology in patients from endemic areas. These tests identify conditions that can reactivate with immunosuppression and that are relatively straightforward to prevent or treat.

Vaccination timing: when feasible, give indicated inactivated vaccines before starting high‑dose steroids. Give seasonal inactivated influenza and complete age‑appropriate pneumococcal vaccination series prior to or early in the course of therapy. Recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is preferred over live vaccine and should be given ideally before immunosuppression or when the patient’s immune suppression is lowest; if treatment is urgent, plan to give RZV once clinically stable and immune suppression reduced (consult local guidance). Live vaccines are contraindicated while on high‑dose steroids (generally ≥20 mg prednisone equivalent daily for ≥14–21 days) and for some interval after stopping; plan vaccination around this.

Antimicrobial prophylaxis: the single highest yield intervention for patients receiving prolonged high‑dose corticosteroids (prednisone ≥20 mg daily for ≥4 weeks or equivalent) is Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis. Trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (one double‑strength tablet three times weekly or one single‑strength daily) is first‑line. Stop when steroid dose is tapered below the threshold and other immunosuppressants are stopped per clinical judgment. Routine antibacterial or antifungal prophylaxis is not indicated unless additional risk factors exist. Hepatitis B prophylaxis (nucleos(t)ide analogues) is indicated for HBsAg‑positive patients and should be strongly considered for anti‑HBc positive patients if the planned immunosuppression confers moderate–high reactivation risk; involve hepatology or ID for management. Treat latent TB if IGRA positive and treatment indicated. For patients from endemic areas, empiric ivermectin for Strongyloides prior to steroids can prevent life‑threatening hyperinfection.

IVIG considerations: IVIG itself is not broadly immunosuppressive but may blunt vaccine responses and is not a substitute for antimicrobial prophylaxis. Screen for renal dysfunction before IVIG (risk of AKI) and use slower infusions or lower‑sugar formulations in high‑risk patients. Standard blood‑borne infection risk with licensed products is negligible.

General supportive measures and monitoring: minimize steroid exposure by using pulse steroids + early steroid‑sparing strategies when appropriate (as in this case where IVIG likely shortened steroid need). Educate patients to report fevers, dysuria, cough, dyspnea or other infection signs promptly. Avoid unnecessary indwelling catheters and limit hospital exposure when safe. Monitor CBC, renal function, liver tests and inflammatory markers during therapy and have low threshold for cultures/imaging and early empiric therapy in febrile or otherwise ill patients.

Apply to this case: this patient received pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone ~1 mg/kg and IVIG. Best practice would have been to screen for HBV and latent TB (and Strongyloides if relevant) before or immediately at admission, to start PJP prophylaxis once prednisone was expected to be ≥20 mg daily for ≥4 weeks (or earlier if additional immunosuppressants added), to ensure inactivated influenza and pneumococcal vaccines were up to date when possible, and to avoid indwelling urinary catheters and other exposures that increase UTI risk. Because she developed a pseudomonal urosepsis after discharge, emphasis on catheter avoidance, hydration, early mobilization and prompt outpatient follow‑up for urinary symptoms would be high‑yield preventive steps.

Bottom line: screen and treat latent infections before high‑dose steroids, vaccinate with inactivated vaccines when possible prior to immunosuppression, give PJP prophylaxis for prolonged high‑dose steroid courses, consider HBV/Strongyloides management when indicated, minimize steroid duration, and maintain vigilant surveillance for early infection. These measures substantially reduce morbidity in IMNM patients treated with steroids and IVIG.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the relationship between IMNM and malignancy risk, and what cancer screening strategy is reasonable at diagnosis?

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is not as strongly linked to occult malignancy as dermatomyositis, but it is not entirely free of cancer risk. Large myositis cohorts show the highest paraneoplastic association with dermatomyositis (especially TIF1‑γ and NXP2 antibodies); by contrast most IMNM series report a lower but still measurable rate of concurrent or shortly preceding cancer (varying by cohort but often in the single‑digit to low‑teens percent). Some signals suggest that seronegative IMNM or atypical presentations may carry a relatively higher likelihood of an occult malignancy than antibody‑positive statin‑associated IMNM, although the data are limited and inconsistent. The practical implication is that IMNM should trigger thoughtful cancer evaluation, particularly around the time of diagnosis and during the first 12–24 months when a paraneoplastic process would be most likely to declare itself.

A reasonable, pragmatic cancer‑screening strategy at diagnosis is therefore to combine guideline‑based age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening with a focused baseline search for occult malignancy guided by symptoms and risk factors. This typically includes a careful history and physical exam (skin, lymph nodes, breasts, pelvic exam), up‑to‑date routine screens (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy as indicated), and targeted imaging — chest/abdomen/pelvis CT (or whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT if available and local practice supports it) when there are concerning features such as older age, systemic symptoms, rapid progression, unexplained weight loss, or seronegativity. Basic labs (CBC, chemistry, LFTs) are reasonable; routine tumor markers are not mandated unless clinically suggested. If the initial workup is negative, maintain a low threshold for repeat evaluation during the first 12–24 months (interval history/exam and consideration of repeat imaging) because paraneoplastic myopathies often present near the time of cancer diagnosis.

In short: IMNM carries a lower but non‑zero malignancy risk compared with dermatomyositis. At diagnosis perform guideline cancer screening plus directed baseline imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET/CT when indicated) and close clinical follow‑up with repeat assessment over the first 1–2 years.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does hypothyroid myopathy differ from IMNM in onset, severity of weakness, CK elevation, and response to therapy?

Think of hypothyroid myopathy as a metabolic, slowly reversible muscle dysfunction, and IMNM as an aggressive autoimmune necrotising process — the history, exam, labs and treatment follow from that difference.

Onset and tempo: hypothyroid myopathy evolves insidiously over weeks to months in the setting of untreated or undertreated hypothyroidism and is often slowly progressive with prominent myalgias, stiffness and exercise intolerance. IMNM is typically subacute to acute — weeks to a few months — with more rapid and progressive proximal weakness that can include bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and, in severe cases, respiratory or cardiac involvement.

Severity of weakness: weakness from hypothyroidism is usually mild-to-moderate, often symmetric and predominantly proximal but patients frequently remain ambulatory and retain most daily function. IMNM more commonly produces severe proximal weakness, marked functional loss (inability to rise from a chair, difficulty swallowing), and can produce profound disability over a short interval.

CK elevation: hypothyroid myopathy produces mild-to-moderate CK elevations (often below 1,000 U/L, commonly a few hundred), and levels fall gradually with thyroid replacement. IMNM usually causes much higher CKs (often in the thousands, sometimes >10,000), although there is overlap; a rising CK in the thousands together with rapidly progressive weakness should heighten concern for IMNM.

Response to therapy: hypothyroid myopathy responds to thyroid hormone replacement alone, with clinical improvement and normalization of CK over weeks to months; immunosuppression is not indicated. IMNM generally requires immunotherapy — high‑dose glucocorticoids plus additional agents such as IVIG or steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants — and often relapse or persistent weakness occurs without treatment; when drug‑triggered, stopping the culprit may help but many patients still need immunosuppression.

Supporting pathologic and serologic clues: muscle biopsy in hypothyroid myopathy shows non‑necrotic changes (fiber atrophy, type II fiber predominance) without prominent necrosis, macrophages or membrane attack complex deposition; IMNM shows myofibre necrosis, macrophage/myophagocytosis and complement deposition, and is often associated with specific autoantibodies (eg, anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) though some cases are seronegative.

In the presented case the rapid, marked functional decline with bulbar symptoms, CK rising into the thousands, biopsy evidence of necrosis and macrophage infiltrate, and steroid/IVIG responsiveness point to IMNM rather than hypothyroid myopathy; a normal TSH further argues against a hypothyroid cause.

### [Morning Report] What electromyographic (EMG) findings are typical of IMNM, and how do they help differentiate myopathic from neuropathic processes?

Electromyography in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) typically shows a myopathic pattern with evidence of active muscle fibre injury rather than the chronic reinnervation pattern of neuropathy. The common findings are short‑duration, low‑amplitude motor unit potentials with early recruitment and increased polyphasia, together with marked spontaneous activity — fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves — reflecting ongoing muscle fibre necrosis and membrane instability. Complex repetitive discharges or myotonic‑like discharges can also be seen, but are not specific.

How this differentiates myopathic from neuropathic processes is a matter of the motor unit geometry and recruitment pattern. In myopathy the individual myofibres comprising a motor unit are lost or electrically unstable, so MUAPs become lower in amplitude and shorter in duration and many motor units are still available, producing early (rapid) recruitment. In neuropathy, axonal loss produces fewer surviving motor units that enlarge by collateral reinnervation; MUAPs are therefore high amplitude and long duration, and recruitment is reduced. Both conditions can show fibrillations if there is active denervation (neuropathy) or active myofibre necrosis (myopathy), so fibrillations by themselves do not distinguish the two — the overall MUAP morphology and recruitment pattern are key.

Clinically useful points: in a patient like this one with high CK and rapidly progressive proximal weakness, a myopathic EMG with abundant fibrillations supports an active necrotising myopathy and helps localize the best biopsy site; EMG cannot replace biopsy or serology for definitive classification (IMNM versus other inflammatory myopathies) but it steers the diagnostic pathway and distinguishes primary muscle disease from motor neuropathy or neuromuscular junction disorders.

### [Morning Report] In patients with inflammatory myopathies, when and how should you screen for interstitial lung disease, and how does this influence management?

Teaching pearl:

Screen for interstitial lung disease (ILD) in every patient with a new inflammatory myopathy at baseline, and then selectively thereafter based on autoantibody profile, symptoms, and disease course — because identification of ILD changes both prognostic framing and initial treatment intensity.

Why and when: ILD is a frequent, potentially progressive extramuscular manifestation of certain myositis subtypes (most classically the antisynthetase syndrome — anti-Jo‑1/PL‑7/PL‑12 etc — and MDA‑5 associated disease, but also seen with anti-PM/Scl and others). ILD can precede, accompany, or follow muscle disease, and when present is a major driver of morbidity and mortality. Therefore perform screening at the time of myopathy diagnosis (before or at initiation of immunosuppression) even if respiratory symptoms are absent, and repeat testing promptly if new dyspnea, cough, hypoxemia, worsening exercise tolerance, or falling pulmonary function tests occur. Patients with “high‑risk” serologies (antisynthetase antibodies, MDA‑5), rapidly progressive myositis, or abnormal baseline studies deserve closer serial surveillance (eg, every 3–6 months during the first year).

How to screen: the pragmatic initial screen is a high‑resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) including spirometry, lung volumes and diffusion capacity (DLCO). Pulse oximetry at rest and with exertion (6‑minute walk) complements PFTs. A chest radiograph is insensitive and should not substitute for HRCT if there is clinical concern. If HRCT/PFTs are equivocal and symptoms persist, involve pulmonary medicine for consideration of bronchoscopy, lung biopsy, or cardiopulmonary exercise testing to separate cardiac from pulmonary causes.

How results influence management: finding ILD shifts management from a muscle‑focused strategy to a combined multidisciplinary approach and usually prompts earlier and more intensive immunosuppression targeted at the lung. In practice this often means choosing steroid‑sparing agents with better evidence for ILD (mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide for severe or rapidly progressive disease, and rituximab for refractory or antibody‑associated ILD), close pulmonary follow‑up, and earlier consideration of adjuncts such as antifibrotic therapy if a progressive fibrosing phenotype develops. Detection of ILD also alters counseling about infection risk, vaccination, monitoring frequency, and need for supplemental oxygen, pulmonary rehab, and, rarely, transplant evaluation. Finally, the autoantibody pattern that predicts ILD guides both the intensity of lung surveillance and choice of therapy.

Apply to this case: immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is less commonly associated with clinically significant ILD than antisynthetase or MDA‑5 myositis, but baseline screening is still appropriate. This patient had an HRCT chest that was negative for ILD and no respiratory symptoms; that result supports focusing therapy on the muscle disease (corticosteroids and IVIG in her case) and avoiding immediately escalating to lung‑directed agents. Nevertheless, because ILD can evolve, clinical vigilance and at least interval PFT/DLCO reassessment if symptoms or serologies change is warranted.

### [Management] Under what circumstances is drug rechallenge inappropriate after suspected drug-induced autoimmune myopathy, and what principles guide selection of alternative therapies for the original indication?

Rechallenge is inappropriate when the index event was a severe, plausibly immune‑mediated reaction with objective confirmation and meaningful clinical consequences — for example, a proximate temporal relationship to the drug, progressive weakness with high CK, biopsy demonstrating necrotising inflammatory myopathy, and need for immunosuppression or hospitalization, as in this case. In those circumstances the likelihood of true drug causality is high enough and the potential harm from recurrence (rapid relapse, respiratory or bulbar compromise, myocarditis, prolonged immunotherapy) great enough that re‑exposure is ethically unjustifiable. Additional features that push you to avoid rechallenge include multiorgan involvement, persistent autoimmunity or autoantibodies, and absence of urgent unmet therapeutic necessity for the culprit drug.

When you must choose an alternative to treat the original indication (here obesity/NAFLD/diabetes risk reduction), apply these principles. First, avoid the same pharmacologic class and agents with closely related mechanisms (other GLP‑1 receptor agonists or dual GIP/GLP‑1 agonists) because of plausible class or mechanistic cross‑reactivity. Second, prioritize nonpharmacologic and nonimmunogenic options: intensive lifestyle intervention, structured weight management programs, and consideration of metabolic/bariatric surgery when appropriate. Third, if a pharmacologic option is essential, select agents with unrelated mechanisms (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin as tolerated, or other diabetes/weight therapies without GLP‑1 agonism), and reassess baseline risk. Fourth, institute a shared decision process with clear documentation of the prior adverse event, discuss alternative efficacy and risks, and obtain informed consent if any re‑exposure is contemplated.

If there is truly no acceptable alternative and the benefit might outweigh the risk (rare), a rechallenge should only occur with multidisciplinary oversight (rheumatology/immunology, the prescribing specialty), a formal risk‑benefit assessment, informed consent, close inpatient monitoring, serial CK and symptom checks, readily available immunomodulatory therapy, and preferably after a prolonged drug‑free interval with demonstrable clinical and biomarker quiescence. Even then, many clinicians would decline rechallenge after biopsy‑proven IMNM.

Finally, report the event to pharmacovigilance, document the drug allergy/adverse reaction prominently in the chart, and plan robust follow‑up (education about early symptoms, scheduled CK checks if re‑exposure considered, and low threshold for restarting immunotherapy if recurrence occurs). These steps balance patient safety with legitimate clinical needs while acknowledging uncertainty about causality in single cases such as this one.
