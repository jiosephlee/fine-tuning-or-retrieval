Title: Blog about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

# GLP‑1s and Weakness: When CK Creeps, Think Beyond Rhabdo

What’s interesting here? Everyone now knows GLP‑1 receptor agonists can cause nausea, weight loss, and — very rarely — rhabdomyolysis. This case flips that script. A woman in her 60s developed progressive, steroid‑responsive immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. The CK didn’t skyrocket and then crater (classic toxic rhabdo); it smouldered and climbed over months while proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia evolved. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage predominance and complement (membrane attack complex) deposition — textbook for IMNM — and she improved after high‑dose steroids + IVIG and stopping the GLP‑1. Causality isn’t proven, but this should change how we think about GLP‑1–exposed patients with evolving weakness.

## Short case recap (the important bits)
A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD received one 0.25 mg dose of subcutaneous semaglutide. Within hours she had severe nausea/vomiting, was hospitalized with AKI and mild CK elevation (337 U/L). Over the next 4 months she developed progressive, painless proximal weakness with dysphagia and dysphonia. CK rose to ~4,000 U/L. Myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative except a low‑titer isolated SS‑B. MRI showed proximal muscle edema. Thigh muscle biopsy: dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage (CD68) myophagocytosis, rare T cells, and membrane attack complex — consistent with IMNM. Treated with IV methylprednisolone (1 g/day ×5), oral prednisone 1 mg/kg, and IVIG 2 g/kg divided over 4 days; semaglutide avoided. CK fell and strength recovered; off immunosuppression and back to baseline at 10‑month follow‑up.

## Why this matters clinically
- Rhabdomyolysis (drug toxic myopathy) and IMNM (autoimmune necrotising myopathy) can both present with elevated CK, but they behave differently. Recognizing the pattern matters because management differs — fluids and withdrawal alone may be enough for toxic rhabdo; autoimmune disease often needs immunosuppression.
- GLP‑1 agonists are hugely more common now. Even rare adverse events become relevant when exposure is widespread. This case raises a red flag to consider immune myopathy, not just rhabdo, in the right clinical context.

## How to think about GLP‑1–associated muscle injury: timeline and phenotype
- Acute toxic rhabdomyolysis pattern: abrupt large CK spike (often many thousands to tens of thousands), myalgias, dark urine, rapid improvement after stopping the drug and aggressive hydration.
- IMNM/autoimmune pattern: progressive, often painless proximal weakness developing over weeks–months, frequently with dysphagia/dysphonia, CK moderately to markedly elevated but not necessarily explosive, and often poor spontaneous recovery after drug withdrawal alone. Biopsy shows necrosis with macrophage predominance and complement deposition; autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) can be positive but up to ~1/3 of IMNM patients are seronegative.

In this case the CK rose over months with progressive weakness and bulbar symptoms — think autoimmune myopathy rather than classic toxic rhabdo.

## Practical diagnostic pivots — what to do when a patient on a GLP‑1 shows weakness or CK elevation
1. Initial ED/ward steps
   - Stop the GLP‑1 pending evaluation.
   - Basic labs: CK trend (don’t rely on a single value), renal function, electrolytes, LFTs, urinalysis (myoglobinuria), troponin if chest concerns.
   - If CK is very high or there are signs of rhabdo (AKI, hyperkalemia, cola urine), give aggressive IV fluids and monitor urine output and electrolytes.

2. Reassess the pattern over days–weeks
   - Toxic rhabdo often improves quickly after withdrawal + fluids. If CK and symptoms fall rapidly, ongoing supportive care may suffice.
   - If CK is rising or symptoms progress despite stopping the drug (especially progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, new cardiopulmonary signs), escalate workup for IMNM.

3. Targeted neuromuscular workup when autoimmune myopathy is suspected
   - Neurology/rheumatology consult early.
   - CK trend, aldolase if available.
   - Myositis/IMNM serologies (include anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP and the standard myositis panel), remembering that serology can be negative.
   - MRI muscle to look for edema pattern and guide biopsy site.
   - EMG if available (helps differentiate neuropathic vs myopathic process).
   - Muscle biopsy if clinical picture suggests IMNM — this is the definitive test and informs therapy.
   - Screen for cardiac involvement (ECG, troponin, echo / cardiac MRI if concern), and investigate alternative triggers (recent statin use, supplements like red yeast rice, infection, paraneoplastic evaluation as indicated).

4. When to start immunosuppression
   - Consider early immunotherapy (high‑dose IV steroids ± IVIG) when there is severe weakness (inability to ambulate, bulbar involvement, respiratory compromise), rising CK despite stopping the drug, or biopsy/MRI supportive of IMNM.
   - Drug‑induced IMNM may respond to a shorter course of immunosuppression after stopping the medication, but idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged combination immunosuppression. Balance treatment urgency against infection risk.

## Biopsy pearls for the busy clinician
- IMNM histology: abundant necrotic and regenerating fibres with relatively few lymphocytes; macrophage/myophagocytic change is common. Membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition on myofibre membranes supports immune‑mediated injury.
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies point to IMNM subtypes, but absence of these antibodies does not exclude IMNM — up to a third can be seronegative.

## Treatment takeaways (from this case and the literature)
- Immediate: stop the suspected offending drug and treat acute rhabdo complications.
- If IMNM suspected or confirmed: high‑dose IV steroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone pulses) and consider IVIG early for severe presentations or bulbar/cardiac involvement. This case responded to pulse steroids + IVIG with successful tapering off prednisone and no long‑term immunosuppression.
- Don’t rechallenge the offending GLP‑1 — unethical and potentially dangerous.

## Mechanism and uncertainty
We don’t know how a GLP‑1 agonist could trigger IMNM. GLP‑1 drugs have been associated with reduced muscle mass and rare rhabdo reports (tirzepatide has a reported rhabdomyolysis case). WHO‑UMC causality in this report was “probable” (reasonable time relationship, improvement on withdrawal). But a pre‑existing smouldering autoimmune myopathy can’t be absolutely excluded because no pre‑drug CK was available. Bottom line: this is a signal, not proof, but it should prompt vigilance.

## FOAM clinical pearls
- Think timeline: immediate massive CK spike → toxic rhabdo; insidious rise + progressive painless proximal weakness ± bulbar symptoms → think IMNM.
- Stop the GLP‑1 and follow the CK trend. Rapid fall → likely toxic and supportive care may suffice; rising CK despite withdrawal → expedite neuromuscular workup.
- Myositis serologies can be negative in IMNM. Don’t delay biopsy if clinical suspicion is high.
- Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysphonia), inability to ambulate, or evidence of cardiac involvement are red flags to start immunotherapy urgently.
- Report suspected serious adverse drug reactions — pharmacovigilance matters when drugs reach millions of users.

## Bottom line
Not every CK elevation after a GLP‑1 injection is classic rhabdomyolysis. If weakness is progressive, CK climbs over weeks–months, or you see bulbar symptoms or MRI/biopsy evidence of necrotising myopathy, think IMNM. Stop the drug, monitor closely, involve neurology/rheumatology early, and be ready to biopsy and treat with immunosuppression (often steroids + IVIG) if the picture points toward autoimmune necrosis. More cases and vigilance will tell us whether this is a rare idiosyncratic reaction or a reproducible drug‑triggered syndrome.



# Seronegative IMNM: When the Panel Is Negative, the Biopsy Is the Biomarker

Why this case matters: a woman in her 60s developed rapidly progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12-fold rise in CK over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Her myositis serologies — including the two antibodies most associated with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP — were negative. The muscle biopsy, not the panel, provided the diagnosis: dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition and only rare T‑cells. Treatment with high‑dose steroids and IVIG led to recovery and sustained remission off immunosuppression while she avoided GLP‑1 receptor agonists. This is a neat reminder: up to one‑third of IMNM is seronegative — and in those patients tissue is the test that matters.

## What is “seronegative” IMNM?

IMNM is an aggressive, predominantly proximal necrotising myopathy classically associated with anti‑HMGCR (statin‑linked) or anti‑SRP antibodies. Histologically it stands apart from polymyositis and dermatomyositis: prominent fibre necrosis/regeneration with a relative paucity of lymphocytes. But serology is not omniscient — roughly 20–30% of IMNM patients have no detectable HMGCR or SRP antibodies. In those patients, clinical suspicion + targeted diagnostics (imaging, biopsy) must drive diagnosis and management.

Key features from this case that scream IMNM despite a negative panel:
- Rapidly progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia).
- Marked CK elevation (peak 3992 U/L).
- MRI showing symmetric proximal muscle edema (vastus lateralis involvement).
- Muscle biopsy: necrotic and regenerating myofibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis, MAC deposition, scarce CD3+ T‑cells.

## Why the biopsy “wins” here

When serologies are negative, the biopsy is the biomarker. Characteristic IMNM histopathology to look for:
- Scattered/patchy necrotic and regenerating fibres rather than a uniform inflammatory infiltrate.
- Macrophage‑predominant infiltrate with myophagocytosis (CD68+).
- Deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9/MAC) on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres.
- Sparse lymphocytes (occasional CD3+ T cells) — i.e., not the dense endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates of polymyositis.

In this patient the biopsy showed exactly that pattern. That pattern, paired with the clinical picture, is diagnostic of IMNM even without HMGCR/SRP antibodies.

## Use MRI to target the biopsy (and the diagnosis)

MRI of muscles is a practical, non‑invasive adjunct and, crucially, helps select the best biopsy site. In this case MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema in the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps — and the biopsy from the vastus lateralis confirmed IMNM.

Practical MRI tips:
- Look for symmetric proximal thigh and pelvic girdle edema.
- Fatty replacement and chronic changes suggest chronic disease; early inflammatory edema favors an active, potentially treatable process.
- Target biopsy to an edematous but not end‑stage fatty replaced area for the best yield.

## How to think about a negative myositis panel — a practical approach

Start with a clear clinical pattern: subacute → rapidly progressive proximal weakness, CK often in the thousands, bulbar symptoms, and constitutional signs or cardiopulmonary involvement. If that pattern exists, negative antibodies do not rule out IMNM.

Suggested sequence in a suspected IMNM when serology is negative or equivocal:
1. Thorough history — meds (statins, supplements like red yeast rice, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and increasingly GLP‑1 RAs), recent infections, exposures.
2. Baseline labs — CK, AST/ALT (remember hepatic vs muscular source), TSH, ESR/CRP, renal function.
3. Myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, Jo‑1 and others) — perform, but don’t stop there if clinical suspicion remains.
4. Muscle MRI to map edema and guide biopsy.
5. Consider EMG as adjunct (can show myopathic units and irritability) — but a normal EMG doesn’t exclude IMNM.
6. Proceed to muscle biopsy early when: progressive weakness, bulbar involvement/dysphagia, CK markedly elevated, MRI/EMG consistent or unclear — especially if serologies are negative.

In short: treat the patient, not the panel. If the clinical picture and imaging point to IMNM, get tissue.

## Red herrings and pitfalls — what tripped up this case (and how to avoid it)

- Isolated low‑titer antibodies: this patient had a low‑titer SS‑B/La with negative ANA and SS‑A. Isolated, low‑titer SS‑B without supportive clinical features is unlikely to explain the picture and was a red herring. Always interpret autoantibodies in clinical context.
- Prior mild transaminase elevation: she had hepatosteatosis documented years earlier. Mild chronic AST/ALT elevation can be hepatic; recall that AST/ALT can also rise from muscle — but biopsy confirmed NAFLD in this patient and CK normalized after treatment, arguing the transaminitis was chronic hepatic disease rather than muscle breakdown.
- Attributing everything to a single exposure: this patient had a striking immediate intolerance to semaglutide and temporal relationship to onset of symptoms supports a probable association, but causality is not proven. Nonetheless, withdrawal of the offending agent (and reporting adverse events) is reasonable.

## Management learning points from the case

IMNM frequently requires prompt immunosuppression. In this case the team used high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a single course of IVIG (2 g/kg split over 4 days). The patient improved clinically and biochemically and remained off immunosuppression for months after a short course — a pattern consistent with drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy in which removal of the trigger can shorten the required immunotherapy.

Practical takeaways:
- Consider early aggressive therapy (steroids ± IVIG) in severe IMNM (bulbar involvement, very high CK, respiratory compromise).
- HMGCR/SRP negativity does not obviate immunotherapy if clinical and histologic findings support IMNM.
- Search for and stop potential triggers (statins, supplements, potentially GLP‑1 RAs in this case).
- Report suspected drug‑induced cases to pharmacovigilance bodies.

## Bottom line

Seronegative IMNM is real and not rare. Don’t be reassured by a “negative myositis panel” when the clinical picture and imaging suggest an aggressive, necrotising myopathy. Muscle biopsy remains the decisive diagnostic tool — it is both the biomarker and the guide to therapy in seronegative cases. This semaglutide‑associated case illustrates that timely recognition, tissue diagnosis and prompt immunotherapy (with withdrawal of suspected offending agent) can restore patients to baseline.

Key take‑home messages
- Up to ~1/3 of IMNM patients are seronegative for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP — a negative panel does not rule out IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy is diagnostic: look for necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis (CD68+), MAC (C5b‑9) deposition and sparse lymphocytes.
- Use MRI to identify active edema and to target the biopsy to the most informative site.
- Proceed early to biopsy when there is rapidly progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, CK in the thousands, or discordant/negative serology.
- Interpret isolated, low‑titer autoantibodies (eg, SS‑B alone) in clinical context — they may be incidental.
- Treat the disease pattern: if biopsy and clinical picture indicate IMNM, start immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) and withdraw suspected offending agents; report suspected drug associations.



# Hitting Hard, Tapering Fast: Managing Suspected Drug‑Triggered IMNM

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12‑fold rise in CK over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage/myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — diagnostic for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She received 5 days of pulse methylprednisolone, IVIG 2 g/kg (split over 4 days) and oral prednisone 1 mg/kg with a relatively rapid taper as her strength and CK normalized. Ten months later she was back to baseline off immunosuppression. This looks like a drug‑triggered IMNM that responded briskly to targeted, short‑course immunotherapy — a useful template for real‑world front‑line management.

Why this matters
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are being used widely for diabetes and obesity. Most adverse effects are GI, but clinicians should be alert for muscle syndromes beyond simple sarcopenia or transient CK small bumps.
- Drug‑associated IMNM can behave differently from idiopathic antibody‑positive IMNM (HMGCR or SRP): when truly drug‑triggered, withdrawal of the trigger plus early immunotherapy (steroids ± IVIG) can produce a rapid and sustained response — avoiding prolonged multi‑agent immunosuppression in some patients.
- The differential includes rhabdomyolysis, statin‑associated HMGCR IMNM, and idiopathic IMNM — muscle biopsy and serial CK/clinical trajectory guide management.

Practical regimen (grounded in the case)
- Indications for an aggressive start: severe proximal weakness (unable to sit/stand), bulbar dysfunction/dysphagia, very high CK or rapidly rising CK, respiratory compromise. In this case dysphagia and loss of basic function justified aggressive early therapy.
- Pulse steroids for fulminant disease: methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily × 3–5 days. This was used here for 5 days with clinical benefit.
- Transition to high‑dose oral steroid: prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (adjust for comorbidities). Use daily outpatient dosing once the acute phase is controlled.
- Early IVIG for severe features: IVIG 2 g/kg total, split over 2–5 days. In this case IVIG was given over 4 days and appeared to accelerate recovery.
- Rapid, guided taper when appropriate: if strength improves and CK normalizes quickly, consider a faster taper (weeks to a couple of months) rather than the prolonged steroid courses often required for idiopathic IMNM. This patient was weaned off prednisone over ~2 months and remained well off therapy.
- Always stop the suspected offending drug and counsel avoidance of rechallenge (in this case, avoid GLP‑1 RAs permanently).

When to add steroid‑sparing or escalation therapy
- Failure to improve clinically or biochemically within 4–8 weeks despite adequate steroid +/− IVIG.
- Inability to reduce prednisone to <15–20 mg/day without relapse.
- Recurrent rises in CK or recurrent weakness on taper.
- Presence of antibody‑positive IMNM known to be refractory (e.g., anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR) — these often need combination therapy.

Common second‑line agents (choose based on phenotype, comorbidity, and local practice)
- Methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil as steroid‑sparing agents for moderate disease.
- IVIG is especially effective in HMGCR‑associated IMNM and is a good early option for severe disease or if steroids are contraindicated/tolerated poorly.
- Rituximab can be considered for refractory antibody‑mediated cases (anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR) or relapsing disease.
- Cyclophosphamide is reserved for severe extramuscular organ involvement (e.g., ILD, severe myocarditis) or life‑threatening refractory disease.

Monitoring cadence and investigations
- Baseline: CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise transaminases), CBC, CMP (renal function especially before IVIG), glucose, TSH, infectious screens as indicated (HBV/HCV, TB if starting high‑level immunosuppression), ECG and troponin if cardiac symptoms.
- Inpatient: daily strength exam and CK every 24–72 hours while acutely ill. Swallow evaluation (speech/swallow therapist) before advancing diet if bulbar symptoms.
- Early outpatient: CK and clinical exam every 1–2 weeks until stable; then spacing to monthly as strength and CK normalize.
- Cardiac surveillance if troponin/proBNP abnormal or symptoms: echocardiogram ± cardiac MRI. Repeat if initial abnormalities were seen (this patient’s myocardium normalized on follow‑up imaging).
- Muscle MRI can help map active inflammation for biopsy; biopsy remains key when diagnosis is unclear.
- Report the reaction to pharmacovigilance systems (FDA MedWatch/EMA) and document in the chart to avoid rechallenge.

Supportive care: rehab, nutrition and prevention
- Early PT/OT and swallow therapy are essential; start mobilization as soon as medically feasible to limit deconditioning.
- Nutrition: consult dietitian; aim for adequate calories and protein (often 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day depending on renal function and catabolic state). Consider enteral support if dysphagia persists.
- Bone and gastric protection for steroid therapy: calcium, vitamin D, and consider bisphosphonate if prolonged steroids planned; PPI if GI risk factors.
- DVT prophylaxis while immobile; attention to skin and pressure‑sore prevention.
- Avoid aggressive exercise until CK normalizes and strength improves.

Mitigating infection risk (learned from the case)
- High‑dose steroids and immunotherapy increase infection risk. This patient developed pseudomonal urosepsis after hospital discharge.
- Strategies:
  - Avoid unnecessary indwelling urinary catheters; remove early.
  - Screen for occult infections before and during therapy (urine, chest, lines).
  - Consider PCP prophylaxis if prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) or when adding other immunosuppressants.
  - Vaccinate when possible before immunosuppression (influenza, pneumococcus), and counsel about live vaccines (avoid on high‑level immunosuppression).
  - Close outpatient follow‑up in the first 2–4 weeks after discharge to pick up early infection signs.

Pitfalls and practical pearls
- Don’t assume every CK elevation after a new med is simple rhabdomyolysis — progressive weakness, bulbar symptoms, biopsy changes and persistent CK elevation suggest IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when the picture is unclear — IMNM classically shows necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage‑predominant infiltrates, and membrane attack complex deposition.
- Serology may be negative — up to ~1/3 of IMNM can be seronegative. Treat the patient, not the antibody panel.
- IVIG dosing: 2 g/kg total, and split dosing limits infusion reactions and volume issues. Check renal function and consider iso‑osmolar preparations in renal impairment.
- Rapid improvement after stopping the offending drug + brief immunotherapy favors a drug‑triggered phenotype; idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged, multi‑agent therapy.
- Always document the suspected drug reaction clearly and counsel permanent avoidance of the implicated agent — rechallenge risks a severe relapse.

Key take‑home messages
- Suspect drug‑triggered IMNM when progressive proximal weakness and sustained CK elevation follow new medication exposure (including GLP‑1 RAs).
- For severe presentations (dysphagia, marked weakness, high/rapidly rising CK), start pulse methylprednisolone (1 g/day × 3–5), transition to prednisone ~1 mg/kg, and add IVIG early (2 g/kg split) if severe features present.
- If function and CK normalize quickly after trigger withdrawal and short immunotherapy, a rapid taper can be appropriate; if not, escalate with steroid‑sparing agents or rituximab per phenotype.
- Monitor closely (CK, strength, swallow, cardiac markers), prioritize early rehab and nutrition, and actively mitigate infection risk.
- Avoid rechallenge with the offending agent and report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance authorities.

This case gives a real‑world template: hit hard up front for severe, drug‑triggered IMNM, reassess frequently, taper fast when safe, and reserve long multi‑drug immunosuppression for refractory or antibody‑positive disease.



# The Quiet Heart in Myositis: Interpreting Troponin, BNP, and CMR

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s with biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) who developed mild high‑sensitivity troponin elevation, a very high pro‑BNP (4358 pg/mL), transient atrial fibrillation, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema and mild regional hypokinesis — but no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). EF was preserved. Clinically she recovered on short course immunosuppression and IVIG. This is a perfect clinical vignette for the problem many clinicians face: subclinical or equivocal cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies — how aggressively to look, how to interpret mildly abnormal tests, and when to treat.

Below I walk through a practical, pragmatic approach: which tests to screen with, what each abnormality likely means in this setting, when to escalate to cardiac MRI or biopsy, and how to follow arrhythmias and imaging over time.

## Why cardiac screening matters in inflammatory myopathies
Inflammatory myopathies (polymyositis, dermatomyositis, IMNM, antisynthetase syndromes) commonly have cardiac involvement that ranges from subclinical biomarker abnormalities to fulminant myocarditis. Cardiac disease may present as arrhythmia, conduction disease, heart failure, or sudden death. Because symptoms can be subtle (fatigue, dyspnoea) and skeletal muscle disease can complicate interpretation of some tests, a low threshold for screening is appropriate.

## Practical baseline screen (what I do when I see a new inflammatory myopathy)
- 12‑lead ECG (look for conduction disease, ischemic changes, arrhythmia)
- High‑sensitivity troponin — prefer troponin I over troponin T when possible
- BNP or NT‑proBNP
- Transthoracic echocardiography (EF, wall motion abnormalities, RV pressures, pericardial effusion)

Why troponin I? Troponin T assays can be confounded by skeletal muscle regeneration or chronic neuromuscular disease (false positives). Troponin I is more cardiac specific in this setting, so when you care about myocarditis vs muscle disease, troponin I is preferable.

BNP/NT‑proBNP help gauge myocardial strain/volume overload even when EF is preserved (as here). A very elevated pro‑BNP (like 4358 pg/mL) is unlikely to be a laboratory fluke and suggests clinically relevant cardiac stress — correlate with volume status, renal function, and pulmonary pressures.

ECG is cheap, and echo gives instant structural/functional context (EF, regional wall motion, RVSP). If screening is normal but patient has new cardiopulmonary symptoms, don’t be reassured.

## When to proceed to cardiac MRI (CMR)
Get CMR if any of the following:
- Elevated cardiac troponin (preferably troponin I) not explained by other causes
- Elevated BNP with symptoms or abnormal echo
- New or unexplained arrhythmia or conduction abnormality (e.g., new AF in this patient)
- Regional wall motion abnormalities on echo without coronary explanation
- Persistent cardiopulmonary symptoms despite normal routine testing

CMR is the non‑invasive gold standard for detecting myocardial inflammation (edema), mapping tissue characteristics (native T1, T2, ECV), and identifying irreversible injury (LGE).

## How to interpret edema without LGE
In this patient: myocardial edema and mild hypokinesis on CMR, but no LGE; troponin only mildly elevated and EF preserved.

Key points for interpretation:
- Edema (T2/STIR or elevated T2 mapping) indicates active or recent myocardial inflammation or injury. It can be transient.
- LGE reflects irreversible focal necrosis/fibrosis. Absence of LGE does not exclude myocarditis — it may mean injury is early, diffuse (below LGE detection), or predominantly cellular/edematous without replacement fibrosis.
- Updated Lake Louise Criteria (2018) require at least one T2‑based criterion (edema) and one T1‑based criterion (elevated native T1, ECV, or LGE) to confidently diagnose myocarditis. If you have only edema but normal T1/ECV/LGE, the diagnosis is less certain but still plausible — clinical context matters.
- Prognosis: presence and extent of LGE predicts worse outcomes (arrhythmia, heart failure). Edema alone often resolves and portends a better short‑term prognosis if the trigger is treated.

In short: edema without LGE can represent early or focal autoimmune myocarditis (or reversible stress cardiomyopathy, septic cardiomyopathy, or even artifact). In this case the constellation (IMNM, troponin up, very high pro‑BNP, new AF, CMR edema) raises reasonable concern for myocardial involvement even though LGE is absent.

## Differentiating myocarditis from stress (Takotsubo) or demand injury
- Takotsubo typically has characteristic apical or midventricular ballooning and regional wall motion abnormalities beyond a single coronary territory, often triggered by stress, and can show edema without LGE early on.
- Sepsis or severe metabolic stress can cause transient myocardial edema and troponin leak.
- Viral myocarditis and autoimmune myocarditis both produce edema; autoimmune myocarditis is more likely when systemic autoimmune disease is present.

Use the clinical context (timing, triggers, systemic inflammatory markers, infection signs) to orient diagnosis. In this case: systemic autoimmune myopathy and no clear infective driver when CMR was done — favor autoimmune myocardial involvement until proven otherwise.

## What to do therapeutically when cardiac involvement is suspected but not definitive
- Hospitalize and monitor if there is troponin elevation, arrhythmia, hemodynamic compromise, or very high BNP.
- Telemetry during acute evaluation — new AF or other arrhythmias warrant inpatient monitoring.
- If autoimmune myocarditis is likely (inflammatory myopathy with cardiac imaging/biomarker abnormalities), treat the systemic disease in coordination with cardiology and rheumatology. High‑dose corticosteroids are commonly used for autoimmune myocarditis; additional immunosuppression can be considered case by case.
- Avoid empiric high‑dose steroid monotherapy for presumed viral myocarditis unless autoimmune cause is more likely — coordinate with specialists.
- Manage cardiac complications per usual guidelines: heart failure therapies for reduced EF, rate/rhythm control and anticoagulation for AF guided by CHA2DS2‑VASc and bleeding risk.
- Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) is reserved for cases with fulminant heart failure, refractory arrhythmia, rapidly progressive disease despite therapy, or when specific histologic diagnosis will change management (e.g., giant cell myocarditis). Most subclinical/indeterminate cases do not require EMB.

In the presented case the team used high‑dose IV methylprednisolone and IVIG for IMNM; the patient had clinical recovery and normalization of cardiac testing over months — a reasonable outcome consistent with inflammatory rather than ischemic or irreversible myocardial injury.

## Arrhythmia surveillance and anticoagulation
- New atrial fibrillation in this context: treat as you would in other settings — control rate/rhythm as clinically indicated and consider anticoagulation based on stroke risk (age, HTN, prior thromboembolism).
- Short, in‑hospital telemetry is mandatory while troponin/BNP/CMR are being worked up.
- After discharge, use ambulatory monitoring if symptoms persist or if there was documented paroxysmal AF. Options: 24–48‑hour Holter, 2–4 week event monitor, or longer insertable loop recorder if suspicion for intermittent significant arrhythmia is high.
- If myocarditis is suspected, thresholds to escalate monitoring are lower because inflammatory myocarditis can cause malignant ventricular arrhythmias.

## Follow‑up imaging and biomarkers
A reasonable follow‑up schedule when cardiac involvement was suspected:
- Repeat troponin I and BNP at 1–2 weeks to confirm trend, and again at 6–12 weeks.
- Repeat TTE at about 3 months (sooner if symptoms or deterioration).
- Consider repeat CMR at 3–6 months if initial CMR showed edema or if symptoms/persistent biomarkers abnormal — resolution of edema and absence of new LGE is reassuring.
- If LGE appears or persists, more prolonged surveillance and possible long‑term cardiology follow‑up are warranted.

In this case the patient’s repeat echo and CMR normalized over months and arrhythmia did not recur — a pattern consistent with reversible inflammatory injury.

## Clinical pearls
- Prefer troponin I over troponin T in inflammatory myopathies because troponin T can be falsely elevated from regenerating skeletal muscle.
- A very high BNP in the setting of preserved EF is a red flag for myocardial strain or right heart involvement — don’t ignore it.
- CMR edema without LGE can indicate early or reversible myocarditis (or stress cardiomyopathy); absence of LGE reduces long‑term adverse prognosis but does not exclude clinically meaningful inflammation.
- Endomyocardial biopsy is for severe, rapidly progressive, or treatment‑refractory cases; most patients with mild/moderate suspected autoimmune myocarditis can be managed noninvasively with close monitoring and multidisciplinary care.
- For new AF in inflammatory myopathy, institute telemetry and follow anticoagulation and rhythm/rate control principles—coordinate with rheumatology if immunosuppression is started.

## Take‑home messages
- Screen all patients with new inflammatory myopathy for cardiac involvement with ECG, troponin I, BNP, and baseline echocardiogram.
- Proceed to CMR if biomarkers, ECG, echo, or symptoms are abnormal — CMR is the most informative noninvasive test for myocarditis.
- Myocardial edema on CMR without LGE is meaningful: it can represent active, potentially reversible inflammation; interpret with biomarkers and clinical context.
- Manage suspected autoimmune myocarditis in a multidisciplinary fashion: monitor closely, treat systemic disease when indicated, and reserve EMB for severe or unexplained cases.
- Plan serial biomarker and imaging follow‑up (1–3 months) — resolution of edema and normalization of troponin/BNP are reassuring.

This patient’s course — transient AF, mild troponin elevation, very high pro‑BNP, CMR edema without LGE, and recovery after immunosuppression — highlights the “quiet” but real cardiac signals in myositis. Don’t miss them; they change monitoring, treatment, and follow‑up.



# AST, ALT, and CK: Untangling Liver From Muscle in Patients With NAFLD

What makes this case interesting: a patient with biopsy-proven NAFLD who develops an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. Her CK and transaminases rose together during the myopathic phase, then CK normalized while AST/ALT stayed mildly elevated — a perfect clinical vignette showing how transaminase elevations can come from muscle, liver, or both, and how anchoring to one diagnosis (usually “it’s the liver”) can mislead management.

## Quick physiology refresh — AST and ALT are not both “liver-specific”
- AST (aspartate aminotransferase) is abundant in liver, heart, skeletal muscle, kidney and red cells. ALT (alanine aminotransferase) is more concentrated in liver but is also present in muscle.
- Because AST is relatively plentiful in muscle, muscle injury often drives AST up more than ALT — sometimes producing AST>ALT.
- GGT (gamma‑glutamyl transferase) and alkaline phosphatase are more liver/cholestasis‑specific. A normal GGT with high AST/ALT points away from hepatic injury.
- CK (creatine kinase) is the most sensitive routine blood test for skeletal muscle injury. Aldolase and LDH can also be elevated in muscle disease.

Bottom line: AST/ALT elevations can be muscle-derived. Don’t assume every transaminase abnormality in a patient with metabolic disease is due to NAFLD.

## What the case teaches us (timeline that matters)
- Three years before the myopathy, the patient had mild transaminase elevations with ALT>AST and biopsy-proven steatohepatitis — consistent with NAFLD.
- Within hours of her first semaglutide dose she had severe GI illness and AKI, then progressive weakness over 4 months.
- At myopathy presentation CK peaked ~4,000 U/L and AST 191/ALT 165 U/L (AST>ALT). MRI showed muscle edema; muscle biopsy confirmed IMNM.
- After IV methylprednisolone + IVIG, CK returned to normal and her weakness improved, but the transaminases remained mildly elevated — consistent with her preexisting hepatic steatosis rather than ongoing muscle injury.

Clinical lesson: tracking trends and timing of CK relative to AST/ALT made the difference here. Early ALT>AST and biopsy evidence pointed to NAFLD as a chronic background problem; the later AST>ALT + parallel CK rise signalled an acute muscle process superimposed on chronic liver disease.

## Practical approach — an algorithm to avoid anchoring errors
1. Clinical assessment first
   - Ask about muscle symptoms (weakness, myalgias, dysphagia), systemic symptoms, recent drugs (statins, GLP‑1 RAs, immune checkpoint inhibitors), trauma, exertion.
   - Look for liver‑specific clues: jaundice, pruritus, hepatomegaly, risk factors for viral/hepatic disease.

2. Initial bloods
   - AST, ALT, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, GGT
   - CK (total), aldolase or LDH if available
   - INR and albumin if you suspect synthetic liver dysfunction

3. Interpret patterns
   - If CK is high and tracks with AST/ALT: likely muscle source. AST often rises more than ALT in muscle injury.
   - If GGT is elevated with AST/ALT: more likely hepatic.
   - If CK normal but clinical suspicion for chronic myopathy is high (insidious weakness, dysphagia), consider that CK can be normal in late/chronic disease — proceed to MRI/muscle enzyme panel or neuromuscular referral.

4. Short‑term management decision
   - If muscle injury suspected (CK markedly elevated, clinical weakness): stop offending agents (statin, consider GLP‑1 RA), hydrate if rhabdomyolysis risk, and pursue further neuromuscular workup (electromyography, MRI, muscle biopsy) where indicated.
   - If liver disease suspected (persistent transaminase elevation independent of CK trends, abnormal GGT/ALP, imaging suggesting steatosis/fibrosis): evaluate for NAFLD/NASH, viral hepatitis, autoimmune/metabolic liver disease.

5. When to image or biopsy
   - Abdominal ultrasound is a reasonable first step for suspected hepatic steatosis.
   - Consider transient elastography or fibrosis scoring if NAFLD suspected and transaminases persist >6 months or if clinical risk for fibrosis.
   - Liver biopsy is for diagnostic uncertainty, staging, or if results will change management (e.g., NASH vs other causes).
   - Muscle MRI and muscle biopsy are indicated when inflammatory myopathy is suspected, especially if CK is very elevated, weakness is progressive, or there are bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia) as in this case.

## Special considerations: drugs and seronegative autoimmune myopathies
- Statins classically associate with HMGCR antibody-positive IMNM; however, up to a third of IMNM patients can be seronegative. Muscle biopsy remains definitive when suspicion is high.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are increasingly used and have been associated with muscle mass loss and rare rhabdomyolysis. This case raises the possibility of an IMNM temporally related to semaglutide; causality is probable but not proven.
- A normal ANA or negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM. If clinical picture and imaging/biopsy support myositis, treat empirically (as was effective here with steroids + IVIG).

## When the liver is really to blame
- NAFLD typically causes ALT>AST in early disease; AST>ALT suggests advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis — or a concurrent non‑hepatic source (muscle, heart). Use trend data: if AST/ALT remain elevated despite CK normalization and liver imaging shows steatosis, the liver is the likely source.
- Persistent elevation out of proportion to prior baseline, or new cholestatic features (high GGT/ALP), or abnormal synthetic function warrants hepatology workup and consideration of biopsy.

## Case-specific takeaways
- Don’t anchor on the known NAFLD diagnosis; new neuromuscular symptoms and a rising CK should immediately prompt evaluation for muscle disease.
- Track CK and transaminases together. If CK and AST/ALT rise in parallel, think muscle. If CK normalizes but AST/ALT persist, think liver.
- Use GGT/ALP to help attribute transaminases to liver vs muscle.
- Muscle biopsy can be diagnostic in seronegative IMNM and changes management — as in this patient who improved after immunosuppression and IVIG.
- Be vigilant with new or recently started drugs (statins, GLP‑1 RAs, others) as potential triggers for muscle injury; rechallenge may be unethical in severe reactions.

Key clinical pearls
- AST is not liver‑specific; it can be bumped by muscle injury — AST>ALT can come from muscle.
- Always check a CK when you see elevated transaminases and any muscle symptoms or weakness.
- A normal GGT with transaminase elevations points away from hepatic origin.
- Persistent transaminase elevation after CK normalizes suggests hepatic rather than muscle source — consider liver imaging/biopsy based on risk factors.
- Negative serology does not rule out IMNM; muscle MRI/biopsy is often decisive.

Bottom line: In patients with metabolic disease and NAFLD, transaminase abnormalities can come from the liver, muscle, or both. Use history, CK trends, GGT/ALP, imaging and, when needed, biopsy (liver or muscle) to avoid diagnostic anchoring. This semaglutide‑associated IMNM case is a reminder to always ask “Could this be muscle?” before assuming the liver is guilty.



# From Blockbuster to Signal: Assessing Semaglutide as a Trigger Using WHO‑UMC

What makes this case interesting — and a little worrying — is the timing and phenotype. A woman in her 60s received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, developed an immediate intolerance with GI upset and AKI, then over the next 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, a CK that climbed into the thousands and a thigh muscle biopsy showing immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative, and she improved after stopping semaglutide and receiving pulse steroids + IVIG. The authors apply WHO‑UMC causality criteria and judge the link “probable.” Below I walk through that reasoning in plain language and translate it to practical ED/clinic actions: what to look for, when to check CK, how to handle confounders (statins, supplements), how to report suspected adverse drug reactions, and how to think about re‑exposure.

## Applying the WHO‑UMC causality framework to this case — a quick readout

WHO‑UMC uses structured categories (certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable/unclassifiable) based on timing, plausible mechanism, alternative explanations, response to withdrawal (dechallenge) and re‑exposure (rechallenge).

- Temporal relationship: present. Symptoms began within hours (GI/AKI) and progressive myopathy developed over weeks–months after the single dose. A delayed immune phenomenon after an exposure can be biologically plausible.
- Dechallenge (withdrawal): supportive but confounded. The patient improved after semaglutide cessation and immunosuppression. Because immunotherapy (steroids + IVIG) was given, improvement is not purely spontaneous, but stopping the drug plus immunosuppression produced durable recovery.
- Alternative explanations: reasonably excluded. No statin exposure (important for HMGCR‑associated IMNM), negative cancer/ILD workup in the report, liver biopsy consistent with NAFLD predating semaglutide (less likely to explain the new phenotype). IMNM can be seronegative; absence of autoantibodies does not rule out autoimmune disease.
- Rechallenge: not done (and would be unethical given severity).

Putting it together: the WHO‑UMC fit here is “probable/likely” — reasonable timing, a response on withdrawal plus appropriate treatment, and no better alternative explanation. Not “certain” because no positive autoantibody, no spontaneous resolution on withdrawal alone, and rechallenge is absent.

That’s not proof of causation, but it’s a signal worth attention.

## What this means at the bedside / clinic — practical takeaways

1) Before you start a GLP‑1 RA (semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, etc.)
- Review baseline risk: prior autoimmune disease, prior myositis, unexplained CK elevations, current statin or red‑yeast‑rice/supplement use, significant muscle symptoms. If the patient has a known inflammatory myopathy, involve rheumatology before initiating.
- Consider baseline CK in patients at higher pretest probability (myalgias, prior CK elevation, on statin or other myotoxic meds). There are no universal guideline mandates — use clinical judgment.

2) What to counsel patients to watch for
- Progressive proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), new or worsening dysphagia or dysphonia, new severe myalgias, dark urine, or inability to perform usual activities. GI side effects are common and expected; progressive weakness or bulbar symptoms are red flags.

3) When to check CK (practical rules)
- Check CK promptly if the patient reports:
  - New or progressive proximal weakness
  - Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia)
  - Severe diffuse myalgias or dark urine
- Consider urgent evaluation if CK is markedly elevated (e.g., >1000 U/L) or if there is rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory compromise, or bulbar involvement — these require prompt specialty input (neurology/rheum) and admission.
- In patients with only mild, intermittent myalgias and no functional impairment, reasonable to monitor and recheck CK if symptoms persist/worsen.

4) How polypharmacy (especially statins) clouds attribution
- Statins are a well‑described trigger for anti‑HMGCR IMNM; cessation alone often does not lead to immediate recovery, and immunosuppression is commonly required. If a patient on a statin develops myopathy on a GLP‑1 RA, both drugs could be contributors.
- Practical approach: hold the statin if severe myopathy is suspected while urgently evaluating (coordinate with cardiology/primary care regarding ischemic risk). Send anti‑HMGCR and myositis panels early. Expect that stopping a statin may not be sufficient if IMNM has developed.

5) Workup and early management pearls
- Basic labs: CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise AST/ALT), creatinine, electrolytes, CRP/ESR, TSH. Obtain myositis autoantibodies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP when IMNM is suspected.
- Imaging/diagnostics: muscle MRI can localize inflammation; EMG may help. Muscle biopsy is diagnostic for IMNM (necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition).
- Early management: stop the suspected offending drug. If severe (high CK, bulbar symptoms, rapidly progressive weakness), involve rheumatology/neurology and start immunosuppression — many IMNM patients require steroids + steroid‑sparing agents and often IVIG. This reported patient received pulse steroids + IVIG and recovered.
- Cardiac evaluation: IMNM can involve the heart; consider troponin, ECG, echocardiogram, and cardiology input for palpitations/arrhythmia/troponin elevation.

6) Reporting suspected cases — make the signal count
- Report to your national pharmacovigilance system (FDA MedWatch in the U.S.; others internationally). Also notify the manufacturer and your hospital safety/quality office.
- What to include in a report: drug name, dose, lot number if available, exact timing of exposure, timeline of symptoms, CK and other labs, imaging/biopsy results, interventions (drug stopped, immunosuppression), and outcome.
- Even if causality is uncertain, report — single case reports feed detection of rare adverse events.

7) Is this a class effect? What about rechallenge?
- We don’t know. There are isolated reports of GLP‑1 RAs associated with muscle adverse events (including a tirzepatide‑associated rhabdomyolysis report), but causality and mechanism remain uncertain.
- Rechallenge after a severe IMNM is generally not recommended. Given the severity of IMNM and the ethical concern of provoking recurrence, avoid re‑exposure unless there is an extraordinarily compelling reason and a multidisciplinary plan (rheum/immunology, informed consent, close monitoring).
- If weight‑loss/diabetes control remains a priority, consider alternative non‑GLP‑1 strategies and discuss risks/benefits with the patient.

## Quick clinical pearls
- IMNM can be seronegative — a negative myositis panel does not exclude a biopsy‑proven autoimmune necrotising myopathy.
- Progressive proximal weakness or bulbar symptoms after starting a new medication should prompt CK testing and early specialty referral.
- In suspected drug‑related myopathy: stop the drug, check CK, consider autoantibodies, and involve rheumatology/neurology early — severe IMNM often needs steroids + IVIG.
- Report suspicious adverse events to pharmacovigilance authorities even when causality is not definitive; signal detection depends on clinicians reporting.

Bottom line: this case is not definitive proof that semaglutide causes IMNM, but it is a credible signal — temporally plausible, with biopsy‑proven disease and reasonable exclusion of alternatives — that meets WHO‑UMC criteria for “probable.” Clinicians should be alert to the red flags (progressive weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia), check CK promptly when indicated, consider confounders like statins, treat severe cases aggressively, and report suspected events so we can all learn faster.
