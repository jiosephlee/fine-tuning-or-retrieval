# Beyond Rhabdo: Recognizing Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy After GLP-1 Agonists

This case is attention-grabbing because it breaks the simple narrative “GLP-1 agonists → nausea, weight loss, maybe rhabdo” and raises the possibility of a post–GLP-1 immune myopathy that behaves very differently from classic toxic rhabdomyolysis. A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and CK that climbed from normal/mild to nearly 4,000 U/L over four months after a single semaglutide dose. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, complement deposition and only rare T cells — a pattern consistent with immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). She improved after pulse steroids + IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression with avoidance of GLP‑1 therapy.

Why this matters
- GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are now ubiquitous. Most adverse effects are GI, but case reports already link them to muscle injury — classically rhabdomyolysis, and now possibly IMNM.
- Distinguishing toxic rhabdomyolysis from IMNM changes management: hydration and stopping the drug may be enough for classic rhabdo, whereas IMNM often requires early immunosuppression and sometimes IVIG to prevent morbidity (dysphagia, respiratory compromise).
- IMNM can be seronegative — lack of anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP does not exclude it. Tissue diagnosis and clinicopathologic correlation still matter.

How to think about GLP-1–associated muscle injury: rhabdo vs IMNM

Timing and clinical course
- Rhabdomyolysis (direct toxic/injury): often acute, temporal relationship to exposure, very high CK at presentation, rapid decline after stopping the offending agent and supportive care (days–weeks).
- IMNM (immune-mediated): subacute to progressive proximal weakness developing over weeks–months, may follow an exposure but continues/worsens despite stopping drug. CK may be moderately to markedly elevated and can persist or worsen over weeks.

CK kinetics and magnitude
- Rhabdo: huge spikes (often >10,000–50,000 U/L) that fall quickly with fluids and elimination of trigger.
- IMNM: CK can be high (thousands) but does not necessarily fall rapidly after cessation. A progressive rise over months after exposure should raise suspicion for IMNM.

Symptoms and exam
- Rhabdo: myalgias, dark urine, AKI from myoglobinuria may dominate; weakness can be present but often more systemic signs of muscle breakdown.
- IMNM: prominent symmetric proximal muscle weakness, difficulty rising from chair, climbing stairs, and importantly dysphagia/dysphonia if bulbar muscles involved. Constitutional features and chronic functional decline are common.

Investigations that help distinguish them
- Serial CK trends after stopping the drug — rapid fall favors rhabdo; persistent elevation or progressive rise favors IMNM.
- Inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR) may be elevated in IMNM.
- MRI of muscles — symmetric edema of proximal muscles supports inflammatory myopathy.
- Myositis autoantibody panel — anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are helpful if positive, but up to ~1/3 of IMNM are seronegative.
- Muscle biopsy — gold standard when the diagnosis is uncertain or in severe cases. IMNM shows necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrate, complement (membrane attack complex) deposition, and relatively few lymphocytes.

Implications for management
- If the picture is consistent with rhabdomyolysis: stop offending drug, aggressive IV fluids, monitor/avoid nephrotoxins, supportive care.
- If IMNM is suspected (progressive weakness, persistent CK elevation despite stopping drug, bulbar involvement, MRI/malignancy screening inconclusive): obtain neurology/rheumatology input early, consider muscle biopsy, and start immunosuppression promptly in severe cases (pulse steroids, consider IVIG, steroid-sparing agents as per specialist advice). Delay of immunotherapy can lead to worse outcomes.
- Avoid re-challenge with the suspected GLP-1 agent; rechallenge would be risky and probably unethical if IMNM occurred.

Practical pearls for the emergency clinician and internist
- Don’t anchor on “rhabdo” just because a patient on semaglutide has high CK. Look at timing, symptom progression and CK trajectory.
- Ask explicitly about functional decline (getting out of chair, swallowing) and screen for bulbar/respiratory weakness.
- Check serial CKs after stopping the drug if initial concern is rhabdo; a failure to fall or a rising trend over days–weeks is a red flag.
- Early MRI ± referral for muscle biopsy is appropriate when the clinical picture is progressive or severe.
- Autoantibody negativity does not rule out IMNM — biopsy and clinical course win out.
- If IMNM is likely, involve rheumatology/neurology early. Severe cases benefit from pulse steroids and IVIG.

Limitations and context
- The case report shows a temporal association and a plausible mechanism but cannot prove causation. IMNM may be idiopathic or triggered by other factors in some cases.
- With widespread GLP‑1 use, vigilance and pharmacovigilance reporting are important — we need more case series and mechanistic studies.

Take-home messages
- Progressive proximal weakness and persistent/rising CK after GLP‑1 exposure should prompt consideration of IMNM, not just rhabdomyolysis.
- Rapid CK normalization after stopping the drug supports toxic rhabdo; persistent/progressive CK elevation plus dysphagia or progressive weakness suggests IMNM.
- Muscle MRI and muscle biopsy are often necessary; autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP) can help but are not required for diagnosis.
- Early specialist involvement and early immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) can be limb- and life‑saving in severe IMNM.
- Avoid re-exposure to the implicated GLP‑1 agonist and report suspected cases to pharmacovigilance systems.

This case is a reminder: as our therapeutics evolve, so do the adverse-event phenotypes. Keep your differential broad — not every high CK on semaglutide is classic rhabdo.



# The Seronegative Trap: Negative Myositis Panels Don’t Rule Out IMNM

What makes this case sharp is how it lures you into a false sense of security. A woman in her 60s develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12-fold rise in CK over months after a single dose of semaglutide. Her extended myositis serology — including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP — is negative, but muscle biopsy shows a necrotising inflammatory myopathy with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition. She improves rapidly after pulse steroids and IVIG and remains well off immunosuppression. This is IMNM — seronegative, steroid/IVIG-responsive, and potentially drug-associated.

Why this matters
- Up to one-third of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) patients are seronegative. A negative commercial myositis panel does not exclude a treatable, potentially severe autoimmune myopathy.
- Waiting for a “positive antibody” can meaningfully delay therapy in patients with progressive weakness, bulbar/respiratory involvement, or markedly elevated CK.
- Modern myositis testing is useful, but histology + clinical trajectory remain the diagnostic backbone for many patients.

Practical approach when SRP/HMGCR and myositis panels are negative

1) Trust the clinical picture first
If a patient has subacute–acute progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, very high CK (often >1000 U/L), and MRI evidence of proximal muscle edema — treat the patient, not the panel. IMNM commonly presents with prominent weakness and high CK; progression over weeks–months despite stopping a potential offending drug argues for autoimmune process rather than transient toxic rhabdomyolysis.

2) Use MRI to guide biopsy
MRI of the thighs (STIR or fluid-sensitive sequences) is extremely helpful. Target areas of active edema for biopsy — the chance of diagnostic yield increases when sampling MRI-hot muscles rather than atrophic, fatty-replaced muscles. In the presented case MRI showed symmetric proximal quadriceps edema, and biopsy from vastus lateralis confirmed the diagnosis.

3) Know the characteristic histology
IMNM classically shows:
- Scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibers
- Prominent macrophage infiltration and myophagocytosis (CD68+)
- Relative paucity of dense lymphocytic endomysial infiltrates (unlike polymyositis)
- Membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition on sarcolemma of necrotic and non-necrotic fibres
- Variable perivascular inflammation; CD3+ T cells may be rare

These features distinguish IMNM from simple toxic rhabdomyolysis (which lacks an inflammatory, complement-mediated picture) and from other inflammatory myopathies.

4) When to start immunosuppression despite negative serologies
Consider early immunotherapy (high-dose corticosteroids ± IVIG) when patients have:
- Rapidly progressive or severe proximal weakness impairing mobility or ADLs
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) or respiratory compromise
- Very high CK with biopsy or MRI supportive of inflammatory myopathy
- Failure to improve after withdrawal of a suspected offending agent and supportive histology
In the case above, pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone and one course of IVIG produced marked clinical and biochemical improvement; she was able to taper off corticosteroids and remain well.

5) Rule in/out drug association, but don’t be paralyzed by uncertainty
Some drugs (statins, rarely GLP-1 receptor agonists have been reported) can trigger or unmask IMNM. Distinguish drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis (acute CK spike that falls after withdrawal) from drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy (persistent/worsening weakness and CK despite stopping the drug). A temporal link supports causality but is not definitive. Rechallenge should usually be avoided.

6) Practical investigations to perform early
- CK, aldolase (if available), AST/ALT, renal function
- TSH, basic autoimmune screen (ANA), myositis panel (including HMGCR, SRP)
- MRI of muscles (STIR sequences)
- EMG if diagnosis unclear or to guide biopsy
- Targeted muscle biopsy (MRI-guided if possible)
- Baseline pulmonary and cardiac evaluation (IMNM can involve myocardium and predispose to arrhythmia)

Clinical pearls
- One-third of IMNM patients will be seronegative — negative antibodies do not rule out disease.
- MRI-targeted biopsy markedly increases diagnostic yield; sample an MRI-hot muscle.
- Histology in IMNM is necrosis + macrophages + complement deposition with scant lymphocytes.
- Severe features (bulbar/respiratory involvement, inability to walk, rapidly rising CK) are indications to start immunotherapy even if serologies are pending or negative.
- Distinguish drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (acute, reversible with withdrawal) from drug-triggered IMNM (progressive, may need immunosuppression).
- IVIG often has a central role in IMNM, particularly when HMGCR or SRP antibodies are present or when severe weakness/bulbar symptoms exist; it can be effective in seronegative patients too.

Take-home messages
- Negative myositis panels do not exclude IMNM; up to ~33% are seronegative.
- Use MRI to pick the biopsy site — target edema, not fatty-replaced muscle.
- Look for necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex on biopsy.
- Start steroids ± IVIG for rapidly progressive weakness, bulbar/respiratory involvement, or supportive biopsy even if serologies are negative.
- Consider drug triggers but avoid reflexively attributing progressive disease to transient toxicity; immunotherapy can be life- and function-saving.

In short: don’t fall into the seronegative trap. When the clinical story, CK, MRI and biopsy line up, act — antibodies are helpful, but they’re not the whole diagnosis.



# One Dose, Big Problem: Pharmacovigilance Lessons from Semaglutide-Associated IMNM

This is a striking case: a woman in her 60s received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, had an immediate severe GI and metabolic illness, and over the next 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia and a 12-fold rise in CK culminating in biopsy-proven immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She responded to high‑dose steroids and IVIG, was weaned off immunosuppression, and remained well after avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

Why this case matters
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are everywhere now — increasingly used for diabetes and obesity. Most adverse effects we expect are GI, but muscle effects (mass loss, rare rhabdomyolysis) have been reported. This report raises the possibility of a drug-associated autoimmune myopathy after a single exposure.
- IMNM is not the same as simple drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. It can be progressive, autoimmune, and may require immunotherapy. Recognising the difference is critical because treatment (and prognosis) diverge.
- Pharmacovigilance needs to catch these rare serious events early so clinicians and regulators can identify true signals from noise.

Putting causality in context — applying WHO‑UMC
The authors apply WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality categories and conclude "probable" — and that’s reasonable here:
- Reasonable time relationship: acute systemic reaction immediately after dose, followed by progressive myopathy over weeks to months.
- Alternative explanations unlikely: no statin exposure, myositis panel negative (though ~1/3 of IMNM are seronegative), muscle biopsy consistent with IMNM.
- Response on withdrawal plus immunotherapy: clinical recovery after stopping semaglutide and receiving steroids/IVIG.
- Rechallenge would be unethical and therefore absent.

Remember WHO‑UMC is a structured clinical judgement tool — it helps sort probable from possible and unlikely, but it does not prove mechanism or absolute causation. Single-case reports can generate signals but cannot establish definitive causality.

Why rechallenge is a bad idea here
- Re-exposure to a culprit that plausibly triggered an autoimmune myopathy risks provoking a severe, potentially life‑threatening relapse (bulbar weakness, respiratory failure, myocarditis).
- In IMNM, drug withdrawal often isn’t enough; re-exposure could lock in a chronic autoimmune process.
- Ethically unacceptable when safer alternatives exist and the initial event was serious.

What to document and report if you suspect a drug-associated myopathy
High-quality documentation makes pharmacovigilance usable. Capture:
- Precise timeline: date/time of drug administration, onset of first symptoms, progression of weakness, CK trajectory.
- Acute reactions around the index exposure (nausea, vomiting, hypovolemia, AKI) — these may be clues or confounders.
- Medication list: prescription drugs, OTC agents, supplements (red yeast rice, mushrooms), recent statin use or prior exposures.
- Objective muscle metrics: serial CK values with dates, EMG if done, MRI muscle findings, and full muscle biopsy report with photos if possible.
- Autoantibody panels and other serologies, thyroid function, liver tests (note if pre‑existing hepatic steatosis), and cardiac markers/imaging.
- Treatments given (steroid dosing, IVIG dose/timing, other immunosuppressants) and clinical response.
- Adverse event severity (hospitalisation, ICU stay, respiratory or feeding support), outcomes at defined intervals.
- Report to national pharmacovigilance systems (FDA MedWatch, EMA, MHRA, your country’s program) and consider notifying the drug manufacturer.

Building the differential when progressive proximal weakness follows even a single GLP‑1 dose
Think through causes systematically — early and late time windows matter:

Immediate (hours–days)
- Acute intolerance / GI dehydration → hypovolemia → AKI, possible modest CK rise from immobility or metabolic stress.
- Medication interaction or unmasked arrhythmia (this patient had new AF).

Subacute to chronic (weeks–months)
- Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis: typically acute massive CK rise that improves after drug cessation and hydration (less likely here because of delayed progressive course).
- Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM): progressive proximal weakness, high CK, necrotic fibres on biopsy, may be seropositive (HMGCR, SRP) or seronegative — can be drug‑triggered.
- Polymyositis/dermatomyositis or other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies — check for rash, interstitial lung disease, extramuscular features; biopsy patterns differ.
- Critical illness myopathy (if prolonged ICU stay) — usually related to prolonged ventilation, neuromuscular blockers, or steroids.
- Metabolic myopathies / endocrine causes — thyroid disease, electrolyte derangements.
- Neurodegenerative or neuromuscular junction disorders — myasthenia gravis can present with bulbar symptoms, but CK is typically normal.
- Paraneoplastic myopathy — consider age-appropriate malignancy workup if suspicious.

How to approach the patient now
- If you see progressive proximal weakness with elevated CK after any new drug exposure (including GLP‑1 agonists): get CK, basic labs (BMP, LFTs, TSH), urinalysis, ECG/monitoring, and consider early rheumatology/neurology consultation.
- MRI of muscles and EMG can help target biopsy; if biopsy is feasible, it may clinch IMNM.
- Early empiric high‑dose corticosteroids ± IVIG are reasonable in severe/progressive presentations while awaiting confirmatory testing, after careful risk/benefit assessment.
- Avoid rechallenge with the suspected agent.

Clinical pearls
- One dose can be enough to trigger a severe idiosyncratic reaction; timing and pattern of evolution matter more than number of doses.
- Distinguish rhabdomyolysis (rapid CK spike, often resolves with withdrawal/hydration) from IMNM (progressive weakness, necrosis on biopsy, may need immunosuppression).
- Up to a third of IMNM cases are seronegative; negative myositis panels do not exclude the diagnosis.
- Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard for IMNM and can show necrotic/regenerating fibres with macrophage predominant infiltrate and membrane attack complex deposition.
- Report thoroughly. A single well-documented case report can generate a safety signal when a drug is widely used.

Take-home messages
- Consider IMNM in patients with progressive proximal weakness and rising CK after GLP‑1 agonist exposure — even after a single dose.
- Use WHO‑UMC criteria to structure causality assessment, but recognise the limits of single-case inference.
- Rechallenge is generally unsafe in suspected immune-mediated drug reactions.
- Document timelines, labs, imaging, biopsy results and treatments; report to your pharmacovigilance authority.
- Early specialty involvement and prompt immunotherapy can lead to good recovery in drug-associated IMNM.

Bottom line: with drugs that are becoming ubiquitous, our job includes vigilance for the rare but serious. When a new exposure precedes progressive myopathy, think beyond GI side effects — collect the data, treat the patient, and report the event.



## AST Up, CK Up? Sorting Muscle vs Liver Injury in NAFLD Patients

You see it all the time in clinic: an overweight patient with known NAFLD and mildly elevated transaminases. Then they present months later with weakness and a big jump in AST — now what is making those enzymes rise? The recent case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that followed a single dose of semaglutide is a useful reminder that not all transaminase elevations in patients with fatty liver are hepatic. Sometimes the liver is innocent; the source is skeletal muscle. Knowing how to separate the two quickly matters: the downstream workup, need for immunosuppression, and biopsy decisions are very different.

Why this case is instructive
- The patient had chronic NAFLD with baseline AST/ALT mild elevation, then after an acute adverse reaction to semaglutide developed progressive proximal weakness and a delayed, large rise in CK and AST. Muscle biopsy confirmed IMNM and she improved with steroids/IVIG and stopping the GLP‑1 receptor agonist. This sequence shows two pitfalls: anchoring on the liver in patients with NAFLD, and assuming all drug-related enzyme elevations are simple toxicity/rhabdo rather than immune-mediated myopathy.

How to think about transaminase elevation when both liver disease and muscle injury are possible
AST and ALT are not organ-specific. AST is present in liver, heart, kidney and skeletal muscle; ALT is more liver-predominant but still in muscle. Patterns and adjunct tests help you allocate probability rapidly.

1) Start with the clinical picture
- Muscle symptoms (proximal weakness, difficulty rising, myalgias, dysphagia, dysphonia) or signs (reduced strength, muscle wasting) strongly point to muscle as the source.
- Hepatic clues: jaundice, pruritus, hepatomegaly, stigmata of chronic liver disease, cholestatic labs (ALP, bilirubin), portal hypertension signs, encephalopathy.
- Timing: acute severe systemic illness with hypovolaemia can cause mild CK rise and transient AST bump; progressive weakness over weeks–months with rising CK suggests myopathy.

2) Order the right quick labs
- CK: the single most useful discriminator. Marked CK elevation (hundreds to tens of thousands) argues muscle. In IMNM, CK is often high but can be variable; rhabdomyolysis gives very high CK that falls rapidly after stopping offending drug and hydration.
- Aldolase: another muscle enzyme; useful if CK is borderline or in setting of confounders (e.g., advanced cirrhosis where CK may be low).
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase: GGT correlates with hepatocellular / cholestatic liver processes. If AST/ALT are up but GGT is normal, think muscle.
- Bilirubin and INR: abnormalities point to hepatic dysfunction, not muscle.
- Troponin/NT‑proBNP: if cardiac involvement suspected (myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy), check cardiac markers and imaging.
- Autoantibody panels and inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP) if inflammatory myopathy suspected.

3) Patterns that help
- AST>>ALT with normal GGT and very high CK — muscle more likely.
- Modest AST/ALT elevation (≤2× ULN) with elevated GGT/ALP or hyperbilirubinaemia — liver more likely.
- Rapid normalization of CK/AST after stopping a drug and hydration — classic for direct toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Progressive weakness with persistent CK elevation despite stopping offending agent, and response only to immunosuppression — think immune-mediated myopathy (IMNM, anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, or seronegative variants).

When imaging and biopsy come into play
- MRI of muscle: excellent noninvasive tool to localize inflammation/edema and guide biopsy. Useful when diagnosis is unclear or to target biopsy site.
- EMG can support myopathic process but is less specific.
- Liver ultrasound/CT/MRI: indicated when hepatic disease progression, biliary obstruction, or mass suspected. It won’t tell you about muscle.
- Muscle biopsy: indicated when you suspect inflammatory myopathy (progressive weakness, high CK, dysphagia, or atypical recovery), when autoantibodies are negative, or to distinguish necrotising myopathy from other myopathies. Histology can show necrosis, macrophage-predominant inflammation, membrane attack complex deposition — the hallmarks seen in IMNM.
- Liver biopsy: reserved for staging/prognostication of NAFLD when noninvasive tests are equivocal and result will change management, or when alternative liver pathology suspected. Don’t reflex to liver biopsy just because AST/ALT are up in a patient with muscle symptoms.

Drug culprits to keep in mind
- Statins: classically associated with HMGCR antibody-associated IMNM; beware persistent weakness/CK after stopping statin.
- Newer metabolic agents: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide) have reports of muscle mass loss and rare rhabdomyolysis; consider them as possible triggers for immune or toxic myopathies.
- Supplements: red yeast rice, certain herbal products can mimic statins or cause toxicity.
- Always review meds and timing; a close temporal relationship supports drug causation but does not prove it.

Practical approach at the bedside or clinic (quick algorithm)
- Patient with NAFLD and new AST/ALT rise:
  - Ask about muscle symptoms and examine proximal strength.
  - Order CK (+/− aldolase), GGT, bilirubin, INR.
  - If CK markedly elevated or weakness present → pursue muscle workup (MRI±EMG, autoimmune myositis panel, consider muscle biopsy).
  - If CK normal/low and cholestatic markers or bilirubin/INR abnormal → pursue liver-directed workup (imaging, consider noninvasive fibrosis assessment, hepatology referral).
  - If both liver and muscle signals present → get both sets of studies and coordinate multidisciplinary care.

When to biopsy muscle vs liver
- Muscle biopsy: progressive proximal weakness, CK elevation persistent or high, dysphagia/dysphonia, systemic features suggesting inflammatory myopathy, or when immunotherapy is being considered and a definitive diagnosis is needed.
- Liver biopsy: when staging NAFLD is required for management decisions, when alternative liver pathology is suspected, or when transaminase abnormalities persist without muscle evidence and noninvasive testing is inconclusive.

Clinical pearls
- CK is your best single test to point toward muscle as the source of transaminases — don’t skip it in any patient with AST/ALT elevation plus weakness.
- Normal GGT with high AST/ALT makes muscle injury much more likely.
- A temporal rise in CK that is progressive despite stopping an offending drug suggests an immune-mediated process rather than simple toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- IMNM can be seronegative; a negative myositis panel does not rule out immune-mediated disease — biopsy may be required.
- In patients on GLP‑1 RAs presenting with new weakness and rising CK, keep both toxic rhabdomyolysis and immune myopathy on your differential.
- Avoid anchoring: pre-existing NAFLD is common and can mislead; assess the whole clinical picture.

Take-home messages
- Always check CK (and consider aldolase) when AST/ALT are elevated and there are any muscle symptoms or weakness.
- Use GGT, bilirubin and INR to help localize the problem — abnormal cholestatic markers or bilirubin point to liver injury.
- Rapid CK fall after drug stop and hydration = likely toxic rhabdo; progressive weakness with persistent CK elevation despite stopping the drug = consider immune-mediated myopathy and muscle biopsy.
- Muscle MRI and biopsy are diagnostic tools for inflammatory myopathies; liver biopsy is for staging or when liver disease is the primary concern.
- Keep medications (including GLP‑1 RAs and supplements) on your differential as potential triggers for myopathy; coordinate early with neurology/rheumatology/hepatology when findings overlap.

In short: in the NAFLD patient with AST up, don’t assume the liver alone. Look for weakness, check CK and GGT, and pursue muscle-directed diagnostics when the clinical picture points that way. The difference between hepatic ALT/AST and myopathic AST/ALT can be the difference between monitoring conservatively and starting aggressive immunosuppression.



# IMNM on Histology: What to Ask Your Pathologist and How to Pick the Right Muscle

This case — a woman who developed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide and had biopsy‑proven necrosis, macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane‑attack‑complex (MAC) deposition — is a great reminder that the diagnosis of IMNM is as much about smart sampling and directed testing as it is about clinical suspicion. IMNM often looks subtle on routine testing (seronegative disease is common), so getting the right muscle, the right MRI, and the right stains can make or break the diagnosis.

Below I’ll walk through practical, FOAM-friendly advice on biopsy logistics: how to choose the biopsy site (MRI STIR is your friend), what you should request on the pathology side, what you should expect to see, and a short checklist to maximise diagnostic yield.

## Why biopsy strategy matters
IMNM is defined histologically by prominent myofibre necrosis and regeneration with relatively scant lymphocytic infiltrate and abundant macrophage involvement. If you biopsy the wrong muscle — either end‑stage muscle with fatty replacement or a site without active inflammation — you can miss active necrosis and get a nondiagnostic result. Likewise, routine H&E alone may not highlight the complement‑mediated injury or macrophage phenotype that supports IMNM. In drug‑associated cases (as in the semaglutide patient), serology may be negative, so the biopsy may be the decisive data point.

## Use MRI STIR to pick the right site
- Get a recent MRI of the limbs with STIR or fat‑suppressed T2 sequences. STIR shows active muscle edema and guides you to focal areas of ongoing necrosis/regeneration.
- Target a muscle that shows STIR hyperintensity but still has preserved bulk (avoid muscles with marked fatty atrophy/replacement — these are chronic and less likely to show active necrosis).
- Choose a proximal muscle when clinical weakness is proximal (vastus lateralis, rectus femoris, deltoid) — the case’s vastus lateralis biopsy fits the classic pattern.
- Coordinate MRI localization with the biopsy team: mark skin over the STIR bright area and document depth/side.

Clinical pearl: STIR hyperintensity correlates with active inflammatory/necrotic change. Multiple small biopsies from the same STIR‑positive region increase yield.

## What to ask the pathologist — a practical order set
When you send a muscle biopsy for suspected IMNM, include clinical context (proximal weakness, CK trend, recent drug exposures such as GLP‑1 RA, serologies). Explicit requests improve diagnostic yield:

- Routine H&E and Gomori trichrome.
- Acid phosphatase (to highlight myophagocytosis).
- Immunohistochemistry:
  - CD68 (macrophage marker) — expect numerous CD68+ cells with myophagocytosis.
  - CD3/CD8 and CD4 — often only rare T cells in IMNM (helps differentiate polymyositis/dermatomyositis).
  - MHC class I (upregulation on myofibres may be present).
  - C5b‑9 or MAC immunostain — deposition on necrotic (and sometimes non‑necrotic) fibres supports complement‑mediated injury.
- Frozen tissue for enzyme histochemistry (NADH, SDH) if requested.
- Electron microscopy if ultrastructural detail is needed (rarely diagnostic but useful for research/atypical cases).
- Consider special stains or PCR if infection/myositis mimics are a concern.
- Snap‑freeze and bank tissue for future serologic or molecular testing (particularly useful when serology is negative).
- Request interpretation that comments on:
  - Pattern of necrosis/regeneration
  - Degree and type of inflammatory infiltrate (macrophage‑predominant vs lymphocytic)
  - Presence/absence of perifascicular atrophy or vasculitis
  - MAC deposition
  - Any features suggestive of chronic denervation or chronic myopathy

Clinical pearl: explicitly ask for CD68 and MAC (C5b‑9). These are the stains that often tip the diagnosis toward IMNM when lymphocytes are scarce.

## What you should expect to see in IMNM
On the correctly targeted, well‑processed biopsy you’ll typically see:
- Scattered to focal necrotic myofibres with prominent myofibre regeneration.
- Abundant macrophages (CD68+) engaged in myophagocytosis of necrotic fibres.
- Sparse endomysial lymphocytic infiltrate (rare CD3+ cells); lack of dense lymphoid aggregates distinguishes IMNM from classic polymyositis/dermatomyositis.
- Deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of necrotic (and sometimes viable) fibres — suggestive of complement‑mediated injury.
- Variable upregulation of MHC‑I on myofibre membranes.
- Minimal perifascicular atrophy (this argues against dermatomyositis) and no vasculitis.
- Regenerating fibres that are basophilic with central nuclei on H&E.

Remember: serology (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) can be negative in a third of IMNM cases. Pathology + clinical context may be the clincher.

## Pitfalls and practical tips
- Don’t biopsy chronically replaced fatty muscle or a completely atrophic muscle — low yield.
- Coordinate with radiology and pathology: pick the STIR‑bright, non‑atrophic target and transport tissue properly (send both fresh frozen and formalin fixed samples).
- If the clinical picture is strong but the first biopsy is nondiagnostic, reimage and consider repeat biopsy from a different STIR‑positive site.
- Communicate medications and timeline — drug‑associated IMNM can be seronegative and improve after withdrawal plus brief immunosuppression (as in the semaglutide case).
- If myocarditis is suspected, coordinate cardiac MRI/biopsy — IMNM can have cardiac involvement.

## Take‑home messages
- Use MRI STIR to choose a biopsy site: target STIR‑hyperintense, non‑atrophic muscle (proximal pattern if clinical weakness is proximal).
- Tell pathology you suspect IMNM and explicitly request CD68 and MAC (C5b‑9) stains, MHC‑I, and frozen tissue banking.
- Expect necrosis/regeneration with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and limited lymphocytes — MAC deposition supports immune/complement‑mediated injury.
- Seronegative IMNM is common; good biopsy technique and directed stains often make the diagnosis when serology is unrevealing.
- If the first biopsy is nondiagnostic but clinical suspicion persists, reimage and rebiopsy.

The semaglutide‑associated IMNM case underscores that in an era of novel drug exposures, the biopsy remains a critical diagnostic step — but only if you pick the right muscle and ask for the right stains.



# Hit Early, Hit Hard: Steroids and IVIG for Severe or Bulbar IMNM

What grabs your attention in the case above is how a single dose of semaglutide was followed—over weeks to months—by a rapidly progressive, biopsy-proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with prominent bulbar features and CK in the thousands, and how the patient recovered after a short, aggressive immunomodulatory course (pulse methylprednisolone then oral prednisone + a single course of IVIG). Whether semaglutide was the trigger or an unlucky temporal association, the take-home clinical lesson is clear: when IMNM presents with severe weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, respiratory compromise or very high CK, early aggressive therapy — “hit early, hit hard” — can be limb- and life‑saving and may even allow a shorter immunosuppressive course when the offending agent is removed.

Below is a pragmatic, bedside-focused approach to initiating pulse steroids plus IVIG for severe or bulbar IMNM, using the case as an anchoring example.

## When to consider pulse steroids + IVIG up front
Consider early combined therapy when IMNM presents with any of the following:
- Rapidly progressive proximal weakness over days–weeks or new inability to sit/stand
- Bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, dysphonia) or aspiration risk
- Respiratory compromise or falling vital capacity
- CK markedly elevated (classically >1000 U/L and especially several‑thousand)
- Severe functional decline or evidence of ongoing muscle necrosis on imaging/biopsy
- Clinical suspicion for drug‑triggered IMNM where withdrawal alone may be insufficient

The case illustrates classic indications: progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement and CK up to ~4000 U/L prompting pulse steroids and IVIG.

## Practical regimen: dosing and timing
There’s no one proven protocol for IMNM, but reasonable, widely used aggressive regimens are:

- Pulse steroid induction:
  - Methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily for 3–5 days (many centers use 5 days for severe disease). This was the regimen used in the case.
- Transition to oral steroids:
  - Prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (or equivalent), started after pulse course.
  - Adjust for age/comorbidity; consider 0.75 mg/kg in frail elderly.
- IVIG:
  - 2 g/kg total dose, delivered as 0.4 g/kg/day over 5 days or 1 g/kg/day for 2 consecutive days (or split as clinically indicated).
  - Give IVIG early—ideally concurrent with or immediately after pulse steroids in severe/bulbar disease.
- Duration:
  - Evaluate response clinically and by CK. If rapid, marked improvement occurs (as in the case), you may be able to wean steroids faster than traditional prolonged courses.
  - Many idiopathic IMNM patients need prolonged immunosuppression; however, suspected drug‑triggered IMNM that responds promptly to withdrawal and initial immunotherapy may permit a shorter steroid course.

## Monitoring during and after treatment
- Strength and function:
  - Frequent bedside strength checks (daily in hospital), timed get‑up tests, and swallow assessments for bulbar patients.
- CK:
  - Baseline, then at least twice weekly inpatient until trending down; outpatient frequency depends on trajectory (e.g., weekly then spacing).
  - Expect CK fall over days–weeks; discordance between CK and strength can occur.
- Respiratory:
  - Baseline and serial forced vital capacity (FVC) if bulbar/respiratory involvement; consider ICU monitoring if FVC declines or bulbar dysfunction is severe.
- Cardiac:
  - Baseline ECG and troponin; consider echo and cardiac MRI if troponin elevated or suspicion for myocarditis (this case had cardiac MRI changes that normalized).
- Labs for therapy safety:
  - CMP (renal function, LFTs), CBC, glucose, electrolytes.
  - With IVIG: watch creatinine, urine output, and signs of volume overload or thrombosis.
- Infection surveillance:
  - Fever workup, cultures as indicated—note that aggressive immunosuppression increases infection risk (this patient had pseudomonal urosepsis during recovery).

## Infection mitigation and general supportive care
- PJP prophylaxis (trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole) is indicated if prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg/day prednisone equivalent for >4 weeks) or combined immunosuppression is anticipated.
- Vaccinations:
  - Update vaccines pre‑immunosuppression when possible; avoid live vaccines while immunosuppressed.
- Glycemic control:
  - Monitor capillary glucose closely; steroids commonly cause hyperglycaemia. Treat promptly.
- Thrombosis prophylaxis:
  - Consider pharmacologic DVT prophylaxis while immobile, and remember IVIG can increase thrombotic risk.
- Renal protection with IVIG:
  - Avoid sucrose‑stabilized IVIG in CK/renal impairment; ensure adequate hydration and use slower infusion rates in at‑risk patients.
- Swallow care:
  - Early speech‑language pathology assessment, consider nasogastric feeding if severe dysphagia/aspiration risk.
- Physical therapy and progressive mobilization once safe.

## Tapering steroids — how fast?
Tapering should be individualized:
- If dramatic improvement clinically and CK normalizes quickly (days–weeks), a more rapid taper over 6–8 weeks may be feasible with close follow‑up. The case patient was weaned off prednisone over ~2 months.
- If improvement is slower, relapse occurs, or autoantibody‑positive IMNM is suspected, taper more slowly and consider maintenance immunosuppression.
- Watch for relapse with decreasing steroid dose; rising CK and recurrent weakness indicate need for escalation.

## When to add steroid‑sparing or escalation agents
Add or switch to additional immunotherapy if:
- Incomplete response after initial steroids + IVIG (persistent severe weakness or rising CK)
- Relapse during steroid taper
- Autoantibody‑positive IMNM (eg, anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) often needs combination therapy
Options to consider:
- Rituximab — increasingly used for refractory IMNM, particularly antibody‑mediated disease
- Methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine — steroid‑sparing agents for maintenance
- Cyclophosphamide — for life‑threatening or organ‑threatening disease (rare)
Choice depends on severity, comorbidities, infection risk, and whether the IMNM appears drug‑triggered (drug‑withdrawal + short aggressive course may obviate prolonged therapy).

## Other diagnostic and safety considerations
- Muscle biopsy and myositis panel remain valuable: biopsy confirms necrotising myofibres and macrophage‑predominant inflammation (as in the case). Note 20–30% of IMNM are seronegative.
- Screen for malignancy appropriately in new‑onset inflammatory myopathy per age and risk factors.
- If a medication trigger is suspected (statins, GLP‑1RA, others), stop the drug and record reaction. Rechallenge is generally avoided.
- Document and counsel patient about future exposure risk to suspected triggers (eg, avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists in this case).

## When to involve ICU or escalate care
- Respiratory compromise (declining FVC, hypoventilation) or severe bulbar dysfunction with aspiration risk → early ICU involvement.
- Rapid deterioration despite therapy or need for airway protection → intubation and ventilatory support as needed.

## Key clinical takeaways
- IMNM can present rapidly and severely; bulbar involvement and very elevated CK justify early aggressive therapy.
- Combined pulse methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily x3–5 days) plus IVIG (2 g/kg total) is a pragmatic first‑line strategy for severe/bulbar IMNM.
- Start oral prednisone (~1 mg/kg) after pulses and tailor taper to clinical and CK response; some drug‑triggered cases may permit a shorter course.
- Monitor strength, CK, respiratory function, cardiac involvement and complications of immunosuppression closely.
- Mitigate infection risk (PJP prophylaxis if prolonged therapy), monitor glucose and renal function, and provide supportive care (swallowing, thrombosis prophylaxis, PT).
- If inadequate response or relapse, escalate with steroid‑sparing or B‑cell targeted agents (rituximab, methotrexate, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide as indicated).

This case is a useful reminder: when IMNM presents with severe weakness or bulbar signs, don’t sit and wait. Early combination therapy can change outcomes.



# When Myopathy Affects the Heart: Troponin Bumps and Cardiac MRI in Inflammatory Myopathies

This case — a woman in her 60s who developed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after semaglutide and who had modest hs‑troponin and marked pro‑BNP elevations with transient regional myocardial dysfunction on cardiac MRI — is a useful prism to review how we think about the heart in inflammatory myopathies. Troponin bumps in myositis are common, BNP can be disproportionately high, and the line between true myocarditis, stress (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy, and non‑cardiac causes is not always obvious. Here’s a practical, FOAM‑style approach to evaluation and monitoring.

## Why this is interesting
- Inflammatory myopathies frequently involve the heart, but manifestations are protean: subclinical myocardial inflammation, conduction disease, arrhythmias, and frank myocarditis can all occur.
- Lab tests used routinely in EDs and wards — hs‑troponin and BNP — have limited specificity in myositis because skeletal muscle disease can confound troponin T, and volume/renal/critical illness can raise BNP.
- Cardiac MRI (CMR) has become the best non‑invasive tool to adjudicate myocarditis vs other causes, but timing and interpretation matter.

## Interpreting modest hs‑troponin and BNP elevations in myositis
- Small troponin elevations are common in active myositis/IMNM. They may reflect:
  - True myocardial injury (myocarditis, ischemia)
  - Cross‑reactivity or re‑expression of troponin T isoforms in diseased skeletal muscle (more relevant to troponin T)
  - Renal dysfunction, sepsis, or supply–demand mismatch
- BNP/pro‑BNP can be high out of proportion to LVEF when there is volume overload, renal impairment, or myocardial strain from systemic illness; very high values (eg, >1,000 pg/mL) raise concern for significant cardiac dysfunction but are not diagnostic for myocarditis.

Clinical context is everything: exercise the same judgement you would for any troponin/BNP result — interpret in light of symptoms (chest pain, dyspnoea), ECG changes, haemodynamics, and exam.

## Troponin I versus troponin T — which to order?
- Troponin I (cTnI)
  - Higher cardiac specificity in the setting of skeletal muscle disease.
  - Less likely to be falsely elevated from skeletal muscle regeneration or chronic myopathies.
- Troponin T (cTnT)
  - Can be falsely positive or elevated by skeletal muscle disease because some assays detect re‑expressed skeletal muscle isoforms.
  - Still useful — large rises or dynamic changes still suggest cardiac injury.
- Practical approach:
  - If myopathy is present, preferentially order troponin I to increase specificity for cardiac injury.
  - If only troponin T is available and it’s mildly elevated without clear cardiac features, treat with caution and consider getting troponin I or imaging for clarification.

## When to obtain echocardiography and when to get CMR
- Echocardiography (echo)
  - Indications: symptoms (dyspnoea, chest pain), abnormal vitals, significant troponin rise, arrhythmia, or elevated BNP.
  - What it gives: LVEF, wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion, pulmonary pressures — quick and bedside‑friendly.
  - Use echo first for triage. A normal echo does not exclude myocarditis.
- Cardiac MRI (CMR)
  - Indications: persistent troponin elevation or ECG changes with unclear diagnosis, unexplained LV dysfunction on echo, suspected myocarditis, or when making decisions about immunosuppression.
  - Strengths: tissue characterization (edema, hyperemia, necrosis/fibrosis) with Lake Louise criteria; great for distinguishing myocarditis from infarction and for mapping extent of involvement.
  - Timing: optimal within days–weeks of symptom onset. Very early scans can miss late gadolinium enhancement; delayed scans may show fibrosis but less edema.
  - Caveat: CMR findings can be non‑specific without clinical correlation. Small regional edema without LGE can be equivocal.

In the case above, echo showed preserved LVEF with elevated RVSP; CMR showed mild regional hypokinesis and myocardial edema without LGE — compatible with early myocarditis or focal stress injury. That pattern warrants close follow‑up.

## Distinguishing myocarditis from stress (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy
- Myocarditis
  - Presentation: viral prodrome or autoimmune features; chest pain, arrhythmia, HF symptoms.
  - Labs: troponin elevated (variable), inflammatory markers may be raised.
  - Imaging: CMR shows myocardial edema and LGE in non‑ischemic patterns (mid‑wall/subepicardial).
- Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy
  - Typical trigger: intense emotional/physical stress (though not always identified).
  - Pattern: regional wall motion abnormality not confined to a single coronary territory; apical ballooning classic but variants exist.
  - Troponins: modestly elevated.
  - CMR: edema may be present but LGE is typically absent; coronary angiography (if done) shows no culprit lesion.
- Practical clues
  - Temporal relation: acute stressor plus rapid normalization of function suggests takotsubo.
  - Distribution: takotsubo has a typical wall motion pattern; myocarditis more patchy.
  - Biomarkers: both can have modest troponin rises; very high troponin favors myocarditis or infarction.
  - Role of biopsy: endomyocardial biopsy is definitive for myocarditis but reserved for fulminant, unexplained cases or where histology will change management.

In our case, a possible severe systemic inflammatory storm (IMNM) with regional edema and no LGE fits early myocarditis or focal stress cardiomyopathy; recovery after immunosuppression and time supports an inflammatory cause, but distinction can remain uncertain.

## Practical monitoring for arrhythmias and conduction disease
- Why worry: inflammatory myopathies can involve the conduction system — atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, conduction block, sudden death risk in some cohorts.
- Inpatient monitoring:
  - Continuous telemetry while troponin is elevated, with periodic reassessment.
  - Consider prolonged monitoring (48–72 h) if initial telemetry shows ectopy, pauses, or supraventricular/ventricular arrhythmias.
- Outpatient monitoring:
  - Ambulatory event monitor (2–4 weeks) if palpitations, syncope, or prior arrhythmia.
  - Consider implantable loop recorder for unexplained syncope or high arrhythmic risk.
- When to involve electrophysiology:
  - High‑grade AV block, sustained ventricular arrhythmia, syncope, or syncope with abnormal ECG.
- Practical tip: even modest troponin or BNP elevations in myositis justify a low threshold for telemetry early in the disease course.

## Putting it together — a stepwise approach at the bedside
1. Evaluate symptoms and haemodynamics. If unstable → resuscitate, treat arrhythmia/HF, call cardiology.
2. Order ECG, hs‑troponin I (prefer I over T if available), BNP, basic labs (renal), and bedside echo.
3. If troponin I is elevated or echo abnormal:
   - Start continuous telemetry.
   - Consult cardiology early.
   - Consider CMR within days for tissue characterization (especially if this will influence treatment decisions such as starting immunosuppression).
4. If troponin only modestly elevated, echo normal, no symptoms:
   - Consider serial troponins, monitor clinically, and obtain troponin I if initial was T.
   - Low threshold for CMR if uncertainty persists or arrhythmias develop.
5. If myocarditis is likely and systemic inflammatory myopathy is active:
   - Coordinate care between rheumatology, neurology, and cardiology to weigh immunosuppression risks/benefits.
   - Follow LVEF and arrhythmia burden; reassess with repeat imaging as clinically indicated.

## Clinical pearls
- Prefer troponin I in patients with active skeletal muscle disease — troponin T may be confounded by regenerating skeletal muscle.
- A normal echo does not exclude myocarditis; CMR is more sensitive for myocardial inflammation.
- Modest troponin/BNP elevations in myositis are common; interpret in clinical context and use imaging/monitoring to guide management.
- Early cardiology involvement and telemetry monitoring are pragmatic defaults when cardiac biomarkers or symptoms suggest myocardial involvement.
- Recovery of function and normalization of biomarkers after immunosuppression and treatment of the underlying myopathy supports an inflammatory cardiac process — but timing and follow‑up imaging are important for confirming resolution.

Take‑home messages
- In inflammatory myopathies, troponin I is the preferred cardiac biomarker for specificity.
- Use echo bedside to triage; use CMR to confirm myocarditis and guide management when the diagnosis is uncertain.
- Continuous telemetry and ambulatory monitoring are reasonable for patients with elevated troponin, abnormal ECG, or arrhythmia symptoms.
- Always interpret biomarkers with the clinical picture — modest elevations are common; look for dynamic changes, symptoms, ECG and imaging abnormalities to decide on escalation.

This semaglutide‑associated IMNM case reminds us that muscle disease can unmask or involve the heart. Keep a low threshold for cardiac workup, choose the right troponin, and coordinate multispecialty care — the difference between watchful waiting and early intervention can be significant.



# Weight Loss Drugs, Lean Mass, and Muscle Injury: What Should Clinicians Tell Patients?

GLP-1 receptor agonists—semaglutide, tirzepatide, and their cousins—have changed the game for obesity and diabetes care. Patients lose substantial weight, cardiometabolic risk improves, and clinic schedules are full. But with widespread use comes fresh safety signals. Most adverse effects are gastrointestinal and expected. What’s less expected (and worth knowing) are effects on skeletal muscle: modest lean mass loss is common, frank rhabdomyolysis has been reported, and now there’s a case report of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) temporally linked to a single semaglutide dose.

Why this case matters
A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, and a CK that rose to nearly 4,000 U/L over 4 months after one dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex—features of IMNM. She improved with high‑dose steroids and IVIG and was off immunosuppression months later after stopping semaglutide. This is not the classic toxic rhabdomyolysis picture (rapid CK spike and quick recovery after drug cessation). It reads more like an immune-mediated myopathy temporally associated with a GLP-1 agonist.

What to tell patients up front
Be transparent about benefits and common side effects (GI symptoms, nausea, decreased appetite). Add a short sentence about muscle: “Weight loss includes some lean mass — most patients tolerate this, but if you get new muscle weakness, trouble swallowing, or a very dark urine, let us know right away.” That simple line primes patients to report red flags early.

Red flags that should prompt urgent evaluation
Progressive proximal limb weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, lifting arms)
New or worsening dysphagia or dysphonia
Marked myalgias with decreased function
Dark/tea‑coloured urine or oliguria (suggesting rhabdomyolysis)
If any of these appear, act early—delay can allow significant disability.

When to check CK (practical approach)
Baseline CK screening for every patient starting GLP‑1 agonist is not required in most guidelines. Consider baseline CK if there are predisposing factors: prior myopathy, statin intolerance with muscle symptoms, known neuromuscular disease, or baseline muscle complaints. After starting therapy:
- Check CK promptly if patient reports new weakness, myalgias with functional decline, dark urine, or dysphagia/dysphonia.
- For isolated mild myalgias without functional impairment, consider a CK to reassure and document; repeat if symptoms progress.

When to stop the GLP-1 agonist
- Stop immediately if there is progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, or CK elevations >5× ULN with symptoms—or any CK elevation with dark urine/AKI.
- For modest CK elevations without symptoms, consider holding vs continuing after shared decision-making, with close follow-up and repeat CK. But have a low threshold to stop if symptoms progress.

Initial workup in the ED/clinic when red flags present
- Stat labs: CK, renal function, electrolytes, AST/ALT
- Urinalysis (myoglobinuria)
- ECG (if troponin or cardiac symptoms)
- Consider hospitalization for very high CK, AKI, dysphagia with aspiration risk, or bulbar weakness.
- Early specialty input: neurology or rheumatology—IMNM and inflammatory myopathies are not diagnoses to delay.

Referral pathways and treatment considerations
If clinical picture suggests an inflammatory myopathy (progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, CK in the thousands, biopsy/MRI supportive), involve neurology and rheumatology early. IMNM often requires immunosuppression—high‑dose steroids, IVIG, and steroid‑sparing agents are typical. In the reported semaglutide‑associated case, pulse steroids and IVIG were used with good recovery after drug cessation.

Counseling on lean mass loss vs pathology
- Some lean mass loss with weight loss is expected. It is usually gradual and accompanied by functional gains (less joint pain, better mobility).
- Pathologic myopathy presents with progressive weakness, loss of function, dysphagia, bulbar symptoms, or very high CK. That’s not “expected” and should trigger evaluation.

Practical patient-facing script (30 seconds)
“I’m starting you on semaglutide because it can really help your weight and health. Most people have nausea at first. Rarely, medicines like this can affect muscles. If you notice new trouble getting out of chairs, lifting your arms, unexplained difficulty swallowing or a very dark urine, call us or go to the ER so we can check a blood test called CK. We’ll stop the medicine and evaluate quickly if anything worrisome comes up.”

Key take-home messages
- GLP‑1 RAs commonly cause GI side effects and modest lean mass loss; serious myopathies are rare but reported (rhabdomyolysis and now a case of IMNM).
- Red flags: progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, dark urine, or AKI—act promptly.
- Check CK if new significant muscle symptoms occur; stop the drug for progressive weakness, dysphagia, or marked CK elevation.
- Inflammatory myopathies (including IMNM) require early neurology/rheumatology involvement—treatment may include steroids and IVIG.
- Counsel patients up front so they report red flags early; shared decision-making is key.

The bottom line: GLP‑1 agonists are powerful tools. Keep prescribing them where indicated, but pair that power with vigilance. A quick CK and a phone call when a patient reports red flags can prevent delayed diagnosis of a treatable and potentially serious muscle disease.
