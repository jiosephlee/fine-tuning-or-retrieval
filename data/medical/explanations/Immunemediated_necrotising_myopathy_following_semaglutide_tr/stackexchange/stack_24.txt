Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the relationship between IMNM and malignancy risk, and what cancer screening strategy is reasonable at diagnosis?

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is not as strongly linked to occult malignancy as dermatomyositis, but it is not entirely free of cancer risk. Large myositis cohorts show the highest paraneoplastic association with dermatomyositis (especially TIF1‑γ and NXP2 antibodies); by contrast most IMNM series report a lower but still measurable rate of concurrent or shortly preceding cancer (varying by cohort but often in the single‑digit to low‑teens percent). Some signals suggest that seronegative IMNM or atypical presentations may carry a relatively higher likelihood of an occult malignancy than antibody‑positive statin‑associated IMNM, although the data are limited and inconsistent. The practical implication is that IMNM should trigger thoughtful cancer evaluation, particularly around the time of diagnosis and during the first 12–24 months when a paraneoplastic process would be most likely to declare itself.

A reasonable, pragmatic cancer‑screening strategy at diagnosis is therefore to combine guideline‑based age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening with a focused baseline search for occult malignancy guided by symptoms and risk factors. This typically includes a careful history and physical exam (skin, lymph nodes, breasts, pelvic exam), up‑to‑date routine screens (mammography, cervical screening, colonoscopy as indicated), and targeted imaging — chest/abdomen/pelvis CT (or whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT if available and local practice supports it) when there are concerning features such as older age, systemic symptoms, rapid progression, unexplained weight loss, or seronegativity. Basic labs (CBC, chemistry, LFTs) are reasonable; routine tumor markers are not mandated unless clinically suggested. If the initial workup is negative, maintain a low threshold for repeat evaluation during the first 12–24 months (interval history/exam and consideration of repeat imaging) because paraneoplastic myopathies often present near the time of cancer diagnosis.

In short: IMNM carries a lower but non‑zero malignancy risk compared with dermatomyositis. At diagnosis perform guideline cancer screening plus directed baseline imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET/CT when indicated) and close clinical follow‑up with repeat assessment over the first 1–2 years.
