### [Pathophysiology] Which histopathologic findings on muscle biopsy distinguish IMNM from polymyositis and dermatomyositis?

The single most important distinguishing feature is the pattern and cellular composition of muscle injury: IMNM is defined by prominent myofibre necrosis with abundant macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and only minimal lymphocytic inflammation, whereas polymyositis and dermatomyositis show characteristic and different inflammatory patterns.

In practice this means that on biopsy IMNM shows widespread necrotic and regenerating fibres, frequent CD68+ macrophages engulfing debris, diffuse sarcolemmal up‑regulation of MHC class I, and often complement (membrane attack complex) deposition on necrotic fibres — but only sparse CD3+/CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates. By contrast polymyositis classically demonstrates an endomysial inflammatory infiltrate rich in CD8+ T cells that invade otherwise non‑necrotic, MHC‑I–expressing fibres (true fibre invasion), implicating direct T‑cell–mediated cytotoxicity. Dermatomyositis shows a perifascicular and perivascular pattern with capillary loss, complement (MAC) deposition in endomysial capillaries, perifascicular atrophy, and a more prominent perimysial/perivascular B‑cell and plasmacytoid dendritic cell–predominant inflammation (MxA staining often positive).

Thus, when necrosis with macrophage predominance and few lymphocytes is the dominant lesion, IMNM is favored; the presence of endomysial CD8+ fibre invasion points to polymyositis, and perifascicular atrophy with capillary/vascular complement deposition points to dermatomyositis. Correlation with serology (eg, anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in IMNM) and clinical phenotype completes the diagnosis.
