### [Morning Report] Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality framework, what factors support a 'probable' drug–adverse event relationship in a patient with suspected drug-induced IMNM?

In the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre framework a “probable/likely” causality assignment rests on a consistent temporal relationship, plausibility, lack of a more likely alternative explanation, and a clinically reasonable response to withdrawal — without requiring a positive rechallenge. Applying those principles to this case explains why the authors judged the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM as probable.

First, the temporal relationship is compatible: the patient had an acute systemic reaction within hours of a single semaglutide dose and then developed progressive proximal weakness with a rising CK over the ensuing weeks to months. Although the full myopathic phenotype evolved over months (as is typical for an immune‑mediated process), the onset followed exposure in a biologically plausible timeframe for a drug‑triggered autoimmune reaction.

Second, there is a clear dechallenge response. After semaglutide was discontinued and the patient received immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids and IVIG), her CK normalized and she returned to baseline functional status and remained well off immunosuppression for many months. That sustained improvement after removal of the suspected agent supports causality under the UMC criteria.

Third, competing causes were reasonably excluded. She had no history of statin use or other known pharmacologic or environmental triggers for IMNM, myositis serologies were negative (acknowledging seronegative IMNM is well described), and biopsy demonstrated necrotizing myopathy with inflammatory features consistent with IMNM rather than pure toxic rhabdomyolysis or an alternative neuromuscular diagnosis. Chronic liver enzyme abnormalities were present before exposure and liver biopsy confirmed NAFLD, reducing the likelihood those transaminase elevations reflected preexisting muscle disease.

Fourth, the clinical picture is biologically plausible. GLP‑1 receptor agonists have been associated with muscle mass loss and isolated reports of severe muscle injury with agents in this class; immune activation after a drug exposure is a recognized mechanism for IMNM. Although mechanistic data are lacking, plausibility strengthens the association.

Finally, the only major criterion absent for a “certain” classification is a positive rechallenge, which is neither ethical nor necessary for a probable designation. Limitations remain — notably the single‑dose exposure with delayed full expression of disease and the absence of a specific autoantibody — so “probable” appropriately reflects the balance of supportive evidence without overcalling certainty.

In short: a compatible time course, marked clinical and laboratory improvement after withdrawal and immunotherapy, exclusion of more likely alternatives, supportive muscle biopsy, and biological plausibility together satisfy the key WHO‑UMC elements that define a “probable/likely” drug–adverse event relationship.
