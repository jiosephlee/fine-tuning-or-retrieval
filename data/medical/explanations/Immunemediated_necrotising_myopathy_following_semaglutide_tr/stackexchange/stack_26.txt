### [Morning Report] What electromyographic (EMG) findings are typical of IMNM, and how do they help differentiate myopathic from neuropathic processes?

Electromyography in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) typically shows a myopathic pattern with evidence of active muscle fibre injury rather than the chronic reinnervation pattern of neuropathy. The common findings are short‑duration, low‑amplitude motor unit potentials with early recruitment and increased polyphasia, together with marked spontaneous activity — fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves — reflecting ongoing muscle fibre necrosis and membrane instability. Complex repetitive discharges or myotonic‑like discharges can also be seen, but are not specific.

How this differentiates myopathic from neuropathic processes is a matter of the motor unit geometry and recruitment pattern. In myopathy the individual myofibres comprising a motor unit are lost or electrically unstable, so MUAPs become lower in amplitude and shorter in duration and many motor units are still available, producing early (rapid) recruitment. In neuropathy, axonal loss produces fewer surviving motor units that enlarge by collateral reinnervation; MUAPs are therefore high amplitude and long duration, and recruitment is reduced. Both conditions can show fibrillations if there is active denervation (neuropathy) or active myofibre necrosis (myopathy), so fibrillations by themselves do not distinguish the two — the overall MUAP morphology and recruitment pattern are key.

Clinically useful points: in a patient like this one with high CK and rapidly progressive proximal weakness, a myopathic EMG with abundant fibrillations supports an active necrotising myopathy and helps localize the best biopsy site; EMG cannot replace biopsy or serology for definitive classification (IMNM versus other inflammatory myopathies) but it steers the diagnostic pathway and distinguishes primary muscle disease from motor neuropathy or neuromuscular junction disorders.
