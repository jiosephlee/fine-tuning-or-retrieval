Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Management] Discuss the known skeletal muscle adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists and how you would counsel patients about future use or rechallenge after suspected IMNM.

Clinical pearl — what we know about GLP‑1 receptor agonists and skeletal muscle, and how to counsel a patient after a suspected immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) are well‑established to cause gastrointestinal adverse effects and weight loss; loss of skeletal muscle mass has been reported as a physiologic consequence of weight loss and calorie restriction, and small observational studies and case reports have described sarcopenic changes after GLP‑1 RA use. Separately, rare case reports link GLP‑1 RAs (and related incretin agonists) to frank muscle injury: most commonly an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis phenotype (very high CK that improves promptly with drug withdrawal and supportive care), and very rarely a necrotising myopathy pattern. The literature remains sparse — there are isolated reports (including the present semaglutide case and a tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis report) but no large signal or mechanistic proof that GLP‑1 RAs provoke autoimmune myopathies.

How to think about mechanisms and diagnosis in practice. Distinguish three patterns because counseling differs by mechanism. First, physiologic muscle loss related to weight reduction or deconditioning — slow, proportional, not inflammatory, CK usually normal. Second, toxic rhabdomyolysis — abrupt, massive CK elevation tied temporally to drug exposure, rapid fall after withdrawal and hydration, no requirement for immunosuppression. Third, immune‑mediated myopathy (IMNM or other autoimmune myositis) — progressive proximal weakness, sustained CK elevation, muscle biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis and complement activation, and often need for immunosuppression. Autoantibodies (eg, HMGCR, SRP) can be present but a substantial minority are seronegative. In this case the time course (progressive weakness over months after one dose), biopsy showing necrosis with inflammatory features, and response to immunotherapy favor IMNM rather than acute toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Counseling about future use / rechallenge after suspected IMNM. For clinical decision‑making you must weigh the probability that the drug caused an immune‑mediated process, the severity of the event, and the availability of alternatives.

If you have high suspicion for immune‑mediated myopathy (biopsy‑proven IMNM, sustained CK elevation, clinical severity, and improvement only after immunosuppression and drug withdrawal): do not rechallenge with the offending agent and generally avoid the entire class unless there is compelling, unavoidable clinical need and the patient consents to risk. The known uncertainty of mechanism and the potential for recurrence or worsening with rechallenge make avoidance the prudent course. Document the reaction clearly, counsel the patient that re‑exposure risks recurrence of severe, potentially life‑threatening muscle disease, and consider alternative therapies for diabetes/weight management. Report the adverse event to local pharmacovigilance/regulatory bodies.

If the presentation is more consistent with toxic rhabdomyolysis (acute, very high CK with rapid resolution after discontinuation and no biopsy evidence of autoimmune inflammation), a cautious rechallenge could be considered in selected situations after full recovery, with informed consent and close monitoring (baseline CK, repeated CK during early weeks, symptom vigilance). Rechallenge should be avoided if other precipitants (statins, drug interactions, dehydration) cannot be corrected.

Practical steps when considering any re‑exposure
1) Convene multidisciplinary discussion (rheumatology/myositis specialist, neurology, endocrinology/diabetes or obesity medicine) and involve the patient in shared decision‑making.
2) Reassess for alternative causes (statins, supplements, infections, malignancy) and check myositis serologies, baseline CK, and consider repeat imaging or biopsy if uncertainty remains.
3) If a rechallenge is contemplated despite prior IMNM, obtain explicit informed consent explaining uncertain risk, institute close objective monitoring (CK twice weekly for several weeks initially, early clinic or phone checks for weakness/dysphagia/respiratory signs), and stop immediately at any new symptoms or CK rise — but even with that infrastructure, rechallenge is generally discouraged.
4) Consider alternative non‑GLP‑1 therapies for glycemic control or weight loss and engage nutrition/exercise/safety plans to preserve muscle mass.

In the presented case the lesion was severe, biopsy‑proven IMNM with dramatic clinical course and improvement only after immunotherapy and withdrawal of semaglutide; rechallenge would be inadvisable and ethically problematic. Counsel the patient that future use of semaglutide or closely related incretin agonists is not recommended, alternatives should be pursued, and the event should be reported so we can better define rare anti‑muscle adverse effects of these widely used drugs.
