### [Clinical Pearls] When encountering new-onset myopathy after exposure to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, what clinical features and time course would increase concern for an autoimmune mechanism rather than direct drug toxicity?

When you see new‑onset myopathy after exposure to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, the key clinical distinction is whether the picture behaves like an acute toxic myonecrosis (rhabdomyolysis) that improves after drug withdrawal and supportive care, or like an immune‑mediated myopathy that progresses despite stopping the drug and requires immunotherapy. Features and time course that should raise concern for an autoimmune mechanism are the following.

First, a subacute to progressive course over weeks to months with worsening proximal weakness (often with bulbar involvement such as dysphagia or dysphonia) is more consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) than with direct toxin‑induced rhabdomyolysis, which is typically abrupt and improves rapidly after removal of the offending agent. In the case presented, weakness and disabling dysphagia evolved over four months after a single semaglutide dose — a pattern that argues against a transient direct toxic effect.

Second, a persistently elevated or rising CK after cessation of the agent (especially CK in the thousands that does not fall quickly with hydration and discontinuation) suggests ongoing immune injury rather than one‑time muscle breakdown. In contrast, drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis usually shows a sharp CK peak and then decline over days once the drug is stopped and the patient is supported.

Third, supportive laboratory and imaging findings that point to inflammation — modestly elevated ESR/CRP, symmetric muscle edema on MRI, and extramuscular inflammatory features (for example possible myocarditis) — favor an autoimmune process. The presence of systemic inflammatory markers or multisystem involvement increases the likelihood that the mechanism is immune‑mediated.

Fourth, muscle biopsy is decisive: necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition, and a relative paucity of classic lymphocytic infiltrates are characteristic of IMNM and distinguish it from pure toxic necrosis. Serologic assays for IMNM autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) can help when positive, but up to a third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative panel does not rule out autoimmune disease.

Finally, the clinical response to immunosuppression is informative. Sustained improvement in strength and normalization of CK with corticosteroids and IVIG — and the ability to taper off immunosuppression without relapse — supports a drug‑triggered immune myopathy. By contrast, toxin‑related injury typically recovers without immunomodulation.

Putting it together: a subacute, progressive proximal myopathy with bulbar signs, persistent or rising CK after drug discontinuation, MRI evidence of muscle edema, inflammatory biopsy features (macrophage infiltration, MAC deposition), systemic inflammatory markers or extramuscular involvement, and clinical response to immunosuppression all point to an autoimmune mechanism rather than direct drug toxicity.
