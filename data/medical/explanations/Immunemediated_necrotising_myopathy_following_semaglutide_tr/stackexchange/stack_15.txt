### [Management] What are the first-line immunomodulatory treatments for severe IMNM with dysphagia or profound weakness, and what is the rationale for adding IVIG early?

In severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with bulbar involvement (dysphagia) or rapidly progressive, profound proximal weakness, initial therapy should be aggressive and combination‑oriented because the disease produces rapid myofibre necrosis and risk of respiratory compromise or aspiration.

First‑line immediate therapy is high‑dose corticosteroids and early intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Practically this is methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days (pulse) followed by oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day, combined up front with IVIG 2 g/kg given over 2–5 days (often split over 3–4 days). In most centers a second immunomodulator is started early as a steroid‑sparing agent (methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate) or, for refractory or antibody‑positive (especially anti‑SRP) disease, rituximab or cyclophosphamide may be used. Plasmapheresis is considered in fulminant cases or severe bulbar/respiratory failure when rapid antibody removal is needed.

The rationale for adding IVIG early is both pathophysiologic and pragmatic. IMNM is frequently antibody‑mediated and shows complement activation and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition on myofibres; IVIG neutralises pathogenic autoantibodies, downregulates complement activation, modulates Fc receptor function on macrophages, and has broad immunomodulatory effects that can arrest ongoing necrosis faster than steroids alone. Clinically, early IVIG is associated with more rapid reductions in CK, faster improvement in strength and bulbar function, and a lower risk of irreversible muscle loss or aspiration that would follow delayed control of immune injury. In the presented case, prompt pulse steroids plus IVIG produced a rapid CK fall and recovery of function, which illustrates the benefit of early combined therapy.

Keep in mind the tradeoffs: IVIG is resource‑intensive and carries risks (volume overload, thromboembolism, infection transmission, renal dysfunction), so monitor closely and tailor subsequent steroid‑sparing therapy based on antibody status, clinical course, and infection risk.
