Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Clinical Pearls] Which autoantibodies are most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (HMGCR, SRP), how does their presence or absence influence diagnostic confidence and expected treatment course, and how should a seronegative panel be interpreted in the context of supportive histology?

Direct answer
- The two autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Their detection substantially increases diagnostic confidence that the clinical syndrome and necrotising muscle biopsy represent autoimmune IMNM rather than another myopathy.
- Presence of a specific antibody influences prognosis and usual treatment intensity: SRP‑positive disease is classically severe, frequently refractory to steroids alone and often requires early multi‑agent immunotherapy (IVIG, rituximab, cyclophosphamide). HMGCR‑positive disease is strongly linked to prior statin exposure, can be persistent despite stopping the statin, and often responds well to IVIG (frequently requires sustained immunosuppression).  
- Absence of HMGCR and SRP (and a negative myositis panel) does not exclude IMNM. About one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative. When biopsy shows the characteristic picture (scattered necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition) and the clinical phenotype (acute/subacute severe proximal weakness, high CK, dysphagia/cardiac/respiratory risk), you should manage as IMNM while searching for triggers (drugs, statins, malignancy, infection) and monitoring response.

Clinical reasoning and application to the presented case
- The patient’s myositis panel was negative for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP yet her muscle biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, CD68+ macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition—histology that is diagnostic of IMNM. This exactly illustrates the key teaching point: seronegativity does not invalidate the histologic diagnosis.
- The clinical course—temporal relationship to a drug exposure (semaglutide), progressive weakness and high CK followed by prompt improvement after drug cessation and a short course of high‑dose steroids plus a single IVIG cycle, and durable remission off maintenance immunosuppression—fits a drug‑associated IMNM phenotype rather than classic antibody‑positive idiopathic IMNM. Antibody‑positive IMNM more often requires prolonged and sometimes escalating immunosuppression to maintain remission.

Practical management implications
- Workup: test for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (and an extended myositis panel), obtain MRI/EMG as needed, and perform muscle biopsy when serology is negative or uncertain. Evaluate for triggers (statin use, new drugs, cancer screening tailored to age and risk).
- Treatment principles: start high‑dose glucocorticoids for severe weakness; add IVIG early for severe disease or when HMGCR positivity is suspected (IVIG has good evidence in HMGCR IMNM) or for poor steroid response. For SRP‑positive or refractory cases, escalate to steroid‑sparing agents (rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) sooner. In drug‑triggered cases, stop the offending agent; if the patient improves rapidly and sustains remission (as in this case), prolonged immunosuppression may not be required.
- Follow‑up: monitor strength, CK, swallowing/respiratory status and cardiac signs; recheck serology only if it would change management (some antibodies are persistent, but lab sensitivity varies). If disease relapses or fails to improve promptly, treat as antibody‑positive IMNM with escalation.

Takeaway clinical pearl
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are highly specific markers that increase diagnostic certainty and predict a more refractory course that often needs aggressive, prolonged immunotherapy. However, a negative serology does not rule out IMNM: biopsy‑proven necrotising myopathy with compatible clinical features warrants treatment as IMNM and a search for triggers. The case illustrates a seronegative, likely drug‑associated IMNM that improved rapidly after drug withdrawal and brief immunotherapy—an expected, more favorable trajectory than typical antibody‑positive IMNM.
