Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Morning Report] What are the core elements of the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) criteria that support a diagnosis of IMNM, and how does seronegativity for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies affect the diagnostic approach?

The ENMC diagnostic framework for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is fundamentally clinicopathologic: it requires a compatible clinical syndrome, supportive laboratory/imaging/EMG findings, a characteristic muscle biopsy, and exclusion of alternate explanations. Clinically the patient typically has subacute to rapidly progressive, symmetric proximal weakness often with dysphagia or respiratory involvement and markedly elevated serum CK. Ancillary testing that supports the diagnosis includes myopathic changes on EMG and muscle edema on MRI. The histopathologic hallmarks are numerous necrotic and regenerating myofibres with macrophage‑predominant myophagocytosis, scant lymphocytic infiltrates, sarcolemmal/microvascular membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition, and relative preservation of endomysial architecture compared with polymyositis. Demonstration of one of the disease‑associated autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP) is highly supportive and helps phenotype prognosis and treatment response. Equally important in the ENMC approach is excluding other causes — toxin/drug myopathy, metabolic myopathies, hereditary myopathies, infection, and paraneoplastic syndromes.

Seronegativity for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP changes the weighting of the diagnostic workup but does not rule out IMNM. About one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so when antibodies are absent you rely more heavily on the clinical picture, imaging/EMG, and, above all, the muscle biopsy and exclusion of mimics. In practice that means: interpret a compatible history (rapid proximal weakness, very high CK), supportive MRI/EMG, and a biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage predominance and C5b‑9 deposition as sufficient to make the diagnosis of seronegative IMNM; search carefully for triggers (statins, other drugs, infections, cancer) and consider repeating autoantibody testing or broader panels if suspicion remains. Finally, an empirical therapeutic trial of immunotherapy (with careful monitoring) may both treat the disease and provide diagnostic confirmation when biopsy and other data point toward IMNM — as in this case, where characteristic biopsy findings plus dramatic clinical and CK improvement after immunosuppression and withdrawal of semaglutide support a diagnosis of IMNM despite negative HMGCR/SRP antibodies.
