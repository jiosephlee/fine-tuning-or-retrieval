Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Differential Diagnosis] Compare and contrast the time course, symptom profile, and CK trajectory of drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis with that of IMNM.

The two syndromes can look similar at a single timepoint because both cause muscle necrosis and CK elevation, but they differ in timing, associated symptoms, laboratory tempo, pathology and treatment response — distinctions that matter when you decide whether to stop a drug and start immunotherapy.

Drug‑induced toxic rhabdomyolysis is an acute toxic event. Symptoms typically begin within hours to days of exposure or of an identifiable stressor, with prominent myalgias, weakness, and often systemic features of muscle breakdown (dark urine, cola‑colored urine, frank myoglobinuria) and frequently acute kidney injury and metabolic derangements from myoglobinuria and hypovolemia. CK usually rises very rapidly to very high levels — often tens of thousands IU/L — and, if the offending drug or trigger is removed and supportive care (aggressive hydration, correcting electrolytes) instituted, CK falls quickly over days to weeks. Muscle biopsy, if performed early, shows myofiber necrosis without a dominant adaptive immune infiltrate; there is no consistent autoantibody signature and immunosuppression is not required in typical toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is subacute to chronic and immune‑driven. Symptoms evolve over weeks to months and are dominated by progressively disabling symmetric proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), with relatively less myalgia and less frequent frank myoglobinuria. Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and cardiomyopathy can occur and point toward an autoimmune myopathy. CK is elevated — often in the low thousands but sometimes higher — and importantly the elevation tends to be sustained or progressive despite withdrawal of a putative offending agent; spontaneous rapid normalization is uncommon. Muscle biopsy characteristically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and only sparse lymphocytes. A proportion of patients have disease‑specific autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP), but a substantial minority are seronegative. IMNM typically requires immunotherapy (corticosteroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) and CK and strength often improve only after immunosuppression.

Applying these principles to the case: the patient had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion illness with only modest CK (337 U/L) at first presentation — an expected picture for volume‑related muscle injury. The subsequent clinical course, however, was incompatible with simple toxic rhabdomyolysis: over four months she developed progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms and a delayed, sustained rise in CK to ~4,000 U/L rather than a single massive spike that resolved with hydration. Muscle biopsy showed dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition — the pathological pattern of IMNM — and her weakness and CK improved only after corticosteroids and IVIG. Those temporal features (subacute progression despite drug cessation), symptom profile (prominent proximal weakness and bulbar involvement with less pain), and CK trajectory (sustained elevation and fall with immunotherapy rather than rapid resolution after stopping drug) therefore favor IMNM over isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Clinically, when a patient on a new medication presents with CK elevation, acutely ill with myoglobinuria and renal failure suggests primary toxic rhabdomyolysis managed with supportive care and drug withdrawal. When weakness is progressive over weeks–months, bulbar features or cardiomyopathy are present, CK elevation is persistent or rising despite stopping the drug, or biopsy shows immune‑mediated necrosis, you should pursue myositis serologies and muscle biopsy and treat as IMNM because delay in immunotherapy risks irreversible weakness.
