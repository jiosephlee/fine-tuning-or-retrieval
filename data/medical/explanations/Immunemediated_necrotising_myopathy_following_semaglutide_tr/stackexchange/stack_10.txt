Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Management] For a patient with likely drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy temporally associated with semaglutide, how would you counsel regarding future use of GLP-1 receptor agonists (class avoidance vs cautious reintroduction) and what alternative strategies would you consider for obesity and NAFLD management?

Bottom line
- In this patient I would recommend permanent avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists (and likely other agents with GLP‑1 activity such as tirzepatide). The myopathy was biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), temporally linked to a single semaglutide dose, required high‑dose steroids and IVIG, and then resolved off the drug. Given the severity of the immune‑mediated reaction and the ethical and clinical risk of provoking recurrence, rechallenge or class‑wide reintroduction is not advisable in routine practice.

Why avoid the class in this case
- The case meets criteria for “probable” drug causality (WHO‑UMC): clear temporal relationship, lack of alternative explanation, clinical improvement after drug withdrawal plus immunotherapy.  
- IMNM is an aggressive, immune‑mediated process rather than a transient toxic rhabdomyolysis. This patient had progressive proximal weakness, CK rise to ~4,000 U/L, and muscle biopsy with necrosis, macrophage myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex — all consistent with IMNM. She required IV methylprednisolone and IVIG to recover.  
- Re‑exposure to a drug that has triggered an immune‑mediated myopathy risks a more rapid and severe recurrence; rechallenge would therefore be unethical unless there were compelling reasons and very close specialist oversight. Case reports of muscle injury have also been described with other incretin agents (e.g., tirzepatide), so cross‑reactivity or a class effect cannot be excluded.

How to counsel the patient (practical script)
- “Your muscle disease was biopsy‑proven IMNM that began after semaglutide. Although we cannot prove causation beyond doubt, the timing and the severity make it likely the drug triggered an autoimmune myopathy. Because this was serious and required IVIG and steroids, I recommend you do not take semaglutide again and avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists in the future. We will document this clearly in your chart and report it to pharmacovigilance. If you are later offered another drug in this class, please bring it to our team first — rechallenge would only be considered in rare, carefully controlled circumstances with full informed consent and specialist oversight.”

Alternatives for treating obesity and NAFLD (practical, evidence‑based options)
1) Reinforce first‑line therapy: intensive lifestyle intervention
   - Structured, multidisciplinary programs targeting a 7–10% weight loss (calorie reduction, increased physical activity, behavioral therapy) remain the cornerstone for NAFLD improvement and are effective for many patients.
   - Refer to dietitians, exercise physiologists, and behavioral health for sustained programs.

2) Bariatric/metabolic surgery
   - If the patient meets guideline criteria (typically BMI ≥40, or ≥35 with obesity‑related comorbidities such as NAFLD/NASH), bariatric surgery offers the most durable weight loss and has been shown to improve steatosis, NASH, and fibrosis in many patients.
   - Discuss surgical risks and a formal surgical evaluation.

3) Non‑GLP‑1 pharmacotherapy for weight loss (choose based on comorbidities and contraindications)
   - Phentermine‑topiramate: high efficacy for weight loss; consider cardiovascular history and tolerability.
   - Naltrexone‑bupropion: alternative with different mechanism; watch for hypertension, psychiatric history, and seizure risk.
   - Orlistat: modest weight loss, acts in the gut; consider GI tolerability and lipid lowering effects.
   - These agents are less effective than GLP‑1 RAs on average but are reasonable alternatives when GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated.

4) Treating NAFLD/NASH specifically
   - Weight loss through lifestyle or surgery is the primary treatment and improves histology.  
   - Pioglitazone can improve steatohepatitis (particularly in biopsy‑proven NASH), including in non‑diabetics, but causes weight gain, edema and has other long‑term risks—use after shared decision‑making and cardiology/hepatology input given her cardiac history and proBNP elevation earlier.  
   - Vitamin E (800 IU/day) is an option in non‑diabetic adults with biopsy‑proven NASH (weigh benefits vs risks).  
   - SGLT2 inhibitors (if she has diabetes) and statins for cardiovascular risk reduction may have modest hepatic benefits; statins should be used when indicated despite prior muscle disease only after careful review (note: patient denied prior statin use and IMNM was seronegative for HMGCR).  
   - Refer to hepatology for consideration of clinical trials of emerging NASH therapies, fibrosis staging (transient elastography), and longitudinal monitoring.

5) Monitoring and documentation
   - Prominently document “severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy attributed to semaglutide — avoid GLP‑1 RAs” in problem list and drug allergy/alert systems.  
   - Report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance registries.  
   - Continue surveillance for NAFLD progression (liver labs, fibrosis assessment) and for recurrent myopathic symptoms; check CK only if symptoms suggest recurrence.

When (rare) cautious re‑exposure might be discussed
- Only in exceptional situations (e.g., patient refuses all other options and the clinical benefit is judged to outweigh risk) would re‑challenge be considered — and then only after multidisciplinary review (rheumatology, neurology, hepatology), with explicit informed consent, baseline CK and muscle testing, very close serial monitoring, and readiness to stop immediately if symptoms or CK rise. Given this patient’s course, that would be a high‑risk choice and is not recommended.

Bottom line for this case
- The safest, evidence‑based approach is class avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists given a biopsy‑proven, severe IMNM temporally linked to semaglutide. Focus management on intensive lifestyle change, consideration of bariatric surgery if eligible, alternative anti‑obesity medications, and hepatology‑directed therapies (weight loss, pioglitazone or vitamin E where appropriate, and clinical trials).
