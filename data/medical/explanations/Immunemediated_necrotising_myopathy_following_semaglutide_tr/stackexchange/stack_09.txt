Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

### [Management] What preventive measures would you implement to mitigate infection risk when initiating high-dose glucocorticoids and IVIG for inflammatory myopathy (e.g., vaccination review, PJP prophylaxis considerations, glucose monitoring, catheter stewardship), and how would you monitor for and manage infectious complications?

Direct answer
When you start high‑dose glucocorticoids (eg, IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by prednisone ≈1 mg/kg) and IVIG for inflammatory myopathy you must treat the patient as newly immunosuppressed and take concrete steps to reduce predictable infectious risks, to screen for latent infections that can reactivate, and to monitor closely so infections are recognized and treated early.

Practical checklist (what to do before or at initiation)
- Vaccination review
  - Give indicated inactivated vaccines if there is time before immunosuppression (ideally ≥2 weeks before). Priority: seasonal influenza, pneumococcal (per schedule), Tdap, hepatitis B if susceptible, SARS‑CoV‑2 booster per guidelines.
  - Do NOT give live vaccines once high‑dose steroids are planned (live vaccines should be given ≥4 weeks before immunosuppression).
  - Recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is non‑live and preferred over live zoster; give ideally before immunosuppression when feasible, but recognize response may be blunted if given after high‑dose steroids/IVIG.
- Screen for latent/reactivable infections
  - Hepatitis B serologies (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, anti‑HBs). If HBsAg+ or anti‑HBc+ (especially if HBsAg−/anti‑HBc+), consult Hepatology/ID and start antiviral prophylaxis (entecavir/tenofovir) when significant immunosuppression is expected.
  - TB screening (IGRA or PPD and CXR as indicated) in persons with risk factors; treat latent TB before prolonged immunosuppression when feasible.
  - HIV test (baseline).
  - Varicella zoster immune status if history unclear (to counsel about risk and therapy).
  - Check baseline CBC, CMP, creatinine, glucose/HbA1c (to guide prophylaxis dosing and steroid‑diabetes planning).
  - Consider testing for IgA deficiency before IVIG infusion (anaphylaxis risk) and be aware that recent IVIG will interfere with some serologies (eg, anti‑HBs).
- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis
  - Offer PJP prophylaxis if prednisone ≥20 mg/day (or equivalent) is expected for ≥4 weeks or if other significant immunosuppression is added (rituximab, high‑dose cyclophosphamide, etc.). First‑line: TMP‑SMX (single‑strength daily or DS 3×/week). Alternatives: atovaquone, inhaled pentamidine, or dapsone (check G6PD).
- Catheter and device stewardship
  - Avoid indwelling urinary catheters unless absolutely necessary; if placed, use strict aseptic insertion and daily review with prompt removal.
  - Follow central line insertion and maintenance bundles (chlorhexidine skin prep, maximal barrier precautions, sterile dressings, daily site assessment).
- Metabolic and glucose management
  - Start routine capillary glucose monitoring (eg, q4–6 hours while inpatient) and plan for early insulin management: steroids commonly cause marked hyperglycemia—use basal‑bolus or scheduled insulin rather than only PRN sliding‑scale if significant elevations are expected or patient has diabetes.
  - Check electrolytes, potassium and magnesium periodically (steroids and infections can alter them).
- IVIG precautions
  - Pre‑medicate for infusion reactions when appropriate (acetaminophen, antihistamine); hydrate to reduce renal risk, infuse slowly in renal insufficiency, monitor for hemolysis, thrombosis, and TRALI. Inform that IVIG may blunt vaccine responses and transiently affect serologies.

Monitoring while on therapy
- Low threshold for surveillance and early evaluation of fever or subtle signs of infection. Immunosuppressed patients may have atypical or muted inflammatory responses.
- Daily assessment of lines/catheters and prompt removal when no longer needed.
- Routine inpatient monitoring: vitals, focused exam, daily review of glucose logs, CBC and creatinine as clinically indicated.
- If new fever or clinical change: obtain directed cultures (blood, urine if urinary symptoms or catheter present, sputum if respiratory), CXR for pulmonary symptoms, and start empiric antibiotics based on source and local ecology—don’t delay cultures or source control.
- Specific for PJP suspicion: monitor oxygenation and consider early LDCT and empiric TMP‑SMX pending diagnostic testing in hypoxemic patients.

Management of infectious complications (principles)
- Early source control: remove infected catheters/foley, drain abscesses, debride if needed.
- Empiric antimicrobial choices should cover likely pathogens and local resistance patterns; for severe healthcare‑associated UTI/sepsis consider anti‑Pseudomonal beta‑lactam (eg, cefepime, piperacillin‑tazobactam) until cultures return—then de‑escalate.
- Balance immunosuppression: for life‑threatening sepsis, hold or reduce immunosuppressive agents in consultation with rheumatology and ID. Short pulses of steroids for inflammatory myopathy may be stopped or tapered during septic shock; however, physiologic stress‑dose steroids may be needed if adrenal insufficiency is a concern.
- Involve Infectious Diseases early for complex or opportunistic infections, HBV reactivation, persistent bacteremia, or multidrug‑resistant organisms.
- For viral reactivation (HBV, VZV, CMV) follow standard antiviral treatments and consider prophylaxis strategies for future immunosuppression.

How this applies to the presented case
- The patient received IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by prednisone ≈1 mg/kg and IVIG and subsequently developed septic shock from a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection about 12 days after discharge. Likely contributors: recent high‑dose steroid exposure, possible urinary catheter use during prolonged hospitalization, steroid‑related hyperglycemia, and immune modulation from IVIG/steroids.
- Measures that could have reduced that risk: strict avoidance/early removal of urinary catheters, daily catheter‑need reassessment and catheter‑care bundles, rigorous glucose monitoring and insulin management, early screening for and treatment of latent infections (HBV/TB/HIV) pre‑treatment, and clear discharge instructions with low threshold to seek care for fever or urinary symptoms.
- PJP prophylaxis may not have prevented her UTI but would have been indicated if prednisone >20 mg/day was expected for ≥4 weeks; in her case prednisone was tapered over ~2 months, so prophylaxis would have been reasonable.

Clinical pearl (one‑line takeaways)
- Before high‑dose steroids ±IVIG: update inactivated vaccines when possible, screen for HBV/TB/HIV/varicella, plan PJP prophylaxis if prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥4 weeks, enforce catheter and line stewardship, and institute proactive glucose monitoring and management; monitor closely for fever or subtle signs of infection and involve ID early for severe or unusual infections.
