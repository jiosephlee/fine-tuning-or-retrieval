Title: Blog about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

# Hitting Hard, Tapering Fast: Managing Suspected Drug‑Triggered IMNM

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12‑fold rise in CK over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage/myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — diagnostic for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She received 5 days of pulse methylprednisolone, IVIG 2 g/kg (split over 4 days) and oral prednisone 1 mg/kg with a relatively rapid taper as her strength and CK normalized. Ten months later she was back to baseline off immunosuppression. This looks like a drug‑triggered IMNM that responded briskly to targeted, short‑course immunotherapy — a useful template for real‑world front‑line management.

Why this matters
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are being used widely for diabetes and obesity. Most adverse effects are GI, but clinicians should be alert for muscle syndromes beyond simple sarcopenia or transient CK small bumps.
- Drug‑associated IMNM can behave differently from idiopathic antibody‑positive IMNM (HMGCR or SRP): when truly drug‑triggered, withdrawal of the trigger plus early immunotherapy (steroids ± IVIG) can produce a rapid and sustained response — avoiding prolonged multi‑agent immunosuppression in some patients.
- The differential includes rhabdomyolysis, statin‑associated HMGCR IMNM, and idiopathic IMNM — muscle biopsy and serial CK/clinical trajectory guide management.

Practical regimen (grounded in the case)
- Indications for an aggressive start: severe proximal weakness (unable to sit/stand), bulbar dysfunction/dysphagia, very high CK or rapidly rising CK, respiratory compromise. In this case dysphagia and loss of basic function justified aggressive early therapy.
- Pulse steroids for fulminant disease: methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV daily × 3–5 days. This was used here for 5 days with clinical benefit.
- Transition to high‑dose oral steroid: prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (adjust for comorbidities). Use daily outpatient dosing once the acute phase is controlled.
- Early IVIG for severe features: IVIG 2 g/kg total, split over 2–5 days. In this case IVIG was given over 4 days and appeared to accelerate recovery.
- Rapid, guided taper when appropriate: if strength improves and CK normalizes quickly, consider a faster taper (weeks to a couple of months) rather than the prolonged steroid courses often required for idiopathic IMNM. This patient was weaned off prednisone over ~2 months and remained well off therapy.
- Always stop the suspected offending drug and counsel avoidance of rechallenge (in this case, avoid GLP‑1 RAs permanently).

When to add steroid‑sparing or escalation therapy
- Failure to improve clinically or biochemically within 4–8 weeks despite adequate steroid +/− IVIG.
- Inability to reduce prednisone to <15–20 mg/day without relapse.
- Recurrent rises in CK or recurrent weakness on taper.
- Presence of antibody‑positive IMNM known to be refractory (e.g., anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR) — these often need combination therapy.

Common second‑line agents (choose based on phenotype, comorbidity, and local practice)
- Methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil as steroid‑sparing agents for moderate disease.
- IVIG is especially effective in HMGCR‑associated IMNM and is a good early option for severe disease or if steroids are contraindicated/tolerated poorly.
- Rituximab can be considered for refractory antibody‑mediated cases (anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR) or relapsing disease.
- Cyclophosphamide is reserved for severe extramuscular organ involvement (e.g., ILD, severe myocarditis) or life‑threatening refractory disease.

Monitoring cadence and investigations
- Baseline: CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise transaminases), CBC, CMP (renal function especially before IVIG), glucose, TSH, infectious screens as indicated (HBV/HCV, TB if starting high‑level immunosuppression), ECG and troponin if cardiac symptoms.
- Inpatient: daily strength exam and CK every 24–72 hours while acutely ill. Swallow evaluation (speech/swallow therapist) before advancing diet if bulbar symptoms.
- Early outpatient: CK and clinical exam every 1–2 weeks until stable; then spacing to monthly as strength and CK normalize.
- Cardiac surveillance if troponin/proBNP abnormal or symptoms: echocardiogram ± cardiac MRI. Repeat if initial abnormalities were seen (this patient’s myocardium normalized on follow‑up imaging).
- Muscle MRI can help map active inflammation for biopsy; biopsy remains key when diagnosis is unclear.
- Report the reaction to pharmacovigilance systems (FDA MedWatch/EMA) and document in the chart to avoid rechallenge.

Supportive care: rehab, nutrition and prevention
- Early PT/OT and swallow therapy are essential; start mobilization as soon as medically feasible to limit deconditioning.
- Nutrition: consult dietitian; aim for adequate calories and protein (often 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day depending on renal function and catabolic state). Consider enteral support if dysphagia persists.
- Bone and gastric protection for steroid therapy: calcium, vitamin D, and consider bisphosphonate if prolonged steroids planned; PPI if GI risk factors.
- DVT prophylaxis while immobile; attention to skin and pressure‑sore prevention.
- Avoid aggressive exercise until CK normalizes and strength improves.

Mitigating infection risk (learned from the case)
- High‑dose steroids and immunotherapy increase infection risk. This patient developed pseudomonal urosepsis after hospital discharge.
- Strategies:
  - Avoid unnecessary indwelling urinary catheters; remove early.
  - Screen for occult infections before and during therapy (urine, chest, lines).
  - Consider PCP prophylaxis if prolonged high‑dose steroids (>20 mg prednisone daily for >4 weeks) or when adding other immunosuppressants.
  - Vaccinate when possible before immunosuppression (influenza, pneumococcus), and counsel about live vaccines (avoid on high‑level immunosuppression).
  - Close outpatient follow‑up in the first 2–4 weeks after discharge to pick up early infection signs.

Pitfalls and practical pearls
- Don’t assume every CK elevation after a new med is simple rhabdomyolysis — progressive weakness, bulbar symptoms, biopsy changes and persistent CK elevation suggest IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when the picture is unclear — IMNM classically shows necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage‑predominant infiltrates, and membrane attack complex deposition.
- Serology may be negative — up to ~1/3 of IMNM can be seronegative. Treat the patient, not the antibody panel.
- IVIG dosing: 2 g/kg total, and split dosing limits infusion reactions and volume issues. Check renal function and consider iso‑osmolar preparations in renal impairment.
- Rapid improvement after stopping the offending drug + brief immunotherapy favors a drug‑triggered phenotype; idiopathic IMNM often requires prolonged, multi‑agent therapy.
- Always document the suspected drug reaction clearly and counsel permanent avoidance of the implicated agent — rechallenge risks a severe relapse.

Key take‑home messages
- Suspect drug‑triggered IMNM when progressive proximal weakness and sustained CK elevation follow new medication exposure (including GLP‑1 RAs).
- For severe presentations (dysphagia, marked weakness, high/rapidly rising CK), start pulse methylprednisolone (1 g/day × 3–5), transition to prednisone ~1 mg/kg, and add IVIG early (2 g/kg split) if severe features present.
- If function and CK normalize quickly after trigger withdrawal and short immunotherapy, a rapid taper can be appropriate; if not, escalate with steroid‑sparing agents or rituximab per phenotype.
- Monitor closely (CK, strength, swallow, cardiac markers), prioritize early rehab and nutrition, and actively mitigate infection risk.
- Avoid rechallenge with the offending agent and report the adverse event to pharmacovigilance authorities.

This case gives a real‑world template: hit hard up front for severe, drug‑triggered IMNM, reassess frequently, taper fast when safe, and reserve long multi‑drug immunosuppression for refractory or antibody‑positive disease.
