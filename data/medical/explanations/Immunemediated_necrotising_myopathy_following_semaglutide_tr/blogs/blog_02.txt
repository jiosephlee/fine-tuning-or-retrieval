# The Seronegative Trap: Negative Myositis Panels Don’t Rule Out IMNM

What makes this case sharp is how it lures you into a false sense of security. A woman in her 60s develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12-fold rise in CK over months after a single dose of semaglutide. Her extended myositis serology — including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP — is negative, but muscle biopsy shows a necrotising inflammatory myopathy with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition. She improves rapidly after pulse steroids and IVIG and remains well off immunosuppression. This is IMNM — seronegative, steroid/IVIG-responsive, and potentially drug-associated.

Why this matters
- Up to one-third of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) patients are seronegative. A negative commercial myositis panel does not exclude a treatable, potentially severe autoimmune myopathy.
- Waiting for a “positive antibody” can meaningfully delay therapy in patients with progressive weakness, bulbar/respiratory involvement, or markedly elevated CK.
- Modern myositis testing is useful, but histology + clinical trajectory remain the diagnostic backbone for many patients.

Practical approach when SRP/HMGCR and myositis panels are negative

1) Trust the clinical picture first
If a patient has subacute–acute progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, very high CK (often >1000 U/L), and MRI evidence of proximal muscle edema — treat the patient, not the panel. IMNM commonly presents with prominent weakness and high CK; progression over weeks–months despite stopping a potential offending drug argues for autoimmune process rather than transient toxic rhabdomyolysis.

2) Use MRI to guide biopsy
MRI of the thighs (STIR or fluid-sensitive sequences) is extremely helpful. Target areas of active edema for biopsy — the chance of diagnostic yield increases when sampling MRI-hot muscles rather than atrophic, fatty-replaced muscles. In the presented case MRI showed symmetric proximal quadriceps edema, and biopsy from vastus lateralis confirmed the diagnosis.

3) Know the characteristic histology
IMNM classically shows:
- Scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibers
- Prominent macrophage infiltration and myophagocytosis (CD68+)
- Relative paucity of dense lymphocytic endomysial infiltrates (unlike polymyositis)
- Membrane attack complex (C5b-9) deposition on sarcolemma of necrotic and non-necrotic fibres
- Variable perivascular inflammation; CD3+ T cells may be rare

These features distinguish IMNM from simple toxic rhabdomyolysis (which lacks an inflammatory, complement-mediated picture) and from other inflammatory myopathies.

4) When to start immunosuppression despite negative serologies
Consider early immunotherapy (high-dose corticosteroids ± IVIG) when patients have:
- Rapidly progressive or severe proximal weakness impairing mobility or ADLs
- Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) or respiratory compromise
- Very high CK with biopsy or MRI supportive of inflammatory myopathy
- Failure to improve after withdrawal of a suspected offending agent and supportive histology
In the case above, pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone and one course of IVIG produced marked clinical and biochemical improvement; she was able to taper off corticosteroids and remain well.

5) Rule in/out drug association, but don’t be paralyzed by uncertainty
Some drugs (statins, rarely GLP-1 receptor agonists have been reported) can trigger or unmask IMNM. Distinguish drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis (acute CK spike that falls after withdrawal) from drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy (persistent/worsening weakness and CK despite stopping the drug). A temporal link supports causality but is not definitive. Rechallenge should usually be avoided.

6) Practical investigations to perform early
- CK, aldolase (if available), AST/ALT, renal function
- TSH, basic autoimmune screen (ANA), myositis panel (including HMGCR, SRP)
- MRI of muscles (STIR sequences)
- EMG if diagnosis unclear or to guide biopsy
- Targeted muscle biopsy (MRI-guided if possible)
- Baseline pulmonary and cardiac evaluation (IMNM can involve myocardium and predispose to arrhythmia)

Clinical pearls
- One-third of IMNM patients will be seronegative — negative antibodies do not rule out disease.
- MRI-targeted biopsy markedly increases diagnostic yield; sample an MRI-hot muscle.
- Histology in IMNM is necrosis + macrophages + complement deposition with scant lymphocytes.
- Severe features (bulbar/respiratory involvement, inability to walk, rapidly rising CK) are indications to start immunotherapy even if serologies are pending or negative.
- Distinguish drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (acute, reversible with withdrawal) from drug-triggered IMNM (progressive, may need immunosuppression).
- IVIG often has a central role in IMNM, particularly when HMGCR or SRP antibodies are present or when severe weakness/bulbar symptoms exist; it can be effective in seronegative patients too.

Take-home messages
- Negative myositis panels do not exclude IMNM; up to ~33% are seronegative.
- Use MRI to pick the biopsy site — target edema, not fatty-replaced muscle.
- Look for necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex on biopsy.
- Start steroids ± IVIG for rapidly progressive weakness, bulbar/respiratory involvement, or supportive biopsy even if serologies are negative.
- Consider drug triggers but avoid reflexively attributing progressive disease to transient toxicity; immunotherapy can be life- and function-saving.

In short: don’t fall into the seronegative trap. When the clinical story, CK, MRI and biopsy line up, act — antibodies are helpful, but they’re not the whole diagnosis.
