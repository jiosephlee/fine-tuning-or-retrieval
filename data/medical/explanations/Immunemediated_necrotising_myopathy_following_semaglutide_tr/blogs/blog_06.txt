Title: Blog about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

# From Blockbuster to Signal: Assessing Semaglutide as a Trigger Using WHO‑UMC

What makes this case interesting — and a little worrying — is the timing and phenotype. A woman in her 60s received a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide, developed an immediate intolerance with GI upset and AKI, then over the next 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, a CK that climbed into the thousands and a thigh muscle biopsy showing immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Myositis serologies (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative, and she improved after stopping semaglutide and receiving pulse steroids + IVIG. The authors apply WHO‑UMC causality criteria and judge the link “probable.” Below I walk through that reasoning in plain language and translate it to practical ED/clinic actions: what to look for, when to check CK, how to handle confounders (statins, supplements), how to report suspected adverse drug reactions, and how to think about re‑exposure.

## Applying the WHO‑UMC causality framework to this case — a quick readout

WHO‑UMC uses structured categories (certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable/unclassifiable) based on timing, plausible mechanism, alternative explanations, response to withdrawal (dechallenge) and re‑exposure (rechallenge).

- Temporal relationship: present. Symptoms began within hours (GI/AKI) and progressive myopathy developed over weeks–months after the single dose. A delayed immune phenomenon after an exposure can be biologically plausible.
- Dechallenge (withdrawal): supportive but confounded. The patient improved after semaglutide cessation and immunosuppression. Because immunotherapy (steroids + IVIG) was given, improvement is not purely spontaneous, but stopping the drug plus immunosuppression produced durable recovery.
- Alternative explanations: reasonably excluded. No statin exposure (important for HMGCR‑associated IMNM), negative cancer/ILD workup in the report, liver biopsy consistent with NAFLD predating semaglutide (less likely to explain the new phenotype). IMNM can be seronegative; absence of autoantibodies does not rule out autoimmune disease.
- Rechallenge: not done (and would be unethical given severity).

Putting it together: the WHO‑UMC fit here is “probable/likely” — reasonable timing, a response on withdrawal plus appropriate treatment, and no better alternative explanation. Not “certain” because no positive autoantibody, no spontaneous resolution on withdrawal alone, and rechallenge is absent.

That’s not proof of causation, but it’s a signal worth attention.

## What this means at the bedside / clinic — practical takeaways

1) Before you start a GLP‑1 RA (semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, etc.)
- Review baseline risk: prior autoimmune disease, prior myositis, unexplained CK elevations, current statin or red‑yeast‑rice/supplement use, significant muscle symptoms. If the patient has a known inflammatory myopathy, involve rheumatology before initiating.
- Consider baseline CK in patients at higher pretest probability (myalgias, prior CK elevation, on statin or other myotoxic meds). There are no universal guideline mandates — use clinical judgment.

2) What to counsel patients to watch for
- Progressive proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs), new or worsening dysphagia or dysphonia, new severe myalgias, dark urine, or inability to perform usual activities. GI side effects are common and expected; progressive weakness or bulbar symptoms are red flags.

3) When to check CK (practical rules)
- Check CK promptly if the patient reports:
  - New or progressive proximal weakness
  - Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia)
  - Severe diffuse myalgias or dark urine
- Consider urgent evaluation if CK is markedly elevated (e.g., >1000 U/L) or if there is rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory compromise, or bulbar involvement — these require prompt specialty input (neurology/rheum) and admission.
- In patients with only mild, intermittent myalgias and no functional impairment, reasonable to monitor and recheck CK if symptoms persist/worsen.

4) How polypharmacy (especially statins) clouds attribution
- Statins are a well‑described trigger for anti‑HMGCR IMNM; cessation alone often does not lead to immediate recovery, and immunosuppression is commonly required. If a patient on a statin develops myopathy on a GLP‑1 RA, both drugs could be contributors.
- Practical approach: hold the statin if severe myopathy is suspected while urgently evaluating (coordinate with cardiology/primary care regarding ischemic risk). Send anti‑HMGCR and myositis panels early. Expect that stopping a statin may not be sufficient if IMNM has developed.

5) Workup and early management pearls
- Basic labs: CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle can raise AST/ALT), creatinine, electrolytes, CRP/ESR, TSH. Obtain myositis autoantibodies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP when IMNM is suspected.
- Imaging/diagnostics: muscle MRI can localize inflammation; EMG may help. Muscle biopsy is diagnostic for IMNM (necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition).
- Early management: stop the suspected offending drug. If severe (high CK, bulbar symptoms, rapidly progressive weakness), involve rheumatology/neurology and start immunosuppression — many IMNM patients require steroids + steroid‑sparing agents and often IVIG. This reported patient received pulse steroids + IVIG and recovered.
- Cardiac evaluation: IMNM can involve the heart; consider troponin, ECG, echocardiogram, and cardiology input for palpitations/arrhythmia/troponin elevation.

6) Reporting suspected cases — make the signal count
- Report to your national pharmacovigilance system (FDA MedWatch in the U.S.; others internationally). Also notify the manufacturer and your hospital safety/quality office.
- What to include in a report: drug name, dose, lot number if available, exact timing of exposure, timeline of symptoms, CK and other labs, imaging/biopsy results, interventions (drug stopped, immunosuppression), and outcome.
- Even if causality is uncertain, report — single case reports feed detection of rare adverse events.

7) Is this a class effect? What about rechallenge?
- We don’t know. There are isolated reports of GLP‑1 RAs associated with muscle adverse events (including a tirzepatide‑associated rhabdomyolysis report), but causality and mechanism remain uncertain.
- Rechallenge after a severe IMNM is generally not recommended. Given the severity of IMNM and the ethical concern of provoking recurrence, avoid re‑exposure unless there is an extraordinarily compelling reason and a multidisciplinary plan (rheum/immunology, informed consent, close monitoring).
- If weight‑loss/diabetes control remains a priority, consider alternative non‑GLP‑1 strategies and discuss risks/benefits with the patient.

## Quick clinical pearls
- IMNM can be seronegative — a negative myositis panel does not exclude a biopsy‑proven autoimmune necrotising myopathy.
- Progressive proximal weakness or bulbar symptoms after starting a new medication should prompt CK testing and early specialty referral.
- In suspected drug‑related myopathy: stop the drug, check CK, consider autoantibodies, and involve rheumatology/neurology early — severe IMNM often needs steroids + IVIG.
- Report suspicious adverse events to pharmacovigilance authorities even when causality is not definitive; signal detection depends on clinicians reporting.

Bottom line: this case is not definitive proof that semaglutide causes IMNM, but it is a credible signal — temporally plausible, with biopsy‑proven disease and reasonable exclusion of alternatives — that meets WHO‑UMC criteria for “probable.” Clinicians should be alert to the red flags (progressive weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia), check CK promptly when indicated, consider confounders like statins, treat severe cases aggressively, and report suspected events so we can all learn faster.
