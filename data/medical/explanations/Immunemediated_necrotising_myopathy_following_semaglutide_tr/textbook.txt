Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

## Clinical Approach to Progressive Proximal Weakness After GLP‑1 Receptor Agonist Exposure

When a patient presents with subacute or progressive proximal muscle weakness after exposure to a new medication such as a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, the clinician must rapidly distinguish between an acute toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis and an immune‑mediated myopathy such as immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). The case above provides a useful anchor: a woman in her 60s developed nausea, vomiting and acute kidney injury within hours of a first semaglutide dose, and then over the ensuing four months developed progressive proximal weakness, new bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, dysphonia) and a 12‑fold rise in creatine kinase (CK) from a modest elevation to 3,992 U/L. That temporal pattern—initial intolerance followed by progressive weakness over weeks to months despite drug cessation—raises concern for an autoimmune necrotizing myopathy rather than a self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis.

Initial evaluation and red flags
Begin by assessing stability and identifying red flags that mandate urgent hospital-level care: rapidly progressive proximal weakness that impairs mobility, new bulbar signs (dysphagia or dysphonia) with aspiration risk, dyspnea or orthopnea suggesting respiratory muscle involvement, frank myoglobinuria or oliguria consistent with rhabdomyolysis with renal compromise, and markedly elevated CK with systemic instability. In the presented patient, the combination of progressive loss of independence, bulbar symptoms, and CK in the thousands prompted admission and expedited neurologic/rheumatologic assessment.

Medication, toxin and exposure history
A focused history is essential. Ask specifically about timing of symptom onset relative to the new drug (hours–days suggests direct toxicity; weeks–months suggests autoimmune activation), other myotoxic medications (especially statins), over‑the‑counter supplements (red yeast rice, certain mushrooms), recent infections, heavy exertion, alcohol or illicit substances, and prior liver enzyme elevations that might reflect chronic muscular disease. In this case there was no statin exposure or other usual triggers, and the proximate exposure was a single dose of semaglutide followed by progressive decline—an association that warrants consideration but not immediate proof of causality.

Targeted initial investigations
Early labs should include CK and/or aldolase, serum electrolytes and renal function (to assess for rhabdomyolysis and need for aggressive hydration), AST/ALT (remember that elevations may be of muscle rather than hepatic origin), urinalysis for myoglobin, ESR/CRP, and TSH (to exclude hypothyroid myopathy). If cardiopulmonary symptoms or elevated CK are present, check cardiac biomarkers (troponin, BNP) and obtain cardiac imaging as indicated—myocardial involvement can coexist with inflammatory myopathies. A myositis autoantibody panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) is appropriate early but may be negative; up to one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative. In the case described, CK rose from 337 U/L during the initial hospitalization to 3,992 U/L four months later, ESR/CRP were modestly elevated, and the myositis panel was negative—consistent with seronegative IMNM as a possibility.

Distinguishing drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis from IMNM
Although both entities can present with muscle weakness and CK elevations, there are clinical differences that guide early management:

- Timing and course: classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis presents acutely with rapid and marked CK elevation, often accompanied by myalgias, myoglobinuria and renal impairment, and improves promptly after stopping the offending agent and aggressive hydration. IMNM usually evolves subacutely over weeks to months, with progressive proximal weakness that does not resolve simply with drug withdrawal and often requires immunosuppression.

- Symptoms: IMNM frequently produces profound proximal weakness and may involve bulbar and respiratory muscles. Pain is variable. Rhabdomyolysis is often painful and systemic manifestations of muscle breakdown (dark urine, markedly elevated CK >10,000–100,000 U/L) and renal injury are common.

- Biomarkers and response: CK kinetics differ—acute rhabdomyolysis typically shows a sharp spike with rapid decline once precipitant removed; IMNM may show persistently elevated CK and progressive weakness despite stopping the drug. Moreover, IMNM demonstrates characteristic biopsy features (myofibre necrosis with macrophage predominance and membrane attack complex deposition) and frequently associates with myositis autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP), though not always.

Early imaging and electrophysiology
Muscle MRI is a sensitive noninvasive tool to localize active muscle inflammation and edema and to guide biopsy. Inflammatory myopathies including IMNM typically show symmetric proximal muscle edema on STIR sequences. Electromyography (EMG) supports a myopathic process and may reveal short duration, small amplitude motor unit potentials with early recruitment and irritability (fibrillation potentials), which are common in IMNM. Both studies contributed to the diagnostic workup in the presented patient (MRI showed proximal muscle edema; biopsy ultimately provided the diagnosis).

When to obtain muscle biopsy
If clinical, serologic and imaging data are inconclusive or if a definitive diagnosis will change management, perform a muscle biopsy. In IMNM the biopsy characteristically shows scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres with macrophage‑predominant inflammation, relatively few lymphocytes, and complement deposition on non‑necrotic fibres. These histologic patterns were seen in the case and helped distinguish IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies and from pure toxic necrosis.

Safety, disposition and urgent management priorities
Until the diagnosis is clarified, ensure airway safety and assess aspiration risk in patients with bulbar dysfunction; perform a swallow evaluation and consider NPO status or feeding support if aspiration is probable. Evaluate respiratory muscle strength with bedside vital capacity measurements or formal spirometry—rapid decline in forced vital capacity or signs of hypoventilation warrant ICU monitoring and ventilatory support. In suspected rhabdomyolysis, initiate aggressive intravenous fluids to protect renal function and monitor electrolytes closely; consider urinary alkalinization and consult nephrology if renal failure develops. Hold the suspected offending agent (in this case, the GLP‑1 receptor agonist) immediately.

Timing of immunosuppression
If IMNM is likely—especially when there is severe weakness, bulbar or respiratory involvement, or biopsy‑proven necrotizing myopathy—initiate immunotherapy without undue delay. High‑dose corticosteroids (eg, pulse methylprednisolone followed by prednisone 1 mg/kg/day) are the usual first step; many clinicians add IVIG early for severe disease or when anti‑HMGCR/anti‑SRP antibodies are present, particularly with bulbar weakness or treatment‑refractory cases. Steroid‑sparing agents (azathioprine, methotrexate, rituximab, mycophenolate) are considered for longer‑term control. In contrast, isolated drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis generally does not require immunosuppression and is managed with supportive care and drug withdrawal. In the reported patient, pulse steroids and IVIG were given with clinical improvement and sustained remission off immunosuppression—supportive of a drug‑associated IMNM phenotype that responded to relatively brief immunotherapy.

Broader differential and interpretive cautions
Always consider other causes of proximal weakness: dermatomyositis (look for characteristic rash), inclusion body myositis (more distal and slowly progressive, older men), metabolic myopathies, hypothyroid myopathy, neuromuscular junction disorders (myasthenia gravis causes fatigable weakness with bulbar involvement but usually without large CK elevations), and motor neuron disease (usually asymmetric, no CK spike). Interpret AST and ALT in the context of CK; muscle sources can account for transaminase elevation. Troponin and BNP elevations may reflect myocardial involvement in inflammatory myopathy and should prompt cardiology evaluation, including echocardiography and consideration of cardiac MRI, as was done in this patient.

Practical algorithm (clinical steps)
1. Assess airway, breathing, and circulation; evaluate swallowing and respiratory muscle function. Admit if bulbar/respiratory compromise, severe weakness, oliguria, or rapid CK rise.
2. Stop suspected offending agent and obtain urgent labs: CK, aldolase, BMP, electrolytes, renal function, AST/ALT, urinalysis for myoglobin, ESR/CRP, TSH, cardiac markers if indicated.
3. Initiate supportive measures: fluids and renal protection if rhabdomyolysis suspected; hold oral intake and arrange swallow study if dysphagia; monitor respiratory status.
4. Obtain muscle MRI and EMG as indicated to localize disease and guide biopsy.
5. Send myositis antibody panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) but do not await results before treating a severe inflammatory presentation.
6. If clinical/imaging/serologic data suggest IMNM or if there is progressive weakness despite drug withdrawal, start immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids with consideration of IVIG) and arrange muscle biopsy for definitive diagnosis.
7. Engage neurology, rheumatology and, where appropriate, nephrology and cardiology early.

Take‑home points anchored to the case
- Rapid recognition of red flags—progressive proximal weakness, new bulbar symptoms, functional decline and CK in the thousands—should trigger admission and expedited workup. In this patient the CK rose from 337 U/L during the initial hospitalization to 3,992 U/L four months after a single semaglutide dose, with progressive weakness and dysphagia prompting aggressive evaluation.
- Distinguish acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis (typically abrupt CK spike that improves with cessation and fluids) from IMNM (subacute–chronic progressive weakness despite drug withdrawal, biopsy‑proven necrosis and frequent need for immunotherapy).
- Early labs, MRI/EMG, myositis serologies and, if needed, muscle biopsy will separate these entities and guide therapy. If IMNM is likely and the patient is symptomatic with severe weakness or bulbar/respiratory involvement, begin immunosuppression promptly rather than waiting for serologies.
- In any suspected drug‑related myopathy, discontinue the culprit and report the adverse event. Given the increasing use of GLP‑1 receptor agonists, maintain a high index of suspicion and involve multidisciplinary teams early when clinical features are severe or progressive.

## Pathophysiology and Immunology of Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy

Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinicopathologic entity among the inflammatory myopathies in which the dominant lesion is myofibre necrosis with brisk regeneration rather than a dense lymphocytic infiltrate. Clinically this translates into a rapidly progressive, often severe proximal-predominant weakness with frequent bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and potential respiratory or cardiac complications. The case presented—an older woman who developed subacute, progressive proximal weakness with markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) and a muscle biopsy showing widespread necrosis with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition—illustrates many of the cardinal features of IMNM and provides a useful entry point for discussing its immunopathology.

Histopathology and cellular players
The histologic hallmark of IMNM is scattered or clustered necrotic myofibres with abundant regenerating fibres and prominent macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of necrotic debris. Unlike classic polymyositis, there is generally a relative paucity of endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion of otherwise intact myofibres; lymphocytes may be present but are typically sparse and perivascular. Immunohistochemistry characteristically shows deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on sarcolemma of necrotic and some non‑necrotic fibres, implicating complement activation as a proximal mediator of muscle injury. Macrophages (CD68+) are abundant and appear to be the primary effector cells removing necrotic fibres; they may also contribute to local inflammation through cytokine production. These features distinguish IMNM from other myopathies on muscle biopsy and point toward a humoral/complement‑driven process rather than a purely cell‑mediated cytotoxic T‑cell attack.

Autoantibody-defined subsets and their implications
IMNM is heterogeneous serologically. Two autoantibodies are most consistently associated with clinicopathologic IMNM: anti‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase (anti‑HMGCR) and anti‑signal recognition particle (anti‑SRP). Anti‑HMGCR myopathy is strongly linked to prior statin exposure in many, but not all, patients; anti‑SRP disease tends to present with severe weakness, often at a younger age, and is associated with a more refractory course and higher rates of extramuscular involvement, including cardiomyopathy. Both antibody subsets are associated with high CKs and the same necrotizing histology with complement deposition, supporting a mechanistic role for circulating autoantibodies. Approximately one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for these known specificities, which presents diagnostic and mechanistic uncertainty and suggests that additional autoantigens or non‑antibody mediated pathways remain to be identified.

Mechanisms of muscle injury
Evidence from biopsy findings and experimental models supports a model in which humoral immunity—autoantibodies binding to muscle antigens—triggers complement activation and formation of the membrane attack complex, resulting in sarcolemmal injury and myofibre necrosis. Macrophages are then recruited to phagocytose necrotic fibres and to sustain local inflammation. In anti‑HMGCR disease there is a plausible feed‑forward mechanism: statins upregulate HMGCR expression in regenerating muscle, increasing antigen availability and potentially breaking peripheral tolerance in predisposed individuals. Anti‑SRP antibodies may similarly target components of the protein translocation machinery. The presence of MAC on non‑necrotic fibres and the rapid clinical response to therapies that interfere with humoral immunity (IVIG, plasmapheresis, B‑cell depletion) in many patients provide further circumstantial support for antibody‑ and complement‑mediated pathogenesis. T cells are less prominent in IMNM than in polymyositis, but they may still play modulatory roles, and macrophage‑derived cytokines are important drivers of local tissue damage and repair.

Triggers: drugs, infections and unmasking of autoimmunity
A key clinical feature of IMNM is its association, in many patients, with identifiable triggers. Statins are the prototype—statin exposure is strongly associated with anti‑HMGCR positivity and the development of a necrotizing myopathy that often will not remit merely with statin cessation. Other drugs and infections have been reported as temporal antecedents in individual cases. The biological mechanisms by which drugs trigger IMNM probably vary: direct myocyte toxicity or metabolic stress can cause necrosis and antigen release (creating a substrate for autoimmunization); agents that modify antigen expression (for example, statin‑induced upregulation of HMGCR on regenerating fibres) can increase immune recognition; and certain agents may act as adjuvants that enhance immune activation or break tolerance. In a subset of patients, an as yet unidentified antigen or non‑antibody mediated mechanism may be responsible—this is the likely explanation for seronegative IMNM.

Potential relationship to GLP‑1 receptor agonists
Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) such as semaglutide have become widely used for diabetes and obesity and have recognized adverse effects that include gastrointestinal symptoms and weight loss; recent reports suggest they may also be associated with loss of skeletal muscle mass and, in isolated cases, rhabdomyolysis. Whether GLP‑1 RAs can trigger true immune‑mediated myopathies is not established. Several biological hypotheses could explain how a GLP‑1 RA might precipitate IMNM in a susceptible host: (1) direct or idiosyncratic myocyte injury from the drug or its metabolic effects leading to antigen release and secondary autoimmune sensitization; (2) metabolic stress from severe nausea, vomiting, dehydration and catabolism (as occurred in the case) increasing muscle turnover and antigen exposure; (3) adjuvant effects on innate immunity that favor loss of tolerance; or (4) unmasking of pre‑existing subclinical autoimmunity. The case under discussion—onset hours to days after a single semaglutide dose with subsequent progressive weakness, biopsy‑proven necrotizing myopathy with MAC deposition, seronegativity for known IMNM autoantibodies, and improvement after immunotherapy and drug avoidance—raises the possibility of a drug‑triggered IMNM, but causality remains unproven and mechanistic data are lacking.

How IMNM differs from toxic rhabdomyolysis and other inflammatory myopathies
Differentiating IMNM from toxic rhabdomyolysis, polymyositis, dermatomyositis and inclusion body myositis is important because management and prognosis differ. Toxic rhabdomyolysis (from drugs, exertion, ischemia, toxins) produces abrupt, often extreme CK elevations, muscle pain, myoglobinuria and risk of acute kidney injury. Morphologically it shows widespread necrosis but typically lacks complement deposition and the chronic regenerative pattern with macrophage predominance seen in IMNM; the clinical course is usually acute with rapid CK fall after withdrawal of the offending agent and supportive care, and immunosuppression is not required. Polymyositis is characterized by endomysial CD8+ T‑cell invasion of non‑necrotic fibres and diffuse MHC‑I upregulation, pointing to T‑cell mediated cytotoxicity; dermatomyositis features complement‑mediated microangiopathy with perifascicular atrophy and characteristic skin findings. Inclusion body myositis has a chronic, often asymmetric distal pattern with rimmed vacuoles and concomitant degenerative features. IMNM occupies a distinct niche where humoral and complement pathways predominate, causing a necrotizing but potentially immunotherapy‑responsive myopathy.

Clinical and therapeutic implications
Recognition of the immunopathology has practical consequences. Because antibody‑ and complement‑mediated mechanisms are central in many cases, therapies targeting humoral immunity—high‑dose corticosteroids, IVIG, plasmapheresis, and B‑cell targeted agents such as rituximab—are commonly used and often effective. The clinical course can differ by subtype: anti‑SRP disease is frequently severe and refractory; anti‑HMGCR disease may be statin‑associated and often requires sustained immunosuppression despite statin withdrawal; drug‑associated cases that are truly induced by a medication may remit after withdrawal plus a shorter immunosuppressive course. The presence of cardiac or respiratory involvement (as can occur in IMNM) mandates vigilant monitoring because myocarditis or diaphragmatic weakness can be life‑threatening.

Practical approach informed by immunopathology
In a patient with subacute proximal weakness and high CK, consider IMNM when biopsy demonstrates necrosis with macrophage predominance and MAC deposition but few lymphocytes. Obtain myositis autoantibodies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP; if these are negative, recognize the possibility of seronegative IMNM and rely on histology and clinical course. Review medication exposures carefully—statins are classic, but any new drug that causes metabolic stress or muscle injury deserves attention. When the presentation is consistent with IMNM, early institution of immunotherapy (often corticosteroids plus IVIG) is reasonable, especially when there is marked weakness, bulbar or respiratory involvement, or cardiac suspicion; the favorable response to such therapies in many patients supports the central role of humoral and complement mechanisms.

In summary, IMNM is a principally antibody‑ and complement‑mediated myopathy characterized by macrophage‑rich necrosis and regeneration. Known autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) define important subgroups with different clinical associations and prognoses, but a sizable seronegative population points to additional triggers and antigens yet to be discovered. Drug exposures can act as precipitants through several plausible mechanisms; the case described here raises the possibility that GLP‑1 receptor agonists may, in rare instances, be associated with IMNM, but further investigation is required to move from association to causation.

## Differential Diagnosis of Subacute Proximal Weakness and CK Elevation

When a patient presents with subacute (weeks to months) proximal muscle weakness accompanied by creatine kinase (CK) elevation, the diagnostic approach should proceed from broad categories—myopathic versus neurogenic causes—and then refine by tempo, associated systemic features, exposure history, serology, electrodiagnostics, imaging and, if needed, muscle biopsy. The case described here—an older woman who developed progressive proximal weakness with bulbar symptoms and a large rise in CK over four months following a single dose of semaglutide—illustrates how these discriminators are used in practice.

Immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
IMNM is an inflammatory myopathy classically characterized by subacute, frequently severe proximal weakness, prominent CK elevation, and pathologic necrotic and regenerating fibers with relatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates and abundant macrophages/myophagocytosis. Complement-mediated (membrane attack complex) deposition on myofibers is common. Clinically, IMNM may include bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and can be associated with specific autoantibodies—most commonly anti-HMGCR (often statin-associated) and anti-SRP—or be seronegative. MRI of affected muscles typically shows diffuse or patchy muscle edema. Because IMNM reflects an autoimmune process, it frequently requires immunosuppressive therapy; many patients respond to high‑dose corticosteroids, IVIG, and steroid‑sparing agents, although refractory disease can occur. In the presented case, the combination of progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement, MRI muscle edema, biopsy showing necrosis with macrophages and membrane attack complex, and clinical improvement after corticosteroids and IVIG supports IMNM as the leading diagnosis despite negative myositis antibodies.

Toxic/drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and toxic myopathies
Drug-induced muscle injury ranges from acute rhabdomyolysis to subacute toxic myopathy. Classic rhabdomyolysis is abrupt, often painful, may produce myoglobinuria and very high CK values (often in the tens of thousands), and typically improves rapidly after withdrawal of the offending agent and supportive care. Histology may show necrosis without the inflammatory features (macrophage predominance, complement deposition) that suggest an immune-mediated process. Common culprits include statins (especially in combination with interacting drugs), fibrates, illicit substances, severe electrolyte derangements, and, less well established, some newer metabolic agents. A recently reported tirzepatide case showed a typical toxic rhabdomyolysis course with rapid CK decline after drug discontinuation. In contrast, the protracted rise in CK and ongoing weakness in our patient over months after a single semaglutide dose argues against a simple toxic rhabdomyolysis and favors an immune‑mediated process.

Polymyositis, dermatomyositis and antisynthetase syndrome
These idiopathic inflammatory myopathies often present with subacute proximal weakness and elevated CK, but there are distinguishing clinical and histologic features. Dermatomyositis classically has cutaneous findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules) and a perifascicular atrophy on biopsy. Antisynthetase syndrome often presents with interstitial lung disease, mechanic’s hands, and antisynthetase (Jo‑1 and related) antibodies. Polymyositis is now less commonly diagnosed and overlaps with IMNM and other entities; it typically shows endomysial CD8+ T cell–mediated fiber injury. Identification of characteristic skin signs, interstitial lung disease, and specific autoantibodies helps distinguish these syndromes. Muscle biopsy patterns—perifascicular atrophy, prominent lymphocytic endomysial infiltrates—contrast with the necrotizing, macrophage‑predominant picture seen in IMNM.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM)
IBM should be considered in older adults (usually >50 years) with an insidious, slowly progressive course, but its pattern is distinctive: asymmetric weakness with early involvement of finger flexors and quadriceps, frequent dysphagia, and poor or absent response to immunotherapy. CK elevation is usually modest. Muscle biopsy typically shows rimmed vacuoles, filamentous inclusions by electron microscopy, and endomysial inflammation. The rapidity and symmetry of decline in our patient, the biopsy without rimmed vacuoles, and the steroid responsiveness make IBM unlikely.

Endocrine and metabolic myopathies
Thyroid dysfunction, hypercortisolism/Cushing’s syndrome, electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia), and certain inherited metabolic myopathies can cause proximal weakness and variable CK elevations. Endocrine causes more often produce chronic, symmetric weakness with modest CK elevations and typically improve with correction of the underlying endocrine disorder. Thyroid function tests, cortisol evaluation or an assessment for electrolyte disturbances are essential early steps, particularly when the history or exam is suggestive. In this case the TSH was normal and there was no clinical evidence pointing to an endocrine etiology.

Critical illness myopathy and neuropathy
Critical illness myopathy (CIM) and critical illness polyneuropathy occur in the setting of prolonged ICU stays, sepsis, severe multiorgan failure, or exposure to high‑dose corticosteroids and neuromuscular blocking agents. Patients show diffuse weakness and difficulty weaning from the ventilator; CK may be normal or mildly elevated. EMG and nerve conduction studies (NCS) are useful to identify the myopathic versus neuropathic patterns typical of CIM/CIP. Although our patient required a brief ICU admission early in her course, her weakness progressed for months after discharge rather than arising as a direct complication of prolonged critical illness, making CIM/CIP less likely.

Neuromuscular junction disorders
Disorders such as myasthenia gravis cause fatigable weakness with a characteristic diurnal fluctuation, prominent ocular and bulbar involvement, and typically normal CK levels. Electrophysiology (repetitive nerve stimulation, single-fiber EMG) and serum acetylcholine receptor or MuSK antibodies are diagnostic. Bulbar symptoms may overlap with IMNM, but the coexistence of markedly elevated CK and inflammatory biopsy findings in our patient point away from a primary NMJ disorder.

Cardiac considerations
When CK and cardiac biomarkers (troponin) are elevated in a patient with myopathy, concomitant myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or coronary ischemia must be considered. Myositis spectrum disorders—particularly some forms of IMNM and anti-SRP–associated disease—can involve the myocardium. Cardiac MRI helps distinguish myocarditis (myocardial edema, late gadolinium enhancement) from ischemia or stress cardiomyopathy. In this case, modest troponin elevation, proBNP elevation and cardiac MRI findings compatible with myocardial edema raised concern for myocardial involvement, which resolved with immunotherapy—the finding is congruent with inflammatory myopathy rather than isolated cardiac ischemia.

Hepatic transaminases: muscle versus liver source
AST and ALT are present in both liver and muscle; AST is more abundant in muscle. In patients with elevated CK, transaminase elevations may partially reflect muscle injury. Discerning the source requires correlating CK trends with AST/ALT, measuring GGT and bilirubin (which are liver‑specific), and hepatic imaging or biopsy if liver disease is suspected. In this patient, longstanding hepatic steatosis documented on prior biopsy explains persistent transaminase abnormalities that did not parallel CK normalization; thus the transaminase elevations were attributed chiefly to hepatic disease rather than ongoing muscle breakdown.

Practical diagnostic approach
Initial evaluation should include a careful drug and exposure history (statins, fibrates, herbal supplements, recent new medications), a targeted autoimmune myositis panel (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP), TSH and basic metabolic panel (electrolytes), urinalysis for myoglobinuria, and cardiac evaluation (ECG, troponin, echocardiogram) if indicated. Electromyography typically demonstrates myopathic motor units with early recruitment and fibrillation potentials in active disease, supporting inflammatory myopathy. MRI of affected regions is sensitive for active muscle edema and can guide biopsy sites. Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when noninvasive testing is inconclusive; interpretation requires recognition of patterns—necrotizing myopathy with macrophage predominance and complement deposition (IMNM) versus necrosis without inflammation (toxic rhabdomyolysis) versus perifascicular atrophy or rimmed vacuoles (dermatomyositis, IBM).

Applying these principles to the case
The temporal sequence (progressive weakness and rising CK over months after exposure), bulbar involvement, MRI evidence of muscle edema, biopsy showing necrotic/regenerating fibers with macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex, and clinical improvement after immunosuppression and avoidance of the putative offending medication all favor an IMNM-like process provoked or unmasked by semaglutide exposure. Alternative explanations—acute toxic rhabdomyolysis, IBM, endocrine myopathy or CIM—are less consistent with the clinical tempo, histopathology and therapeutic response. Although seronegative status leaves some diagnostic uncertainty and causality to semaglutide cannot be conclusively proven, the constellation of findings outlines a rational differential and illustrates how integrating history, serology, electrodiagnostics, imaging and biopsy directs both diagnosis and management.

## Diagnostic Workup and Causality Assessment in Suspected Drug-Associated IMNM

When faced with a patient who develops subacute, progressive proximal weakness and marked CK elevation after exposure to a new medication, clinicians must pursue a systematic diagnostic workup that both establishes the presence and subtype of an inflammatory myopathy and assesses whether the drug is a likely trigger. The case above — a woman in her 60s who developed progressive dysphagia, proximal weakness and a 12‑fold rise in CK after a single dose of semaglutide — illustrates how a stepwise approach weaves clinical, laboratory, imaging, electrophysiologic and histopathologic data together with a structured causality assessment to reach a defensible conclusion.

Initial laboratory evaluation should include muscle enzymes and inflammatory markers, and should be repeated serially to follow disease activity. Serum CK is the most useful biomarker in IMNM; aldolase, AST and ALT are often elevated as well and may rise in parallel with CK. Because AST and ALT can derive from skeletal muscle, always interpret transaminase elevations in the context of contemporaneous CK. ESR and CRP are commonly elevated but nonspecific and may help track systemic inflammation. Baseline thyroid function tests and basic metabolic panels help exclude endocrine and metabolic contributors to weakness. In patients with potential cardiac involvement, obtain cardiac biomarkers (prefer troponin I over troponin T where available, because troponin T can be falsely elevated with skeletal muscle disease) and natriuretic peptides; abnormal cardiac testing should prompt cardiac imaging. In the case patient, CK rose from a modest elevation on initial admission to nearly 4,000 U/L over months, and simultaneous AST/ALT elevations required correlation with CK and liver imaging/biopsy to determine hepatic versus muscle origin.

Autoantibody testing for myositis is a central element of the diagnostic workup but must be interpreted cautiously. A comprehensive myositis panel should include antibodies most closely linked to IMNM (anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP) and the broader myositis-specific and -associated antibodies (antisynthetase antibodies, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP2, etc.). Importantly, up to roughly one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative; a negative panel therefore does not exclude the diagnosis. Anti-HMGCR is strongly associated with statin exposure but can be seen without it; anti-SRP is associated with a more severe, often cardiac‑involved phenotype. In our patient the myositis panel was negative, a reminder that seronegativity is common and that final diagnosis rests on clinicopathologic correlation.

Muscle MRI has become an indispensable, noninvasive tool in suspected inflammatory myopathy. Fluid-sensitive sequences (STIR/T2) typically show symmetric edema of affected proximal muscle groups in active disease and can identify optimal biopsy sites by highlighting areas with active inflammation or necrosis. The pattern of involvement (for example, predominant paraspinal/pelvic girdle versus distal or asymmetric involvement) helps narrow the differential. In the case presented, MRI showed symmetric proximal thigh edema that guided the vastus lateralis biopsy and supported a diagnosis of active myositis.

Electrodiagnostic testing (EMG/NCS) is helpful but not pathognomonic. EMG in IMNM generally demonstrates an “irritable” myopathic pattern with short‑duration, low‑amplitude motor unit potentials, early recruitment, and frequent spontaneous activity such as fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves. These findings support a myopathic process and can help exclude neuropathic disorders, but they cannot reliably distinguish IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies or toxic myopathies. Because immunotherapy can blunt EMG abnormalities, perform electrodiagnostics before starting high‑dose steroids if clinically feasible.

Muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard and is crucial when antibody testing is negative or when a drug‑induced mechanism is suspected. Histopathology in IMNM characteristically shows myofibre necrosis and regeneration with abundant macrophage (CD68+) infiltration and myophagocytosis, variable acid phosphatase activity, and deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on myofibre membranes. In contrast to polymyositis or dermatomyositis, lymphocytic infiltrates are typically sparse; only rare CD3+ T cells and perivascular inflammatory cells may be present. Upregulation of MHC class I can be variable. These features — prominent necrosis with macrophages and scant lymphocytes — were documented in our patient’s vastus lateralis biopsy and are strongly supportive of IMNM rather than a purely toxic or metabolic myopathy. Immunostains for CD68 and complement, and careful assessment for inflammatory patterns and vasculopathy, provide additional mechanistic clues.

Extramuscular evaluation is guided by presenting symptoms and by known associations of myositis subtypes. Dysphagia merits prompt instrumental assessment (modified barium swallow or videofluoroscopic study) and measurement of respiratory muscle strength (forced vital capacity, maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures) because respiratory compromise can be life‑threatening. Cardiac screening — ECG and echocardiogram — should be performed when there are cardiac symptoms, arrhythmias or elevated cardiac biomarkers; cardiac MRI is valuable for detecting myocarditis and for tissue characterization. Screening for interstitial lung disease with pulmonary function tests and high‑resolution chest CT is indicated in patients with cough, dyspnea, hypoxia, or positive antisynthetase antibodies. Age‑appropriate cancer screening should be considered, especially in older adults, because some inflammatory myopathies have paraneoplastic associations, though that association is less well defined in IMNM than in certain dermatomyositis phenotypes.

Because many systemic illnesses and exposures can produce muscle injury, a broad differential must be considered and excluded. Statin exposure and other myotoxic agents (including certain supplements, antivirals, or illicit drugs), viral infections, critical illness myopathy, endocrine disorders (hypothyroidism, Cushing’s), electrolyte disturbances, metabolic myopathies, and metabolic liver disease all can raise CK and cause weakness. A careful medication and supplement history is essential; obtaining prior CK measurements (if available) helps determine whether the myopathy is new or smouldering. In the reported patient, the absence of statin use, the temporal relationship to semaglutide, the biopsy pattern and clinical course argued against many alternative etiologies.

Interpreting liver tests in the setting of a myopathy requires triangulation of CK, imaging and, when needed, histology. Skeletal muscle breakdown commonly elevates AST out of proportion to ALT, but both transaminases can be raised. If CK is substantially elevated, assume some component is muscle‑derived until proven otherwise. Persistent transaminase elevation after CK normalization or imaging evidence of hepatic steatosis warrants hepatic evaluation; in this case the patient had prior biopsy‑proven NAFLD that predated the myopathy, which supported a hepatic origin for the chronic transaminase elevations.

After the diagnostic pieces are in place, a structured causality assessment helps determine whether the drug is a likely trigger. The WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality categories provide a pragmatic framework: causality is judged as certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, or unassessable/unclassifiable. Key elements include a reasonable temporal relationship between drug exposure and symptom onset, biologic plausibility, exclusion of alternative explanations, objective evidence linking the drug to the adverse event (e.g., biopsy compatible with an immune mechanism), and a positive dechallenge (improvement after drug withdrawal). Rechallenge (symptom recurrence on re‑exposure) strongly favors causality but is usually unethical in severe immune‑mediated reactions. In our patient, the rapid onset of gastrointestinal and renal side effects within hours of the first semaglutide dose followed by progressive weakness over months establishes a plausible temporal sequence; the muscle MRI and biopsy provided objective evidence of an immune necrotising process; other causes (statins, chronic viral hepatitis, endocrine disease) were not identified; and the patient improved clinically and biochemically after corticosteroids/IVIG and, importantly, with continued avoidance of semaglutide. These attributes fit the WHO‑UMC category of “probable/likely” rather than “certain.”

Practical points that follow from this case and the broader literature include: obtain imaging and, if possible, biopsy prior to prolonged high‑dose immunosuppression because therapy can alter histopathologic and electrodiagnostic findings; use MRI to direct biopsy to the most inflamed region; recognize that lack of myositis‑specific antibodies does not exclude IMNM and should not delay biopsy when clinical suspicion is high; use troponin I when assessing possible cardiac involvement to avoid misattribution of troponin T elevations to myocarditis; and document dechallenge rigorously — serial CK and strength measurements following drug withdrawal are powerful evidence. Finally, clinicians should report suspected serious drug‑associated myopathies to pharmacovigilance authorities so that rare associations can be identified and studied.

In sum, diagnosis of suspected drug‑associated IMNM rests on synthesis of clinical timing, serial biomarkers, targeted imaging, electrodiagnostics, and muscle histopathology; causality is best assessed using a structured approach such as the WHO‑UMC criteria. The presented case — seronegative but biopsy‑proven IMNM with MRI‑guided biopsy and clinical/biochemical improvement after immunotherapy and semaglutide avoidance — exemplifies how these elements combine to support a probable drug association while acknowledging the limits of certainty.

## Management of IMNM: Immunotherapy, Supportive Care, and Comorbidity Considerations

The goals of management in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are to halt immune‑mediated muscle injury, preserve and restore muscle function, protect the airway and respiratory muscles, prevent treatment complications, and address comorbidities that influence long‑term outcomes. Treatment decisions are driven by severity at presentation (degree of proximal weakness, presence of bulbar or respiratory involvement, very high CK), autoantibody status (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP when present), and whether a drug trigger is suspected. Drug‑associated IMNM may behave differently than idiopathic, autoantibody‑positive disease and, as in the case described, can sometimes respond to a shorter course of immunotherapy after withdrawal of the culprit agent.

Initial immunotherapy
High‑dose glucocorticoids remain the usual first‑line therapy for moderately severe to severe IMNM. A commonly used regimen is intravenous methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg daily for 3–5 days (pulse therapy) for patients with marked weakness, bulbar dysfunction, or impending respiratory compromise, followed by oral prednisone approximately 1 mg/kg/day (typical adult range 60–80 mg/day) with adjustment for comorbidities and body weight. The aim of initial high‑dose therapy is to rapidly reduce inflammatory activity; clinical and biochemical response usually guides the pace of taper.

Adjunctive IVIG for severe disease or steroid‑resistance
IVIG is an established early adjunct in IMNM, especially when weakness is severe, when bulbar or respiratory muscles are affected, when rapid improvement is needed, or when there is inadequate response to steroids. A typical dosing strategy is 2 g/kg total (ideal body weight or adjusted body weight may be used in obesity), given over 2–5 days. Some patients require repeated monthly IVIG cycles for several months until sustained improvement is achieved. In anti‑HMGCR IMNM and many anti‑SRP cases, IVIG frequently produces substantive clinical benefit and may be required long‑term; in suspected drug‑induced cases, a single course plus steroid therapy can be sufficient, as illustrated by the patient who improved markedly after pulse steroids and a single IVIG cycle.

Steroid‑sparing and escalation strategies
Because prolonged high‑dose glucocorticoids carry substantial morbidity, steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants are commonly introduced early (often within weeks) or when steroid tapering is attempted:

- Conventional steroid‑sparing agents: methotrexate (typically 15–25 mg weekly, with folate supplementation), azathioprine (2–3 mg/kg/day after TPMT testing), and mycophenolate mofetil (1–1.5 g twice daily). Choice is individualized based on comorbidities (hepatic or renal dysfunction), fertility considerations, monitoring needs, and clinician familiarity.
- Biologic therapy: rituximab (anti‑CD20) is frequently used for refractory disease, severe anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR IMNM that fails conventional agents, or when rapid B‑cell depletion is desired. Other agents (cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus) have been used in refractory cases.
- Treatment duration: for idiopathic/autoantibody‑positive IMNM, many patients require prolonged combination immunosuppression (months to years) and slow tapering to avoid relapse. In suspected drug‑induced IMNM, shorter courses may suffice once the offending drug is stopped; the clinical course should determine length of therapy.

Monitoring: clinical and laboratory follow‑up
Close, systematic monitoring guides therapy and detects complications:

- Strength and function: document baseline proximal muscle strength, timed tests (e.g., timed up‑and‑go, 30‑second sit‑to‑stand), and activities of daily living. Reassess frequently—at least weekly during the acute inpatient phase and then every 2–4 weeks during early outpatient management.
- CK and other labs: obtain CK at baseline and serially (e.g., twice weekly while changing therapy, then weekly until stable). AST/ALT should be interpreted in context of muscle injury versus hepatic disease. Monitor CBC, renal function, hepatic panel, and glucose during steroid therapy and while on steroid‑sparing agents.
- Respiratory assessment: measure forced vital capacity (FVC), maximal inspiratory force (MIF/NIF) and oxygenation when there is any suggestion of respiratory muscle involvement. Frequent reassessment is essential in patients with bulbar symptoms or declining FVC; consider ICU transfer and ventilatory support if FVC falls below thresholds or respiratory failure is imminent.
- Swallowing and airway protection: early bedside swallow assessment and prompt referral to speech‑language pathology are mandatory in patients with dysphagia or dysphonia; consider instrumental evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow study or FEES) when indicated.
- Cardiac surveillance: IMNM can be associated with myocarditis or arrhythmia. Obtain ECG and troponin at baseline; perform transthoracic echocardiography for suspected involvement and consider cardiac MRI if myocarditis is suspected. Repeat imaging as clinically indicated.
- Infection surveillance: leukocyte counts and clinical vigilance for infection. The case underscores the real risk: the patient developed a severe pseudomonal UTI while receiving immunosuppression.

Safety, infection prevention, and other prophylaxis
Reducing therapy‑related harm is an essential component of management.

- Vaccinations: review and update immunizations prior to initiating long‑term immunosuppression when feasible. Give inactivated influenza, pneumococcal vaccines, recombinant zoster (non‑live) vaccine, and COVID‑19 vaccines per guidelines. Live vaccines should be avoided during significant immunosuppression.
- Screening before biologics or prolonged immunosuppression: test for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C, and HIV as indicated. Treat or manage latent infections before starting certain agents.
- PJP prophylaxis: consider prophylaxis (trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole single‑strength daily or three times weekly) when prednisone is expected to exceed ~20 mg/day for more than 4 weeks or when multiple immunosuppressive agents are used in combination.
- Bone and metabolic protection: initiate calcium and vitamin D, and consider bisphosphonate therapy for patients expected to have prolonged glucocorticoid exposure; monitor glucose and blood pressure closely.
- Infection recognition and early treatment: educate patients and caregivers about fever, urinary symptoms, respiratory symptoms and wound care; have a low threshold for evaluation and empiric treatment of infections in immunosuppressed patients.
- IVIG cautions: monitor renal function and hydration when giving high‑dose IVIG (risk of renal dysfunction and thrombosis), and weigh thrombotic risk (especially in the context of prior thromboembolism).

Rehabilitation, nutrition, and supportive care
Medical therapy should be paired with multidisciplinary supportive measures to restore function and prevent complications of immobility and malnutrition.

- Early physical and occupational therapy: begin gentle range‑of‑motion and bedside mobility as soon as medically safe, progressing to supervised strengthening and endurance training as strength returns. Avoid excessive early exertion that could worsen weakness or rhabdomyolysis; individualized progression improves outcomes.
- Swallowing therapy and nutritional support: speech‑language pathology for swallow retraining; consider dietary modification, texture changes, and aggressive calorie/protein support. For severe, persistent dysphagia with aspiration risk, enteral feeding (nasogastric or gastrostomy tube) may be necessary.
- Thrombosis prevention: immobile patients are at increased risk for deep venous thrombosis—implement mechanical prophylaxis and chemical prophylaxis per institutional protocols unless contraindicated.
- Psychological and social support: coordinate case management, address loss of independence, and arrange home modifications or rehabilitation facility placement when necessary.

Comorbidity management and medication counseling
IMNM management must incorporate safe strategies for treating coexisting metabolic disease and cardiovascular risk while avoiding agents that may have precipitated disease.

- Avoid re‑exposure to suspected triggers: when a temporal relationship implicates a drug (such as a GLP‑1 receptor agonist in this case), patients should be advised to avoid that class. Rechallenge is generally ill‑advised given potential for recurrence or worsening.
- Diabetes and obesity alternatives: in patients who must avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists, consider evidence‑based alternatives tailored to the patient’s comorbidities:
  - Lifestyle therapy: structured diet, exercise, and behavioral interventions remain foundational for weight loss and NAFLD management.
  - Antihyperglycemics: metformin is first‑line for type 2 diabetes if not contraindicated; sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 (SGLT2) inhibitors confer cardiovascular and renal benefits and are weight‑neutral or modestly weight‑reducing; DPP‑4 inhibitors are weight‑neutral and generally safe but less potent for weight loss; insulin should be used when necessary to control hyperglycemia.
  - Anti‑obesity pharmacotherapy alternatives: options such as naltrexone‑bupropion or orlistat may be considered, but selection should be individualized and reviewed with endocrinology.
  - Surgical options: bariatric referral for eligible patients is effective for durable weight loss and improvement in NAFLD/NASH.
- Lipid management: in anti‑HMGCR IMNM (or when statins are suspected triggers), statins are typically avoided during active disease and often permanently if autoantibody‑positive. Alternative lipid‑lowering strategies include ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors, which can be effective and are not associated with HMGCR autoantibody production.
- Liver disease (NAFLD/NASH): aggressive control of metabolic syndrome components (weight loss, glycemic control, blood pressure management, lipid optimization) is central to slowing progression. Coordinate care with hepatology when histologic disease is advanced.

When to escalate or de‑escalate therapy
Decisions to escalate therapy (adding another immunosuppressant, repeating IVIG, or starting rituximab) are driven by lack of clinical improvement after an appropriate trial of steroids and IVIG, continued steroid dependence, rising CK and progressive weakness, or significant relapse. Conversely, in cases where a drug trigger is removed and clinical plus biochemical indices rapidly normalize—particularly when a single course of IVIG plus a limited steroid taper produces sustained remission—longer‑term immunosuppression may not be required. Idiopathic or autoantibody‑positive IMNM more commonly requires prolonged multi‑agent therapy.

Case anchor and practical lessons
This patient’s course illustrates several practical principles. She experienced rapid onset of severe proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms and marked CK elevation after a single dose of semaglutide and improved substantially with pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and one cycle of IVIG. Her CK and function normalized and she was successfully weaned off steroids over about 2 months without further immunotherapy. This pattern supports a probable drug‑associated IMNM in which removal of the trigger plus a limited immunomodulatory course resulted in remission—distinct from the more protracted course typically seen in autoantibody‑positive IMNM. The case also highlights the real risk of infection during immunotherapy (she developed a pseudomonal UTI and septic shock), underscoring the need for infection prevention strategies and vigilance.

Summary
Management of IMNM requires an integrated approach: prompt high‑dose corticosteroids and early IVIG for severe presentations, timely transition to steroid‑sparing agents when prolonged treatment is anticipated, rigorous clinical and laboratory monitoring for muscle, respiratory and cardiac involvement, aggressive prevention and detection of infections, and comprehensive supportive care including rehabilitation and nutritional management. Comorbid conditions and the need to avoid implicated drugs (for example, GLP‑1 receptor agonists when suspected) must be addressed with safe alternative strategies for metabolic disease and cardiovascular risk reduction. Individualization of therapy—guided by severity, autoantibody status, comorbidities and response to treatment—is essential.

## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis
Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is heterogeneous in its course. Many patients, particularly those in whom a trigger can be identified and eliminated, improve substantially with timely immunotherapy; some achieve sustained remission and can be tapered off immunosuppression. Conversely, a subset—especially those with autoantibodies such as anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR—may have a more protracted course that requires prolonged, multiagent immunosuppression and maintenance therapy. Seronegative IMNM (no detectable myositis‑associated autoantibodies) comprises a substantial minority of cases and can also remit, but seronegativity does not exclude a serious autoimmune process; outcomes in seronegative patients are variable and depend on disease severity, rapidity of diagnosis, and treatment intensity.

The case presented illustrates a more favorable trajectory: after an early course of high‑dose corticosteroids and a single course of IVIG followed by cessation of the suspected offending agent (semaglutide), the patient had progressive clinical and biochemical improvement, normalization of CK and cardiac imaging, and remained off immunosuppression for many months without relapse. This pattern—marked improvement after withdrawal of a suspected drug and a limited immunomodulatory course—is more consistent with drug‑triggered IMNM than with idiopathic IMNM, which often relapses on steroid taper and more commonly requires second‑line agents.

Follow‑up and monitoring
Early and frequent follow‑up is essential. During the acute and early recovery phase, see the patient every 2–4 weeks to assess strength, bulbar symptoms, respiratory status, adverse effects of therapy, and laboratory trends. Core elements of follow‑up include:

- Clinical exam: manual muscle testing (focus on proximal limb muscles), assessment of gait, timed functional measures (e.g., timed up‑and‑go), and specific screening for bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, dysarthria), which may be subtle but carry risk for aspiration.
- Serum studies: CK should be obtained at each early visit (every 2–4 weeks) until stable and trending down, then at progressively longer intervals (every 1–3 months) during tapering and maintenance. Monitor AST/ALT (remember they can reflect muscle as well as liver), complete metabolic panel, CBC, fasting glucose/HbA1c (if on corticosteroids), and other tests guided by concurrent immunosuppression (e.g., TPMT prior to azathioprine, hepatitis screening prior to rituximab).
- Respiratory monitoring: baseline assessment of respiratory muscle strength (spirometry with supine FVC or maximal inspiratory pressure) if there is any dyspnea or concern for respiratory muscle involvement; repeat testing if symptoms develop or worsen.
- Bulbar assessment: for any dysphagia or cough with liquids, obtain formal swallow evaluation (modified barium swallow) and involve speech‑language pathology early; consider enteral feeding support if aspiration risk or caloric intake is inadequate.
- Cardiac surveillance: when troponin or natriuretic peptides are elevated, or when cardiac symptoms/signs are present, obtain ECG and echocardiography and consider cardiac MRI to evaluate myocarditis or cardiomyopathy. Repeat cardiac imaging after clinical improvement to document resolution, as done in this case.
- Imaging and biopsy: muscle MRI can help localize active disease and monitor response; muscle biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard when serologies are non‑diagnostic.

Once strength and CK are stable and improving, interval visits can be spaced out (e.g., every 6–12 weeks) with continued vigilance for relapse. In patients on long‑term immunosuppression, more frequent monitoring is required for drug toxicity and infectious complications.

Relapse prevention and immunosuppressive strategy
Tapering and longer‑term immunosuppression must be individualized. Typical principles are:

- Taper steroids gradually once clinical strength and CK have substantially improved; a too‑rapid taper risks relapse. The pace of taper depends on disease severity, response, and steroid side effects.
- For idiopathic or relapsing IMNM, add steroid‑sparing agents early (azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil) or consider IVIG or rituximab for refractory disease. IVIG is particularly effective in many cases of IMNM and is often used as first‑line adjunct for severe weakness, dysphagia, or when rapid response is needed.
- In suspected drug‑triggered cases with a clear temporal relationship and good response after drug withdrawal and a short immunotherapy course, long‑term immunosuppression may not be necessary—yet close monitoring is required to detect recurrence.
- Be proactive about infection prevention (vaccinations prior to planned immunosuppression when feasible), and counsel patients on infection risks. The case highlights that serious infections can complicate immunosuppressive therapy (patient developed Pseudomonas urosepsis during recovery).

Avoid re‑exposure to suspected triggers
When a medication is suspected to have triggered IMNM, avoid re‑challenge and communicate this clearly:

- Counsel the patient to avoid all agents in the same class (here, GLP‑1 receptor agonists) unless compelling evidence later suggests otherwise; cross‑reactivity is possible but not guaranteed, and rechallenge risks recurrence of severe myopathy.
- Document the adverse reaction prominently in the medical record and in allergy/adverse‑drug reaction lists.
- Inform the patient’s primary care provider and any specialists (endocrinology, cardiology, bariatric providers) that GLP‑1 RAs are contraindicated for this patient. If future obesity/diabetes therapy is needed, consider alternative classes and coordinate care.

Pharmacovigilance and reporting
Suspected cases of drug‑associated IMNM should be reported to regulatory bodies (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the United States) and local pharmacovigilance systems. Reporting helps build a safety signal for rare but serious adverse events; clinicians should also consider case reports or registry entries when appropriate. Given the expanding use of GLP‑1 RAs, systematic reporting is important to clarify whether associations are causal.

Clinical pearls and common pitfalls
- IMNM can be seronegative. A negative myositis panel, including SRP and HMGCR antibodies, does not rule out IMNM. Muscle biopsy is decisive when clinical suspicion is high. In this case, biopsy demonstrated necrosis, myophagocytosis, macrophage infiltration and membrane attack complex deposition—findings diagnostic of IMNM despite seronegativity.
- Time course helps distinguish IMNM from classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect typically presents with an abrupt, very high CK that improves rapidly after drug withdrawal and supportive care. IMNM often shows progressive weakness and rising CK after withdrawal of the suspected agent and requires immunomodulation for recovery. In our patient the CK rose progressively over months after a single dose and improved with immunosuppression—supporting an immune‑mediated process.
- Do not attribute transaminase elevations automatically to liver disease. AST and ALT can be elevated from muscle breakdown; consider CK measurement when transaminases are disproportionate to other liver tests or when muscle symptoms are present.
- Screen for and monitor extramuscular involvement. Bulbar weakness and cardiac involvement (myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia) occur in IMNM and significantly affect prognosis. Early recognition and targeted evaluation (swallow study, troponin/BNP, ECG, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI) prevent morbidity.
- Do not delay initiation of immunotherapy in patients with severe weakness, bulbar symptoms or respiratory compromise while awaiting serologies or biopsy—early treatment improves outcomes. Tissue diagnosis and antibody testing remain important, but treatment decisions are often made on clinical grounds.
- Be vigilant for steroid complications and infections; balance immunosuppression risks with the need to control muscle inflammation.

Case outcome reiterated
This patient’s course exemplifies a likely drug‑triggered IMNM: rapid intolerance to semaglutide followed by progressive proximal weakness, marked CK elevation over weeks to months, diagnostic muscle biopsy, and substantial recovery after prompt immunotherapy (pulse steroids and IVIG) plus avoidance of the suspect drug. She regained baseline function, had normalization of CK and cardiac imaging, and remained off immunosuppression for months without recurrence. While this favorable outcome cannot definitively prove causality, it illustrates the potential for remission in drug‑associated IMNM when the trigger is withdrawn and immune therapy is instituted early.

Bottom line
Early recognition, prompt immunomodulatory therapy when clinically indicated, systematic monitoring for muscle and extramuscular involvement, careful tapering and consideration of steroid‑sparing agents for relapsing disease, and avoidance and documentation of re‑exposure to suspected offending drugs are the cornerstones of managing IMNM. Report suspected drug‑related cases to pharmacovigilance systems to improve collective understanding of rare adverse events.
