# Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy in the Era of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: Clinical Reasoning, Mechanisms, and Management

## Case as a Starting Point

A woman in her 60s with obesity, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, hypertension, and hypothyroidism received a first subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg) for obesity. Within hours she developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance with emesis, dehydration, and acute kidney injury. Although creatine kinase (CK) on admission was only modestly elevated (337 U/L), over the ensuing 4 months—off semaglutide—she developed progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia and dysphonia. CK rose to 3992 U/L. Autoantibody testing for myositis-specific and myositis-associated antibodies, including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, was negative, aside from a low-titer isolated anti–SS-B. MRI showed symmetric thigh muscle edema; muscle biopsy revealed a necrotizing myopathy with macrophage-predominant inflammation and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on myofibers, consistent with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM). She improved promptly with pulse methylprednisolone followed by high-dose oral prednisone and adjunctive IVIG, then maintained remission with drug withdrawal alone and tapered off immunosuppression within 2 months. She returned to baseline function, with normal CK and no relapse at 10 months while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

This case illustrates the key clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic features of IMNM and raises the possibility—requiring further study—of GLP-1 receptor agonists as a rare trigger of immune-mediated myopathy.

## Clinical Approach to Progressive Proximal Weakness with Dysphagia

Proximal limb-girdle weakness developing over weeks to months with dysphagia and dysphonia suggests an inflammatory myopathy, neuromuscular junction disorder, motor neuron disease, or steroid/thyroid/toxic myopathy. The initial sorting hinges on tempo, distribution, systemic features, and drug exposures:

- Time course: Subacute (weeks–months) progression points to idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) or drug-induced immune myopathy; hyperacute (hours–days) suggests rhabdomyolysis or myasthenic crisis; chronic years of insidious weakness suggests inclusion body myositis or hereditary myopathy.
- Distribution: Symmetric proximal weakness with neck flexor involvement and bulbar symptoms favors IIM; ocular involvement suggests myasthenia; distal predilection raises inclusion body myositis or neuropathy.
- Systemic/contextual clues: Rash points toward dermatomyositis; interstitial lung disease, mechanic’s hands, Raynaud phenomenon to antisynthetase syndrome; statin or immune checkpoint inhibitor exposure raises drug-associated IMNM; thyroid and Cushingoid features suggest endocrine/steroid myopathy.
- Laboratory: CK is typically elevated in IIM and rhabdomyolysis; normal or mildly elevated CK with severe weakness suggests steroid myopathy, neuromuscular junction disease, or chronic myopathy with advanced muscle loss.

In this patient, the subacute progressive proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, CK in the thousands, MRI muscle edema, and a necrotizing biopsy established an IIM, specifically IMNM. The absence of cutaneous features or antisynthetase manifestations narrowed the differential away from dermatomyositis and antisynthetase syndrome. Lack of ocular involvement argued against myasthenia gravis. Negative thyroid studies reduced the likelihood of endocrine myopathy. The failure of weakness to improve in the months after stopping semaglutide, followed by a brisk response to immunosuppression, argued against direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, favoring an immune-mediated process.

## Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy: Pathophysiology

IMNM is a major subtype of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies characterized by:

- Clinical phenotype: Rapidly progressive, often severe, symmetric proximal weakness; frequent neck flexor and bulbar involvement; myalgia is variable; CK elevations are usually marked (often >10 times the upper limit of normal, but can be lower).
- Histopathology: Myofiber necrosis and regeneration with a relative paucity of lymphocytes; macrophage-rich myophagocytosis; sarcolemmal deposition of the complement MAC (C5b-9); frequent upregulation of MHC class I on myofibers; endomysial edema and scattered perivascular cells may be seen but without the perifascicular atrophy of dermatomyositis.
- Seroimmunology: Autoantibody-defined subsets include anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP; approximately one-third are “seronegative” by current panels, implying unrecognized autoantigens or heterogeneous mechanisms.

Mechanistically, IMNM is thought to reflect antibody- and complement-mediated injury to muscle fibers. In anti-HMGCR myopathy, exposure to statins can upregulate HMGCR expression in regenerating myofibers, leading to autoantibody production that persists after drug cessation and drives ongoing immune attack. In anti-SRP myopathy, antibodies target a ribonucleoprotein involved in protein translocation into the endoplasmic reticulum; the disease often runs a fulminant course with prominent necrosis and more frequent cardiac involvement. Across IMNM subtypes, deposition of MAC on myofibers and macrophage-dominant infiltration support a paradigm of humoral immunity initiating fiber necrosis, followed by innate immune clearance and regeneration. T cells are less abundant than in polymyositis, but CD8+ cells may be present and contribute to injury. Cytokines, damage-associated molecular patterns, and local complement activation amplify tissue damage.

The seronegative group likely includes patients with autoantibodies not yet captured by existing assays, non–B-cell–dependent mechanisms, and post–drug exposure phenotypes in which the inciting antigen or adjuvant effect is transient. Clinically, seronegative IMNM behaves similarly to antibody-positive disease but sometimes requires careful exclusion of mimics and malignancy.

## Drug-Associated Myopathy: Rhabdomyolysis versus IMNM

Many drugs can injure skeletal muscle. Two broad patterns are important:

- Direct toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis: Acute myofiber breakdown with marked CK elevation, myoglobinuria, and potential renal failure. When the drug is stopped and hydration is provided, CK falls within days to weeks and strength usually recovers without immunosuppression. Statins, fibrates, antiretrovirals, and illicit drugs are common culprits.
- Immune-mediated myopathy (e.g., statin-associated anti-HMGCR IMNM, immune checkpoint inhibitor–associated myositis): Persistent or progressive weakness and CK elevation despite drug withdrawal; biopsy shows necrotizing myopathy with immune features; responds to immunosuppression.

In this case, the 4-month progression of weakness with rising CK after a single semaglutide dose, the necrotizing biopsy with MAC deposition, and the brisk steroid/IVIG response are typical of IMNM rather than a self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis.

## GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Skeletal Muscle: What Might Link Them?

GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) such as semaglutide are widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Known adverse effects include gastrointestinal intolerance and, in some reports, loss of lean mass during weight reduction. Rare case reports have described rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA, with CK normalization after drug cessation—consistent with a direct toxic effect rather than immune activation.

Could GLP-1 RAs trigger IMNM? A definitive mechanism has not been established. Plausible, still speculative, pathways include:

- Immune modulation: GLP-1 signaling can influence innate and adaptive immune responses; in susceptible hosts this might unmask or amplify autoimmunity.
- Antigenic exposure after muscle injury: Severe catabolic stress (e.g., dehydration, hypoxemia, or metabolic derangements during acute gastrointestinal illness) could cause subclinical myofiber injury, exposing antigens and initiating complement activation and bystander inflammation that evolves into autoimmune necrotizing myopathy.
- Molecular mimicry/adjuvanticity: A drug or its formulation could serve as an adjuvant in genetically predisposed individuals, promoting autoantibody formation against a muscle antigen.
- Metabolic effects on regenerating muscle: Shifts in anabolism/catabolism during rapid weight change might upregulate proteins on regenerating myofibers that become immune targets, akin to HMGCR upregulation in statin-associated disease.

At present, these remain hypotheses. The temporal association, pathologic pattern, and therapeutic response in this patient support a probable drug association by WHO-UMC criteria. Larger pharmacovigilance datasets and mechanistic studies are needed to discern causality and risk factors.

## Diagnostic Workup

When evaluating suspected inflammatory myopathy, adopt a structured approach:

1. History and examination
   - Define tempo; pattern of weakness (proximal vs distal); bulbar, respiratory, and neck flexor involvement; myalgia; rash; Raynaud phenomenon; sicca; arthritis; dyspnea; constitutional symptoms.
   - Exposures: statins, red yeast rice (monacolin K), mushrooms/supplements, immune checkpoint inhibitors, antithyroid drugs, antipsychotics, antiretrovirals, alcohol, cocaine; infections; recent vaccinations.
   - Family history of myopathy or neuropathy. Review endocrine status (thyroid, Cushing’s), and medications that cause steroid myopathy.
   - Screen for malignancy risk factors and systemic symptoms.

2. Laboratory testing
   - CK, aldolase, AST/ALT, LDH: recognize that AST/ALT rise with muscle injury; GGT and bilirubin help localize to liver.
   - Inflammatory markers: ESR/CRP may be elevated but are nonspecific.
   - Thyroid function, electrolytes, vitamin D; screen for infection when appropriate.
   - Myositis-specific and -associated antibodies: anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP (IMNM); anti–Mi-2, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, MDA5 (dermatomyositis subsets); antisynthetase antibodies (Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ); PM/Scl, Ku, U1-RNP. Note that about one-third of IMNM is seronegative.
   - Cardiac biomarkers: Use cardiac troponin I preferentially; troponin T may be elevated in skeletal myopathies due to cross-reactivity. BNP/proBNP can reflect cardiomyopathy or strain.

3. Electrophysiology and imaging
   - EMG: irritable myopathy with fibrillations, positive sharp waves, short-duration, small-amplitude polyphasic motor units. EMG helps exclude neuropathic processes and guides biopsy site.
   - MRI of affected muscles: T2/STIR hyperintensity indicating edema/inflammation; T1 changes reflect fatty replacement in chronic disease; MRI guides biopsy to active areas.

4. Tissue diagnosis
   - Muscle biopsy is key in IMNM, especially in seronegative patients. Hallmarks include:
     - Myofiber necrosis and regeneration out of proportion to inflammation
     - Macrophage-rich myophagocytosis (CD68+)
     - Sarcolemmal MAC deposition (C5b-9) on necrotic and non-necrotic fibers
     - Variable MHC class I upregulation
     - Sparse lymphocytes (rare CD3+ T cells), minimal vasculopathy
   - Absence of perifascicular atrophy (dermatomyositis) and rimmed vacuoles (inclusion body myositis) helps differentiate subtypes.

5. Evaluate extramuscular involvement
   - Pulmonary: high-resolution CT and pulmonary function tests for interstitial lung disease (particularly in antisynthetase and MDA5 dermatomyositis).
   - Cardiac: ECG, echocardiography, and cardiac MRI if biomarkers/symptoms suggest myocarditis or cardiomyopathy. Myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement may indicate early or resolving myocarditis. Cardiac involvement is more common in anti-SRP IMNM but can occur across IIM.
   - Malignancy screening: Age-appropriate cancer screening for all IIM; consider enhanced screening in older adults, those with systemic symptoms, or seronegative IIM when clinical suspicion is high, recognizing that malignancy risk is highest in dermatomyositis.

6. Distinguishing mimics and complications
   - Rhabdomyolysis: abrupt CK surge with myoglobinuria and rapid CK fall after trigger removal; biopsy typically not needed.
   - Steroid myopathy: proximal weakness without muscle pain; normal CK; develops after steroid exposure and worsens with dose escalation.
   - Endocrine myopathies (hypo-/hyperthyroid), metabolic myopathies, motor neuron disease, myasthenia gravis.

In the presented patient, negative myositis panels, MRI evidence of edema, and a necrotizing biopsy established seronegative IMNM. Mildly elevated hs-troponin with transient MRI evidence of myocardial edema underscored the need to consider cardiac involvement in IIM. Her longstanding transaminase elevations were hepatic by biopsy, illustrating the importance of not attributing all AST/ALT elevations to muscle when dual pathologies coexist.

## Management Principles

1. Remove potential triggers
   - Discontinue the suspected inciting agent (e.g., statin, checkpoint inhibitor, or, in this case, semaglutide). Rechallenge is generally avoided when a serious immune-mediated event is suspected.

2. Immunosuppression
   - Glucocorticoids: Initiate promptly for moderate-to-severe weakness (e.g., prednisone 0.75–1 mg/kg/day). For severe or rapidly progressive disease, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, or marked CK elevation, give intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (e.g., 500–1000 mg daily for 3–5 days), then transition to oral dosing.
   - IVIG: Effective adjunct in IMNM, particularly in severe weakness, bulbar or respiratory involvement, or when rapid response is needed. Typical total dose is 2 g/kg over 2–5 days, repeated monthly in refractory disease.
   - Steroid-sparing agents: Methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil are commonly used for maintenance and to facilitate steroid tapering. Rituximab is effective in refractory IMNM and in some seronegative cases; cyclophosphamide may be considered when there is vital organ involvement (e.g., myocarditis). Many anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR patients require combination therapy and prolonged courses; some seronegative or drug-triggered cases—like this patient—enter sustained remission after a short course.
   - Plasma exchange can be considered in fulminant, refractory cases, especially with life-threatening dysphagia or myocarditis.

3. Supportive and preventive care
   - Swallowing/airway: Early speech and swallow evaluation; dietary texture modification; aspiration precautions; enteral nutrition if needed. Consider ICU care when bulbar or respiratory muscles are compromised.
   - Rehabilitation: Physical and occupational therapy to prevent contractures, deconditioning, and falls; progressive resistance and aerobic training as inflammation abates.
   - Bone and metabolic health: Calcium/vitamin D, bisphosphonates when indicated; glucose monitoring; PJP prophylaxis with prolonged high-dose steroids and additional immunosuppression; vaccination review and updates before or early during immunosuppression.
   - Infection vigilance: Prompt evaluation and treatment; counsel on infection risk. This patient’s pseudomonal urosepsis soon after discharge underscores the need for prophylaxis and monitoring during high-dose steroid therapy.

4. Monitoring and tapering
   - Track CK and strength serially; CK often normalizes before full strength returns.
   - Taper glucocorticoids gradually once CK and function improve; avoid overly rapid taper that risks relapse but balance against steroid toxicity. In drug-triggered cases that remit, a quicker taper may be successful.
   - Reassess cardiac and pulmonary involvement if initially abnormal; repeat echocardiography or cardiac MRI can document resolution, as in this case.

## Prognosis

Outcomes vary by autoantibody status, severity at presentation, and rapidity of treatment:

- Anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR IMNM often require aggressive, sustained immunosuppression and carry higher risks of relapse and residual weakness, particularly when treatment is delayed.
- Seronegative IMNM is heterogeneous; some patients respond briskly and can taper off therapy, especially when a removable trigger is implicated.
- Early recognition and initiation of therapy, management of dysphagia/respiratory compromise, and rehabilitation improve functional recovery.

This patient’s remission after a short course of steroids and IVIG, with sustained recovery off immunosuppression, is more typical of drug-associated IMNM than of idiopathic, antibody-defined IMNM.

## Putting the Case in Context: Causality Assessment

Applying WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality principles:

- Time relationship: Onset of intolerance immediately after semaglutide; progressive immune myopathy evolved over months—compatible with an immune process rather than acute toxicity.
- Alternative explanations: No statin or checkpoint inhibitor exposure; broad autoantibody panel negative; endocrine causes excluded; biopsy supports IMNM.
- Dechallenge: Withdrawal of semaglutide with subsequent improvement after immunosuppression; sustained remission without re-exposure.
- Rechallenge: Not ethical given severity.

Thus, the association is “probable,” not certain. Pharmacovigilance and mechanistic studies are needed to define whether GLP-1 RAs can, rarely, trigger IMNM and to identify predisposing factors.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

- Progressive symmetric proximal weakness with dysphagia over weeks to months and CK elevation should prompt evaluation for IIM; MRI and biopsy help subtype disease.
- IMNM is defined histologically by myofiber necrosis with macrophage-predominant inflammation and MAC deposition, often with minimal lymphocytes. About one-third of patients are seronegative for anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP.
- Distinguish toxic rhabdomyolysis (acute, self-limited after drug withdrawal) from immune-mediated myopathy (persistent/progressive, requires immunosuppression).
- Do not misattribute all AST/ALT elevations to liver or muscle without context; use GGT, bilirubin, and, when needed, liver biopsy to localize injury.
- Troponin T may be falsely elevated in skeletal myopathies; troponin I is preferred for cardiac assessment in myositis. Consider cardiac MRI when myocarditis is suspected.
- Severe bulbar weakness is a medical emergency. Early steroids and IVIG can be lifesaving; secure the airway and nutrition and involve speech therapy promptly.
- Infection risk rises with high-dose steroids and IVIG; provide prophylaxis when indicated and monitor closely.
- In suspected drug-associated IMNM, discontinue the agent and avoid rechallenge; remission with a shorter immunosuppressive course is possible.
- Isolated low-titer anti–SS-B without ANA or anti–SS-A is often nonspecific and should not anchor diagnosis.
- Screen for cancer with age-appropriate modalities in all IIM; tailor intensity to clinical suspicion and myositis subtype.

## Take-Home Points

- IMNM is a necrotizing autoimmune myopathy that presents with rapidly progressive proximal weakness, often with bulbar involvement, marked CK elevation, and a biopsy showing myofiber necrosis with macrophage-rich inflammation and MAC deposition.
- Serologic testing for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP aids diagnosis, but seronegative disease is common; biopsy remains decisive.
- Management requires prompt high-dose glucocorticoids, often with IVIG and a steroid-sparing agent; drug withdrawal is essential in suspected drug-associated cases.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are not established causes of IMNM, but this case highlights a probable association and the need for clinician awareness and pharmacovigilance.
- Early recognition, support of airway/nutrition, careful infection monitoring, and rehabilitation are central to good outcomes.
