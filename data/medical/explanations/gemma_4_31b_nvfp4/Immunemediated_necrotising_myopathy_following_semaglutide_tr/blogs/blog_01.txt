Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Beyond the Nausea: GLP-1 Agonists and a Rare Muscle Mystery

We all know the "GLP-1 glow"—or, more accurately, the GLP-1 nausea. With the meteoric rise of semaglutide and tirzepatide for obesity and diabetes, we've become well-acquainted with the gastrointestinal fallout: vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, and the occasional bout of pancreatitis. But what happens when a "weight-loss wonder drug" triggers a systemic immune attack on the skeletal muscles?

Enter a case that should make every clinician pause: a woman in her 60s who developed profound, progressive muscle weakness and dysphagia after receiving just *one* single dose of semaglutide.

## The Case: A Slow-Burn Decline

The story starts with a common clinical scenario: a patient with obesity and NAFLD is started on semaglutide (0.25 mg). The immediate reaction was classic—severe nausea and vomiting leading to hypovolemic acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. She spent a few days in the ICU on non-invasive ventilation, recovered from the acute crisis, and went home. At discharge, her CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L.

However, the real mystery began in the following months. Instead of returning to baseline, the patient began a steady decline. Over four months, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphonia, and dysphagia. By the time she returned to the hospital, she couldn't sit or stand without help and was struggling to swallow. Her CK had skyrocketed to 3,992 U/L—a twelve-fold increase from her initial admission.

## Toxic vs. Immune-Mediated: The Crucial Distinction

When we see high CK levels and muscle weakness after a drug, our brains often jump to "toxic myopathy" or rhabdomyolysis. But there is a critical difference in the clinical trajectory.

In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (as seen in some reports with tirzepatide), we typically see a massive, acute spike in CK that resolves rapidly with drug cessation and aggressive hydration. In this case, the patient's symptoms *progressed* while the drug was already out of her system. This "slow burn" suggests the drug didn't just damage the muscle directly; it may have triggered an immune response against it.

The diagnosis was Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM). A muscle biopsy confirmed the suspicion: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a heavy presence of macrophages (CD68+ staining) and the membrane attack complex, signaling an active inflammatory attack.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

What makes this case even more interesting is that the patient was seronegative. She lacked the hallmark autoantibodies usually associated with IMNM, such as anti-HMGCR (often linked to statins) and anti-SRP. This reminds us that a significant portion of IMNM patients are seronegative, and the absence of a known antibody does not rule out a necrotising myopathy.

While she had a mild elevation in transaminases, the team correctly attributed this to her known NAFLD rather than muscle breakdown, as the liver enzymes remained stable even after the CK normalized.

## The Road to Recovery

The treatment was aggressive and targeted: pulse-dose methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The response was striking. Her CK levels normalized, and her functional status returned to baseline. Notably, because this was likely a drug-triggered immune event, she was able to be weaned off immunosuppression rapidly once the offending agent (semaglutide) was permanently removed.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Temporal Trap:** Not all drug-induced muscle injuries happen instantly. An immune-mediated response can develop or peak weeks or months after the initial exposure.
*   **Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** If the CK continues to rise or the weakness persists/progresses *after* the drug is stopped and the patient is hydrated, stop thinking "toxic" and start thinking "immune-mediated."
*   **Seronegative doesn't mean "Negative":** A negative myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, etc.) does not exclude IMNM. When the clinical suspicion is high, biopsy remains the gold standard.
*   **Dysphagia as a Red Flag:** In the context of proximal muscle weakness, the development of swallowing difficulties (dysphagia/dysphonia) suggests a severe myopathy that requires urgent intervention to protect the airway and nutrition.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Heighten Pharmacovigilance:** While rare, GLP-1 receptor agonists have been linked to skeletal muscle loss and, in this case, a probable trigger for IMNM.
*   **Recognize the Pattern:** IMNM presents with aggressive proximal weakness and high CK that does not resolve simply by removing the drug.
*   **Treatment:** Steroids and IVIG are the mainstays for IMNM and can lead to full functional recovery if administered promptly.
*   **The Single-Dose Signal:** A single dose of a medication can be enough to trigger a systemic immune-mediated adverse reaction.
