Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Seronegative Trap: Diagnosing IMNM Without the Antibodies

We live in the era of the "panel." Whether it's a comprehensive metabolic panel or a specialized myositis antibody screen, we’ve become accustomed to letting a set of lab values dictate our diagnostic trajectory. But what happens when the panel comes back clean, but the patient is still crashing?

That is the "Seronegative Trap." In the world of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM), the absence of the classic antibodies—specifically anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP—can lead to a dangerous diagnostic plateau. If you stop looking because the antibodies aren't there, you miss the window for immunosuppression.

## The Case: A Slow Burn After a Sharp Start

Consider a patient in her 60s with a history of obesity and NAFLD. She receives a single dose of semaglutide. The immediate reaction is classic GLP-1 intolerance: nausea, vomiting, and a hypovolemic hit leading to acute renal failure. She is treated and discharged, but then something strange happens.

Over the next four months, she doesn't just stay "deconditioned"—she declines. She develops progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presents again, she can't sit upright without assistance. Her CK has climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000 U/L.

On paper, this looks like a classic myopathy. The team orders a full myositis panel. The result? **Negative.** No SRP, no HMGCR, no Jo-1. Just a low-titer SS-B antibody that doesn't explain the profound muscle necrosis.

## Why the Panel Can Lie to You

In IMNM, we often lean on anti-HMGCR (often associated with statins) and anti-SRP antibodies. When these are positive, the diagnosis is streamlined. However, an estimated one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. 

The danger here is conflating "seronegative" with "non-immune." If a clinician sees a negative antibody panel and a history of a drug like semaglutide, they might misdiagnose this as a simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. But rhabdomyolysis typically behaves like a sprint: an acute spike in CK that resolves quickly upon drug cessation and hydration. 

This patient's course was a marathon of decline. The weakness progressed *after* the drug was gone, and the CK continued to rise. That is the hallmark of an immune-mediated process—the drug didn't just poison the muscle; it flipped a switch in the immune system that kept the attack going.

## The Gold Standard: Getting to the Tissue

When the serology fails, the biopsy is the only way out of the trap. In this case, a biopsy of the vastus lateralis revealed the truth: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a macrophage infiltrate (CD68+). Crucially, the membrane attack complex (MAC) was positive on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres.

This histological pattern—necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates—is the definitive signature of IMNM. It doesn't matter if the antibodies are missing; the tissue tells the story.

The patient was treated with pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and IVIG. She made a full recovery and returned to her baseline functional status, emphasizing that the "cure" required targeting the immune system, not just hydrating the kidneys.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Antibodies are suggestive, not definitive:** A negative myositis panel (SRP/HMGCR) does not rule out IMNM. Roughly 30% of cases are seronegative.
*   **Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis usually improves rapidly after removing the offending agent. Progressive weakness or persistent CK elevation after drug cessation suggests an immune-mediated process.
*   **The Biopsy is King:** When clinical suspicion for myositis is high but serology is negative, a muscle biopsy is the gold standard to identify necrotic fibers and inflammatory infiltrates.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** While GI side effects are common with semaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists, be vigilant for rare but severe immune-mediated muscle complications.
*   **Treatment Response:** Seronegative IMNM typically responds well to corticosteroids and IVIG, though the course may be shorter if the trigger (the drug) is removed.
