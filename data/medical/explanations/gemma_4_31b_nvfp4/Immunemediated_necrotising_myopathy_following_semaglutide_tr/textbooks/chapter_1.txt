Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Chronology

The patient, a female in her 60s, presented with a medical history characteristic of metabolic syndrome, including obesity, hypertension, and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). These comorbidities provide the clinical context for her treatment; due to persistent obesity and fatty liver, she was prescribed semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. Understanding these baseline conditions is essential, as metabolic disorders often necessitate medications that can potentially interact with or trigger systemic inflammatory responses.

The clinical course was marked by two distinct phases: an immediate acute reaction and a subsequent subacute progression of muscle weakness. The acute phase began within hours of the patient receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). She experienced severe gastrointestinal distress, including nausea and vomiting, which progressed to a systemic crisis requiring hospitalization. During this initial five-day admission, she developed acute renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis, and dyspnea, necessitating non-invasive ventilation in the intensive care unit. While her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at this time (337 U/L), she lacked the hallmark symptoms of acute rhabdomyolysis, such as severe muscle pain or myoglobinuria (cola-colored urine).

Following discharge from the acute episode, the patient entered a subacute phase characterized by a progressive decline in functional status over the next four months. This phase was defined by the insidious onset of symmetric proximal muscle weakness, meaning she struggled with movements requiring the large muscles of the hips and shoulders. As the condition advanced, she developed bulbar symptoms, specifically dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (difficulty speaking), indicating that the myopathy was affecting the muscles of the upper aerodigestive tract. By the end of this four-month window, her weakness had progressed to the point where she could no longer sit or stand upright without assistance.

The temporal relationship between the administration of the GLP-1 receptor agonist and the onset of myopathy is a critical diagnostic detail. Unlike typical drug-induced toxic myopathies or rhabdomyolysis, where CK levels peak rapidly and improve upon drug cessation, this patient experienced a delayed and escalating course. Her CK levels rose from a mild elevation during the acute reaction to a peak of 3,992 U/L four months later—a 12-fold increase. This chronology—initial drug exposure followed by a progressive, inflammatory-style muscle breakdown—suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an immune-mediated process rather than causing direct muscle toxicity.
