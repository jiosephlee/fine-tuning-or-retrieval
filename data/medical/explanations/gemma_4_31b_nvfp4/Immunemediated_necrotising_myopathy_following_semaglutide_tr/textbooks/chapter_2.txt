Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness and a specific histological pattern. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies, such as dermatomyositis or polymyositis, which feature prominent lymphocytic infiltration of the endomysium or perimysium, IMNM is defined by the presence of widespread necrotising myofibres with a notable paucity of inflammatory cell infiltrates. On biopsy, this manifests as dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, often accompanied by myophagocytosis—where macrophages, identified by markers such as CD68, clear the debris of dead muscle cells. The presence of a membrane attack complex (MAC) on the myofibres further indicates an immune-mediated process, suggesting that the muscle damage is driven by the complement system rather than a direct toxic insult.

The diagnosis of IMNM is often guided by the identification of specific autoantibodies, most notably anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies. Anti-HMGCR antibodies are strongly associated with statin use, as these medications inhibit the HMGCR enzyme, potentially triggering an autoimmune response in susceptible individuals. However, a significant challenge in clinical practice is that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, meaning they lack these hallmark antibodies. In such cases, the diagnosis relies heavily on the clinical presentation of progressive weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels, and characteristic biopsy findings. The existence of seronegative IMNM suggests that other, as-yet unidentified autoantibodies or triggers may be driving the disease, as seen in this case where semaglutide is proposed as a potential contributing factor.

It is critical to distinguish IMNM from toxic rhabdomyolysis, as the two conditions require fundamentally different management strategies. Toxic rhabdomyolysis is a direct result of muscle injury (e.g., from a drug's direct toxic effect or severe trauma) and typically follows a rapid kinetic pattern: CK levels spike acutely and then normalize within days or weeks after the offending agent is removed and the patient is hydrated. In contrast, IMNM is an autoimmune process; the trigger (such as a medication) may initiate the immune response, but the subsequent muscle destruction persists or even progresses after the trigger is gone. 

In this patient, the diagnostic reasoning centered on this distinction. While the patient experienced an acute reaction to semaglutide, her CK levels did not normalize upon drug cessation. Instead, she suffered a 12-fold increase in CK over four months and progressive functional decline. This "smouldering" and progressive course is the hallmark of an autoimmune process rather than direct toxicity. Consequently, while toxic rhabdomyolysis is managed with supportive care, IMNM requires immunosuppression—such as high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—to arrest the immune-mediated attack on the myofibres and allow for muscle regeneration.
