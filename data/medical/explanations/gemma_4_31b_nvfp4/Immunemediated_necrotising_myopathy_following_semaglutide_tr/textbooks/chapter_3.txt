Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Work-up and Evidence Synthesis

The diagnostic approach to this patient required a synthesis of biochemical markers, advanced imaging, and histological analysis to differentiate between toxic myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The primary biochemical indicator was the marked elevation of creatine kinase (CK), which peaked at 3,992 U/L—a 12-fold increase from the initial measurement taken shortly after the semaglutide dose. While an acute, massive spike in CK often suggests rhabdomyolysis (direct muscle toxicity), the progressive nature of this patient's weakness over four months, despite the drug being discontinued, pointed toward a sustained autoimmune process. Furthermore, while aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were elevated, these markers are present in both hepatic and muscle tissue. In this case, the patient's history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and the persistence of transaminase elevation after CK normalization confirmed that the liver, rather than muscle breakdown, was the primary source of these enzymes.

Imaging played a critical role in localizing the pathology and screening for systemic involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the bilateral femurs revealed symmetric muscular edema, particularly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. In the context of proximal weakness, these findings are highly suggestive of an inflammatory myopathy and served as a guide for the biopsy site. Because autoimmune myopathies can affect the myocardium and lungs, the team performed a cardiac MRI and high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest. The HRCT was negative for interstitial lung disease, helping to rule out antisynthetase syndrome. The cardiac MRI, however, showed mild myocardial edema and hypokinesis, suggesting that the immune-mediated process may have extended to the heart, manifesting as mild myocarditis or a stress-induced cardiomyopathy.

The definitive diagnosis was established through a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis, which is the gold standard for identifying necrotising myopathies. The histological hallmark was the presence of dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies (such as polymyositis), IMNM is characterized by a relative paucity of endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates. Instead, the biopsy showed prominent myophagocytosis, evidenced by immunohistochemistry staining for CD68, a macrophage marker. The discovery of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres provided critical evidence of a complement-mediated inflammatory response, confirming that the muscle destruction was immune-mediated rather than purely toxic.

Finally, the diagnostic synthesis was completed by ruling out known autoantibodies. The patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM markers, anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), as well as antibodies associated with other connective tissue diseases. The absence of these markers classifies this as seronegative IMNM. The temporal relationship between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of symptoms, combined with the rapid response to corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), allowed the clinicians to conclude that the semaglutide likely acted as a trigger for this immune-mediated reaction.
