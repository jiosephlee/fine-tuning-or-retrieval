Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis and Causality Reasoning

When evaluating a patient with progressive muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) following medication initiation, the clinician must distinguish between direct drug toxicity (rhabdomyolysis), an idiopathic autoimmune process, and a drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). In this case, the diagnostic reasoning centered on the temporal evolution of symptoms and the biological source of laboratory abnormalities.

A key challenge in this patient's work-up was interpreting the elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Because these enzymes are present in both skeletal muscle and hepatic tissue, they often rise in tandem with CK during myopathic episodes. However, this patient had a documented history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), confirmed by biopsy three years prior to the onset of myopathy. The fact that her transaminases remained borderline elevated even after her CK levels normalized suggests that the hepatic steatosis, rather than muscle breakdown, was the primary driver of her liver enzyme elevations. This distinction is critical to avoid misattributing hepatic dysfunction to muscle necrosis or vice versa.

The patient's serological profile presented further diagnostic nuances. She tested positive for the SS-B autoantibody but was negative for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) and SS-A. While SS-A and SS-B are hallmarks of Sjögren’s syndrome, isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A is uncommon and not a recognized trigger for IMNM. Given the lack of other connective tissue disease features, this finding was likely an incidental or non-specific marker rather than the cause of the myopathy. Furthermore, the patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM-associated antibodies, anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This underscores a significant diagnostic challenge in IMNM: approximately one-third of patients are seronegative, requiring clinicians to rely on muscle biopsy and clinical response to treatment for a definitive diagnosis.

To establish a link between semaglutide and the myopathy, the clinical team compared this presentation with other GLP-1 receptor agonist cases, such as reports of tirzepatide-induced rhabdomyolysis. In typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, there is an acute, massive spike in CK that resolves rapidly upon drug discontinuation and aggressive hydration. In contrast, this patient experienced a more protracted course: a progressive 12-fold increase in CK over four months and a failure to improve without the introduction of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This pattern—sustained muscle necrosis and the requirement for immunosuppression—is characteristic of an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxic necrosis.

To formalize the relationship between the drug and the adverse event, the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories were applied. The causality was determined to be "probable" based on three main criteria: a reasonable temporal relationship between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of decline, the absence of other plausible triggers (such as statins or other supplements), and a clinically reasonable response to the withdrawal of the agent and subsequent treatment. While a "certain" causality would typically require a positive rechallenge, such a step was deemed unethical given the severity of the patient's reaction. This systematic approach allows clinicians to move from a suspected association to a probable causal link, highlighting the importance of pharmacovigilance in the use of increasingly common metabolic medications.
