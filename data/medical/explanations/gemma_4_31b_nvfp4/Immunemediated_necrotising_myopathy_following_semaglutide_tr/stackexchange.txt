Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness and a significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level following the initiation of a new medication. How does the clinical course of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) typically differ from that of direct drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis?

The primary distinction between immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and direct drug-induced toxic rhabdomyolysis lies in the **temporal evolution of symptoms**, the **trajectory of creatine kinase (CK) levels**, and the **therapeutic response**.

In **direct toxic rhabdomyolysis**, the drug acts as a direct toxin to the myocyte, leading to acute membrane rupture and an immediate release of intracellular contents. The clinical course is typically acute: patients present with a rapid, severe spike in CK levels that begins to normalize within days of drug discontinuation and aggressive hydration. As seen in the case of tirzepatide mentioned in the report, toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves without the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

In contrast, **IMNM** is an autoimmune process where a medication (or other trigger) precipitates a loss of immune tolerance, leading to a T-cell or antibody-mediated attack on muscle fibers. This results in a distinct clinical pattern:
*   **Temporal Progression:** Rather than an immediate spike and fall, the weakness and CK elevation are often progressive over weeks or months. In this case, the patient experienced a 12-fold increase in CK over four months following a single dose of semaglutide, indicating a simmering autoimmune process rather than a transient toxic insult.
*   **Clinical Features:** IMNM often involves systemic features of inflammatory myopathy, such as the progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia observed in this patient.
*   **Treatment Response:** Because the pathology is driven by an aberrant immune response, simply removing the offending agent is often insufficient. IMNM requires immunosuppression—such as the corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) used in this patient—to arrest the muscle destruction and allow for regeneration.
*   **Histopathology:** While both may show necrotic fibers, IMNM is characterized by an inflammatory infiltrate (e.g., CD68+ macrophages) and the presence of the membrane attack complex (MAC), as seen on this patient's muscle biopsy, signifying an active immune-mediated attack.

In summary, while toxic rhabdomyolysis is a rapid "hit-and-run" event, IMNM is a triggered autoimmune cascade that persists after the drug is gone and necessitates targeted immunomodulation for recovery.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the evaluation of a patient with suspected inflammatory myopathy, the myositis panel is negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. Does this exclude a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and what is the estimated prevalence of seronegative IMNM?

A negative myositis panel for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies does **not** exclude a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). While these autoantibodies are highly associated with the condition—particularly anti-HMGCR in the context of statin use—they are not present in all patients. It is estimated that approximately **one-third** of patients with IMNM are seronegative.

The diagnostic challenge posed by seronegativity underscores the importance of muscle biopsy, which remains the gold standard for diagnosis. In cases of suspected IMNM where serology is non-contributory, histology is required to identify the characteristic hallmarks of the disease: widespread myofiber necrosis and regeneration with a relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. 

This principle is illustrated in the presented case. Despite a comprehensive myositis panel that was negative for anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, and several other myositis-specific antibodies, the diagnosis of IMNM was confirmed via a biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, myophagocytosis (evidenced by CD68+ macrophage staining), and membrane attack complex (MAC) positivity on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. These histological findings, combined with the patient's clinical presentation of progressive proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), allowed for a definitive diagnosis of IMNM despite the absence of the hallmark autoantibodies.

### [Pathophysiology] On muscle biopsy, what are the characteristic histological hallmarks of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that distinguish it from other inflammatory myopathies like polymyositis or dermatomyositis?

The histological hallmark of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is the presence of prominent **myofiber necrosis and regeneration with a striking paucity of inflammatory infiltrates**. This distinguishes it from other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies where the primary pathological process is driven by an active, dense cellular inflammatory infiltrate.

In the provided case, the muscle biopsy of the vastus lateralis demonstrated these characteristic features: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers accompanied by myophagocytosis (confirmed by CD68+ macrophage staining), while T-cell markers (CD3+) revealed only rare positive cells and minimal perivascular inflammation.

To distinguish IMNM from other myopathies, we look for the following:

*   **IMNM (The "Necrotising" Pattern):** The dominant finding is the death of muscle fibers (necrosis) without a corresponding "invasion" of the muscle by lymphocytes. A critical diagnostic clue is the deposition of the **membrane attack complex (MAC)** on the surface of both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers, as seen in this patient. This suggests that the muscle injury is mediated by complement-driven pathways or antibody-mediated toxicity rather than direct T-cell attack.
*   **Polymyositis (The "Endomysial" Pattern):** In contrast to the "silent" necrosis of IMNM, polymyositis is characterized by an endomysial inflammatory infiltrate, specifically CD8+ T-cells that surround and invade non-necrotic muscle fibers.
*   **Dermatomyositis (The "Perifascicular" Pattern):** Dermatomyositis typically shows perivascular and perimysial inflammation, with the pathognomonic finding of **perifascicular atrophy** (degeneration of fibers at the periphery of the muscle fascicle).

In summary, while polymyositis and dermatomyositis are defined by *where* the inflammation is located (endomysial vs. perivascular), IMNM is defined by the *absence* of significant inflammation despite widespread fiber necrosis. When a patient presents with an aggressive rise in creatine kinase (CK) and proximal weakness—as seen in this case with a 12-fold CK increase—the biopsy finding of MAC-positive necrotic fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration strongly directs the diagnosis toward IMNM.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with progressive proximal muscle weakness accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia. What is the clinical significance of these bulbar symptoms in the context of an inflammatory myopathy, and what are the primary risks associated with them?

In the context of an inflammatory myopathy, such as the immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) seen in this patient, the presence of bulbar symptoms—specifically dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (difficulty speaking)—is a critical clinical finding. These symptoms signify that the inflammatory process is not limited to the proximal limb muscles but has extended to the muscles of the pharynx, larynx, and upper esophagus.

The clinical significance of bulbar involvement is twofold: first, it serves as a marker of severe disease and an aggressive clinical course. Second, it represents a high-risk state that can lead to rapid decompensation. In this case, the patient's progression from proximal weakness to the inability to swallow liquids and solids indicates a widespread and systemic myopathic process.

The primary risks associated with bulbar symptoms in inflammatory myopathy include:

1.  **Aspiration Pneumonia:** This is the most immediate and life-threatening risk. Weakness of the pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles impairs the patient's ability to protect the airway during swallowing, leading to the inhalation of food, liquids, or oropharyngeal secretions.
2.  **Respiratory Failure:** While the case emphasizes bulbar symptoms, there is often an overlap with respiratory muscle weakness. Patients with severe dysphagia are at higher risk for respiratory compromise, either through aspiration or concurrent diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle involvement.
3.  **Nutritional and Hydration Compromise:** As demonstrated by this patient's difficulty with both liquids and solids, severe bulbar weakness can lead to an inability to maintain adequate oral intake, necessitating nutritional support (e.g., enteral feeding) to prevent malnutrition and dehydration during the acute phase of the illness.

Recognizing these symptoms early is paramount, as they often necessitate more aggressive intervention—such as the pulse corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) used in this case—to prevent permanent disability or fatal pulmonary complications.

### [Clinical Pearls] When evaluating a patient with severe muscle breakdown and elevated CK, why is it critical to cautiously interpret elevations in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and how can a clinician differentiate between hepatic and myogenic origins of these enzymes?

When evaluating a patient with severe muscle breakdown and a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), it is critical to cautiously interpret elevations in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) because these enzymes are not exclusive to the liver; they are also present in skeletal muscle. In the setting of extensive myocyte necrosis, these enzymes are released into the systemic circulation, which can lead to a "pseudo-hepatotoxicity" profile, potentially misleading the clinician toward a diagnosis of acute liver injury.

To differentiate between hepatic and myogenic origins of these enzymes, clinicians should employ the following strategies:

**1. Temporal Correlation with Muscle Enzymes**
The most reliable clue is the kinetic pattern of the enzymes. If AST and ALT levels mirror the rise and fall of the CK, the elevations are likely myogenic. In this case, the patient had a peak CK of 3,992 U/L associated with AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) elevations. Crucially, the clinicians noted that the patient's borderline elevation in transaminases persisted even after her CK levels normalized, which indicated that the underlying cause of the transaminitis was hepatic rather than myogenic.

**2. Analysis of the AST/ALT Ratio and Other Liver Markers**
While both enzymes are found in muscle, AST is generally more abundant in skeletal muscle than ALT. However, this is not a definitive rule. A more robust approach is to evaluate the broader hepatic panel. The absence of cholestatic markers (normal alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin) and preserved synthetic function (normal albumin) often suggests that the liver's primary architecture and function remain intact despite the transaminase elevation. In this patient, the normal bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase helped shift the focus away from acute liver failure.

**3. Integration of Baseline Data and Ancillary Testing**
Clinicians must consider the patient's medical history and baseline labs. This patient had a documented history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with baseline transaminase elevations (ALT > AST) years prior to the onset of myopathy. The use of liver ultrasound and repeat biopsy in this case confirmed that the hepatosteatosis was the primary driver of the chronic enzyme elevation, independent of the acute immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

In summary, when presented with the triad of proximal weakness, high CK, and elevated transaminases, the clinician should not assume hepatic dysfunction. Instead, track the enzymes longitudinally; if the transaminases remain elevated after the CK returns to baseline, the origin is hepatic. If they resolve in tandem, the origin is myogenic.

### [Management] The patient in this case responded rapidly to a short course of high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and was able to be weaned off immunosuppression quickly. How does this response pattern help differentiate a drug-triggered autoimmune process from an idiopathic autoimmune myopathy?

The response pattern described in this case—rapid clinical recovery and the ability to quickly wean off immunosuppression—is a key diagnostic clue that differentiates a drug-triggered autoimmune process from an idiopathic autoimmune myopathy.

In idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the autoimmune drive is intrinsic and persistent. These patients typically require prolonged, intensive, and often multi-drug immunosuppressive regimens (e.g., high-dose corticosteroids combined with steroid-sparing agents such as rituximab or azathioprine) over several months or years to achieve and maintain remission. Attempting to taper corticosteroids rapidly in idiopathic IMNM often results in immediate clinical relapse and a rebound of creatine kinase (CK) levels.

In contrast, when an autoimmune process is triggered by an external agent—such as the semaglutide in this case—the drug acts as the primary stimulus for the immune dysregulation. Once the offending agent is withdrawn, the "fuel" for the inflammatory process is removed. While the immune system may continue to attack the muscle for a period after drug cessation (explaining why this patient continued to decline for four months after a single dose), the underlying pathology is more susceptible to a "reset." 

In this patient, the administration of pulse methylprednisolone, a short course of oral prednisone (1 mg/kg), and a single course of IVIG was sufficient to halt the inflammatory cascade. The fact that she successfully returned to her baseline functional status and remained in remission for eight months without further immunosuppression strongly suggests a triggered event rather than a chronic, idiopathic disease. This clinical trajectory aligns with other drug-induced autoimmune phenomena, where the removal of the trigger allows for a more rapid and durable recovery following a brief period of potent immunosuppression.
