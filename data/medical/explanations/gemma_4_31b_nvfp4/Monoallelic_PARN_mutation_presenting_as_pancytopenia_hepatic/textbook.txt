Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Initial Clinical Presentation and Multisystem Evaluation

The patient, a woman in her late 20s, presented with a five-year history of progressive fatigue and generalized weakness. The gradual decline in her functional capacity—progressing from mild fatigue to a state where she required assistance with daily self-care—suggests a systemic, chronic process rather than an acute illness. In cases of young adults presenting with such profound constitutional decline, clinicians must look beyond common causes of fatigue to identify patterns of multisystem failure or accelerated biological aging.

Physical examination revealed several hallmarks of premature aging, which are critical clues in the context of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The patient exhibited premature greying of the hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue characterized by the atrophy of lingual papillae. These ectodermal findings are significant because tissues with high cellular turnover, such as the skin and oral mucosa, are particularly susceptible to telomere attrition. While the absence of the classic "mucocutaneous triad" (nail dystrophy, leukoplakia, and pigmentation) often delays the diagnosis of dyskeratosis congenita, the presence of these subtle aging markers should prompt a search for an underlying genetic syndrome.

Further evaluation identified significant organ involvement and respiratory distress. The patient reported exertional dyspnea and chest discomfort, and examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, pointing toward pulmonary pathology. Simultaneously, the discovery of hepatosplenomegaly (with a liver span of 18 cm) indicated hepatic involvement. The combination of pulmonary and hepatic dysfunction in a young adult without a clear infectious or autoimmune etiology suggests a systemic fibrotic process, which is a common manifestation of telomere-related dysfunction affecting the lung and liver parenchyma.

Initial hematological investigations showed pancytopenia with a severely low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating a failure of the bone marrow to produce new red blood cells. The peripheral smear demonstrated dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. These findings are characteristic of ineffective erythropoiesis, where precursors are destroyed within the marrow before reaching maturity. While severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies were confirmed and contributed to these findings, the clinical severity and the persistence of cytopenias despite nutritional correction suggested a more fundamental marrow failure syndrome.

The multisystem nature of the presentation was further highlighted by musculoskeletal and neurological findings. The patient exhibited thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies confirmed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes. When combined with osteoporosis and recurrent fractures, these findings illustrate that the pathology extended beyond the hematologic and visceral systems, reflecting the widespread cellular senescence associated with a global defect in telomere maintenance.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Distinguishing Nutrient Deficiency from Systemic Disease

When a patient presents with pancytopenia and neuropathy, nutritional deficiencies—specifically vitamin B12 and folate—are primary considerations. In this case, the initial laboratory findings of dimorphic erythrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, and severe B12 and folate deficiency provided a compelling explanation for the patient's hematologic and neurologic symptoms. Vitamin B12 is essential for DNA synthesis; its deficiency leads to ineffective erythropoiesis, where hematopoietic precursors undergo apoptosis within the bone marrow before they can mature. This process is characterized by the "ineffective" production of cells despite a cellularly active marrow.

The interpretation of the bone marrow biopsy is critical in distinguishing between different types of marrow failure. In this patient, the biopsy revealed erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, rather than the hypocellularity characteristic of aplastic anemia. These findings confirmed that the bone marrow was attempting to respond to the anemia, but the cells were not maturing properly—a hallmark of megaloblastic anemia due to nutrient deficiency. To identify the root cause of the deficiency, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and duodenal biopsy were performed. The discovery of chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells provided a physiological explanation for the B12 deficiency: a lack of parietal cells leads to impaired production of intrinsic factor, which is necessary for the absorption of vitamin B12 in the terminal ileum.

A challenging aspect of this case was the presence of "diagnostic noise"—findings that suggested other etiologies. The patient had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG and elevated inflammatory markers, which could have pointed toward an immune-mediated hemolytic anemia or a systemic autoimmune disease. However, clinical reasoning must prioritize the most consistent data; the markedly low reticulocyte count and the marrow's erythroid hyperplasia were inconsistent with peripheral immune-mediated destruction and more consistent with a production failure. Similarly, the hepatic dysfunction required the systematic exclusion of more common mimics, including viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, and infectious causes, all of which were negative.

The pivotal moment in the diagnostic process occurred when the clinical team recognized that the patient's symptoms were "disproportionate" to the nutritional deficiency alone. While B12 deficiency explains pancytopenia and neuropathy, it cannot account for pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, premature greying of the hair, or early-onset osteoporosis. When a patient presents with a constellation of multisystem failures—particularly those involving fibrosis of multiple organs and signs of premature aging—the suspicion must shift from a single acquired deficiency to a systemic genetic syndrome. In this case, these disproportionate features directed the clinicians toward the hypothesis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD), eventually confirmed by the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* mutation.

## Pathophysiology of Telomere Biology Disorders (TBDs)

Telomeres are repetitive TTAGGG DNA sequences that cap the ends of linear chromosomes, acting as protective buffers that prevent the cell's DNA repair machinery from recognizing chromosome ends as double-stranded breaks. During each round of DNA replication, the cellular machinery is unable to completely replicate the very end of the lagging strand—a phenomenon known as the "end-replication problem." Consequently, telomeres shorten with every cell division. When telomeres reach a critically short length, the cell triggers a DNA-damage response that leads to cellular senescence or apoptosis, effectively limiting the replicative lifespan of the cell.

To counteract this progressive attrition, specialized cells utilize the telomerase complex, a ribonucleoprotein that elongates telomeres. This complex consists of two primary components: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), which provides the catalytic activity to synthesize DNA, and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which serves as the essential template for the addition of telomeric repeats. Pathogenic variants in the genes encoding TERT or TERC lead to insufficient telomerase activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and the premature onset of cellular senescence.

The PARN gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that plays a critical role in the maturation of the telomerase complex. Specifically, PARN is responsible for the deadenylation—the removal of post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails—from the 3-prime end of TERC RNA. This processing is necessary for the proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC, which in turn is required for the stable assembly and function of the telomerase enzyme. In the presented case, the monoallelic missense variant in PARN disrupts this maturation process, leading to telomerase insufficiency and subsequent telomere biology disorder (TBD), despite the mutation not being biallelic.

The clinical manifestations of TBDs are a direct result of this premature cellular senescence. When the replicative capacity of stem cells is exhausted, the body cannot replace dead or damaged cells, leading to organ atrophy and the replacement of functional parenchyma with fibrotic tissue. This manifests as a "premature ageing" phenotype, seen in this patient as premature greying of the hair and osteoporosis.

Tissue specificity in TBDs is determined by the rate of cellular turnover. Organs that rely heavily on the constant proliferation of stem cells are preferentially affected. In the bone marrow, telomere attrition in hematopoietic stem cells leads to bone marrow failure and pancytopenia. In the lungs and liver, the inability of alveolar epithelial cells and hepatocytes to regenerate in response to stress or injury triggers a fibrotic wound-healing response. This explains why the patient presented with the triad of pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic cirrhosis, as these high-turnover tissues are the most vulnerable to the loss of genomic stability caused by PARN dysfunction.

## Genetics and the Clinical Spectrum of PARN Mutations

In this case, the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) was reached through clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). In clinical genetics, a VUS is a genetic alteration where the evidence is insufficient to definitively classify it as either pathogenic or benign. However, the clinicians in this case utilized ACMG/AMP standards to argue for its relevance. They noted that the mutation occurred in the highly conserved R3H domain—a critical functional region of the protein—and was absent in population databases. When the genetic finding correlates strongly with a patient's multisystem phenotype (such as this patient's combined pulmonary, hepatic, and marrow dysfunction), a VUS may be interpreted as the likely driver of the disease.

The *PARN* gene encodes a protein that acts as an exoribonuclease, specifically responsible for the deadenylation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). This process is essential for the proper maturation of TERC; without it, telomerase cannot effectively maintain telomere length, leading to premature cellular senescence. This patient’s monoallelic mutation demonstrates the principle of haploinsufficiency, where a single functional copy of the gene is insufficient to maintain normal protein levels or activity, resulting in a disease state. The R3H domain is particularly sensitive to missense mutations, as it is vital for the enzyme's catalytic activity.

The clinical manifestation of *PARN* mutations depends heavily on whether the variant is monoallelic or biallelic. Biallelic variants (mutations on both alleles) typically result in more severe, early-onset phenotypes, such as autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) or Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome. In contrast, monoallelic variants—like the one seen in this patient—are more frequently associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). While these cases are generally less severe than the biallelic forms, they still cause significant morbidity in young adults.

This case highlights the broad phenotypic spectrum of TBDs. Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototype TBD, classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, as seen in this patient, the classical triad may be entirely absent. Instead, TBDs can present as "cryptic" multisystem diseases where the primary manifestations are organ-specific fibrosis or bone marrow failure. In this patient, the absence of the triad was replaced by premature greying of the hair and early-onset osteoporosis, which are also markers of accelerated biological ageing.

Finally, the association between *PARN* variants and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is well-documented, but this case expands the understanding of the *PARN* phenotype. While monoallelic *PARN* mutations are strongly linked to IPF, this patient also exhibited significant hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis. This suggests that the downstream impact of *PARN* dysfunction on telomerase may affect multiple high-turnover organs beyond the lungs and bone marrow, reinforcing the need to screen for hepatic involvement in patients with identified *PARN* variants.

## Management, Surveillance, and Clinical Lessons

The management of this patient was multifaceted, addressing both the immediate nutritional deficiencies and the underlying genetic predisposition to telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The most urgent priority was the correction of severe vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$ and folate deficiencies, which had contributed to ineffective erythropoiesis and pancytopenia. The patient received a loading regimen of intravenous cyanocobalamin (1000 $\mu$g daily for five days), followed by weekly and then monthly maintenance doses. This was supplemented with daily oral folic acid. To address her secondary hyperparathyroidism and osteoporosis, she was started on high-dose weekly vitamin D (60,000 IU) and elemental calcium. Symptomatic anemia was managed with supportive whole blood transfusions, and physiotherapy was implemented to address her functional decline.

While the nutritional corrections provided symptomatic relief, the systemic fibrosis of the lungs and liver—driven by the monoallelic *PARN* mutation—does not have a targeted curative therapy. Management of organ fibrosis in TBDs is primarily supportive and focused on the mitigation of risk factors that could further accelerate telomere attrition. Because TBDs are progressive, long-term surveillance is essential. This patient requires regular monitoring of pulmonary function and hepatic stability to track the progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and hepatic fibrosis. Furthermore, ongoing hematological surveillance is critical; although her cytopenia was exacerbated by $\text{B}_{12}$ deficiency, the underlying telomere dysfunction renders her bone marrow more susceptible to failure over time.

In cases of progressive bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS) within the TBD spectrum, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only potentially curative option for the hematological manifestations. However, HSCT in TBD patients is associated with higher morbidity and mortality compared to other causes of marrow failure, often due to the systemic nature of the disease (such as pre-existing pulmonary fibrosis) and the potential for poor graft tolerance. Therefore, the decision to pursue transplantation requires a careful risk-benefit analysis based on the severity of the cytopenia and the stability of other organ systems.

This case provides several critical clinical pearls for the recognition of TBDs. First, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly when it involves a combination of cytopenia and organ fibrosis (pulmonary or hepatic). Second, it is vital to recognize that the "classical triad" of dyskeratosis congenita—nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation—is frequently absent in adult-onset or monoallelic cases. The absence of these mucocutaneous features should not rule out a telomere biology disorder. Finally, TBDs often mimic more common acquired conditions, such as nutritional deficiencies or autoimmune disorders; when the clinical response to standard treatment is disproportionate or incomplete, genetic evaluation, including clinical exome sequencing, should be considered.
