Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] The patient was found to have a monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene. What is the specific biochemical role of the PARN protein in the maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC), and how does its dysfunction contribute to telomere attrition?

The **PARN protein** is a member of a highly conserved family of exoribonucleases whose primary biochemical function is the deadenylation of mRNA—specifically, the removal of poly(A) tails—to regulate gene expression and RNA stability. In the context of telomere maintenance, PARN plays a specialized and critical role in the maturation of the **telomerase RNA component (TERC)**.

During the processing of TERC, post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails are added to the RNA. These tails typically serve as signals that target nuclear RNAs for degradation. PARN is responsible for the removal of these oligo(A) tails, a step that is essential for the proper **3'-end maturation of TERC**. Without this precise trimming, TERC cannot be correctly processed or stabilized.

When PARN is dysfunctional—as seen in this patient with a monoallelic mutation (p.Cys205Arg) affecting the critical R3H domain—the maturation of TERC is impaired. Because TERC is a fundamental structural and catalytic component of the telomerase ribonucleoprotein complex (alongside telomerase reverse transcriptase, or TERT), its deficiency or instability leads to a failure of the telomerase enzyme to effectively elongate telomeres.

This biochemical failure results in **premature telomere attrition**. As telomeres shorten beyond a critical threshold, cells enter premature senescence or undergo apoptosis. This process disproportionately affects tissues with high replicative demand, such as the bone marrow, pulmonary epithelium, and hepatocytes. In this case, this telomere biology disorder (TBD) manifested as the constellation of pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis, demonstrating how a specific defect in RNA processing translates into multisystem organ failure.
