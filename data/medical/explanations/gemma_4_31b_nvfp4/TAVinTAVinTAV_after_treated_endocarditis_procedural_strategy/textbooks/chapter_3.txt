Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Pre-Procedural Imaging and Strategic Planning

The decision to perform a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) requires a rigorous diagnostic workup to determine if the aortic root can anatomically accommodate another prosthesis without causing catastrophic complications. In this case, the patient presented with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction following a history of infective endocarditis and two previous TAVIs (a Lotus valve and an Evolut valve). Because the patient's surgical risk was prohibitive—evidenced by a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and comorbidities including COPD and diabetes—the multidisciplinary Heart Team focused on whether a transcatheter approach could safely restore valve function.

Multislice CT (MSCT) served as the primary tool for anatomical mapping and procedural simulation. A critical finding was the measurement of the minimum lumen diameter within the existing Evolut prosthesis, which was 13.7 mm. This measurement is essential for valve selection and for predicting the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), as the cumulative thickness of three valve frames significantly reduces the effective orifice area (EOA). By precisely mapping the landing zone and the interaction between the existing frames, the team could select a prosthesis—the Acurate neo2—that offered a favorable hemodynamic profile for a narrow, triple-stented anatomy.

A primary concern in repeat TAVI procedures is the risk of coronary artery obstruction. When a new valve is implanted within an old one, the previous leaflets are displaced outward, creating a "neo-skirt" of tissue and metal that can obstruct the coronary ostia. To mitigate this, the team used MSCT to analyze coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and the valve-to-coronary distance. Based on these metrics, a "high implantation" strategy was planned. By deploying the third valve higher in the aortic root, the team aimed to fix the previous leaflets against the stent frame—preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the lumen—while ensuring the neo-skirt remained above the coronary ostia to preserve perfusion and future coronary access.

The strategic planning also accounted for the "frame-in-frame" interaction, referring to the cumulative mesh burden of three overlapping stent frames. This layering increases the rigidity of the aortic root and complicates the ability to cross the valve with catheters for future coronary interventions. To address this, the team emphasized commissural alignment during the planning phase, using CT-guided modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the new valve's leaflets with the previous ones. This alignment is intended to maximize the EOA and keep the "windows" of the stent frames open for potential future coronary access.

Finally, the feasibility of the procedure was confirmed by integrating these imaging findings with a comprehensive clinical assessment. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and blood cultures were utilized to ensure that the prior infective endocarditis had fully resolved, as performing a TAVI in the presence of active infection is contraindicated. Once the Heart Team confirmed the absence of active vegetations and the anatomical feasibility of the high-implantation strategy, the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV was deemed the most viable option to improve the patient's quality of life while avoiding the extreme risks of a surgical total aortic root replacement.
