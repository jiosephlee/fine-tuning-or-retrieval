Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and the Evolution of Prosthetic Valve Failure

The patient in this case is a man in his late 70s with a complex medical profile that significantly influenced the selection of transcatheter interventions over surgical options. His history is marked by extensive cardiovascular and systemic comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary artery disease—requiring multiple percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) between 2009 and 2014—arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Additionally, he had developed a third-degree atrioventricular block, which necessitated the implantation of a pacemaker in 2014. For a clinician, these factors are critical because they contribute to a high surgical risk profile, making the patient a candidate for transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) rather than conventional open-heart surgery.

The patient's history of prosthetic valve failure began in 2014, when progressive degeneration of the native aortic valve led to severe stenosis. Due to the aforementioned comorbidities, he underwent his first TAVI with the implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve. While the procedure initially restored valve function, the patient eventually experienced structural valve deterioration (SVD). SVD is a common long-term complication of bioprosthetic valves, characterized by mechanical wear, calcification, or leaflet fibrosis, which gradually narrows the valve orifice and impairs hemodynamic performance.

By late 2021 and early 2022, the clinical manifestation of this deterioration became evident. The patient presented with classic symptoms of severe aortic stenosis: exertional dyspnea, dizziness, and fatigue. In elderly patients, these symptoms often reflect the heart's inability to increase cardiac output during physical activity. The hemodynamic markers of this failure were profound; echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient that had risen to 83 mmHg, indicating severe obstruction to left ventricular outflow. This chronic pressure overload led to a decline in the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) from 54% to 42%, signaling the onset of heart failure secondary to the failing prosthesis.

To address this failure, a TAV-in-TAV procedure was performed in May 2022, where a 23 mm Evolut valve was implanted inside the existing Lotus valve. This strategy is designed to treat a failed transcatheter prosthesis by deploying a new valve frame within the old one, thereby restoring a functional orifice without the need for surgical explantation. Following this second intervention, the patient's hemodynamic status improved, as evidenced by the recovery of the EF to 54%. This sequence of events illustrates the iterative nature of managing structural valve disease in high-risk patients, where each subsequent failure requires a careful balance of anatomical feasibility and hemodynamic optimization.

## Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) and Post-Infectious Degeneration

In this case, the patient’s clinical course was complicated by prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) occurring after his second transcatheter intervention. The diagnosis of PVE was established through a combination of clinical presentation, microbiology, and imaging. The patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure, which, alongside positive blood cultures for *Enterococcus faecalis* and echocardiographic evidence of severe restenosis, confirmed the diagnosis. *E. faecalis* is a common pathogen in PVE, often requiring targeted, prolonged antimicrobial regimens due to its resilience and propensity for biofilm formation on prosthetic material.

The management of PVE requires a rigorous antimicrobial strategy to ensure the complete eradication of the pathogen before any further invasive procedures are considered. In this patient, a targeted 6-week course of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy was administered, followed by a transition to oral antibiotics. This duration is consistent with standard guidelines for treating endocarditis to prevent relapse, particularly when the infection involves prosthetic material where drug penetration can be limited.

A critical challenge in managing PVE is distinguishing between active infection and the structural damage left in the wake of a resolved infection. Active endocarditis is characterized by the presence of vegetations, perivalvular abscesses, and positive blood cultures. In contrast, post-infectious degeneration manifests as permanent structural changes to the valve. To ensure the patient was a candidate for a third TAVI, the clinical team utilized transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) to confirm the absence of active vegetations or paravalvular complications. These imaging modalities allowed the team to conclude that the infection had resolved and that the remaining valve dysfunction was due to structural failure rather than ongoing sepsis.

The pathophysiology of this valve failure was likely multifactorial. PVE often leads to accelerated prosthetic valve deterioration through two primary mechanisms: the direct destruction of leaflet tissue by bacteria and the subsequent inflammatory response that triggers fibrosis. In this patient, the infection likely caused leaflet injury and subsequent fibrotic scarring, leading to mechanical restriction and severe restenosis. This is evidenced by the markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm² and an elevated mean gradient (49 mmHg) observed after the infection had been treated.

The timing of re-intervention after PVE is a high-stakes decision. Intervening too early, while the infection is active, carries an extremely high risk of seeding the new prosthesis. However, waiting until the patient is hemodynamically unstable increases surgical and procedural risk. In this case, the decision to proceed with a third TAVI in 2024 was based on the complete resolution of the infection, as confirmed by negative cultures and imaging, balanced against the patient's prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%). This highlights the necessity of a multidisciplinary "heart team" approach to determine when the benefit of restoring valve function outweighs the risks associated with a triple-stented anatomy and a history of PVE.

## Pre-Procedural Imaging and Strategic Planning

The decision to perform a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) requires a rigorous diagnostic workup to determine if the aortic root can anatomically accommodate another prosthesis without causing catastrophic complications. In this case, the patient presented with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction following a history of infective endocarditis and two previous TAVIs (a Lotus valve and an Evolut valve). Because the patient's surgical risk was prohibitive—evidenced by a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and comorbidities including COPD and diabetes—the multidisciplinary Heart Team focused on whether a transcatheter approach could safely restore valve function.

Multislice CT (MSCT) served as the primary tool for anatomical mapping and procedural simulation. A critical finding was the measurement of the minimum lumen diameter within the existing Evolut prosthesis, which was 13.7 mm. This measurement is essential for valve selection and for predicting the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), as the cumulative thickness of three valve frames significantly reduces the effective orifice area (EOA). By precisely mapping the landing zone and the interaction between the existing frames, the team could select a prosthesis—the Acurate neo2—that offered a favorable hemodynamic profile for a narrow, triple-stented anatomy.

A primary concern in repeat TAVI procedures is the risk of coronary artery obstruction. When a new valve is implanted within an old one, the previous leaflets are displaced outward, creating a "neo-skirt" of tissue and metal that can obstruct the coronary ostia. To mitigate this, the team used MSCT to analyze coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and the valve-to-coronary distance. Based on these metrics, a "high implantation" strategy was planned. By deploying the third valve higher in the aortic root, the team aimed to fix the previous leaflets against the stent frame—preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the lumen—while ensuring the neo-skirt remained above the coronary ostia to preserve perfusion and future coronary access.

The strategic planning also accounted for the "frame-in-frame" interaction, referring to the cumulative mesh burden of three overlapping stent frames. This layering increases the rigidity of the aortic root and complicates the ability to cross the valve with catheters for future coronary interventions. To address this, the team emphasized commissural alignment during the planning phase, using CT-guided modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the new valve's leaflets with the previous ones. This alignment is intended to maximize the EOA and keep the "windows" of the stent frames open for potential future coronary access.

Finally, the feasibility of the procedure was confirmed by integrating these imaging findings with a comprehensive clinical assessment. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and blood cultures were utilized to ensure that the prior infective endocarditis had fully resolved, as performing a TAVI in the presence of active infection is contraindicated. Once the Heart Team confirmed the absence of active vegetations and the anatomical feasibility of the high-implantation strategy, the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV was deemed the most viable option to improve the patient's quality of life while avoiding the extreme risks of a surgical total aortic root replacement.

## Technical Execution of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV Procedure

Performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) presents significant technical hurdles, primarily due to the "triple-stented" anatomy. With each successive valve, the cumulative thickness of the prosthetic frames increases, which can severely restrict the effective orifice area (EOA) and obstruct access to the coronary arteries. In this case, the procedural strategy was meticulously planned using multislice CT (MSCT) to optimize hemodynamic performance and ensure patient safety.

The selection of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis was a critical technical decision. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenario, the available lumen is markedly reduced—in this patient, the minimum lumen diameter within the existing Evolut prosthesis was only 13.7 mm. The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height, which are advantageous in narrow anatomies to maintain a more favorable hemodynamic profile and reduce the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A central component of the procedural strategy was the "high implantation" technique. Rather than seating the third valve deep within the previous frames, the team positioned the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. The goal of this approach is twofold: first, by pressing the pre-existing leaflets outward against the stent frame, the team prevented the leaflets from folding inward, which would have further decreased the EOA. Second, high implantation minimizes the creation of a "neo-skirt"—the accumulation of displaced tissue and prosthetic material at the base of the valve—below the coronary ostia, thereby reducing the risk of coronary obstruction.

To preserve future coronary access, the team focused on commissural alignment. With three overlapping layers of metallic mesh, future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) become exponentially more difficult. By aligning the commissures of the Acurate neo2 with those of the previous Evolut valve—guided by pre-procedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks—the operators attempted to maintain "windows" of lower metal density to facilitate future catheter access to the coronary arteries.

The intraprocedural execution involved several key maneuvers to ensure stability and function. After establishing access, balloon pre-dilation of the existing prosthetic complex was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm). This prepared the site for the third valve. Following the deployment of the Acurate neo2, high-pressure post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon was utilized. This step was essential to optimize the expansion of the new valve and likely contributed to the radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame, improving overall leaflet motion and central flow dynamics.

Notably, the procedure was performed without the use of coronary protection (such as stents or guidewires in the coronary ostia). This decision was based on the pre-procedural MSCT, which provided precise measurements of the coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the valve-to-coronary distance. The CT modelling confirmed that the planned high implantation strategy provided sufficient clearance of the coronary ostia, rendering the risks of coronary obstruction low enough to forgo protection.

## Outcome Analysis and Lifetime Management Strategies

The evaluation of success in transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is standardized using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria. These criteria provide a rigorous framework for assessing procedural success, valve performance, and complications. In this case, procedural success was defined by the successful deployment of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis without immediate major complications, such as coronary obstruction or severe paravalvular leak. However, the immediate hemodynamic data highlighted a common challenge in repeat TAVI procedures: prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM).

PPM occurs when the effective orifice area (EOA) of the implanted prosthesis is too small to adequately support the patient's cardiac output, leading to persistent high gradients. Immediately following the third TAVI, the patient exhibited a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 $\text{cm}^2$. To determine the severity of PPM, clinicians use the indexed AVA (AVA divided by the patient's body surface area) and the Doppler Velocity Index (DVI). An indexed AVA of 0.4 $\text{cm}^2/\text{m}^2$ and a DVI of 0.24 in this patient were indicative of severe PPM. This phenomenon is expected in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios, as each subsequent valve is constrained by the frames of the previous prostheses, progressively reducing the available lumen for blood flow.

Despite the initial severe PPM, the patient showed significant hemodynamic improvement by the one-month follow-up. The mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA increased to 1.1 $\text{cm}^2$. This improvement is often attributed to a combination of factors: the partial mechanical expansion of the older frames (the Evolut prosthesis) due to post-dilation, the resolution of initial procedural inflammation, and left ventricular (LV) remodeling. The reduction in LV chamber volumes and the stabilization of the ejection fraction (EF 65%) at one year confirm that the third valve provided sufficient hemodynamic relief to allow the heart to recover from chronic pressure overload.

This case underscores the concept of "lifetime management" in structural heart disease. Every TAVI procedure alters the anatomy of the aortic root; by the third implantation, the "cumulative mesh burden"—the layering of multiple metal frames—creates significant challenges for future interventions. Specifically, the overlapping frames can obstruct access to the coronary ostia, making future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) technically difficult. The procedural strategy in this case—using CT-guided high implantation and deliberate commissural alignment—was specifically designed to mitigate these risks by preserving coronary flow and maintaining a potential pathway for future access.

Looking forward, if a triple-stented prosthesis fails, surgical explantation is often prohibitively high-risk due to the deep integration of the frames into the aortic wall. In such cases, emerging catheter-based "salvage" therapies are considered. These include bioprosthetic valve fracture (using high-pressure balloons to intentionally crack the frame to increase the orifice area) and the BASILICA technique (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration), which involves using a catheter to lacerate the leaflets of a failed valve to create a passage for a new valve without obstructing coronary flow. While these techniques are complex, they represent the next frontier in managing patients who have reached the limit of traditional TAV-in-TAV strategies.
