Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAVI after Endocarditis: When is the Coast Clear?

Imagine a "Matryoshka doll" of aortic valves. Now imagine that the second doll in the set was infected with *Enterococcus faecalis*. Now imagine you're being asked to put a third valve inside those two.

This is the "edge of the map" for interventional cardiology. We are seeing a growing population of TAVI patients who are living long enough to outlast their first—and even second—transcatheter prostheses. But when you add prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) to the mix, the clinical decision-making becomes a high-stakes balancing act between guideline-directed therapy and the brutal reality of surgical risk.

## The Case: A Triple-Stented Challenge

The patient was a man in his late 70s with a medical history that would make any surgeon sweat: three-vessel CAD, COPD, diabetes, and a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. His valve history was a chronological roadmap of TAVI evolution: a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022.

Shortly after the second procedure, the patient developed PVE caused by *E. faecalis*. He was treated with a grueling six-week course of IV ampicillin-based therapy. By 2024, he returned with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction—a mean gradient of 49 mmHg and an aortic valve area of just 0.5 cm². He was symptomatic, failing, and essentially "un-surgetable." Given the deep integration of two prior frames into his aortic wall, a surgical replacement would have required a total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.

The team opted for a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, implanting an Acurate neo2 valve.

## The Conflict: Guidelines vs. Reality

If you open the ESC guidelines, the answer to PVE is clear: surgery is the gold standard, especially when heart failure or uncontrolled infection is present. But guidelines are written for populations; clinicians treat individuals.

In this case, the "surgical gold standard" was a death sentence. The tension here lies in whether it is safe to implant a new prosthetic device into a site that has previously been infected. The fear is twofold: that the infection is still simmering (leading to immediate failure of the new valve) or that the previous infection left behind a "niche" that will inevitably seed the new prosthesis.

## When is the Coast Clear?

How do you decide the infection is gone? In this case, the "clearance" wasn't based on a single test, but a multimodal confirmation:

1.  **Microbiological Silence:** Completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy and negative repeat blood cultures.
2.  **Imaging Clearance:** Both transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice CT (MSCT) were used to confirm the absence of vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications.
3.  **Clinical Stability:** The patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable.

When the infection is dormant and the surgical risk is prohibitive, the decision shifts from "Can we avoid a TAVI?" to "How do we make this TAVI work?"

## The Technical Play: Avoiding the "Neo-Skirt"

A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV isn't just about pushing a valve through; it's about managing the "mesh burden." Every new valve added reduces the effective orifice area (increasing the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch) and pushes the leaflets of the previous valve outward, potentially creating a "neo-skirt" that can obstruct coronary flow.

The team used a strategy of **high implantation**. By positioning the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets, they achieved two things:
*   They pinned the previous leaflets against the stent frame, preventing them from folding inward and further restricting the orifice.
*   They kept the neo-skirt well below the coronary ostia, preserving coronary perfusion without needing coronary protection.

The result? After an initial period of severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (mean gradient 67 mmHg), the patient's LV remodeled, and by the 1-year mark, he was asymptomatic with a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an EF of 65%.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Guideline Gap:** While surgery is the preference for PVE, the "prohibitive surgical risk" caveat is real. In triple-stented roots, the morbidity of surgical explantation may outweigh the risk of a repeat TAVI.
*   **Multimodal Confirmation:** Never rely on a single imaging modality to clear a patient for TAVI post-endocarditis. Use a combination of blood cultures, TEE, and CT to ensure no active vegetations remain.
*   **The "High" Strategy:** In sequential TAV-in-TAV, high implantation can be a deliberate move to preserve coronary access and minimize the impact of the neo-skirt on the effective orifice area.
*   **The Long Game:** As TAVI becomes the norm, "lifetime management" is no longer a theoretical discussion. We must consider coronary access and valve-in-valve feasibility at the time of the *first* implant.

## Take-Home Messages

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is feasible** but requires meticulous CT-guided planning.
*   **Post-PVE TAVI** requires absolute confirmation of infection resolution via clinical, microbiological, and imaging markers.
*   **Strategic valve selection** (e.g., supra-annular leaflets, low frame height) and precise deployment height are critical to mitigating patient-prosthesis mismatch and coronary obstruction in multi-layer configurations.



# The Shrinking Orifice: Fighting PPM in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Imagine a Matryoshka doll, but instead of carved wood, we’re talking about cobalt-chromium and bovine pericardium. Now imagine that the "dolls" are all fighting for the same few millimeters of space in the aortic root. This is the reality of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—a procedure so rare it's barely documented in the literature, and one that pushes the limits of hemodynamics to the breaking point.

The case involves a man in his late 70s who had already navigated two TAVIs. He started with a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by an Evolut in 2022. Then, things got messy. He developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. While the infection was successfully treated with a six-week course of ampicillin, the aftermath was a disaster: severe degeneration and restenosis. By 2024, his aortic valve area (AVA) had shrunk to a staggering 0.5 $\text{cm}^2$. He was symptomatic, failing, and—given his surgical risk and the "metal-on-metal" complexity of his root—totally unsuitable for an open-heart redo.

The central challenge here is the "shrinking orifice." Every time you nest a transcatheter valve inside another, you add layers of stent frames and fabric. This cumulative "mesh burden" doesn't just threaten coronary access; it creates a physical barrier that reduces the effective orifice area, leading to severe Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). In a triple-stent scenario, you aren't just fighting the native anatomy; you're fighting the ghost of two previous procedures.

The technical win in this case wasn't just the successful deployment of a third valve (an Acurate neo2), but the specific strategy used to mitigate PPM. The team utilized a "high implantation" technique. Rather than seating the third valve deeply, they positioned it high, aligning it specifically with the leaflets of the previous Evolut valve. The goal was to pin the old, degenerated leaflets against the stent frame of the second valve. If you seat a valve too low in a TAV-in-TAV, the previous leaflets can fold inward, creating what is known as a "neo-skirt"—a ring of displaced tissue that further narrows the lumen and increases the gradient. By implanting high, they minimized this neo-skirt effect and preserved as much flow area as possible.

This wasn't a "wing it" procedure. It was entirely driven by pre-procedural multislice CT (MSCT) and 3D modeling. They needed to know exactly where the coronary ostia were and how the third frame would interact with the existing two. This planning allowed them to avoid coronary protection during the procedure and ensure the Acurate neo2’s supra-annular design was optimized for the narrow space available.

The immediate post-op result was a reminder of how narrow the margin for error is in these cases. Initially, the mean gradient was high (67 mmHg) and the AVA was only 0.9 $\text{cm}^2$, which is textbook severe PPM. However, the clinical magic happened over the following month. As the heart remodeled and the valve settled, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA improved to 1.1 $\text{cm}^2$. One year later, the patient remains asymptomatic with an EF of 65%.

This case serves as a glimpse into the future of structural heart disease. As TAVI patients live longer, we are moving away from "one-and-done" interventions and toward "lifetime management." We are now entering the era of the "redo-redo," where the challenge isn't just putting a valve in, but planning for the third or fourth valve that might be necessary twenty years down the line.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **The Neo-Skirt Effect:** In TAV-in-TAV procedures, seating the new valve too low can push old leaflets inward, creating a "neo-skirt" of tissue that severely restricts the orifice area.
*   **High Implantation Strategy:** Implanting the third valve high can pin previous leaflets against the frame, reducing the neo-skirt effect and mitigating Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM).
*   **CT-Driven Planning:** In multi-prosthesis interventions, MSCT is non-negotiable for assessing coronary ostial height and predicting the interaction between overlapping stent frames.
*   **Hemodynamic Lag:** Initial post-procedural gradients in complex TAV-in-TAV cases may be high due to PPM, but can improve over time as the left ventricle remodels and the prosthesis stabilizes.
*   **Lifetime Management:** As TAVI longevity increases, procedural choices (like commissural alignment) must be made with future re-interventions in mind.



# The Coronary Maze: Preserving Access in a Triple-Stented Aorta

Imagine your patient's aortic root as a Russian nesting doll, but instead of wooden figures, you have three overlapping layers of cobalt-chromium and nitinol. Now imagine that this patient also has three-vessel coronary artery disease. This is the anatomical nightmare of the "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" procedure.

When we perform a TAVI, we often talk about the "neo-skirt"—the excess tissue and displaced leaflets that bunch up at the base of the valve. In a single TAVI, the neo-skirt is a known risk for coronary obstruction. But in a triple-stented aorta, we aren't just dealing with one skirt; we are dealing with a cumulative wall of metal and displaced bioprosthetic tissue that can effectively "wall off" the coronary ostia, turning a future PCI into an impossible mission.

## The Case: A Decade of Valve Iterations

The patient was a man in his late 70s with a complex cardiac history: 3-vessel CAD, COPD, and a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. His journey began in 2014 with a 27mm Lotus valve. By 2022, he developed severe bioprosthetic stenosis and received a TAV-in-TAV with a 23mm Evolut. Shortly after, he hit a major complication: *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis.

While the infection was successfully treated with six weeks of targeted antibiotics, the aftermath was a disaster for his hemodynamics. By 2024, he presented with severe restenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. His aortic valve area (AVA) had plummeted to 0.5 $\text{cm}^2$. With surgical explantation essentially off the table due to the depth of stent integration into the aortic wall and his high comorbid burden, the team opted for a third transcatheter intervention.

## Navigating the Maze: The Strategy

The goal for the third valve (an Acurate neo2) wasn't just to restore flow, but to do so without obstructing the coronaries or permanently sealing them off from future access. The team relied on three critical pillars:

**1. The MSCT Roadmap**
Multislice CT (MSCT) is not optional here; it is the blueprint. The team used MSCT to map the landing zone, measuring the minimum lumen diameter (which was only 13.7mm in the Evolut prosthesis) and calculating the exact distance from the anticipated neo-skirt to the coronary ostia. This allowed them to plan the implantation depth with millimeter precision.

**2. High Implantation Strategy**
A common mistake in TAV-in-TAV is implanting too deep, which pushes the "skirt" of the new valve directly across the coronary ostia. In this case, the team utilized a high implantation strategy. By deploying the Acurate neo2 higher up, they pinned the previous Evolut leaflets against the stent frame. This served two purposes: it prevented the old leaflets from folding inward (which would further reduce the effective orifice area) and it kept the neo-skirt clear of the coronary openings.

**3. Commissural Alignment**
In a triple-stented aorta, the "mesh burden" is immense. If the commissures of the three valves aren't aligned, you create a chaotic web of metal struts that can make it impossible to engage a coronary artery with a guidewire. The team used CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the Acurate neo2 commissures with the previous frames, consciously preserving a "window" for future PCI.

## The Result: Hemodynamic Recovery

The immediate post-procedure TTE showed a high mean gradient (67 mmHg) and an AVA of 0.9 $\text{cm}^2$, suggesting significant patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). However, as is often seen with self-expanding valves and LV remodeling, the hemodynamics improved. At one year, the patient remained asymptomatic with a mean gradient of 26 mmHg and an EF of 65%.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **The "Metal Burden" is Real:** Every subsequent TAV-in-TAV increases the risk of coronary obstruction and makes future coronary access exponentially harder.
*   **High Implantation = Coronary Safety:** In redo-TAVI, aiming for a higher deployment helps prevent the neo-skirt from covering the ostia and minimizes the inward folding of previous leaflets.
*   **CT is the Compass:** Precise measurements of the landing zone and coronary height via MSCT are mandatory for navigating triple-stented anatomy.
*   **Lifetime Management:** We are entering an era where we must plan the *third* valve at the time of the *first*. Commissural alignment isn't just a technical preference; it is a requirement for future viability in patients with known CAD.
*   **PPM may evolve:** Initial post-dilation and LV remodeling can improve the effective orifice area over the first few months following a complex TAV-in-TAV.



# The 'Russian Doll' Strategy: Lifetime Management of Structural Valve Disease

We used to think of TAVI as the "end-game" for patients with severe aortic stenosis—a way to provide quality of life to those too frail for the knife. But as the procedure moves into younger, lower-risk populations and patients live longer, we are entering the era of the "procedural trajectory." We aren't just placing a valve; we are choosing the first step in a potential sequence of interventions. 

Every now and then, we see a case that pushes the boundaries of this trajectory to the absolute limit. Enter the "Russian Doll" scenario: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

## The Case: Three Valves, One Root

A man in his late 70s presented with worsening dyspnea, dizziness, and fatigue. His cardiac history was a roadmap of intervention: three-vessel CAD, multiple stents, a pacemaker, and two prior TAVIs. 

In 2014, he received a 27mm Lotus valve. By 2022, that valve had degenerated, leading to a TAV-in-TAV with a 23mm Evolut. Shortly after, the plot thickened: he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. While the infection was successfully treated with a six-week course of ampicillin-based therapy, the aftermath was a severely stenotic, degenerated prosthesis.

By 2024, he was back in the clinic with a mean gradient of 49 mmHg and an aortic valve area (AVA) of just 0.5 cm². He was clinically failing. With a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and a root essentially reinforced by two layers of metal mesh, open-heart surgery was a suicide mission. The Heart Team decided on a third transcatheter intervention.

## The Technical Chess Match

The challenge with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV isn't just "fitting the valve." It's the cumulative "mesh burden." Every new frame added narrows the effective orifice area (EOA) and increases the risk of coronary artery obstruction. 

The team used multislice CT (MSCT) and 3D modeling to map the landing zone. They identified a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7mm within the existing Evolut prosthesis. To avoid the "neo-skirt" effect—where the prosthetic tissue of the new valve blocks coronary flow—they opted for a high implantation strategy. By positioning a self-expanding Acurate neo2 (Size S) high in the aortic root, they pinned the old leaflets against the existing frame and kept the coronary ostia clear.

The immediate post-op result looked scary: a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and an AVA of 0.9 cm². This is classic Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). However, after a month of LV remodeling and the effects of post-dilation, the gradient dropped to 27 mmHg. At one year, the patient remains asymptomatic with an EF of 65% and a stable, functioning valve.

## Clinical Pearls: Planning for the Future

This case highlights a critical shift in how we view structural heart disease. We can no longer view the index TAVI as an isolated event.

**The Choice of the First Valve Matters.** The "procedural trajectory" begins at the first intervention. The frame height, radial strength, and commissural alignment of the first valve dictate what is possible for the second and third. If you over-expand or choose a frame that severely compromises coronary access early on, you are robbing the patient of future options.

**CT is the Map, not just the Mirror.** In complex redo cases, CT isn't just for sizing; it's for strategic planning. The ability to measure the valve-to-coronary distance and simulate the position of the "neo-skirt" is the difference between a successful outcome and a catastrophic coronary occlusion.

**The PPM Paradox.** In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, the indexed AVA will almost always be low. However, we must distinguish between *hemodynamic* PPM (where the valve is too small for the patient's needs) and *transient* PPM, where the LV needs time to remodel and the valve needs to settle.

## When the Dolls Run Out

What happens if this third valve fails? We are reaching the biological and mechanical limit of "valve-in-valve." When we can no longer fit a smaller valve inside the existing mess, we have to look at "destructive" or "reconstructive" catheter techniques.

Valve fracture (using high-pressure balloons to intentionally crack the frame of a previous valve) can increase the EOA. Similarly, techniques like BASILICA (bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration) may be required to create coronary access when the "Russian Doll" layers have completely walled off the ostia.

## Take-Home Points

*   **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is feasible** but requires meticulous CT-guided planning to avoid coronary obstruction and minimize Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch.
*   **High implantation strategy** can help preserve coronary flow and optimize the effective orifice area by pinning old leaflets against the frame.
*   **Lifetime Management** means thinking about the 3rd valve during the 1st procedure. Consider frame height and coronary access as part of the long-term trajectory.
*   **Multidisciplinary Heart Teams** are non-negotiable in these cases to balance the prohibitive risks of surgical explantation against the technical limits of transcatheter options.
