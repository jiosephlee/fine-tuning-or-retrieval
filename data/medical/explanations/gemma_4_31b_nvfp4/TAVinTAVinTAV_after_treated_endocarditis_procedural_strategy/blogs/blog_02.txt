Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The Shrinking Orifice: Fighting PPM in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Imagine a Matryoshka doll, but instead of carved wood, we’re talking about cobalt-chromium and bovine pericardium. Now imagine that the "dolls" are all fighting for the same few millimeters of space in the aortic root. This is the reality of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—a procedure so rare it's barely documented in the literature, and one that pushes the limits of hemodynamics to the breaking point.

The case involves a man in his late 70s who had already navigated two TAVIs. He started with a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by an Evolut in 2022. Then, things got messy. He developed prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. While the infection was successfully treated with a six-week course of ampicillin, the aftermath was a disaster: severe degeneration and restenosis. By 2024, his aortic valve area (AVA) had shrunk to a staggering 0.5 $\text{cm}^2$. He was symptomatic, failing, and—given his surgical risk and the "metal-on-metal" complexity of his root—totally unsuitable for an open-heart redo.

The central challenge here is the "shrinking orifice." Every time you nest a transcatheter valve inside another, you add layers of stent frames and fabric. This cumulative "mesh burden" doesn't just threaten coronary access; it creates a physical barrier that reduces the effective orifice area, leading to severe Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM). In a triple-stent scenario, you aren't just fighting the native anatomy; you're fighting the ghost of two previous procedures.

The technical win in this case wasn't just the successful deployment of a third valve (an Acurate neo2), but the specific strategy used to mitigate PPM. The team utilized a "high implantation" technique. Rather than seating the third valve deeply, they positioned it high, aligning it specifically with the leaflets of the previous Evolut valve. The goal was to pin the old, degenerated leaflets against the stent frame of the second valve. If you seat a valve too low in a TAV-in-TAV, the previous leaflets can fold inward, creating what is known as a "neo-skirt"—a ring of displaced tissue that further narrows the lumen and increases the gradient. By implanting high, they minimized this neo-skirt effect and preserved as much flow area as possible.

This wasn't a "wing it" procedure. It was entirely driven by pre-procedural multislice CT (MSCT) and 3D modeling. They needed to know exactly where the coronary ostia were and how the third frame would interact with the existing two. This planning allowed them to avoid coronary protection during the procedure and ensure the Acurate neo2’s supra-annular design was optimized for the narrow space available.

The immediate post-op result was a reminder of how narrow the margin for error is in these cases. Initially, the mean gradient was high (67 mmHg) and the AVA was only 0.9 $\text{cm}^2$, which is textbook severe PPM. However, the clinical magic happened over the following month. As the heart remodeled and the valve settled, the mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA improved to 1.1 $\text{cm}^2$. One year later, the patient remains asymptomatic with an EF of 65%.

This case serves as a glimpse into the future of structural heart disease. As TAVI patients live longer, we are moving away from "one-and-done" interventions and toward "lifetime management." We are now entering the era of the "redo-redo," where the challenge isn't just putting a valve in, but planning for the third or fourth valve that might be necessary twenty years down the line.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **The Neo-Skirt Effect:** In TAV-in-TAV procedures, seating the new valve too low can push old leaflets inward, creating a "neo-skirt" of tissue that severely restricts the orifice area.
*   **High Implantation Strategy:** Implanting the third valve high can pin previous leaflets against the frame, reducing the neo-skirt effect and mitigating Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM).
*   **CT-Driven Planning:** In multi-prosthesis interventions, MSCT is non-negotiable for assessing coronary ostial height and predicting the interaction between overlapping stent frames.
*   **Hemodynamic Lag:** Initial post-procedural gradients in complex TAV-in-TAV cases may be high due to PPM, but can improve over time as the left ventricle remodels and the prosthesis stabilizes.
*   **Lifetime Management:** As TAVI longevity increases, procedural choices (like commissural alignment) must be made with future re-interventions in mind.
