Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Morning Report] A patient who has previously undergone a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) presents with fever, new-onset dyspnea, and worsening heart failure. What are the primary diagnostic steps and imaging modalities required to distinguish between structural valve deterioration and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)?

In the evaluation of a patient with a prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) presenting with the triad of fever, new-onset dyspnea, and worsening heart failure, the clinician must urgently differentiate between active prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) and structural valve deterioration (SVD). While both can manifest as severe prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation, the diagnostic approach relies on combining microbiologic evidence with multi-modal imaging.

**Diagnostic Steps and Microbiologic Evaluation**
The primary diagnostic step is the immediate acquisition of multiple sets of blood cultures prior to the initiation of antibiotics. In this case, blood cultures were definitive, identifying *Enterococcus faecalis*, which provided the microbiologic cornerstone for the diagnosis of PVE. Clinical markers such as persistent fever further support an infectious process over isolated mechanical failure.

**Imaging Modalities and Clinical Reasoning**
The distinction between PVE and SVD requires a tiered imaging strategy:

1.  **Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE):** This is the initial screening tool to assess valve hemodynamics. In this patient, TTE first identified severe restenosis (mean gradient 35 mmHg), but it lacked the resolution to definitively distinguish between an infectious vegetation and structural fibrosis.
2.  **Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE):** TEE is the gold standard for evaluating prosthetic valve function and detecting PVE. It provides superior sensitivity for identifying small vegetations, leaflet perforations, or paravalvular abscesses. To distinguish SVD from active PVE, TEE must be used to confirm the *absence* of active vegetations or new paravalvular complications.
3.  **Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT):** MSCT is critical for anatomical precision. While TEE assesses motion, MSCT allows for the measurement of the minimum lumen diameter (which was 13.7 mm in this patient) and the assessment of the valve frame's relationship to the aortic wall. In the setting of suspected PVE, CT can identify perivalvular fluid collections or gas that may be missed on ultrasound.

**Distinguishing Active Infection from Post-Infectious Degeneration**
A critical clinical pearl highlighted by this case is that PVE can lead to SVD. After the patient completed a six-week course of targeted antimicrobial therapy, the active infection was resolved, as confirmed by repeated negative blood cultures, TEE, and MSCT. However, the patient later presented with worsening heart failure and severe stenosis (AVA 0.5 cm²). 

At this stage, the diagnostic challenge shifted: the physician had to determine if the infection had recurred or if the valve had undergone "endocarditis-related degeneration." By using TEE and MSCT to confirm the absence of active vegetations, the team could conclude that the current dysfunction was due to structural failure—likely a combination of leaflet fibrosis and mechanical restriction resulting from the prior infectious episode. 

In summary, the diagnosis of PVE is made through the combination of positive blood cultures and the visualization of vegetations on TEE/MSCT. SVD is diagnosed by the presence of severe hemodynamic compromise in the absence of systemic inflammatory markers, negative cultures, and imaging that shows structural restriction without active infectious foci.
