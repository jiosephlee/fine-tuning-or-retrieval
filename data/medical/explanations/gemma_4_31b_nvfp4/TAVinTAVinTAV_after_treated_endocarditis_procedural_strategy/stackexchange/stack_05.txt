Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] Following the third TAVI in this case, the patient initially exhibited a high mean gradient (67 mmHg) and a low indexed aortic valve area (0.4 cm²/m²), suggesting severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), yet these parameters improved significantly after one month. What physiological or technical factors can explain this early post-procedural hemodynamic evolution?

The early post-procedural hemodynamic evolution observed in this patient—transitioning from severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) immediately after the third TAVI to a significantly improved gradient at one month—can be attributed to a combination of technical mechanical factors and physiological ventricular remodeling.

**Technical Factors: Frame Expansion and Orifice Optimization**
In a "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV" scenario, the cumulative mesh burden of three overlapping stent frames can severely restrict the effective orifice area (EOA). In this case, the implantation of the Acurate neo2 was followed by high-pressure post-dilation using a 20/40 mm balloon. Although intentional frame fracture was not performed, this post-dilation likely induced a secondary radial expansion of the preceding Evolut frame. By mechanically pushing the prior frames further against the aortic wall, the procedure increased the total diameter of the conduit, thereby reducing the transvalvular gradient and increasing the calculated aortic valve area (AVA) from 0.9 cm² to 1.1 cm².

**Physiological Factors: Left Ventricular Remodeling**
The immediate post-operative period is often characterized by hemodynamic instability and LV dysfunction. The case report specifically notes that by one month, "left ventricular remodelling was observed, with a reduction in chamber volumes." 

From a pathophysiology perspective, the mean pressure gradient across a valve is proportional to the square of the flow rate (the Gorlin equation). Immediately after the procedure, a dilated, failing left ventricle may generate high-velocity jets or maintain a high-volume state that exaggerates the gradient. As the LV remodels and chamber volumes decrease, the stroke volume and the flow dynamics stabilize. This reduction in the volume of blood being ejected through the prosthetic orifice, combined with the increased EOA from frame expansion, results in the marked decrease in the mean gradient (from 67 mmHg to 27 mmHg).

In summary, the improvement in this patient's hemodynamics reflects a synergy between the mechanical expansion of nested prosthetic frames via post-dilation and the physiological reduction in LV volumes during the recovery phase.
