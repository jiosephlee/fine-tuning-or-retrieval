Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Pathophysiology of HIT and the aHIT Variant

To understand the clinical progression of this case, it is first necessary to understand the interaction between unfractionated heparin (UFH) and platelet factor 4 (PF4). PF4 is a cationic protein released from the alpha-granules of platelets. When UFH—a polyanionic molecule—is administered, it binds to PF4, forming complexes. In a small percentage of patients, this complex is recognized as foreign, leading to the production of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies against the PF4/heparin complex.

In classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), these IgG antibodies bind to the PF4/heparin complexes on the surface of platelets. The "tail" (Fc portion) of the IgG antibody then interacts with the Fc$\gamma$RIIa receptors on the platelet membrane. This interaction causes the receptors to cluster, which triggers massive platelet activation. This activation leads to a paradoxical state: while platelets are consumed and their numbers drop (thrombocytopenia), the activated platelets release potent procoagulant microparticles that generate thrombin, creating a highly prothrombotic environment. This explains why the patient in this case developed an acute cerebral infarct despite a rapidly falling platelet count.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is a more severe variant of this disease. In classic HIT, the antibodies require heparin to act as a bridge to activate the platelet. However, in aHIT, the antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. This means they can bind to and activate platelets even in the absence of exogenous heparin. This mechanism explains the patient's atypical serotonin-release assay (SRA) results, where strong serotonin release was observed at 0 U/mL of heparin. Because these antibodies do not require heparin for activation, aHIT typically presents with a more aggressive clinical course, characterized by more profound thrombocytopenia and a higher propensity for severe thrombosis.

The severity of aHIT is often reflected in its resistance to standard laboratory inhibition. In a typical HIT diagnostic profile, a monoclonal antibody (such as IV.3) that blocks the Fc receptor should almost entirely inhibit platelet activation. In this case, the patient exhibited a unique serological phenotype where the IV.3 antibody only partially inhibited serotonin release. This suggests an unusually potent form of aHIT where the antibodies are so reactive that the standard blockade of the Fc receptor is insufficient to stop platelet activation.

Clinically, this variant is distinguished by its ability to cause severe consumptive coagulopathy. While classic HIT usually presents with a moderate drop in platelets, the patient in this case reached a nadir of $13 \times 10^9$/L. The subsequent appearance of schistocytes (red cell fragments) and a rise in d-dimer levels during argatroban treatment further indicate the extreme hypercoagulability associated with aHIT, where the continuous activation of platelets and the coagulation cascade lead to the consumption of clotting factors and platelets alike.

## Clinical Presentation and Early Diagnostic Workup

Thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery is a common clinical finding, often resulting from hemodilution during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) or transient platelet dysfunction. However, a progressive and severe decline in platelet count—as seen in this patient, whose count dropped from 155 $\times 10^9$/L on postoperative day 1 (POD1) to 50 $\times 10^9$/L by POD5—should prompt a rigorous investigation into more serious etiologies. In the postoperative period, clinicians must distinguish between benign transient drops and life-threatening conditions such as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), or sepsis-induced coagulopathy.

A critical component of the diagnostic reasoning for HIT is the "temporal window." Classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 following cardiac surgery. In this case, the platelet count began to fall earlier than expected. This accelerated onset can be explained by preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH). This patient received 2000 U of UFH during a diagnostic heart catheterization one week prior to surgery. This initial exposure likely primed the immune system, allowing the subsequent high-dose UFH administered during CPB to act as a booster, thereby triggering an earlier and more aggressive immune response than is typically seen in heparin-naive patients.

The clinical suspicion of HIT was further heightened by the development of a systemic thrombotic event. While HIT is defined by a drop in platelets, its most dangerous manifestation is a paradoxical hypercoagulable state. The patient’s worsening postoperative delirium and subsequent CT imaging, which revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe, provided a critical clinical clue. In the context of falling platelets, an acute stroke should be viewed as a potential manifestation of HIT-associated thrombosis rather than a simple postoperative complication, necessitating urgent anticoagulation and serological testing.

The initial diagnostic workup focused on ruling out other causes of severe thrombocytopenia. Sepsis and cardiogenic shock were unlikely given the patient's hemodynamic stability and negative blood and urine cultures. TTP was ruled out based on the absence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film and a normal haptoglobin level, indicating the absence of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was considered but deemed unlikely because the timeline did not align with the administration of postoperative medications. Additionally, since the patient received no blood transfusions, post-transfusion purpura was excluded.

To confirm HIT, the medical team utilized a PF4/polyanion ELISA, a screening immunoassay that detects IgG antibodies against the platelet factor 4 (PF4)/heparin complex. This test is highly sensitive but lacks the specificity to be diagnostic on its own. In this patient, the ELISA was strongly positive (2.2 OD units), which, when combined with the temporal history of heparin exposure and the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke, made HIT the most probable diagnosis. This high-sensitivity screening result served as the trigger for more specific confirmatory testing via the serotonin-release assay (SRA).

## Advanced Diagnostics: The Serotonin Release Assay (SRA)

While screening immunoassays like the PF4/polyanion ELISA are highly sensitive for detecting heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies, they cannot determine if those antibodies are functionally active. To confirm the diagnosis, clinicians rely on functional assays—the gold standard of which is the serotonin-release assay (SRA). The SRA measures the amount of serotonin released by donor platelets when exposed to the patient's serum and varying concentrations of unfractionated heparin (UFH). In classic HIT, antibodies only activate platelets in the presence of heparin, which acts as a bridge between the antibody and the platelet factor 4 (PF4) on the platelet surface.

Interpreting SRA profiles allows clinicians to differentiate between classic HIT and autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In a typical HIT profile, serotonin release is absent at 0 U/mL of heparin but becomes strong at low concentrations (e.g., 0.1 to 0.3 U/mL). Notably, at very high concentrations of heparin (e.g., 100 U/mL), serotonin release is inhibited because the excess heparin prevents the formation of the immune complexes necessary for platelet activation. In this case, the patient’s serum showed a characteristic lack of activation at 100 U/mL heparin, but it demonstrated a profound atypicality: 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. This "heparin-independent" activation is the hallmark of aHIT, where antibodies can activate platelets without the need for a heparin bridge.

To further characterize the mechanism of activation, laboratories use the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Because classic HIT antibodies activate platelets by clustering these Fc receptors, the addition of IV.3 typically inhibits serotonin release to less than 20%. In this patient's case, the SRA was initially labeled "indeterminate" because IV.3 failed to fully suppress the reaction, with 76% of serotonin still being released. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade indicated that the patient's antibodies were unusually reactive, potentially utilizing alternative pathways or possessing an affinity for the Fc receptor that surpassed the blocking capacity of the monoclonal antibody.

The significance of this unique serological profile lies in its correlation with clinical severity. The patient experienced a profound platelet nadir of $13 \times 10^9/\text{L}$, developed a thrombotic stroke, and showed an unusually poor response to high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), which typically corrects thrombocytopenia in aHIT patients. This case demonstrates that aHIT exists on a spectrum of severity; a "hyper-reactive" antibody profile—characterized by heparin-independent activation and resistance to Fc blockade—can lead to a more aggressive clinical course and more challenging diagnostic interpretation.

## Management of aHIT and Hypercoagulability

The primary goal in managing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), and specifically its autoimmune variant (aHIT), is the immediate cessation of all heparin products and the initiation of a non-heparin anticoagulant to mitigate the high risk of arterial and venous thrombosis. In this case, the clinical team faced a complex balancing act: the patient was in a profound hypercoagulable state (evidenced by a thrombotic stroke) but also possessed an extreme bleeding risk due to a platelet nadir of $13 \times 10^9/\text{L}$ and a recent cerebral infarct.

Initially, argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI), was selected for anticoagulation. DTIs are preferred in HIT because they do not react with HIT antibodies. Argatroban was specifically chosen over other options because of its short half-life, which allows for rapid reversal or dose adjustment should the patient experience a hemorrhagic transformation of the stroke or other bleeding complications. However, the patient’s clinical course suggested that argatroban provided insufficient control of the underlying thrombotic process. The development of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy—characterized by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer levels, and the appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film—indicated that thrombin generation was continuing unabated, leading to the consumption of clotting factors and microangiopathic hemolysis.

To address the autoimmune component of aHIT, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered. In classic aHIT, IVIg typically prompts a brisk recovery of the platelet count by blocking the Fc$\gamma$RIIA receptors on platelets, thereby preventing the antibodies from triggering platelet activation and subsequent clearance. In this patient, the response to IVIg was unusually modest (platelets increasing from $13 \times 10^9/\text{L}$ to $54 \times 10^9/\text{L}$), reflecting a particularly severe serological phenotype where the antibodies exhibited partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Given the persistence of hypercoagulability despite argatroban, the treatment was switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux, a potent selective factor Xa inhibitor. Unlike DTIs, fondaparinux is an indirect anticoagulant that requires antithrombin (AT) as a cofactor to inhibit factor Xa. Because the patient’s AT levels were mildly reduced ($0.70\text{ U/mL}$), a supplement of AT concentrate ($1000\text{ U}$) was administered prior to the first dose of fondaparinux. This step is clinically critical; without adequate AT levels, fondaparinux cannot effectively bind to factor Xa, rendering the anticoagulation ineffective. Following this transition, the patient showed a gradual recovery of platelet counts and complete neurological resolution, demonstrating that aggressive, targeted anticoagulation and AT optimization were necessary to overcome the severe hypercoagulable state of aHIT.

## Clinical Synthesis and Lessons Learned

The clinical course of this patient underscores the complexity of diagnosing and managing autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) in the perioperative setting. Following an initial course of severe thrombocytopenia and a thrombotic stroke, the patient achieved a full recovery of both platelet counts and neurological function. This recovery was facilitated by a transition from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux—driven by evidence of persisting hypercoagulability (rising d-dimers, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytosis)—and the administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The eventual return to long-term anticoagulation with apixaban marked the resolution of the acute crisis.

A critical pedagogical lesson from this case is the importance of a meticulous medication history to explain atypical timing of HIT. While HIT traditionally manifests between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10, this patient experienced a platelet decline starting on POD1. The diagnostic breakthrough occurred when the clinical team identified preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) during a heart catheterization one week prior to surgery. This demonstrates that the "clock" for HIT begins at the first exposure to heparin, not necessarily the start of the most recent surgical procedure. Clinicians should remain vigilant for HIT even when the postoperative timeline seems too short, provided there was prior heparin exposure.

This case also highlights the "red flags" that distinguish aHIT from classic HIT. While classic HIT is characterized by heparin-dependent platelet activation, aHIT involves antibodies that can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. Clinically, aHIT often presents with more profound thrombocytopenia (reaching a nadir of $13 \times 10^9$/L in this patient) and a higher propensity for severe thrombosis, such as the parietal stroke seen here. Furthermore, aHIT may exhibit a refractory response to IVIG; unlike typical aHIT cases where IVIG causes a brisk rise in platelets, this patient showed only a modest increase, signaling a particularly severe serological phenotype.

The longitudinal monitoring of this patient provides a rare insight into the evolution of HIT antibodies. The serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) results demonstrated that the patient’s antibodies transitioned over time. Initially, the profile was characteristic of aHIT (strong serotonin release without heparin), but by POD124, the profile had shifted toward a predominantly heparin-dependent state. This suggests that the autoimmune, heparin-independent properties of the antibodies may wane as the patient recovers, eventually returning to a more typical HIT phenotype.

Finally, this case introduces a novel serological finding: a profile of partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade (IV.3 monoclonal antibody) during the SRA. In most HIT cases, blocking the Fc receptor significantly inhibits serotonin release. The fact that this patient's serum maintained significant activation despite blockade indicates an unusually reactive antibody. For the clinician and the pathologist, this serves as a warning that "indeterminate" or atypical SRA results should not be dismissed; instead, they may represent a highly aggressive form of aHIT requiring intensified management to prevent catastrophic thrombotic events.
