Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient following cardiac surgery, what are the most common causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia, and which of these should be considered in the differential diagnosis for this patient?

Postoperative thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery is a diagnostic challenge, as the etiology can range from benign, transient consumption to life-threatening immune-mediated prothrombotic states.

### Common Causes of Postoperative Thrombocytopenia
In the general postoperative cardiac surgery population, the most frequent causes include:
1.  **Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB) Effect:** Platelets are activated and consumed due to contact with the non-endothelial surfaces of the bypass circuit. This typically manifests as an immediate, transient drop in platelet count.
2.  **Critical Illness and Sepsis:** Severe systemic inflammation or septic shock can lead to thrombocytopenia via disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) or direct bone marrow suppression.
3.  **Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT):** An immune-mediated reaction to unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), characterized by antibodies against the PF4-heparin complex.
4.  **Drug-Induced Immune Thrombocytopenia:** Reactions to postoperative medications (e.g., certain antibiotics).
5.  **Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TMA):** Including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or hemoglobinuria-palliative hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS).
6.  **Post-Transfusion Purpura:** An immune reaction occurring in patients who received platelet transfusions.

### Differential Diagnosis for this Patient
In the context of this specific case, the differential is narrowed by the clinical and laboratory evidence:

*   **Excluded via Clinical Course:** Cardiogenic shock and sepsis were ruled out by the patient's normal hemodynamics and negative blood/urine cultures. Post-transfusion purpura was eliminated as the patient received no blood transfusions.
*   **Excluded via Laboratory Findings:** TTP was considered unlikely due to the normal haptoglobin levels and initial absence of schistocytes on the peripheral smear. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was deemed improbable because the platelet decline occurred too early to be attributed to the postoperative medications administered.
*   **The Role of HIT:** HIT must be strongly considered given the strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA and the development of a thrombotic stroke, which is a hallmark of the HIT-associated hypercoagulable state. 

**Clinical Pearl: The "Temporal Window" of HIT**
Classically, HIT occurs 5 to 10 days after heparin exposure. In this patient, the platelet count began falling as early as postoperative day 1 (POD1), which is unusually early for a patient whose primary heparin exposure was intraoperative. However, the clinician must look for **prior heparin exposure**. This patient underwent heart catheterization one week prior to surgery, where UFH was administered. This preoperative exposure "primed" the immune system, shifting the temporal window and causing the thrombocytopenia to manifest almost immediately after the surgical dose of heparin.

Ultimately, the diagnosis was **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**, a severe variant where antibodies can activate platelets independently of heparin. This was confirmed by the serotonin-release assay (SRA) showing platelet activation even in the absence of heparin, and the patient's profound nadir (13×10⁹/L) and refractory nature to standard therapy.
