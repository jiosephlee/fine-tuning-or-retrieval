Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## Management of aHIT and Hypercoagulability

The primary goal in managing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), and specifically its autoimmune variant (aHIT), is the immediate cessation of all heparin products and the initiation of a non-heparin anticoagulant to mitigate the high risk of arterial and venous thrombosis. In this case, the clinical team faced a complex balancing act: the patient was in a profound hypercoagulable state (evidenced by a thrombotic stroke) but also possessed an extreme bleeding risk due to a platelet nadir of $13 \times 10^9/\text{L}$ and a recent cerebral infarct.

Initially, argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI), was selected for anticoagulation. DTIs are preferred in HIT because they do not react with HIT antibodies. Argatroban was specifically chosen over other options because of its short half-life, which allows for rapid reversal or dose adjustment should the patient experience a hemorrhagic transformation of the stroke or other bleeding complications. However, the patient’s clinical course suggested that argatroban provided insufficient control of the underlying thrombotic process. The development of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy—characterized by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer levels, and the appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film—indicated that thrombin generation was continuing unabated, leading to the consumption of clotting factors and microangiopathic hemolysis.

To address the autoimmune component of aHIT, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered. In classic aHIT, IVIg typically prompts a brisk recovery of the platelet count by blocking the Fc$\gamma$RIIA receptors on platelets, thereby preventing the antibodies from triggering platelet activation and subsequent clearance. In this patient, the response to IVIg was unusually modest (platelets increasing from $13 \times 10^9/\text{L}$ to $54 \times 10^9/\text{L}$), reflecting a particularly severe serological phenotype where the antibodies exhibited partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Given the persistence of hypercoagulability despite argatroban, the treatment was switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux, a potent selective factor Xa inhibitor. Unlike DTIs, fondaparinux is an indirect anticoagulant that requires antithrombin (AT) as a cofactor to inhibit factor Xa. Because the patient’s AT levels were mildly reduced ($0.70\text{ U/mL}$), a supplement of AT concentrate ($1000\text{ U}$) was administered prior to the first dose of fondaparinux. This step is clinically critical; without adequate AT levels, fondaparinux cannot effectively bind to factor Xa, rendering the anticoagulation ineffective. Following this transition, the patient showed a gradual recovery of platelet counts and complete neurological resolution, demonstrating that aggressive, targeted anticoagulation and AT optimization were necessary to overcome the severe hypercoagulable state of aHIT.
