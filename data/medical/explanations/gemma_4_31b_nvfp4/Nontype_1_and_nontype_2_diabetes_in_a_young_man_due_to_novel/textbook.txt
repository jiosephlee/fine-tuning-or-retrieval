## Understanding MODY and HNF1A Pathophysiology

Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) represents a group of monogenic diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in the development and function of pancreatic $\beta$-cells. Unlike type 1 diabetes, MODY is non-autoimmune, and unlike type 2 diabetes, it is not primarily driven by insulin resistance or polygenic metabolic factors. MODY is inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion, meaning a mutation in a single allele is sufficient to cause the disease. This inheritance pattern typically manifests as a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations, as seen in this case where the patient’s grandmother, mother, and brother were all affected.

While several genes can cause MODY, the most common subtypes involve mutations in the *HNF1A*, *HNF4A*, *HNF1B*, and glucokinase (*GCK*) genes. Of these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY cases. These subtypes are distinguished by their unique genetic origins and their specific effects on glucose homeostasis and organ function.

The molecular basis of HNF1A-MODY lies in the role of the Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) protein. HNF1A acts as a transcription factor—a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences to control the transcription of other genes—and is expressed predominantly in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreatic $\beta$-cells, HNF1A is critical for the expression of genes essential for glucose sensing and insulin secretion, including the insulin gene itself and the *GLUT2* gene, which encodes the primary glucose transporter in $\beta$-cells. When a pathogenic mutation occurs in *HNF1A*, the resulting protein dysfunction leads to the impaired expression of these genes, causing a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function. This molecular failure manifests clinically as glucose intolerance and insufficient insulin secretion, particularly evident during postprandial glucose challenges.

Beyond the pancreas, HNF1A plays a significant role in renal physiology. It transcriptionally regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. SGLT2 is responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption from the glomerular filtrate back into the bloodstream. In HNF1A-MODY, the reduced expression of SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why patients with this specific subtype often present with glycosuria (glucose in the urine) even when blood glucose levels are not severely elevated, a key diagnostic clue that differentiates it from other forms of diabetes.

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The initial presentation of this patient highlights the diagnostic challenge of differentiating Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) from more common forms of diabetes. The patient, a young adult, presented with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia: increased thirst (polydipsia), increased appetite (polyphagia), and recent weight loss. While these symptoms are common to all forms of diabetes, two specific clinical markers served as important "red flags." First, the patient exhibited glycosuria, which in HNF1A-MODY is often more pronounced due to a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Second, and most critically, the patient lacked diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The absence of DKA in a young person with significant hyperglycemia and weight loss should prompt clinicians to look beyond Type 1 diabetes, as MODY patients generally maintain enough endogenous insulin secretion to prevent ketosis.

A thorough family history is perhaps the most powerful tool in identifying monogenic diabetes. In this case, the patient's history revealed a clear autosomal dominant pattern: his maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother were all diagnosed with diabetes, with onset occurring between their 20s and 30s. When diabetes appears in multiple generations across a young age of onset (typically <25 years), it strongly suggests a hereditary monogenic etiology rather than the polygenic or autoimmune causes seen in Type 2 or Type 1 diabetes.

The case also illustrates a "metabolic syndrome paradox" often encountered in modern clinics. Traditionally, MODY is associated with a lean phenotype and an absence of insulin resistance, contrasting with the obesity and acanthosis nigricans (darkened skin folds) typically seen in Type 2 diabetes. While this patient lacked acanthosis nigricans and had a relatively normal BMI (24.6 kg/m²), his lipid profile revealed high triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL (1.01 mmol/L). This demonstrates that while BMI may be lower than in classic Type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia can still occur, and the absence of obesity alone is not sufficient to rule out Type 2 diabetes or confirm MODY.

The initial laboratory findings confirmed severe hyperglycemia. The patient's glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was significantly elevated at 10.2%, and his fasting glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. A 75g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) showed a 2-hour glucose level of 19.8 mmol/L, indicating a profound failure of the $\beta$-cells to respond to a glucose load. This marked postprandial hyperglycemia is a hallmark of HNF1A-MODY, reflecting the progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function and insulin secretion.

Finally, the evaluation identified early evidence of diabetic complications. Sudomotor testing via electrochemical skin conductance revealed severely reduced function in the hands. This indicates autonomic neuropathy, specifically affecting the small fiber nerves. This finding serves as a reminder that although MODY is monogenic and may have a slower onset than Type 1 diabetes, it is a progressive disease that can lead to microvascular and autonomic complications if glycemic control is not optimized.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and the Workup Process

Differentiating between the various forms of diabetes in a young patient requires a systematic approach, as Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) is frequently misdiagnosed as either type 1 diabetes (T1D) or type 2 diabetes (T2D) due to overlapping clinical features. In this case, the diagnostic reasoning began with the identification of hyperglycemia and glycosuria in a young adult with a significant family history of early-onset diabetes. Because MODY is an autosomal dominant monogenic disorder, the presence of diabetes across three generations (grandmother, mother, and brother) strongly suggested a hereditary form of the disease rather than the sporadic nature of T1D or the polygenic, lifestyle-influenced nature of T2D.

The first step in the differential diagnosis is to exclude T1D by assessing for autoimmune markers. Patients with T1D typically possess autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A), or zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8). In this patient, both GADA and IA-2A were negative, shifting the suspicion away from an autoimmune etiology. To distinguish the case from T2D, clinicians look for signs of insulin resistance, such as a high BMI, acanthosis nigricans, or a clear metabolic syndrome. While this patient exhibited hypertriglyceridemia, the absence of acanthosis nigricans and a relatively normal BMI (24.6 $\text{kg/m}^2$) made T2D less likely, despite the increasing prevalence of obesity in MODY populations.

To further evaluate the status of pancreatic $\beta$-cell function, the glucagon stimulation test was employed. Unlike a fasting C-peptide level, which can be misleading in early-stage T1D, the glucagon stimulation test probes the $\beta$-cell's functional reserve by measuring the C-peptide response after a glucagon injection. A stimulated C-peptide level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. This patient’s result of 0.83 nmol/L confirmed that while his insulin secretion was insufficient to maintain euglycemia, he had not undergone the absolute $\beta$-cell loss characteristic of T1D, further supporting a diagnosis of MODY.

Clinicians may use tools like the MODY probability calculator (developed by the Exeter group) to prioritize genetic testing. In this case, the calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY. However, students should note a significant limitation: these algorithms are primarily derived from White European populations and may not be as accurate for patients of Asian or other ethnic backgrounds, potentially overestimating or underestimating risk. Consequently, these tools are meant to guide clinical suspicion rather than serve as definitive diagnostic markers.

The definitive diagnosis of monogenic diabetes is achieved through molecular genetic testing, typically using Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS). NGS allows for the simultaneous screening of multiple genes associated with MODY (such as *HNF1A*, *GCK*, and *HNF4A*) in a single assay. In this patient, NGS identified a heterozygous substitution in exon 4 of the *HNF1A* gene (p.N270S). Because this specific mutation had not been previously reported in databases like ClinVar or VarSome, the clinical team had to determine its pathogenicity using the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines.

Analyzing a novel variant requires integrating multiple lines of evidence. The pathogenicity of the p.N270S mutation was supported by its location within the DNA-binding domain of the HNF1A protein, its segregation with the disease within the family, and a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), which is an AI-based tool used to predict the effect of missense variants on protein function. By applying these standards, the variant was classified as likely pathogenic, confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and allowing for a precision medicine approach to treatment.

## Management Strategies and Precision Medicine

In the initial phase of this case, the patient was treated empirically based on a suspected diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Given the patient's significantly elevated HbA1c (10.2%) and a lipid profile suggestive of metabolic syndrome, clinicians initiated a combination of basal insulin degludec (8 units/day), metformin (1500 mg/day), and saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This regimen aimed to achieve rapid glycemic control while addressing potential insulin resistance and reducing glucose output. However, the patient's strong family history of early-onset diabetes and negative islet autoantibodies pointed toward a monogenic form of diabetes, necessitating a shift from a general empirical approach to precision medicine.

Once genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic variant in *HNF1A* (p.N270S), the management strategy was tailored to the specific pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY. The goal of treatment in this condition is to optimize insulin secretion and maintain $\beta$-cell function. While low-dose sulfonylureas are traditionally the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY—acting on potassium-sensitive ATP channels to stimulate insulin release—GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) provide a safe and effective alternative. In this case, the patient was transitioned from insulin to dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly). GLP-1 RAs are particularly useful in patients with declining $\beta$-cell function as they offer effective glycemic control with a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas.

The precision medicine approach also involves identifying medications that may be contraindicated. In HNF1A-MODY, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors is cautioned. The *HNF1A* gene transcriptionally controls the expression of SGLT2 in the renal tubules; consequently, patients with this mutation already exhibit reduced SGLT2 expression and a lower renal threshold for glucose, explaining the glycosuria seen in this patient. Further pharmacological inhibition of SGLT2 may lead to severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and an increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis.

The clinical outcome of this tailored approach was highly successful. After the discontinuation of insulin and the introduction of dulaglutide, the patient's HbA1c decreased from 9.1% to 6.0% over six months. Once glycemic stability was achieved, the regimen was simplified to a maintenance dose of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This transition from an insulin-heavy, generic T2D regimen to a targeted non-insulin approach demonstrates the critical value of genetic diagnosis in avoiding unnecessary insulin therapy and selecting the most physiologically appropriate agents for the patient.

## Clinical Significance and Lessons for Practice

The misdiagnosis of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) as either type 1 (T1D) or type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a common clinical challenge due to overlapping presentations. In this case, the patient was initially treated for T2D with a regimen of basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin. Such a misdiagnosis carries significant implications for both quality of life and safety. For instance, patients misdiagnosed with T1D may be subjected to lifelong, unnecessary insulin therapy, while those misdiagnosed with T2D may be prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors. In HNF1A-MODY, the expression of SGLT2 is already transcriptionally reduced; adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can exacerbate the risk of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, making an accurate diagnosis a matter of patient safety.

This case underscores the necessity of genetic screening in young adults who present with diabetes of an unclear etiology. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MODY when a patient exhibits a strong autosomal dominant family history, presents before age 25, and lacks markers of autoimmunity (such as GADA and IA-2A antibodies). The preserved endogenous insulin secretion in this patient—evidenced by a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L—was a critical biochemical clue that shifted the diagnostic focus away from T1D and toward a monogenic cause.

A particular challenge in precision endocrinology is the discovery of novel genetic variants. In this report, the p.N270S mutation was absent from major databases like ClinVar or VarSome. When encountering a variant of unknown significance, clinicians and geneticists must rely on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. Pathogenicity in this case was established by analyzing the mutation's location within the DNA-binding domain, its high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and the fact that the variant segregated with the disease within the family. This illustrates that the absence of a mutation in a database does not preclude its clinical relevance.

The transition from a general diabetes regimen to a genetically tailored approach represents the core of precision medicine. By identifying the HNF1A mutation, the medical team was able to discontinue insulin and implement a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide). Because HNF1A-MODY involves a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function and a specific sensitivity to insulin secretagogues, GLP-1 RAs provide an effective alternative to sulfonylureas with a lower risk of hypoglycemia. This targeted approach led to a rapid and significant reduction in HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0%.

Finally, while the genetic diagnosis allows for optimized pharmacological therapy, it does not eliminate the risk of diabetic complications. Despite the patient's positive response to the revised treatment and the absence of microalbuminuria or retinopathy at the time of reporting, long-term surveillance remains mandatory. This case serves as a reminder that the goal of precision medicine is not only to select the correct medication but to provide a framework for the lifelong monitoring of microvascular complications tailored to the patient's specific genetic driver.
