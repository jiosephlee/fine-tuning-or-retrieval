Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Understanding MODY and HNF1A Pathophysiology

Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) represents a group of monogenic diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in the development and function of pancreatic $\beta$-cells. Unlike type 1 diabetes, MODY is non-autoimmune, and unlike type 2 diabetes, it is not primarily driven by insulin resistance or polygenic metabolic factors. MODY is inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion, meaning a mutation in a single allele is sufficient to cause the disease. This inheritance pattern typically manifests as a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations, as seen in this case where the patient’s grandmother, mother, and brother were all affected.

While several genes can cause MODY, the most common subtypes involve mutations in the *HNF1A*, *HNF4A*, *HNF1B*, and glucokinase (*GCK*) genes. Of these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY cases. These subtypes are distinguished by their unique genetic origins and their specific effects on glucose homeostasis and organ function.

The molecular basis of HNF1A-MODY lies in the role of the Hepatocyte Nuclear Factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) protein. HNF1A acts as a transcription factor—a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences to control the transcription of other genes—and is expressed predominantly in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreatic $\beta$-cells, HNF1A is critical for the expression of genes essential for glucose sensing and insulin secretion, including the insulin gene itself and the *GLUT2* gene, which encodes the primary glucose transporter in $\beta$-cells. When a pathogenic mutation occurs in *HNF1A*, the resulting protein dysfunction leads to the impaired expression of these genes, causing a progressive decline in $\beta$-cell function. This molecular failure manifests clinically as glucose intolerance and insufficient insulin secretion, particularly evident during postprandial glucose challenges.

Beyond the pancreas, HNF1A plays a significant role in renal physiology. It transcriptionally regulates the expression of the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal tubules. SGLT2 is responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption from the glomerular filtrate back into the bloodstream. In HNF1A-MODY, the reduced expression of SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why patients with this specific subtype often present with glycosuria (glucose in the urine) even when blood glucose levels are not severely elevated, a key diagnostic clue that differentiates it from other forms of diabetes.
