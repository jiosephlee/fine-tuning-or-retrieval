Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Wait, They're Coming Back? Navigating Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We’ve all been there: the high-stakes adrenaline of the ED, the prompt administration of epinephrine, the dramatic reversal of hypotension and airway edema, and the satisfying feeling of a "win." You’ve stabilized the patient, the rash is fading, and you’re thinking about the disposition. Then, hours later, the patient crashes. Again.

Usually, we talk about *biphasic* anaphylaxis—the classic "second wave" that hits after an initial resolution. But every so often, you run into a case that defies the standard textbook narrative. Today, we’re looking at a case of *multiphasic* anaphylaxis that serves as a sobering reminder that sometimes, the "win" is only temporary.

## The Case: The Revolving Door of Shock

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED in full-blown anaphylactic shock after taking a single 500mg dose of oral clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxemic (88% on room air), and covered in urticaria and facial swelling. The team acted quickly: 0.3mg of IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV antihistamines, oxygen, and fluid resuscitation.

The response was textbook. Blood pressure normalized, the swelling went down, and the patient was admitted to a monitored ward. But the story didn't end there. 

Over the next 12 hours, the patient suffered *three additional* episodes of anaphylactic shock. Despite repeated doses of IM epinephrine and the administration of corticosteroids, she kept cycling back into crisis. While the intensity of each episode gradually diminished—moving from deep hypoxemia and cyanosis to primarily dyspnea and rash—the clinical instability was profound. She eventually required ICU admission for close monitoring.

After 24 hours of stability, the cycling finally stopped. Workup revealed elevated baseline and peak tryptase levels, suggesting a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though she didn't meet all the formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

## The Biphasic (and Multiphasic) Dilemma

Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the recurrence of symptoms after initial resolution, without re-exposure to the allergen. While the reported incidence varies wildly (from 0.4% to 23%), most data suggests it happens in about 4-6% of cases. This case takes it a step further into "multiphasic" territory, which is exceptionally rare and leaves clinicians in a difficult spot: do you treat preemptively with an epinephrine infusion, or do you wait and treat each wave as it comes?

The most frustrating part about biphasic reactions is that we aren't great at predicting them. Some suggest that a severe initial presentation or the need for multiple epinephrine doses are red flags, but there is no gold-standard predictor that allows us to confidently send a patient home after a few hours of observation.

## The Steroid Myth and the Dosing Trap

This case highlights two common pitfalls in anaphylaxis management: the reliance on corticosteroids and the "auto-injector dose" trap.

For years, we were taught that steroids prevent biphasic reactions. However, the evidence has shifted. Recent systematic reviews show virtually no benefit in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, and some data even suggests that the side effects of steroids may complicate the clinical picture. In this case, steroids were given, but they didn't stop the multiphasic course.

Then there is the dosing. In this patient, the first two doses of epinephrine were 0.3mg via auto-injector. For an adult weighing 104kg, 0.3mg is an underdose. The standard adult dose is 0.5mg. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing "treatment hesitancy" and speeding up delivery in the ED, they can leave a significant portion of the adult population under-treated. A 2025 retrospective study noted that patients receiving 0.3mg were significantly more likely to require escalated care than those receiving 0.5mg.

## The Tryptase Window

Finally, let's talk about the diagnostics. If you suspect a mast cell disorder like systemic mastocytosis, timing is everything. Tryptase levels peak quickly. To get an accurate picture, you need a "sandwich" of samples: 
1. As soon as feasible (without delaying treatment).
2. 1–2 hours after symptom onset.
3. A baseline sample at least 24 hours after full resolution.

In this case, the first sample wasn't drawn until the third episode, missing the window for the initial reaction. If you have a patient who is "refractory" or "cycling," get that tryptase early.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Observation is Key:** Biphasic reactions typically occur within 24 hours, but can happen up to 78 hours later. There is no "safe" short window for high-risk patients.
*   **Dose for the Patient, Not the Device:** If your patient is over 50kg, 0.3mg is likely an underdose. Don't let the convenience of an auto-injector dictate your dosing strategy in a clinical setting.
*   **Steroids $\neq$ Prevention:** Current evidence suggests corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Use them for their other indications, but don't rely on them to "stop the second wave."
*   **Tryptase Timing:** To diagnose mast cell disorders, you need peak and baseline levels. Don't wait until the patient is stable to start thinking about the workup.
*   **Multiphasic is Rare but Real:** If a patient keeps crashing despite adequate epinephrine, consider underlying conditions like systemic mastocytosis and consider ICU-level monitoring.



# The Auto-Injector Trap: Is 0.3mg Enough for Adults?

We’ve all been there: the "textbook" anaphylaxis presentation. Epinephrine is slammed, the wheezing stops, the blood pressure recovers, and the patient looks great. You start thinking about discharge or a short observation window, only for the patient to crash again three hours later. Now imagine that happening not once, but three more times.

That is the nightmare of multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it highlights a dangerous gap between our "convenience" tools and our clinical guidelines.

## The Case: A Recurrent Nightmare

A woman in her 60s (weight 104 kg) presented to the ED in anaphylactic shock after taking a single dose of clarithromycin. She was hypotensive, hypoxemic, and covered in urticaria. The team acted fast, administering 0.3 mg of epinephrine via an auto-injector, along with fluids and oxygen.

The initial response was "textbook." She stabilized, the facial swelling subsided, and she was moved to an acute internal ward. But the victory was short-lived. Over the next 12 hours, she suffered three additional episodes of anaphylactic shock. Each relapse required more epinephrine to resolve. By the time she reached the ICU, the team was grappling with a multiphasic course that defied the typical "one and done" or even the "biphasic" pattern.

Ultimately, she stabilized and was discharged, but workup revealed a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis—an underlying condition that essentially "primes" the body for more severe and prolonged mediator release.

## The Trap: Convenience vs. Dose

Here is where we need to talk about the "Auto-Injector Trap." In this case, the first two doses of epinephrine were delivered via 0.3 mg auto-injectors. While these devices are fantastic for reducing treatment delay and removing the "fear factor" for staff who aren't comfortable drawing up meds, they create a dosing discrepancy for the average adult.

The standard guideline recommendation for adult IM epinephrine is 0.5 mg (or 0.01 mg/kg). For a 104 kg patient, 0.3 mg is a significant underdose. 

We often default to the auto-injector because it’s fast and "safe," but for patients over 50 kg, we are essentially providing a sub-therapeutic dose. Recent data suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg are significantly more likely to require escalation of care or repeated dosing than those who receive the full 0.5 mg. In a patient with an underlying mast cell disorder, underdosing isn't just a minor error—it's a recipe for a refractory or multiphasic course.

## The Steroid Myth and the Monitoring Gap

While the team in this case used corticosteroids (prednisone and dexamethasone) to prevent further relapses, the evidence suggests we might be chasing a ghost. Modern systematic reviews have largely dismantled the idea that steroids prevent biphasic reactions. In some cases, they may even delay the recognition of a relapse or lead to increased ICU admissions due to side effects.

The real challenge isn't the steroids; it's the monitoring. Biphasic reactions are rare (4–6%), but they are unpredictable. While the "6-hour observation window" is common, many reactions occur in the 6–12 hour mark. When you have a patient who required multiple doses of epi or had a severe initial presentation (like our 104 kg patient), the risk of a relapse is higher, and a standard observation period may not be enough.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Mind the Gap:** 0.3 mg is often insufficient for adults >50 kg. If you use an auto-injector in the ED, recognize that you may be underdosing your patient. When in doubt, draw up 0.5 mg.
*   **Multiphasic is Possible:** While biphasic is the common term, be prepared for "multiphasic" episodes, especially in patients with suspected mast cell disorders or those who were underdosed initially.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** If you suspect a mast cell issue, timing is everything. Get a sample as soon as feasible, another 1–2 hours after symptom onset (peak), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after resolution.
*   **Steroids are Not Magic:** Don't rely on corticosteroids to "stop" a biphasic reaction. They are not evidence-based for this purpose; vigilant monitoring and prompt epinephrine are the only real tools we have.

## Take-Home Points
*   **Standard Adult Dose:** Aim for 0.5 mg IM epinephrine for adults >50 kg.
*   **Auto-Injector Caution:** Be aware that 0.3 mg devices may lead to underdosing and increased need for repeat doses.
*   **Observation:** High-risk patients (severe initial shock, multiple epi doses) require extended monitoring beyond the standard 6 hours.
*   **Mast Cell Suspicion:** Consider systemic mastocytosis in patients with atypical, multiphasic, or refractory anaphylaxis.



# Steroids for Anaphylaxis: Time to Stop the Habit?

Imagine this: you’ve just managed a classic case of anaphylaxis in the ED. You gave the epinephrine, the hypotension resolved, the wheezing stopped, and the patient looks great. You admit them to the ward for observation, feeling confident that the crisis has passed. Then, two hours later, the nurse calls: the patient is hypotensive and gasping for air again. You treat them, they recover, and then—six hours later—it happens a third time. 

This is the nightmare of biphasic (and in rare cases, multiphasic) anaphylaxis. In a recent case report, a woman in her 60s experienced a staggering four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock within 12 hours after taking a single dose of clarithromycin. Each time, she responded to epinephrine, but the "recovery" was only temporary. 

When we see this pattern, our reflexive "old school" reach is for the corticosteroids. For decades, we’ve been taught that steroids are the shield that prevents the "second wave" of anaphylaxis. In this case, the patient received both IV prednisone and dexamethasone based on traditional protocols. But as we dig into the evidence, we have to ask: are we treating the patient, or are we just following a habit?

The evidence for steroids in anaphylaxis is surprisingly thin. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that 21 of them showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduced the risk of biphasic reactions. Even more concerning is a 2019 study suggesting that prehospital corticosteroid treatment might actually be associated with an *increased* likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission. While the case report describes a patient who eventually recovered, the authors highlight a troubling gap: we are routinely administering a medication with a significant side-effect profile for a benefit that the data simply doesn't support.

While the steroid debate is the focal point, this case offers another critical pearl regarding epinephrine dosing. The patient’s first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto-injector. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing treatment delay and practitioner hesitancy, 0.3 mg is often an underdose for adults over 50 kg. The standard recommended dose is 0.5 mg. The authors note that patients receiving 0.3 mg may require escalation of care more frequently than those receiving the full 0.5 mg. If you are using an auto-injector in a large adult, be aware that you may be under-dosing your patient.

So, why did this patient have such a bizarre, multiphasic course? The investigation pointed toward systemic mastocytosis. Although she didn't meet every formal diagnostic criterion, she had significantly elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression and memory problems). In patients with mast cell disorders, the mediator release is more profound and prolonged, which may explain why this patient kept crashing long after the clarithromycin had left her system.

The take-home message here is a call for clinical humility. Anaphylaxis is a diagnosis we feel we know, but the "tail" of the reaction—the biphasic and multiphasic window—is still poorly understood. We cannot reliably predict who will relapse, and we cannot reliably prevent it with steroids. Our best tools remain prompt, appropriately dosed epinephrine and vigilant monitoring.

## Key Take-Home Points

* **Steroids aren't a shield:** Current evidence suggests corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and may be associated with worse outcomes.
* **Watch the dose:** 0.3 mg auto-injectors may underdose adults >50 kg; the recommended adult dose is 0.5 mg IM.
* **The "Second Wave" is unpredictable:** Biphasic reactions typically occur within 24 hours, but there are no reliable predictors to tell you who will relapse.
* **Consider the underlying "Why":** Patients with multiphasic or refractory courses may have underlying mast cell disorders, such as systemic mastocytosis.
* **Tryptase timing matters:** To diagnose mast cell issues, you need a sample as soon as possible, another 1–4 hours after symptom onset, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after resolution.



# Tryptase Timing: Getting the Diagnosis of Mastocytosis Right

We’ve all been there: the patient with anaphylaxis who responds beautifully to epinephrine, the symptoms resolve, and you think you're in the clear. Then, hours later, they crash again. And then again. 

While "biphasic" reactions are a known entity in the emergency department, we rarely encounter "multiphasic" anaphylaxis—where a patient cycles through multiple episodes of shock despite standard treatment. When this happens, it's time to stop thinking about the *trigger* and start thinking about the *patient*. Specifically, why is this patient's immune system behaving like a runaway train?

In a recent case, a woman in her 60s presented after a single dose of clarithromycin. She didn't just have one reaction; she had four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Despite receiving intramuscular epinephrine and fluids, she kept rebounding into crisis. This isn't just a "bad reaction"—it's a red flag for an underlying mast cell disorder, likely systemic mastocytosis.

## The Tryptase Trap

The tragedy of this case wasn't the clinical management—the patient survived and eventually stabilized—but the diagnostic window. In the chaos of treating four successive waves of shock, the team missed the "golden window" for tryptase sampling.

Tryptase is the key biomarker for mast cell activation, but its utility depends entirely on *when* you draw the blood. To diagnose systemic mastocytosis or confirm an anaphylactic event, you need a comparison between peak levels and baseline levels.

The recommended protocol is a three-point check:
1. **The Peak:** A sample as soon as feasible, ideally 1–2 hours (and certainly within 4 hours) of symptom onset. This captures the mast cell surge.
2. **The Resolution:** A second sample if symptoms recur or evolve.
3. **The Baseline:** A sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution.

In this case, tryptase wasn't drawn during the first few episodes. It was only captured during the third episode and again 27 hours later. While they still found elevated levels, the lack of early samples makes it harder to map the severity of the mast cell surge. In the ED, we are great at the "treat" part of the algorithm, but we often forget that the "diagnose" part of anaphylaxis requires precise timing.

## Looking Beyond the Rash: Subtle Clues

If you have a patient with refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, look for the "quiet" clues that suggest systemic mastocytosis. It isn't always just hives and wheezing. 

This patient had several subtle findings that, in hindsight, pointed toward a systemic mast cell disorder:
* **Hepatomegaly:** Found on abdominal sonography.
* **Neuropsychiatric symptoms:** A history of depression and memory problems.
* **Atypical course:** Recurrent shocks from a single, low-dose exposure to a medication (clarithromycin).

When you see a patient who is "too allergic" for their history—perhaps someone who has only had mild reactions in the past but is suddenly crashing violently—start looking for these systemic markers.

## A Quick Note on Epinephrine Dosing

While not the primary focus of the diagnosis, this case highlights a dangerous trend in the use of auto-injectors. The patient initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector. For an adult weighing 104 kg, this is underdosing. 

The gold standard remains 0.5 mg IM for adults. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing treatment delay and hesitancy, we must be careful not to let the convenience of a 0.3 mg device override the clinical need for a full 0.5 mg dose. Evidence suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg are more likely to require escalation of care than those receiving the full dose.

## The Bottom Line

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but when it happens, it's a diagnostic signal. Don't just treat the shock; hunt for the cause.

**Clinical Pearls:**
* **Tryptase Timing:** Draw at peak (1-2 hours post-onset) and at baseline (24+ hours post-resolution). Missing the peak means missing the diagnosis.
* **Think Mastocytosis:** Suspect systemic mastocytosis in patients with multiphasic reactions, hepatomegaly, or unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms.
* **Dose Correctly:** 0.3 mg auto-injectors are often insufficient for adults over 50 kg; aim for 0.5 mg IM.
* **Steroid Reality Check:** While traditionally used to prevent biphasic reactions, current evidence suggests corticosteroids have little to no benefit in preventing relapse. Focus on aggressive epinephrine and monitoring instead.



# Epi-Drip or Expectant? Managing the Multiphasic Nightmare

We’ve all been there: the anaphylaxis patient who responds beautifully to a dose of epinephrine, the swelling goes down, the blood pressure stabilizes, and you start thinking about discharge or a low-acuity observation bed. We’re taught to watch for the "biphasic" reaction—that second wave of symptoms that hits a small percentage of patients hours later. But what happens when the wave doesn't just return once, but keeps crashing back over and over again?

Enter a case that turns a standard emergency into a clinical nightmare: multiphasic anaphylaxis.

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED after taking a single 500mg dose of oral clarithromycin. This wasn't a mild rash; she was in frank anaphylactic shock. We're talking a blood pressure of 74/50 mmHg, oxygen saturation of 88%, facial swelling, and generalized urticaria. She received 0.3mg of epinephrine via auto-injector, fluids, and oxygen, and she responded well. The crisis seemed averted.

But then the pattern emerged. Despite the initial success, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Not once, not twice, but a total of four distinct episodes. Each time, she crashed, and each time, she required further intramuscular epinephrine to recover.

This is where the clinician's dilemma kicks in. When you are staring at a patient who keeps relapsing despite adequate treatment, you are faced with two precarious choices: the "Epi-drip" or the "Expectant" approach.

On one hand, you can start a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion. The logic is pre-emptive: keep the receptors occupied and the vasculature tight to stop the next crash before it starts. But this comes with its own set of risks—tachycardia, potential ischemia, and the general instability of a potent vasopressor. On the other hand, you can remain expectant, treating reactively as symptoms appear. This is "safer" in terms of medication side effects, but it means watching your patient repeatedly descend into shock, which is a terrifying prospect for both the provider and the patient.

In this case, the team leaned toward the expectant approach, noting that the intensity of the episodes was gradually decreasing. Eventually, the cycle broke, and the patient stabilized after 24 hours without further relapse.

While the management of the "crash" is intuitive, this case highlights a few critical gaps in how we handle anaphylaxis in the real world. First, there is the "auto-injector trap." In this case, the first two doses were 0.3mg via auto-injector. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing treatment delay, 0.3mg is an underdose for most adults over 50kg. The guidelines suggest 0.5mg. When a patient is in refractory or multiphasic shock, every milligram counts.

Then there is the steroid myth. In this patient, prednisone and dexamethasone were administered to prevent further biphasic reactions. However, the modern literature is increasingly clear: corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. We are likely giving a drug with a delayed onset and a long list of side effects for a benefit that may not actually exist.

So, why did this happen? The "why" is perhaps the most interesting part of the case. Further workup revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. While she didn't meet every formal diagnostic criterion, she had elevated baseline and peak tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression. Systemic mastocytosis creates a biological "tinderbox" where mast cells are more numerous and more prone to massive mediator release, potentially explaining why this patient didn't just have one reaction, but a sustained, multiphasic series of them.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Dose Matters:** Be wary of the 0.3mg auto-injector in adults. If the patient is >50kg and crashing, they need 0.5mg. Don't let the convenience of the device lead to under-dosing.
*   **Steroids Aren't Magic:** Evidence suggests corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic reactions. Their role in the acute management of anaphylaxis is increasingly questioned.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** If you suspect a mast cell disorder or need to confirm anaphylaxis, timing is everything. Grab a sample as soon as feasible, another 1–2 hours after symptom onset (the peak), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after resolution.
*   **Think Beyond Biphasic:** While biphasic reactions are rare (4-6%), "multiphasic" courses are even rarer and more challenging. If a patient relapses multiple times, consider underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis.
*   **Observation is Key:** Because we cannot reliably predict who will have a biphasic or multiphasic course, a period of observation remains the safest strategy for severe presentations.
