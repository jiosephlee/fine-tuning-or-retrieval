Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the context of a severe allergic reaction, how do clinicians differentiate between biphasic anaphylaxis and refractory anaphylaxis?

The differentiation between biphasic and refractory anaphylaxis rests on the clinical trajectory of the patient’s symptoms and their response to epinephrine.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is characterized by a recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after the initial episode has completely resolved, creating a distinct asymptomatic phase. This recurrence occurs without further exposure to the triggering allergen. While these episodes typically manifest within 24 hours of the initial reaction, they can occur up to 78 hours later. In the provided case, the patient exhibited a "multiphasic" course—a severe variant of biphasic anaphylaxis—where she experienced an initial good response to epinephrine and became asymptomatic, only to suffer multiple subsequent relapses into anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission.

**Refractory anaphylaxis**, by contrast, is defined by the persistence of anaphylactic symptoms despite the correct administration of epinephrine. Unlike the biphasic pattern, there is no period of complete resolution; rather, the patient remains unstable or fails to respond sufficiently to standard therapy.

Clinically, the challenge lies in distinguishing true biphasic reactions from "sustained anaphylaxis," where a patient may show a transient improvement following epinephrine but never fully recovers. Clinicians must also consider whether symptoms are recurring due to ongoing allergen absorption (such as slow-release medications or residual food in the GI tract) rather than a biphasic immune response. 

As illustrated by this case, patients with underlying mast cell disorders, such as systemic mastocytosis, may be predisposed to these complex, multiphasic presentations. When a patient responds well to initial treatment but then relapses, the clinical focus shifts from managing a refractory state to implementing a period of observation to monitor for these biphasic recurrences.

### [Management] The patient in this case initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via an auto-injector. For an adult weighing over 50 kg, what is the standard recommended dose, and what clinical evidence suggests that the 0.3 mg dose may be suboptimal?

For an adult weighing over 50 kg, the standard recommended dose of epinephrine for the treatment of anaphylaxis is **0.5 mg administered intramuscularly**. While guidelines specify a weight-based dose of 0.01 mg/kg, this is typically simplified to 0.5 mg for the majority of the adult population.

In the presented case, the patient weighed 104 kg, yet she initially received only 0.3 mg of epinephrine via an auto-injector. This represents a significant underdose based on current guidelines. The clinical evidence suggesting that a 0.3 mg dose may be suboptimal is highlighted by a retrospective study (referenced as 2025 in the report) which found that patients receiving an initial dose of 0.3 mg required escalation of care—such as additional epinephrine injections—significantly more often than those who received the recommended 0.5 mg dose.

This discrepancy often arises because many commercially available epinephrine auto-injectors are standardized at 0.3 mg, creating a gap between readily available delivery devices and evidence-based dosing guidelines. In this patient’s multiphasic course, it is noteworthy that the first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg doses, while the subsequent two episodes were treated with the full 0.5 mg dose. This underscores the importance of adhering to the 0.5 mg standard in adults to maximize the initial therapeutic response and potentially reduce the need for subsequent dosing.

### [Management] Corticosteroids were administered to this patient to prevent the recurrence of symptoms. Based on recent systematic reviews and contemporary guidelines, what is the current consensus on the efficacy of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis?

Based on recent systematic reviews and contemporary guidelines, there is currently **no evidence that corticosteroids are effective in preventing the recurrence of symptoms in biphasic anaphylaxis.**

For many years, corticosteroids were routinely administered following the initial resolution of anaphylaxis based on the traditional belief that they could mitigate a second wave of mediator release. However, this practice has been challenged by high-quality evidence. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that 21 of those studies showed no benefit in reducing the risk of biphasic reactions, with only a single study supporting their use. 

Furthermore, contemporary data suggests that routine steroid administration may be counterproductive. For example, a 2019 study indicated an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most current guidelines have shifted away from the routine use of steroids in the management of anaphylaxis.

In the presented case, the patient received both intravenous prednisone and dexamethasone after her first recurrence, following traditional hospital protocols. While this reflects common clinical practice, the case highlights the tension between historical "expert-based" habits and current evidence-based guidelines. In the absence of a specific co-morbidity that would necessitate steroid therapy (such as an underlying chronic inflammatory condition), the routine use of corticosteroids in this patient likely provided no preventative benefit against further multiphasic episodes.

### [Clinical Pearls] To accurately diagnose anaphylaxis and evaluate for underlying mast cell disorders, what is the recommended timing and sequence for collecting serum tryptase samples?

To accurately diagnose anaphylaxis and screen for underlying mast cell disorders, such as systemic mastocytosis, serum tryptase should be collected in a strategic sequence of at least two, and ideally three, samples. Tryptase is a protease released primarily by mast cells during degranulation; because its half-life is short, timing is critical to capture the peak release and establish a true baseline.

The recommended sequence for collection is as follows:

1.  **Initial Sample:** This should be drawn as soon as feasible upon presentation to the emergency department, provided it does not delay the administration of life-saving treatment (e.g., epinephrine).
2.  **Peak Sample:** A second sample should be collected 1 to 2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after the onset of symptoms. This window typically corresponds with the peak concentration of tryptase in the serum.
3.  **Baseline Sample:** A third sample must be drawn at least 24 hours after the full clinical resolution of the event. This is essential to determine the patient's baseline tryptase level.

In the reported case, the patient experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis, but the recommended timing for tryptase collection was not adhered to during the initial episodes. The first sample was not collected until the third anaphylactic episode, and the subsequent sample was taken 27 hours after the final recurrence. Despite this suboptimal timing, the patient exhibited elevated tryptase levels during the episode and an increased baseline level during follow-up. This pattern, combined with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, pointed toward a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although the patient did not meet the full formal diagnostic criteria due to limited bone marrow biopsy material.

For the clinician, this case underscores a common pitfall: in the chaos of managing a severe systemic reaction, the window for the "peak" sample is easily missed. Establishing both a peak and a baseline is the only way to differentiate a transient rise in tryptase due to an acute anaphylactic event from a chronically elevated baseline characteristic of mast cell activation syndromes.

### [Pathophysiology] This patient showed evidence suggestive of systemic mastocytosis. Pathophysiologically, how does systemic mastocytosis predispose an individual to more severe or atypical anaphylactic courses?

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a clonal hematopoietic disorder characterized by the abnormal proliferation and accumulation of mast cells in various organs, most notably the bone marrow, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. This condition predisposes individuals to more severe and atypical anaphylactic courses—such as the multiphasic presentation seen in this patient—through three primary pathophysiologic mechanisms:

First, there is a significant increase in the **total mast cell burden**. In a healthy individual, mast cells are primarily localized to mucosal and cutaneous surfaces. In SM, the systemic infiltration of these cells means there is a vastly larger "reservoir" of inflammatory mediators (including histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes) available for release. When a trigger occurs—in this case, clarithromycin—the resulting mediator release is not localized or moderate, but massive and systemic, leading to the profound hypotension and hypoxemia observed in the patient’s initial presentation.

Second, mast cells in SM often exhibit **altered activation thresholds**. These clonal mast cells may be more hypersensitive to triggers or may release mediators more readily and in greater quantities than normal mast cells. This explains why the patient experienced a severe systemic reaction despite having only a history of mild allergic reactions in the past.

Third, the **prolonged or recurrent nature of the reaction** (multiphasic anaphylaxis) may be linked to this expanded mast cell population and the resulting "mediator storm." While the specific link between SM and biphasic/multiphasic courses is still being researched, the excessive load of mast cells and the potential for staggered or prolonged mediator release can make the clinical course more refractory to standard treatment. This is evidenced in our patient by the requirement for four separate doses of epinephrine over 12 hours to achieve resolution.

The clinical clues in this case—specifically the **elevated baseline and peak tryptase levels**, the finding of **hepatomegaly** on ultrasound (suggesting organ infiltration), and **neuropsychiatric symptoms**—all point toward the systemic nature of this mast cell disorder, which fundamentally shifted her allergic response from a typical single-episode event to a complex, life-threatening multiphasic course.

### [Morning Report] When managing a patient after the initial resolution of anaphylaxis, what are the proposed clinical predictors for a biphasic reaction, and why is the optimal observation window still a subject of debate?

In the management of anaphylaxis, a biphasic reaction is defined as the recurrence of symptoms after the initial resolution of the primary event, occurring without re-exposure to the offending allergen. While most patients respond to initial treatment without further complication, a small subset (reported incidence between 4% and 6%) will experience this second peak of symptoms.

**Proposed Clinical Predictors**
Reliable, evidence-based predictors for biphasic reactions are currently lacking, but several clinical markers have been proposed to help risk-stratify patients:
*   **Severity of initial presentation:** Patients with more profound initial shock or respiratory failure.
*   **Epinephrine requirements:** A need for multiple doses of epinephrine to achieve the initial resolution.
*   **Treatment delay:** Delayed administration of the first dose of epinephrine.
*   **Patient history:** A prior history of biphasic reactions to allergens.

**The Observation Window Debate**
The optimal duration of observation after the resolution of symptoms remains a subject of significant clinical debate due to the tension between patient safety and healthcare resource utilization. 

Traditionally, observation periods have ranged from 4 to 6 hours. However, data from the World Allergy Organization and other guidelines suggest that approximately 50% of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours after the initial event. This creates a clinical paradox: while observing all patients for 12 to 24 hours would maximize safety, it would result in the unnecessary detention of the vast majority of patients who will never experience a relapse. Consequently, some frameworks—such as those proposed by the UK guidelines—suggest a risk-stratified, three-tier approach to monitoring rather than a one-size-fits-all window.

**Clinical Application and Case Correlation**
The case of the woman in her 60s underscores the volatility of these reactions. This patient did not merely experience a biphasic course but a *multiphasic* one, with four distinct episodes of anaphylaxis requiring epinephrine within the first 12 hours of admission. This progression highlights why a truncated observation window can be dangerous. Furthermore, her suspected systemic mastocytosis—indicated by elevated tryptase levels and hepatomegaly—serves as a reminder that underlying mast cell disorders can predispose patients to more severe and complex clinical courses, potentially necessitating more vigilant monitoring than the average patient.
