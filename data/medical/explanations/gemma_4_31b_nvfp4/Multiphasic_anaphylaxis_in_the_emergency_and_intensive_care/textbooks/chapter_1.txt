Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Presentation and the Clinical Recognition of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic Type I hypersensitivity reaction characterized by a rapid onset of symptoms that can lead to respiratory failure and cardiovascular collapse. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the sudden onset of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, or circulation, typically accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal manifestations such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. In the case presented, the patient’s clinical picture—combining respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, and widespread skin involvement—met these criteria, necessitating immediate emergency intervention to prevent fatality.

The pharmacological trigger in this case was clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic. While the patient had a documented history of mild allergy to beta-lactams and a recent rash associated with clindamycin, this episode represented her first experience of true anaphylaxis. It is important to recognize that patients with a history of multiple drug allergies may be more predisposed to severe reactions, though the triggers may be chemically unrelated. Clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis is relatively rare, but the rapid onset of symptoms (one hour after the first dose) is classic for an IgE-mediated reaction in a sensitized individual.

Analyzing the patient's initial presentation reveals a multi-system failure characteristic of anaphylactic shock. From a circulatory perspective, the patient exhibited profound hypotension (74/50 mmHg), indicating a systemic distributive shock caused by massive histamine release and subsequent vasodilation. Respiratory compromise was evident through hypoxemia (oxygen saturation of 88% on room air) and tachypnea (22 breaths per minute), alongside the subjective feeling of throat swelling. These findings suggest a combination of upper airway edema and potential lower airway bronchoconstriction. The cutaneous manifestations—generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, and pruritus—served as the most visible diagnostic clues, though it is critical to remember that skin signs are absent in a significant minority of anaphylactic cases.

Immediate stabilization in the emergency department (ED) followed the "ABC" (Airway, Breathing, and Circulation) priorities. For the airway and breathing, the patient was promptly started on high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) to treat hypoxemia. The circulation was addressed using the gold-standard treatment: intramuscular epinephrine. Epinephrine acts as an $\alpha_1$-adrenergic agonist to increase systemic vascular resistance (treating hypotension) and a $\beta_2$-adrenergic agonist to induce bronchodilation. To support the volume deficit caused by capillary leak and vasodilation, the patient received an initial bolus of 500 mL of normal saline. Finally, an antihistamine (clemastine) was administered to address the cutaneous symptoms, though it is important to note that antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjunctive therapies and do not treat the life-threatening components of anaphylaxis.
