Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Wait, They're Coming Back? Navigating Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

We’ve all been there: the high-stakes adrenaline of the ED, the prompt administration of epinephrine, the dramatic reversal of hypotension and airway edema, and the satisfying feeling of a "win." You’ve stabilized the patient, the rash is fading, and you’re thinking about the disposition. Then, hours later, the patient crashes. Again.

Usually, we talk about *biphasic* anaphylaxis—the classic "second wave" that hits after an initial resolution. But every so often, you run into a case that defies the standard textbook narrative. Today, we’re looking at a case of *multiphasic* anaphylaxis that serves as a sobering reminder that sometimes, the "win" is only temporary.

## The Case: The Revolving Door of Shock

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED in full-blown anaphylactic shock after taking a single 500mg dose of oral clarithromycin. She was hypotensive (74/50 mmHg), hypoxemic (88% on room air), and covered in urticaria and facial swelling. The team acted quickly: 0.3mg of IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV antihistamines, oxygen, and fluid resuscitation.

The response was textbook. Blood pressure normalized, the swelling went down, and the patient was admitted to a monitored ward. But the story didn't end there. 

Over the next 12 hours, the patient suffered *three additional* episodes of anaphylactic shock. Despite repeated doses of IM epinephrine and the administration of corticosteroids, she kept cycling back into crisis. While the intensity of each episode gradually diminished—moving from deep hypoxemia and cyanosis to primarily dyspnea and rash—the clinical instability was profound. She eventually required ICU admission for close monitoring.

After 24 hours of stability, the cycling finally stopped. Workup revealed elevated baseline and peak tryptase levels, suggesting a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though she didn't meet all the formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

## The Biphasic (and Multiphasic) Dilemma

Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as the recurrence of symptoms after initial resolution, without re-exposure to the allergen. While the reported incidence varies wildly (from 0.4% to 23%), most data suggests it happens in about 4-6% of cases. This case takes it a step further into "multiphasic" territory, which is exceptionally rare and leaves clinicians in a difficult spot: do you treat preemptively with an epinephrine infusion, or do you wait and treat each wave as it comes?

The most frustrating part about biphasic reactions is that we aren't great at predicting them. Some suggest that a severe initial presentation or the need for multiple epinephrine doses are red flags, but there is no gold-standard predictor that allows us to confidently send a patient home after a few hours of observation.

## The Steroid Myth and the Dosing Trap

This case highlights two common pitfalls in anaphylaxis management: the reliance on corticosteroids and the "auto-injector dose" trap.

For years, we were taught that steroids prevent biphasic reactions. However, the evidence has shifted. Recent systematic reviews show virtually no benefit in reducing the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, and some data even suggests that the side effects of steroids may complicate the clinical picture. In this case, steroids were given, but they didn't stop the multiphasic course.

Then there is the dosing. In this patient, the first two doses of epinephrine were 0.3mg via auto-injector. For an adult weighing 104kg, 0.3mg is an underdose. The standard adult dose is 0.5mg. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing "treatment hesitancy" and speeding up delivery in the ED, they can leave a significant portion of the adult population under-treated. A 2025 retrospective study noted that patients receiving 0.3mg were significantly more likely to require escalated care than those receiving 0.5mg.

## The Tryptase Window

Finally, let's talk about the diagnostics. If you suspect a mast cell disorder like systemic mastocytosis, timing is everything. Tryptase levels peak quickly. To get an accurate picture, you need a "sandwich" of samples: 
1. As soon as feasible (without delaying treatment).
2. 1–2 hours after symptom onset.
3. A baseline sample at least 24 hours after full resolution.

In this case, the first sample wasn't drawn until the third episode, missing the window for the initial reaction. If you have a patient who is "refractory" or "cycling," get that tryptase early.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Observation is Key:** Biphasic reactions typically occur within 24 hours, but can happen up to 78 hours later. There is no "safe" short window for high-risk patients.
*   **Dose for the Patient, Not the Device:** If your patient is over 50kg, 0.3mg is likely an underdose. Don't let the convenience of an auto-injector dictate your dosing strategy in a clinical setting.
*   **Steroids $\neq$ Prevention:** Current evidence suggests corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Use them for their other indications, but don't rely on them to "stop the second wave."
*   **Tryptase Timing:** To diagnose mast cell disorders, you need peak and baseline levels. Don't wait until the patient is stable to start thinking about the workup.
*   **Multiphasic is Rare but Real:** If a patient keeps crashing despite adequate epinephrine, consider underlying conditions like systemic mastocytosis and consider ICU-level monitoring.
