Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Tryptase Timing: Getting the Diagnosis of Mastocytosis Right

We’ve all been there: the patient with anaphylaxis who responds beautifully to epinephrine, the symptoms resolve, and you think you're in the clear. Then, hours later, they crash again. And then again. 

While "biphasic" reactions are a known entity in the emergency department, we rarely encounter "multiphasic" anaphylaxis—where a patient cycles through multiple episodes of shock despite standard treatment. When this happens, it's time to stop thinking about the *trigger* and start thinking about the *patient*. Specifically, why is this patient's immune system behaving like a runaway train?

In a recent case, a woman in her 60s presented after a single dose of clarithromycin. She didn't just have one reaction; she had four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Despite receiving intramuscular epinephrine and fluids, she kept rebounding into crisis. This isn't just a "bad reaction"—it's a red flag for an underlying mast cell disorder, likely systemic mastocytosis.

## The Tryptase Trap

The tragedy of this case wasn't the clinical management—the patient survived and eventually stabilized—but the diagnostic window. In the chaos of treating four successive waves of shock, the team missed the "golden window" for tryptase sampling.

Tryptase is the key biomarker for mast cell activation, but its utility depends entirely on *when* you draw the blood. To diagnose systemic mastocytosis or confirm an anaphylactic event, you need a comparison between peak levels and baseline levels.

The recommended protocol is a three-point check:
1. **The Peak:** A sample as soon as feasible, ideally 1–2 hours (and certainly within 4 hours) of symptom onset. This captures the mast cell surge.
2. **The Resolution:** A second sample if symptoms recur or evolve.
3. **The Baseline:** A sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution.

In this case, tryptase wasn't drawn during the first few episodes. It was only captured during the third episode and again 27 hours later. While they still found elevated levels, the lack of early samples makes it harder to map the severity of the mast cell surge. In the ED, we are great at the "treat" part of the algorithm, but we often forget that the "diagnose" part of anaphylaxis requires precise timing.

## Looking Beyond the Rash: Subtle Clues

If you have a patient with refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis, look for the "quiet" clues that suggest systemic mastocytosis. It isn't always just hives and wheezing. 

This patient had several subtle findings that, in hindsight, pointed toward a systemic mast cell disorder:
* **Hepatomegaly:** Found on abdominal sonography.
* **Neuropsychiatric symptoms:** A history of depression and memory problems.
* **Atypical course:** Recurrent shocks from a single, low-dose exposure to a medication (clarithromycin).

When you see a patient who is "too allergic" for their history—perhaps someone who has only had mild reactions in the past but is suddenly crashing violently—start looking for these systemic markers.

## A Quick Note on Epinephrine Dosing

While not the primary focus of the diagnosis, this case highlights a dangerous trend in the use of auto-injectors. The patient initially received 0.3 mg of epinephrine via auto-injector. For an adult weighing 104 kg, this is underdosing. 

The gold standard remains 0.5 mg IM for adults. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing treatment delay and hesitancy, we must be careful not to let the convenience of a 0.3 mg device override the clinical need for a full 0.5 mg dose. Evidence suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg are more likely to require escalation of care than those receiving the full dose.

## The Bottom Line

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but when it happens, it's a diagnostic signal. Don't just treat the shock; hunt for the cause.

**Clinical Pearls:**
* **Tryptase Timing:** Draw at peak (1-2 hours post-onset) and at baseline (24+ hours post-resolution). Missing the peak means missing the diagnosis.
* **Think Mastocytosis:** Suspect systemic mastocytosis in patients with multiphasic reactions, hepatomegaly, or unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms.
* **Dose Correctly:** 0.3 mg auto-injectors are often insufficient for adults over 50 kg; aim for 0.5 mg IM.
* **Steroid Reality Check:** While traditionally used to prevent biphasic reactions, current evidence suggests corticosteroids have little to no benefit in preventing relapse. Focus on aggressive epinephrine and monitoring instead.
