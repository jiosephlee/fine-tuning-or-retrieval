Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The CA-125 Trap: Interpreting Biomarkers in Pregnancy

Imagine you’re in the ER or clinic and you encounter a patient in her second trimester of pregnancy presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On exam, her abdomen is massively distended—far beyond what you’d expect for 21 weeks. Imaging reveals two huge cystic masses: one filling the left subphrenic space and another occupying the pouch of Douglas. Then comes the lab result: the CA-125 is elevated and trending upward.

In almost any other clinical context, this constellation of findings—massive adnexal-appearing masses and a rising CA-125—would send an oncological alarm bell ringing through the entire multidisciplinary team. But in the context of pregnancy, this is exactly where the "CA-125 trap" lies.

## The Case: Massive Masses and Mimics

This case involves a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation. She presented with acute abdominal pain and a restrictive-pattern dyspnea caused by the sheer "steric bulk" of her abdominal contents. Physical exam revealed a taut, elastic 18 cm mass in the left upper quadrant and a 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis.

Imaging was complex. Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI showed two unilocular cystic masses. The lower mass seemed adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. The upper mass was more ambiguous, mimicking a decidualized endometrioma. When the CA-125 came back at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35) and was noted to be increasing from a previous value of 36 U/mL, the suspicion for an ovarian malignancy increased.

However, because of the worsening pain, the risk of torsion or rupture, and the mechanical restriction of her breathing, the team opted for a laparotomy. The surprise? Both masses were uterine leiomyomas. The cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated leiomyoma that had actually twisted and begun leaking—which explained the low-grade fever she developed during her admission.

## The Physiology: Why CA-125 Lies

CA-125 is a mainstay for monitoring ovarian cancer, but its specificity plummets in the presence of peritoneal irritation or physiological changes in the uterus. In pregnancy, CA-125 follows a non-linear path. It typically peaks in the first trimester, dips in the second, and rises again in the third. 

In this case, the patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was higher than the median for the second trimester, but it was far from diagnostic of malignancy. CA-125 can be elevated by a laundry list of benign conditions: endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and—crucially—leiomyomas.

When we see a rising CA-125 in a pregnant patient, we have to ask: is this a marker of cancer, or is it a marker of "abdominal chaos"? In this patient, the marker was likely reflecting the presence of massive myomas and the inflammatory response to a twisting pedicle, rather than an epithelial ovarian tumor.

## Management Nuances

The surgical management of myomas during pregnancy is a high-stakes balancing act. Generally, we avoid surgery to prevent preterm labor or uterine rupture. But when you have a pedunculated mass that is torsioning, the risk of necrosis and sepsis outweighs the risk of the surgery.

The surgeons in this case performed a targeted myomectomy of the upper mass but left the caudal mass in situ. This is a critical clinical pearl: removing every mass isn't always the goal. By leaving the pelvic mass alone, the team minimized the risk to the pregnancy. Because that residual mass would have blocked the birth canal, an elective C-section was performed at 37 weeks, resulting in an excellent outcome for both mother and baby.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **CA-125 is not a binary switch:** In pregnancy, isolated elevations of CA-125 are common and non-specific. Trend data is more useful than a single value, but neither replaces histology.
*   **The "Steric Bulk" Effect:** Large abdominal masses in pregnancy can cause restrictive lung disease and dyspnea. If a pregnant patient is short of breath but has normal O2 sats and a clear chest X-ray, look at the abdomen.
*   **Myomas are Chameleons:** Uterine leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration, making them look exactly like ovarian cysts or endometriomas on ultrasound and MRI.
*   **Surgical Restraint:** In the second trimester, the goal is pregnancy preservation. If a mass is causing acute complications (torsion/necrosis), intervene; otherwise, consider leaving non-symptomatic masses until the postpartum period.
*   **MRI Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a safe and powerful tool for differentiating intramural from adnexal masses when ultrasound is inconclusive.

**Bottom Line:** Don't let a slightly elevated CA-125 drive you straight to an oncological diagnosis in pregnancy. Consider the physiological shifts of gestation and the propensity of leiomyomas to mimic malignancy.
