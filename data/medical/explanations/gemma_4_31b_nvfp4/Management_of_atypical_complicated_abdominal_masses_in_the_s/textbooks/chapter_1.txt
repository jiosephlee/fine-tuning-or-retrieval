Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The initial presentation of this patient—a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation—highlights a challenging clinical triad of severe abdominal pain, mild dyspnoea, and a second-trimester pregnancy. While some dyspnoea can be associated with the physiological changes of pregnancy, the acute onset of pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis, coupled with respiratory distress, suggests a mechanical cause. In this case, the symptoms were not cardiovascular or pulmonary in origin but were instead the result of massive abdominal lesions exerting pressure on the diaphragm and pelvic structures.

Physical examination was critical in characterizing the extent of the abdominal pathology. Upon examination, the patient's abdomen appeared larger than expected for 21 weeks of gestational age. Two distinct masses were identified: a large, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant and a separate, fixed, hard mass of approximately 9 cm in the lesser pelvis, identified via bimanual vaginal examination as bulging at the posterior fornix. The presence of two separate, large masses—one subphrenic and one pelvic—raised a broad differential diagnosis, ranging from large uterine leiomyomas to adnexal masses or ovarian neoplasms.

Immediate management focused on stabilizing the patient and alleviating the mechanical distress caused by the masses. The patient was placed in a semi-orthostatic position, a maneuver designed to shift the gravitational weight of the gravid uterus and the abdominal masses away from the diaphragm, thereby reducing respiratory compression. Combined with intravenous paracetamol for analgesia, this approach successfully reduced the patient's reported pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale and mitigated the dyspnoea without the need for supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation.

Baseline laboratory evaluations revealed several concurrent comorbidities that complicated the clinical picture. The patient presented with iron deficiency anaemia, evidenced by a haemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L and low serum ferritin (25 mcg/L). Additionally, the development of a low-grade fever and an elevated neutrophilic white blood cell count (11.29 $\times 10^3$/uL) and C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) suggested an inflammatory or infectious process. While these elevations can be seen in normal pregnancy, further investigation identified a *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection. These findings were essential for the multidisciplinary team to establish a baseline of maternal health and manage systemic infections before proceeding with diagnostic imaging and potential surgical intervention.
