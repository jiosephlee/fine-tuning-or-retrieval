Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Surgical Decision-Making and Intraoperative Management

In the management of uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, conservative treatment is the gold standard. Surgical intervention is typically delayed until the postpartum period to avoid the high risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, and the induction of preterm labor. However, the clinical priority shifts when a patient presents with "complicated" masses. Indications for urgent myomectomy during pregnancy include acute complications such as torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma, rupture, or severe symptomatic compression that impairs maternal function. In this case, the patient’s severe abdominal pain and restrictive lung disease—manifesting as dyspnea due to the mechanical compression of the diaphragm by an 18 cm mass—provided a clear clinical indication for surgical intervention at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

The choice of surgical approach depends on the size, number, and location of the masses. While laparoscopic approaches may be considered for smaller, non-intramural masses in the first or early second trimester, a laparotomy is preferred for large, multiple, or critically located masses. In this patient, the sheer volume of the two masses and the presence of an intramural-subserosal component made a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy the safest option. This approach allowed for a comprehensive exploration of the abdominal cavity and provided the necessary exposure to handle large masses without risking uterine rupture or fetal injury.

Intraoperative findings are critical for refining the diagnosis and directing the surgical strategy. Upon exploration, the upper mass was identified as a huge, highly vascularized, pedunculated leiomyoma. The surgical team discovered that the mass had twisted (torsion) and was leaking, which likely contributed to the patient's intraoperative fever and acute pain. Torsion of a pedunculated myoma is a surgical emergency, as it leads to ischemic necrosis and potential infection, necessitating immediate excision to prevent further maternal morbidity.

The primary intraoperative goal in pregnancy is to balance the removal of pathological tissue with the preservation of the pregnancy. To minimize obstetric risk and the potential for uterine trauma, the surgical team employed a selective myomectomy strategy. The upper pedunculated mass was excised using a double clamp technique to control bleeding, and the uterine root was carefully sutured. Conversely, the caudal mass—which was fixed and appeared as a cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—was left in situ. Because this second mass showed no signs of torsion or acute complication, removing it would have unnecessarily increased the risk of hemorrhage and preterm delivery. To further protect the pregnancy during and after the procedure, the team utilized serial fetal heart rate monitoring and administered intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis to reduce the risk of preterm labor.
