Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Reasoning

Diagnosing abdominal masses during pregnancy is challenging due to the anatomical displacement caused by the gravid uterus and the physiological changes of pregnancy that can mimic pathology. In this case, the patient presented with two large masses—one in the left subphrenic space and one in the pelvis—requiring a multimodal imaging approach to differentiate between ovarian and uterine origins.

Ultrasonography (US) served as the initial screening tool, which is the standard first-line modality in pregnancy due to its safety and accessibility. In this patient, US identified two unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. While the upper mass appeared isolated, the lower mass was found to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. For the clinician, the presence of cystic components and peripheral flow on Doppler can either suggest a degenerating myoma or an ovarian cyst, such as an endometrioma; however, the "steric bulk" of the pregnant uterus often obscures the exact point of origin (the pedicle or stalk), making it difficult to determine if a mass is truly adnexal or pedunculated from the uterus.

When US findings are inconclusive, non-contrast MRI is the preferred second-line imaging modality. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy due to potential fetal risks, but non-contrast MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast and diagnostic accuracy for identifying the relationship between a mass and the uterine cavity. In this case, MRI revealed thick-walled cysts with inhomogeneous fluid content and focal solid components. Crucially, MRI suggested that the caudal mass was in continuity with the posterior uterine leiomyoma, a finding that strongly shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a uterine origin rather than an independent ovarian lesion.

The interpretation of serum tumor markers, specifically CA-125, requires caution during pregnancy. CA-125 is often used as a marker for ovarian malignancy or endometriosis, but it can be physiologically elevated during the first and third trimesters. In this patient, the CA-125 was slightly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. While this value exceeded the standard oncological cutoff of 35 U/mL, the clinical team had to balance this with the knowledge that CA-125 can rise in benign conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, and even some leiomyomas. In this context, the slight elevation of CA-125 initially favored a diagnosis of decidualised endometriomas.

The differential diagnosis ultimately centered on distinguishing between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. Endometriomas often present as cystic adnexal masses and correlate with elevated CA-125. Conversely, leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration—particularly during pregnancy due to rapid growth and subsequent outstripping of their blood supply—which can mimic a cyst on imaging. The clinical reasoning in this case integrated the imaging and laboratory data: the cystic appearance and CA-125 elevation suggested endometriomas, but the MRI-confirmed continuity with the uterine wall pointed toward leiomyomas. This highlights a common diagnostic pitfall: pedunculated leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses by appearing displaced from the uterus, requiring high suspicion and detailed imaging to identify the uterine stalk.
