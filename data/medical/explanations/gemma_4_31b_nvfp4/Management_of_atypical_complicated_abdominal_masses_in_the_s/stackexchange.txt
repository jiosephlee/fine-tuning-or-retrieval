Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient in her second trimester of pregnancy presents with a pelvic mass and a slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL). How should the interpretation of CA-125 differ in a pregnant patient compared to a non-pregnant patient, and why is a single measurement often insufficient for diagnosis?

In the evaluation of a pelvic mass during pregnancy, the interpretation of Cancer Antigen 125 (CA-125) must be handled with caution, as the physiological state of pregnancy significantly alters its diagnostic utility compared to non-pregnant patients.

In non-pregnant women, CA-125 is primarily used as a marker for ovarian malignancy, with a standard clinical cut-off of $\geq 35$ U/mL. However, CA-125 is not a tumor-specific marker; it is expressed by various mesothelial surfaces, including the peritoneum, pleura, and the lining of the fallopian tubes. During pregnancy, CA-125 levels undergo physiological fluctuations, typically peaking in the first and third trimesters. Because of these normal elevations, a value that would be considered "abnormal" or suggestive of malignancy in a postmenopausal woman may be a normal physiological finding in a pregnant patient.

A single measurement of CA-125 is often insufficient for diagnosis because it provides a static snapshot of a dynamic process. In this case, the patient presented in her second trimester with a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL. While this exceeds the standard cut-off of 35 U/mL and is higher than the median value typically seen in the second trimester (approximately 14 U/mL), it must be interpreted in the context of her baseline. The patient had a value of 36 U/mL only two weeks prior, indicating a slight upward trend rather than a dramatic, high-magnitude elevation typically associated with aggressive malignancy.

Furthermore, CA-125 lacks specificity. It can be elevated in various benign conditions common in the pelvic region, such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, and uterine leiomyomas. In this patient, the slight elevation of CA-125 initially steered the differential diagnosis toward decidualized endometriomas. However, the final pathological diagnosis revealed benign uterine leiomyomas. 

The clinical pearl here is that while serial measurements of CA-125 can provide useful trends to help differentiate between benign and malignant processes, they should never be used in isolation. The diagnosis of a pelvic mass in pregnancy must rely on a multidisciplinary approach combining clinical examination, safe imaging modalities—such as ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—and the longitudinal trend of markers rather than a single laboratory value.

### [Pathophysiology] The patient presented with an enlarged abdomen and dyspnea. In the context of large abdominal masses during pregnancy, what are the primary mechanisms leading to respiratory distress, and how does the 'semi-orthostatic position' mentioned in the case help alleviate these symptoms?

In the context of pregnancy complicated by large abdominal masses, respiratory distress is primarily driven by a **restrictive ventilatory pattern** caused by mechanical cephalad displacement of the diaphragm.

### Pathophysiology of Respiratory Distress
During a normal pregnancy, the growing uterus gradually elevates the diaphragm, which can reduce functional residual capacity (FRC). However, the presence of large abdominal masses—such as the 18 cm subphrenic mass and 9.5 cm pelvic mass seen in this patient—exacerbates this process significantly. These masses increase intra-abdominal pressure and occupy the subphrenic space, directly pushing the diaphragm upward into the thoracic cavity. 

This mechanical compression restricts the excursion of the diaphragm during inspiration, leading to decreased tidal volumes and an increased work of breathing. In this case, the "restrictive lung disease dyspnoea" noted by the clinicians was not due to primary pulmonary or cardiac pathology (as evidenced by normal oxygen saturation and ECG), but rather a purely mechanical failure of the thoracic pump to expand fully against the increased abdominal volume.

### The Semi-Orthostatic Position
The "semi-orthostatic position" refers to elevating the head of the bed or reclining the patient at a partial angle (typically 30 to 45 degrees). This position utilizes gravity to alleviate respiratory distress through two primary mechanisms:

1.  **Gravitational Displacement:** By shifting the patient from a supine to an inclined position, the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus and the associated abdominal masses is shifted caudally (downward) toward the pelvis.
2.  **Diaphragmatic Decompression:** As the abdominal contents move inferiorly, the direct pressure on the diaphragm is reduced. This allows for greater diaphragmatic descent and expansion of the lower lung lobes during inspiration, thereby improving ventilation and reducing the sensation of dyspnea.

In this patient, the implementation of this position, combined with analgesics, successfully reduced her pain and dyspnea, confirming that her symptoms were primarily a result of mechanical compression rather than a systemic or pulmonary process.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The initial ultrasound suggested decidualized endometriomas, but the final diagnosis was uterine leiomyomas. Which imaging characteristics or findings on MRI are most useful for differentiating a primary ovarian mass from a pedunculated or subserosal uterine leiomyoma during pregnancy?

Differentiating a primary ovarian mass from a pedunculated or subserosal uterine leiomyoma during pregnancy is a common diagnostic challenge, as both can present as large, complex adnexal-appearing masses. The most critical imaging characteristic on MRI for this differentiation is the **demonstration of continuity between the mass and the uterine myometrium**.

In this case, the MRI was instrumental in identifying the caudal mass as a leiomyoma because it showed the lesion was in continuity with a known posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. When a "bridge" of tissue or a clear anatomical connection to the uterus is visualized, the diagnosis shifts away from a primary ovarian origin.

However, this case also highlights a significant diagnostic pitfall: **pedunculated leiomyomas**. When a leiomyoma is attached by a thin stalk, the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus and the sheer size of the mass can obscure the connective stalk on MRI, as occurred with the upper mass in this patient. When the connection to the uterus is not visualized, these masses frequently mimic primary ovarian tumors, such as decidualized endometriomas.

Beyond anatomical continuity, clinicians should consider the following MRI and sonographic characteristics:

1.  **Signal Intensity and Degeneration:** While classic leiomyomas are T2-hypointense, they frequently undergo **cystic or myxoid degeneration** during pregnancy due to rapid growth and subsequent ischemia. This results in inhomogeneous fluid content and thick walls (as seen in this patient’s MRI), which can closely mimic the appearance of an endometrioma.
2.  **Vascularity:** The presence of a "feeding vessel" or a distinct vascular pedicle originating from the uterus is highly suggestive of a leiomyoma, although this can be difficult to appreciate on non-contrast MRI.
3.  **The Role of Biomarkers:** As demonstrated in this case, CA-125 is a poor differentiator. While a slight elevation (41.8 U/mL) initially steered the team toward a diagnosis of endometrioma, CA-125 is non-specific; it can be physiologically elevated during pregnancy or associated with leiomyomas and adenomyosis.

In summary, while ultrasound is the first-line screening tool, non-contrast MRI is the gold standard for determining the organ of origin. The definitive clue is the identification of the uterine attachment. In the absence of a visible stalk or continuity, a high index of suspicion for a pedunculated leiomyoma must be maintained, particularly when the mass is associated with other known uterine fibroids.

### [Management] The surgical team performed a non-contrast MRI in this case. What are the current guidelines regarding the use of gadolinium-enhanced MRI during pregnancy, and what are the specific safety considerations for the timing and field strength (Tesla) of MRI in pregnant patients?

In patients who are pregnant, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a valuable diagnostic tool when ultrasound is insufficient, provided that specific safety precautions are observed regarding contrast use, timing, and field strength.

**Gadolinium-Enhanced MRI**
Current guidelines recommend that gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) be avoided during pregnancy unless strictly necessary. This caution is based on the fact that gadolinium readily crosses the placenta and is excreted by the fetus into the amniotic fluid, where it can persist. The risks associated with fetal exposure are not fully characterized, but the potential for adverse effects necessitates a high threshold for use. As noted in this case, GBCAs should be reserved for critical scenarios, such as diagnosing severe rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin conditions, or when the imaging is essential to investigate risks of stillbirth or neonatal death. In the presented case, the multidisciplinary team correctly opted for a non-contrast MRI to characterize the abdominal masses while avoiding fetal exposure to gadolinium.

**Timing and Field Strength**
Regarding the timing of MRI, while there is no evidence that MRI is teratogenic, it is generally avoided during the first trimester whenever possible to minimize any theoretical risk during organogenesis. Once the patient has progressed beyond the first trimester—as was the case with this patient at 21 weeks' gestational age—MRI is considered reasonably safe.

Regarding field strength, the standard recommendation is to utilize magnets with a strength of 1.5 Tesla (T) or less. While higher-field strengths (e.g., 3.0T) offer superior image resolution, they are typically avoided in pregnant patients due to a lack of extensive safety data and the potential for increased acoustic noise and peripheral nerve stimulation. The surgical team in this case adhered to these safety standards by ensuring the MRI did not exceed 1.5T.

In summary, for the pregnant patient, the preferred imaging strategy for complex pelvic or abdominal masses is a tiered approach: beginning with ultrasound and proceeding to non-contrast MRI ($\le 1.5$T), specifically avoiding gadolinium unless the diagnostic benefit outweighs the potential fetal risk.

### [Management] While most uterine leiomyomas are managed expectantly during pregnancy, this patient underwent a laparotomy at 21 weeks. What specific clinical 'red flags' or complications justify surgical intervention for myomectomy during the second trimester rather than delaying until the postpartum period?

While the standard of care for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy is expectant management to avoid risks of hemorrhage, preterm labor, and pregnancy loss, certain "red flags" necessitate urgent surgical intervention. In this case, the decision to perform a myomectomy at 21 weeks of gestation was driven by a constellation of severe symptomatic and diagnostic indicators.

The primary clinical justifications for second-trimester surgical intervention include:

**1. Severe Mechanical Compromise and Symptomatic Distress**
When the "steric bulk" of a mass causes systemic dysfunction, conservative management is no longer appropriate. In this patient, the 18 cm upper mass caused restrictive lung disease, manifesting as dyspnoea and diaphragmatic compression. Severe, acute abdominal pain (VAS 5) that does not respond sufficiently to analgesics further indicates a need for decompression.

**2. High Suspicion of Acute Complications (Torsion, Rupture, or Infection)**
Pedunculated leiomyomas are prone to torsion, which can lead to ischemic necrosis, hemorrhage, and secondary infection. The patient’s development of a low-grade fever and acute pain were critical warning signs. Intraoperatively, this was confirmed: the cranial mass was found to be twisted and leaking, with pathological evidence of ischemic necrosis near the pedicle.

**3. Oncological Suspicion**
While CA-125 can be physiologically elevated during pregnancy, a rising trend or a value significantly above the median for the second trimester (as seen here with a value of 41.8 U/mL) can raise suspicion for malignancy. When combined with atypical imaging features (inhomogeneous fluid content and thick walls on MRI), the need for a definitive histological diagnosis may outweigh the risks of surgery.

**4. Limitation of Uterine Expansion**
Masses that severely limit the space available for fetal growth or the physiological enlargement of the uterus may necessitate removal to prevent further maternal morbidity or fetal compromise.

**Clinical Pearl:** The goal of surgery in the second trimester is to be as minimally invasive and brief as possible to preserve the pregnancy. As demonstrated in this case, the surgical team exercised selective myomectomy—removing the symptomatic, twisted pedunculated mass while leaving the fixed caudal mass in situ—thereby balancing the immediate need for symptom relief with the need to minimize obstetric risk.

### [Clinical Pearls] The patient had a large caudal mass that was left in situ during the initial surgery but ultimately necessitated an elective caesarean delivery. How does the anatomical location and size of a uterine leiomyoma influence the decision between a trial of vaginal delivery and a planned caesarean section?

The decision between a trial of vaginal delivery and a planned caesarean section in the presence of uterine leiomyomas is primarily driven by the **anatomical location** and **size** of the mass, specifically whether the myoma creates a mechanical obstruction of the birth canal.

While most uterine leiomyomas do not interfere with parturition, those located in the **lower uterine segment (isthmus)** or the **cervix** can act as a physical barrier to fetal descent. In this case, the patient had a large caudal mass (approximately 9–9.5 cm) originating from the uterine torus and adherent to the posterior wall of the isthmic site. Because this mass occupied the lesser pelvis, it would have functioned as a significant obstacle to the progression of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor, effectively creating a situation of mechanical cephalopelvic disproportion.

From a clinical reasoning perspective, the "steric bulk" of a myoma in the lower segment increases the risk of obstructed labor, which can lead to prolonged uterine contractions, fetal distress, or an increased likelihood of emergency surgical intervention. When a myoma is large and positioned such that it occludes the pelvic inlet or the lower uterine segment, an elective caesarean delivery is indicated to ensure a safe outcome for both the mother and the neonate. In this patient, the elective procedure was further optimized by utilizing the existing surgical scar from her second-trimester myomectomy, thereby minimizing additional surgical trauma.

### [Pathophysiology] During the operation, the cranial mass was found to be 'twisted and leaking,' which likely contributed to the patient's low-grade fever. What is the pathophysiology of 'red degeneration' or cystic degeneration of leiomyomas during pregnancy, and how does this process lead to acute clinical symptoms?

**Clinical Teaching Pearl: Red Degeneration of Uterine Leiomyomas in Pregnancy**

Red degeneration, also known as hemorrhagic infarction, is a specialized form of aseptic necrosis that occurs within a uterine leiomyoma, most commonly during the second trimester of pregnancy. 

**Pathophysiology**
The primary driver of this process is a mismatch between the rapid growth of the leiomyoma and its blood supply. During pregnancy, high levels of estrogen and increased uterine vascularity stimulate the myoma to enlarge rapidly. When the growth of the tumor exceeds the capacity of its nourishing vessels, the center of the mass becomes ischemic. This leads to venous obstruction, hemorrhagic infarction, and subsequent coagulative necrosis. The term "red degeneration" derives from the characteristic gross appearance of the tumor, which becomes reddish-brown due to the extensive hemorrhage. Cystic degeneration is a related process where the necrotic tissue undergoes liquefaction, resulting in the fluid-filled cavities seen on imaging.

**Clinical Manifestations**
The acute clinical symptoms are triggered by the inflammatory response to this tissue necrosis. The release of intracellular contents and the resulting sterile inflammation lead to:
1.  **Acute Abdominal Pain:** Localized tenderness and severe pain resulting from the rapid expansion of the mass and the inflammatory response of the surrounding myometrium and peritoneum.
2.  **Systemic Inflammatory Response:** The necrosis can trigger a low-grade fever and an elevated white blood cell count (leukocytosis), which can mimic an infectious process (e.g., chorioamnionitis or appendicitis).

**Application to the Case**
In this patient, the pathophysiology was exacerbated by the anatomical nature of the "cranial" mass. Because it was a **pedunculated leiomyoma**, it was susceptible to **torsion**. The surgical finding that the mass was "twisted and leaking" indicates that torsion likely caused an acute cessation of arterial inflow and venous outflow, accelerating the ischemic necrosis. The "leaking" suggests the rupture of the degenerated, necrotic cyst, spilling inflammatory fluid into the peritoneal cavity. This explains the patient's severe left-upper quadrant pain and the low-grade fever observed during her admission. The pathological confirmation of "areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle" directly supports this diagnosis of torsion-induced red degeneration.

### [Morning Report] The patient received intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis starting intraoperatively. What is the physiological rationale for administering progesterone during an intra-pregnancy myomectomy, and what is the primary obstetric complication this is intended to prevent?

The administration of intramuscular progesterone during and after an intra-pregnancy myomectomy is intended to maintain **uterine quiescence** and prevent the primary obstetric complication of **preterm labor or premature delivery**.

The physiological rationale is grounded in the role of progesterone as the dominant hormone for maintaining a non-contractile state of the myometrium throughout pregnancy. Progesterone inhibits uterine contractions by reducing the expression of contraction-associated proteins (CAPs), such as oxytocin receptors and gap junctions, and by modulating calcium signaling within the smooth muscle cells. 

In the context of the presented case, the patient underwent a laparotomy and excision of a large pedunculated leiomyoma at 21 weeks of gestation. Surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus—particularly the mechanical stimulation involved in clamping and suturing the uterine wall—can trigger uterine irritability and spontaneous myometrial contractions. By providing pharmacological progesterone prophylaxis starting intraoperatively, the clinical team aimed to counteract this surgical stimulation and stabilize the uterine environment.

In this patient, the use of intramuscular progesterone followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate served as a stabilizing measure to ensure that the surgical trauma did not precipitate premature uterine activity, thereby supporting the continuation of the pregnancy until the planned elective caesarean delivery at 37 weeks.
