Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The initial presentation of this patient—a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation—highlights a challenging clinical triad of severe abdominal pain, mild dyspnoea, and a second-trimester pregnancy. While some dyspnoea can be associated with the physiological changes of pregnancy, the acute onset of pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis, coupled with respiratory distress, suggests a mechanical cause. In this case, the symptoms were not cardiovascular or pulmonary in origin but were instead the result of massive abdominal lesions exerting pressure on the diaphragm and pelvic structures.

Physical examination was critical in characterizing the extent of the abdominal pathology. Upon examination, the patient's abdomen appeared larger than expected for 21 weeks of gestational age. Two distinct masses were identified: a large, taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant and a separate, fixed, hard mass of approximately 9 cm in the lesser pelvis, identified via bimanual vaginal examination as bulging at the posterior fornix. The presence of two separate, large masses—one subphrenic and one pelvic—raised a broad differential diagnosis, ranging from large uterine leiomyomas to adnexal masses or ovarian neoplasms.

Immediate management focused on stabilizing the patient and alleviating the mechanical distress caused by the masses. The patient was placed in a semi-orthostatic position, a maneuver designed to shift the gravitational weight of the gravid uterus and the abdominal masses away from the diaphragm, thereby reducing respiratory compression. Combined with intravenous paracetamol for analgesia, this approach successfully reduced the patient's reported pain score from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale and mitigated the dyspnoea without the need for supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation.

Baseline laboratory evaluations revealed several concurrent comorbidities that complicated the clinical picture. The patient presented with iron deficiency anaemia, evidenced by a haemoglobin level of 9.9 g/L and low serum ferritin (25 mcg/L). Additionally, the development of a low-grade fever and an elevated neutrophilic white blood cell count (11.29 $\times 10^3$/uL) and C-reactive protein (1.39 mg/dL) suggested an inflammatory or infectious process. While these elevations can be seen in normal pregnancy, further investigation identified a *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection. These findings were essential for the multidisciplinary team to establish a baseline of maternal health and manage systemic infections before proceeding with diagnostic imaging and potential surgical intervention.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Reasoning

Diagnosing abdominal masses during pregnancy is challenging due to the anatomical displacement caused by the gravid uterus and the physiological changes of pregnancy that can mimic pathology. In this case, the patient presented with two large masses—one in the left subphrenic space and one in the pelvis—requiring a multimodal imaging approach to differentiate between ovarian and uterine origins.

Ultrasonography (US) served as the initial screening tool, which is the standard first-line modality in pregnancy due to its safety and accessibility. In this patient, US identified two unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. While the upper mass appeared isolated, the lower mass was found to be adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. For the clinician, the presence of cystic components and peripheral flow on Doppler can either suggest a degenerating myoma or an ovarian cyst, such as an endometrioma; however, the "steric bulk" of the pregnant uterus often obscures the exact point of origin (the pedicle or stalk), making it difficult to determine if a mass is truly adnexal or pedunculated from the uterus.

When US findings are inconclusive, non-contrast MRI is the preferred second-line imaging modality. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is generally avoided during pregnancy due to potential fetal risks, but non-contrast MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast and diagnostic accuracy for identifying the relationship between a mass and the uterine cavity. In this case, MRI revealed thick-walled cysts with inhomogeneous fluid content and focal solid components. Crucially, MRI suggested that the caudal mass was in continuity with the posterior uterine leiomyoma, a finding that strongly shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a uterine origin rather than an independent ovarian lesion.

The interpretation of serum tumor markers, specifically CA-125, requires caution during pregnancy. CA-125 is often used as a marker for ovarian malignancy or endometriosis, but it can be physiologically elevated during the first and third trimesters. In this patient, the CA-125 was slightly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. While this value exceeded the standard oncological cutoff of 35 U/mL, the clinical team had to balance this with the knowledge that CA-125 can rise in benign conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, and even some leiomyomas. In this context, the slight elevation of CA-125 initially favored a diagnosis of decidualised endometriomas.

The differential diagnosis ultimately centered on distinguishing between decidualised endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. Endometriomas often present as cystic adnexal masses and correlate with elevated CA-125. Conversely, leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration—particularly during pregnancy due to rapid growth and subsequent outstripping of their blood supply—which can mimic a cyst on imaging. The clinical reasoning in this case integrated the imaging and laboratory data: the cystic appearance and CA-125 elevation suggested endometriomas, but the MRI-confirmed continuity with the uterine wall pointed toward leiomyomas. This highlights a common diagnostic pitfall: pedunculated leiomyomas can mimic adnexal masses by appearing displaced from the uterus, requiring high suspicion and detailed imaging to identify the uterine stalk.

## Surgical Decision-Making and Intraoperative Management

In the management of uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, conservative treatment is the gold standard. Surgical intervention is typically delayed until the postpartum period to avoid the high risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, and the induction of preterm labor. However, the clinical priority shifts when a patient presents with "complicated" masses. Indications for urgent myomectomy during pregnancy include acute complications such as torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma, rupture, or severe symptomatic compression that impairs maternal function. In this case, the patient’s severe abdominal pain and restrictive lung disease—manifesting as dyspnea due to the mechanical compression of the diaphragm by an 18 cm mass—provided a clear clinical indication for surgical intervention at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation.

The choice of surgical approach depends on the size, number, and location of the masses. While laparoscopic approaches may be considered for smaller, non-intramural masses in the first or early second trimester, a laparotomy is preferred for large, multiple, or critically located masses. In this patient, the sheer volume of the two masses and the presence of an intramural-subserosal component made a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy the safest option. This approach allowed for a comprehensive exploration of the abdominal cavity and provided the necessary exposure to handle large masses without risking uterine rupture or fetal injury.

Intraoperative findings are critical for refining the diagnosis and directing the surgical strategy. Upon exploration, the upper mass was identified as a huge, highly vascularized, pedunculated leiomyoma. The surgical team discovered that the mass had twisted (torsion) and was leaking, which likely contributed to the patient's intraoperative fever and acute pain. Torsion of a pedunculated myoma is a surgical emergency, as it leads to ischemic necrosis and potential infection, necessitating immediate excision to prevent further maternal morbidity.

The primary intraoperative goal in pregnancy is to balance the removal of pathological tissue with the preservation of the pregnancy. To minimize obstetric risk and the potential for uterine trauma, the surgical team employed a selective myomectomy strategy. The upper pedunculated mass was excised using a double clamp technique to control bleeding, and the uterine root was carefully sutured. Conversely, the caudal mass—which was fixed and appeared as a cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—was left in situ. Because this second mass showed no signs of torsion or acute complication, removing it would have unnecessarily increased the risk of hemorrhage and preterm delivery. To further protect the pregnancy during and after the procedure, the team utilized serial fetal heart rate monitoring and administered intramuscular progesterone prophylaxis to reduce the risk of preterm labor.

## Postoperative Care and Obstetric Follow-up

Following the surgical excision of the upper pedunculated leiomyoma, postoperative management focused on stabilizing the patient's hematologic and infectious status while prioritizing the maintenance of the pregnancy. The patient experienced a postoperative drop in hemoglobin (Hb 7.5 g/L) and hematocrit (24.3%), compounding a pre-existing iron deficiency. To restore maternal oxygen-carrying capacity and minimize fetal risk, the clinical team utilized a combination of intravenous iron supplementation (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) and two blood transfusions, which successfully stabilized her hemoglobin at 10.0 g/L. Simultaneously, the *Klebsiella pneumoniae* urinary tract infection identified preoperatively was treated with a targeted regimen of intravenous cefazolin and clarithromycin, guided by the culture's antibiogram.

To mitigate the risk of preterm labor or uterine irritability following major uterine surgery during the second trimester, the patient received prophylactic progesterone. This began intraoperatively with 100 mg intramuscular (IM) doses, continuing daily until discharge. Long-term pregnancy maintenance was then managed with intramuscular caproate hydroxyprogesterone (341 mg every three days) until delivery. Such pharmacological support is critical in high-risk scenarios where surgical manipulation of the uterus may trigger premature uterine contractions.

Obstetric follow-up was intensified, with evaluations occurring every three weeks to monitor fetal viability, the amniotic fluid index, and fetal growth. Despite the presence of a large residual caudal mass, the fetus showed healthy development, reaching the 86th percentile on the WHO fetal growth chart. This rigorous monitoring was essential to ensure that the residual uterine mass did not compromise placental perfusion or fetal development as the pregnancy progressed into the third trimester.

The final stage of management involved determining the safest mode of delivery. Although vaginal delivery is generally preferred, the residual caudal leiomyoma—located in the pelvis and originating from the uterine torus—presented a significant mechanical obstruction. The multidisciplinary team concluded that this mass would likely impede the progression of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor, increasing the risk of obstructed labor and fetal distress. Consequently, an elective caesarean section was performed at 37 weeks of gestation. By utilizing the existing surgical scar from the previous laparotomy, the surgical team minimized further uterine trauma, resulting in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

## Clinical Significance: Leiomyomas in the Gravid Uterus

Uterine leiomyomas, while common, present unique challenges when diagnosed during pregnancy due to their unpredictable growth patterns and the potential for acute complications. As seen in this case, the size of these tumors does not follow a linear trajectory. Research indicates that leiomyomas often grow most rapidly during the first and second trimesters, with an average increase of 6.90% per week. This growth is most pronounced in the first trimester (6.12%) and slows slightly in the second (3.10%). In the third trimester and the immediate postpartum period, these masses often undergo regression, as demonstrated by the slight reduction in size of the residual caudal mass in this patient. This physiological trend explains why symptomatic presentations, such as the acute pain and dyspnea experienced by this patient at 21 weeks, often occur during the mid-gestation period.

The surgical management of leiomyomas during pregnancy is generally avoided unless absolutely necessary, as the gravid uterus is highly vascular and prone to severe hemorrhage. Myomectomy carries significant risks, including pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. However, surgical intervention is indicated when conservative management fails or when acute complications arise. In this case, the "cranial" mass presented as a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and leakage, leading to fever and severe pain. Such complications—torsion, rupture, or infection—necessitate immediate surgical exploration to prevent maternal instability and fetal loss.

The anatomical location of a leiomyoma—whether submucosal, intramural, or subserosal—critically influences both the symptoms and the mode of delivery. Submucosal myomas may distort the uterine cavity and increase the risk of miscarriage or malpresentation. Intramural and subserosal masses, particularly those located in the lower uterine segment or the pelvic torus, can act as mechanical obstructions. In this patient, while the pedunculated upper mass was safely excised, the caudal mass was left in situ to minimize the risk of preterm labor. Because this residual mass occupied the lesser pelvis and would have obstructed the progression of the fetal head during the expulsive phase of labor, an elective caesarean delivery was the safest obstetric choice.

Managing complex obstetric-surgical cases requires a multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprising surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians. This collaborative approach is essential for balancing maternal surgical needs with fetal viability. In this case, the MDT ensured a safe diagnostic pathway by utilizing non-contrast MRI to avoid gadolinium exposure and implemented intraoperative safeguards, such as continuous fetal heart rate monitoring and progesterone prophylaxis, to reduce the risk of preterm uterine contractions. Such a coordinated strategy allows for the successful management of atypical masses while prioritizing the outcome for both the mother and the neonate.
