Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and the Pitfalls of 'Pseudo-angioedema'

When evaluating a patient with acute or subacute neck swelling, the clinician must prioritize airway stability while differentiating between infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic etiologies. In this case, the patient's presentation—a three-week history of progressive neck swelling—initially pointed toward a deep neck space infection. A primary consideration in such cases is Ludwig’s angina, a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the submandibular space. The initial CT scan in this patient supported this suspicion, showing diffuse edema of the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. However, the clinical picture was inconsistent with a typical bacterial infection; the patient was afebrile and lacked the acute, toxic appearance usually associated with Ludwig's angina.

The subacute nature of the swelling—progressing over three weeks without fever—shifted the diagnostic focus away from infection and toward an immunological or inflammatory process. To exclude structural or obstructive causes, such as tumors or anatomical abnormalities of the upper airway, a flexible nasendoscopy was performed. While the examination confirmed that the soft palate, larynx, and post-cricoid space were anatomically normal, it revealed increased secretions and coughing, which served as an early, subtle hint of potential aspiration or pharyngeal dysfunction.

As the focus shifted to immunology, the swelling was viewed through the lens of angioedema. In clinical practice, angioedema is typically categorized by its mediator: histaminergic (allergic) reactions, which often present with pruritus and urticaria; bradykinin-mediated reactions, such as those caused by ACE inhibitors or hereditary C1 esterase deficiency; and non-immune-mediated edema. This patient lacked a history of ACE inhibitor use or familial angioedema, and his presentation was atypical for a standard allergic reaction. The "pseudo-angioedema" seen in this case was not caused by the typical vascular leak associated with histamine or bradykinin, but rather by severe muscle inflammation and subcutaneous tissue edema secondary to dermatomyositis.

The diagnostic pitfall in this case was the tendency to view neck swelling as an isolated ENT or immunological symptom. The diagnosis remained elusive until a broader systemic survey was performed. The discovery of proximal muscle weakness in the hip flexors, mild myalgia, and subtle cutaneous clues—specifically periungual erythema and a rash on the lateral thigh—provided the necessary context to link the "angioedema" to a systemic inflammatory myopathy. Recognizing that dermatomyositis can manifest with profound soft tissue swelling of the neck highlights the importance of a comprehensive physical examination and a high index of suspicion for systemic disease when a localized presentation is atypical for its most common differentials.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Pivoting from Localized to Systemic Disease

In clinical practice, a "narrow" initial presentation can often lead to premature diagnostic closure. In this case, the patient presented with severe neck swelling, which initially directed the clinical focus toward a localized ENT or immunological emergency. The initial suspicion of Ludwig’s angina was reasonable given the CT findings of diffuse neck edema and inflammation in the retropharyngeal space. However, when the ENT evaluation failed to find a structural or infective cause, the clinical reasoning had to pivot from a localized focus to a systemic search.

The first step in this pivot occurred during the evaluation for angioedema. While the patient had significant soft tissue swelling, several "red flags" suggested that this was not a typical histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated event. Specifically, the patient lacked the common triggers for acquired angioedema—he was not taking ACE inhibitors or DPP-4 inhibitors—and had no personal or family history of hereditary angioedema. Furthermore, the presence of generalized facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, was atypical for simple angioedema and suggested a more complex inflammatory or dermatological process.

When a patient presents with an unexplained "pseudo-angioedema," a comprehensive physical examination is essential to uncover systemic clues. In this case, the pivot to a systemic diagnosis was driven by two critical findings: subtle cutaneous changes and proximal muscle weakness. A detailed skin and nail examination revealed periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy. While the classic "heliotrope rash" and Gottron’s papules of dermatomyositis were absent, the discovery of a rash on the lateral thigh and a reduction in hip flexor power (4/5) shifted the differential toward an inflammatory myopathy. Assessing proximal muscle strength—specifically the hip flexors and shoulder girdle—is a high-yield maneuver when systemic autoimmune disease is suspected.

Once the clinical suspicion shifted toward myopathy, targeted laboratory testing provided the definitive evidence. The request for a creatine kinase (CK) level is a critical diagnostic step in any patient with suspected muscle inflammation. In this patient, the CK was massively elevated at 16,184 U/L, signaling significant muscle fiber necrosis. This was supported by elevations in aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). It is a common clinical pitfall to attribute elevated ALT and AST solely to liver dysfunction; however, in the context of high CK and proximal weakness, these enzymes serve as markers of muscle breakdown.

Ultimately, this case demonstrates that subcutaneous soft tissue edema can be a manifestation of underlying muscle inflammation rather than a primary vascular leak. The "angioedema" in this patient was actually the result of severe myositis affecting the strap muscles of the neck, leading to secondary edema of the overlying tissues. By transitioning from a localized ENT perspective to a systemic autoimmune perspective—prompted by a thorough skin exam, a proximal muscle assessment, and a CK level—the clinicians were able to diagnose a rare presentation of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis.

## The Diagnostic Workup of Dermatomyositis

Confirming a diagnosis of dermatomyositis requires a multidisciplinary approach that integrates serological testing, advanced imaging, histopathology, and functional assessments of systemic involvement. In this case, the diagnostic challenge began with a presentation of neck swelling that mimicked angioedema. However, the presence of proximal muscle weakness and subtle cutaneous signs—such as periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages—prompted a shift in reasoning toward an inflammatory myopathy. This was first supported by a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L and elevated transaminases (AST and ALT), which are markers of muscle breakdown.

Serological screening for autoimmune diseases typically begins with an anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test. In this patient, a high-titer speckled ANA (1:1280) indicated a systemic autoimmune process. To refine the diagnosis, a myositis-specific antibody panel was employed. This panel was strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) antibodies. Anti-SAE1 antibodies target an enzyme involved in "sumoylation," a post-translational modification of proteins. Clinically, SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is associated with a high prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD), as well as an increased risk of malignancy, necessitating the comprehensive cancer screening (PET scans and endoscopies) performed in this case.

Imaging plays a critical role in identifying the distribution and activity of muscle inflammation. While MRI of the thighs in this patient confirmed active myositis in the obturator and iliopsoas muscles, an FDG-PET scan provided a more comprehensive systemic view. The PET scan revealed diffuse, abnormal FDG accumulation not only in the limbs but prominently in the head and neck, as well as the chest wall and abdomen. This finding is crucial because it demonstrates that the patient's "pseudo-angioedema" was actually a manifestation of severe muscle and soft tissue inflammation in the cervical region, rather than a histamine- or bradykinin-mediated event.

The gold standard for confirming dermatomyositis is a muscle biopsy. The histopathological hallmark of this disease is a perifascicular distribution of inflammation. In this patient, the biopsy revealed necrotic and regenerative myofibres concentrated at the periphery of the muscle fascicles. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry showed the deposition of the membrane attack complex (complement component C5b-9) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding is diagnostic of the complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies where inflammation may be more centrally located within the fascicle.

Finally, because dermatomyositis often affects multiple organ systems, functional evaluations are necessary to guide management. Pulmonary function tests in this patient revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, and a CT chest showed non-cavitating nodules and peribronchial consolidation. To evaluate the severity of the patient's dysphagia, a video fluoroscopic barium swallow was performed, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids into the vocal cords. This explained the patient's risk for aspiration pneumonitis and the subsequent need for nasogastric tube feeding during the acute phase of treatment.

## Pathophysiology of anti-SAE1 Positive Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by a systemic autoimmune attack targeting the microvasculature of the skin and muscle. The primary pathophysiological mechanism involves the activation of the complement cascade, leading to the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) within the endomysial capillaries. This capillary destruction results in microvascular ischemia and subsequent myofibre necrosis. A hallmark of this process is perifascicular atrophy, where the muscle fibres at the periphery of a fascicle undergo degeneration and necrosis due to their precarious blood supply. In the provided case, the muscle biopsy confirmed this pattern, showing necrotic, vacuolated myofibres in a perifascicular distribution with C5b-9 deposition in the capillaries.

The specific autoantibody found in this patient targets the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1). This enzyme is a critical component of the SUMO pathway, which governs "sumoylation"—a post-translational modification process similar to ubiquitination. In sumoylation, Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) proteins are covalently attached to target proteins to regulate nuclear-cytoplasmic transport, transcriptional regulation, and protein stability. While the precise mechanism by which anti-SAE1 antibodies trigger the inflammatory cascade of dermatomyositis is not fully elucidated, the presence of these antibodies serves as a significant biomarker for a specific clinical phenotype.

Patients positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies often exhibit a distinct constellation of systemic involvements. There is a high prevalence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and dysphagia, both of which were present in this patient. The involvement of the pharyngeal and esophageal muscles leads to impaired bolus propulsion and a failure of the protective airway reflexes. This pharyngeal muscle weakness results in micro-aspiration, where small amounts of food or secretions enter the trachea and lower respiratory tract. This explains the "patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation" and "aspiration pneumonitis" seen on the patient's chest CT, which are distinct from the parenchymal changes seen in classic ILD.

Cutaneous manifestations are a diagnostic cornerstone of dermatomyositis. While the "heliotrope rash" (violaceous eruption on the upper eyelids) and "Gottron’s papules" (erythematous papules over the knuckles) are classic signs, DM can present with more varied patterns. The "shawl sign" refers to a rash across the shoulders and upper back, while the "Holster sign"—present in this patient—manifests as erythema on the lateral thighs, mimicking the area where a firearm holster would rest. These skin changes, along with the periungual telangiectasias and microhaemorrhages observed on nailfold capillaroscopy in this case, reflect the underlying systemic vasculopathy and capillary fragility characteristic of the disease.

## Therapeutic Management and Long-term Surveillance

The management of severe dermatomyositis requires a multi-modal immunosuppressive strategy tailored to the patient's organ involvement and risk profile. In this case, the patient presented with severe cervical soft tissue swelling and dysphagia, which posed a significant threat to his airway and nutritional status. To address this acute inflammation, the patient was treated with "pulse" therapy—high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for three days). This aggressive initial approach is intended to rapidly suppress the systemic inflammatory response before transitioning to a lower-dose oral prednisone tapering regimen.

To minimize the long-term side effects of high-dose corticosteroids, steroid-sparing agents are introduced early in the course of treatment. In this case, a combination of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and rituximab was utilized. MMF is frequently chosen for its established efficacy in inflammatory myopathies and its relatively favorable tolerability. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody against CD20, is often employed in refractory or severe cases to deplete B cells and reduce the production of pathogenic autoantibodies, such as the anti-SAE1 antibody seen here. While azathioprine is another common steroid-sparing option, MMF was preferred in this instance based on the patient's preference and tolerability. 

Notably, methotrexate—a cornerstone of many autoimmune treatments—was specifically avoided. In patients with dermatomyositis, the presence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) or abnormal pulmonary function tests (as seen in this patient's restrictive pattern and CT findings) can make methotrexate a risky choice due to its potential for pulmonary toxicity. In such cases, MMF is often a safer and more effective alternative for both the skin and lung manifestations of the disease.

For patients with severe muscle weakness or those who do not respond sufficiently to first-line agents, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) plays a critical role. This patient received a loading dose of 2 g/kg followed by maintenance cycles every four weeks. IVIG is thought to work by neutralizing circulating antibodies and modulating the immune response, providing a bridge to recovery while slower-acting steroid-sparing agents take effect. Due to the intensity of this immunosuppression, the patient required prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia; notably, nebulized pentamidine was used after sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim triggered a hemolytic anemia.

Beyond the immediate inflammatory crisis, long-term surveillance for malignancy is a mandatory component of dermatomyositis management, as the disease can occur as a paraneoplastic phenomenon. This patient was categorized as "high risk" according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) criteria. His risk factors included the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself, the presence of dysphagia, his male sex, and the presence of SAE1 antibodies. Consequently, a comprehensive screening program was implemented, including PET scans, CT imaging, and tumor markers, with planned endoscopic evaluations of the gastrointestinal tract.

Monitoring for remission involves both biochemical markers and clinical functional assessments. In this case, the normalization of serum creatine kinase (CK)—which had peaked at 16,184 U/L—served as a primary objective marker of reduced muscle destruction. However, clinical recovery is the ultimate goal; the resolution of cervical swelling and the eventual ability to swallow soft solids (allowing for the removal of the nasogastric tube) were the definitive indicators of therapeutic success. By the 12-month mark, the patient achieved stable respiratory function and complete resolution of the "pseudo-angioedema," demonstrating the efficacy of the combined immunosuppressive approach.
