Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] A middle-aged patient presents with progressive, non-tender neck swelling and facial erythema. After excluding deep neck space infections and typical bradykinin- or histamine-mediated angioedema, what autoimmune inflammatory condition should be considered, and what clinical 'clues' in this case suggested a systemic rather than a localized process?

In the clinical scenario of progressive, non-tender neck swelling and facial erythema where infection and classic angioedema (histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated) have been excluded, clinicians should consider **dermatomyositis** as a critical differential diagnosis. While facial and neck edema—often termed "pseudoangioedema"—is an uncommon presentation of dermatomyositis, it can masquerade as a primary otolaryngologic disorder or an idiopathic angioedema.

In this case, several key clinical "clues" shifted the diagnostic focus from a localized neck process to a systemic autoimmune inflammatory condition:

*   **Neuromuscular Findings:** The patient presented with proximal muscle weakness (specifically in the hip flexors) and mild myalgias. This systemic distribution of weakness is a hallmark of inflammatory myopathies and is inconsistent with a localized neck infection or isolated angioedema.
*   **Cutaneous and Ungual Markers:** While the classic heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules were absent, the patient exhibited generalized facial erythema (including the nasolabial folds), a rash on the lateral thigh (the "holster sign"), and periungual erythema. Notably, nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhemorrhages and avascular areas, which are highly suggestive of a systemic connective tissue disease.
*   **Laboratory Evidence of Myonecrosis:** The discovery of a profoundly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level (16,184 U/L), along with elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), provided objective evidence of widespread muscle inflammation and damage.
*   **Multisystem Involvement:** The presence of severe pharyngeal dysphagia and an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function tests pointed toward a systemic process affecting the striated muscles of the upper esophagus and the pulmonary parenchyma.

The pathophysiology of the neck swelling in this patient was not due to vasoactive mediators but rather reflected severe inflammatory edema arising from myositis of the strap muscles and surrounding soft tissues. This case underscores the importance of a comprehensive physical examination—specifically checking for proximal weakness and periungual changes—and the utility of a CK level when "angioedema" presents with atypical features.

### [Clinical Pearls] Dermatomyositis is characterized by several pathognomonic cutaneous findings. Beyond the classic heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules, what is the 'Holster sign,' and what is the significance of nailfold capillaroscopy in these patients?

The **holster sign** is a cutaneous manifestation of dermatomyositis characterized by macular erythema located on the lateral aspect of the thighs, mirroring the position where a handgun holster would typically rest. In this case, although the patient lacked the classic heliotrope rash and Gottron’s papules, the discovery of a rash on the right lateral thigh was a critical clue that pointed toward a diagnosis of dermatomyositis.

**Nailfold capillaroscopy** is a non-invasive diagnostic technique used to visualize the capillaries at the nail fold. In patients with dermatomyositis, this tool is significant because it reveals the underlying systemic microangiopathy that characterizes the disease. Key findings include:
*   **Periungual telangiectasias:** Dilated, enlarged capillaries.
*   **Microhemorrhages:** Small areas of bleeding within the nail fold.
*   **Avascular areas:** Regions where capillaries have dropped out, leaving "ghost" vessels or empty spaces.

In the presented case, the finding of proximal nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy provided early objective evidence of microvascular damage, which—alongside the elevated creatine kinase and the holster sign—allowed the clinical team to look beyond the initial presentation of "pseudoangioedema" and correctly diagnose SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis.

### [Pathophysiology] In the setting of suspected inflammatory myopathy, the muscle biopsy for this patient showed C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. What is the pathophysiologic significance of this finding, and how does it help differentiate dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies?

The deposition of **C5b-9** (the membrane attack complex) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries is a hallmark of the pathophysiology of **dermatomyositis (DM)**. 

From a pathophysiologic standpoint, this finding signifies that dermatomyositis is primarily a **complement-mediated microangiopathy** rather than a primary muscle disease. The activation of the complement cascade leads to the formation of the membrane attack complex on the walls of the endomysial capillaries, resulting in capillary destruction, microvascular ischemia, and subsequent myofiber necrosis. Because the blood supply to a muscle fascicle is most precarious at its periphery, the resulting ischemia and necrosis occur in a characteristic **perifascicular distribution**, as seen in this patient's biopsy.

This finding is critical for differentiating dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies:

*   **Dermatomyositis:** The pathology is characterized by a humoral-mediated attack on the vasculature. The presence of C5b-9 deposition and perifascicular atrophy/necrosis distinguishes it as a vasculopathy.
*   **Polymyositis:** In contrast, polymyositis is primarily a cell-mediated process. It is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates (predominantly CD8+ T cells) that directly invade non-necrotic muscle fibers, without the primary microvascular complement deposition seen here.
*   **Immune-Mediated Necrotizing Myopathy (IMNM):** While IMNM presents with prominent myofiber necrosis and regeneration (similar to the necrotic fibers seen in this patient), it typically lacks the specific perifascicular distribution and the characteristic complement-mediated capillary damage.

In this case, the combination of significantly elevated creatine kinase, anti-SAE1 antibodies, and the biopsy finding of C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular capillaries confirms the diagnosis of dermatomyositis and explains the systemic nature of the patient's presentation, including the atypical "pseudoangioedema" caused by profound muscle inflammation and edema.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient presented with significantly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels alongside a very high creatine kinase (CK). In a patient with suspected dermatomyositis, how should an attending interpret an isolated elevation of transaminases in the absence of jaundice or liver failure?

In a patient with suspected dermatomyositis or other inflammatory myopathies, an isolated elevation of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in the absence of jaundice, conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, or coagulopathy should be interpreted as a reflection of **skeletal muscle injury** rather than primary hepatic dysfunction.

This phenomenon occurs because AST and ALT are not liver-specific enzymes. While ALT is more specific to the liver, both are present in striated muscle. AST, in particular, is found in high concentrations within skeletal muscle cells. When extensive myonecrosis or inflammation occurs—as evidenced by the profoundly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L in this patient—these enzymes leak from the damaged myocytes into the systemic circulation.

In this case, the patient's AST (396 U/L) and ALT (143 U/L) elevations are "pseudo-transaminitis." The clinical reasoning is supported by the discordance between the laboratory values and the clinical state; the patient lacked the hallmarks of liver failure or cholestasis, yet demonstrated severe muscle involvement confirmed by MRI and biopsy.

The key clinical pearl for residents is that in the setting of severe myositis or rhabdomyolysis, the transaminases serve as additional markers of muscle breakdown. Therefore, an attending should avoid unnecessary hepatic workups or diagnostic delays when these elevations are seen in tandem with a markedly elevated CK and a clinical picture consistent with myopathy.

### [Management] According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines, this patient was categorized as 'high risk' for malignancy. Which specific clinical and serological features of this patient's presentation contributed to this risk stratification?

Based on the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines for cancer screening in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, this patient was stratified as **high risk** for malignancy due to a combination of high-risk and intermediate-risk clinical and serological features.

Specifically, the features contributing to this risk stratification in this patient were:

*   **High-Risk Factors:**
    *   **Diagnosis of Dermatomyositis:** The patient's clinical presentation (periungual erythema, Holster sign) and biopsy findings (perifascicular distribution of necrosis and C5b-9 deposition) confirmed dermatomyositis, which is more strongly associated with malignancy than other inflammatory myopathies.
    *   **Presence of Dysphagia:** The patient exhibited severe pharyngeal dysphagia, as confirmed by a video fluoroscopic barium swallow study. In the context of myositis, dysphagia is considered a high-risk marker for associated malignancy.

*   **Intermediate-Risk Factors:**
    *   **Serological Markers (Anti-SAE1 Antibodies):** The patient tested strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies. While not as strongly linked as some other myositis-specific antibodies (e.g., anti-TIF1-$\gamma$), anti-SAE1 positivity is an intermediate risk factor.
    *   **Demographics (Male Sex):** Being male is categorized as an intermediate risk factor for malignancy in the setting of dermatomyositis.

In accordance with the IMACS framework, the presence of multiple high-risk factors, supplemented by intermediate-risk factors, necessitated a comprehensive malignancy workup. In this patient, this included CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, a PET scan, and tumor marker screening, with planned gastrointestinal endoscopies to exclude occult malignancy.

### [Management] The medical team chose to use mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab as steroid-sparing agents but specifically avoided methotrexate. Based on the patient's pulmonary function tests and CT chest findings, what was the clinical reasoning for avoiding methotrexate in this case?

The decision to avoid methotrexate in this patient was based on the presence of baseline pulmonary dysfunction, which increases the risk of drug-induced pulmonary toxicity.

Methotrexate is a cornerstone steroid-sparing agent in the management of dermatomyositis; however, it is associated with a well-documented risk of methotrexate-induced pneumonitis, a hypersensitivity reaction that can lead to interstitial lung disease (ILD) and permanent pulmonary fibrosis. In clinical practice, the presence of pre-existing lung disease or abnormal pulmonary function tests (PFTs) is often considered a relative contraindication or a factor necessitating extreme caution, as these patients may be more susceptible to pulmonary toxicity or may have their existing respiratory compromise exacerbated.

In this specific case, the patient's pulmonary function tests demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. Furthermore, his CT chest revealed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and patchy peribronchial consolidation. While the consolidation was attributed to aspiration pneumonitis secondary to severe pharyngeal dysphagia, the combination of a restrictive pattern on PFTs and parenchymal abnormalities on imaging indicated that the patient already had compromised pulmonary reserve. 

To minimize the risk of precipitating a severe drug-induced pneumonitis or worsening his restrictive lung disease, the medical team opted for alternative immunosuppressants—mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab—which provide effective steroid-sparing activity without the same specific risk profile for methotrexate-induced pulmonary fibrosis.
