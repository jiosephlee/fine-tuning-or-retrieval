Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The Silent Airway Threat: Dysphagia and Aspiration in Myositis

When a patient walks into the ED with a swollen neck and difficulty swallowing, our brains immediately go to the "can't breathe" folder. We think Ludwig’s angina, epiglottitis, or a massive angioedema reaction. We call ENT, we grab the glide-scope, and we start thinking about airway management. But what happens when the swelling isn't an infection or an allergic reaction, but rather the manifestation of a systemic autoimmune attack on the muscles themselves?

Enter a case that serves as a stark reminder that dermatomyositis is not just a "skin and proximal limb" disease. A male in his 40s presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and mild hip weakness. Early imaging (CT neck) showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and the retropharyngeal space. Because he was afebrile and the swelling was non-tender, the suspicion shifted from Ludwig's angina to idiopathic angioedema. On the surface, it looked like a classic "pseudo-angioedema" presentation—non-pitting, non-pruritic swelling of the neck and face.

The "aha!" moment came from a combination of a thorough physical exam and a high index of suspicion. Subtle clues were hiding in plain sight: periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, and a mild weakness in the hip flexors (4/5 power). When the team ordered a creatine kinase (CK) level, the result was staggering: 16,184 U/L. This shifted the differential entirely from "allergic/vascular" to "myopathic."

While the neck swelling is a dramatic external sign, the real danger in this case was internal. The patient was exhibiting "silent" aspiration. During an ENT nasendoscopy, the only clues were increased secretions and some coughing. It wasn't until a video fluoroscopic swallow study was performed that the severity became clear: the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia, with thin and thick fluids penetrating the vocal cords and residue clogging the pyriform fossae. This wasn't just a "difficulty swallowing" issue; it was an airway protection failure. This had already led to aspiration pneumonitis, evidenced by patchy peribronchial consolidation on CT chest.

This case highlights a critical a subtype of dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme) antibodies. This specific antibody profile is often linked to a high incidence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. In this patient, the "angioedema" was actually muscle inflammation so severe it caused massive soft-tissue edema in the neck, which, coupled with pharyngeal muscle weakness, created a perfect storm for respiratory failure via aspiration.

The management of these patients requires aggressive, multi-modal immunosuppression. This patient required pulse-dose methylprednisolone (1g daily for 3 days), followed by a combination of IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. Because the pharyngeal muscles were compromised, he required a nasogastric tube for two months to prevent further aspiration while the inflammation subsided.

The takeaway here is that we cannot view myositis as purely a "limb weakness" condition. When the pharyngeal muscles are hit, the patient is at risk of aspiration that can be subtle and silent until they develop pneumonia or respiratory failure. If you see "angioedema" that doesn't respond to standard therapy, or an unusual skin rash paired with any systemic weakness, check a CK and look closely at the nailfolds.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Beyond the Limbs:** Dermatomyositis can present as "pseudo-angioedema," specifically involving the neck and facial soft tissues, mimicking bradykinin or histamine-mediated swelling.
*   **The Silent Threat:** Pharyngeal involvement in myositis can lead to aspiration pneumonitis. A "mild cough" during an exam may actually be a sign of severe dysphagia.
*   **Diagnostic Utility:** In suspected myositis with swallowing complaints, a video fluoroscopic swallow study is essential to quantify aspiration risk and guide nutritional support (e.g., NGT).
*   **The "Quick" Screen:** If a patient presents with unexplained edema and skin changes, a CK level is a rapid, high-yield screen for inflammatory myopathies.
*   **High-Risk Markers:** Anti-SAE1 antibodies are specifically associated with a higher prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, and they carry a significant association with underlying malignancy.
