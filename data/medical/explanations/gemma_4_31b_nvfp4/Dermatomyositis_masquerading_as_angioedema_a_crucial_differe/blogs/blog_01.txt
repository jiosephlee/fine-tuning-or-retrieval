Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When 'Angioedema' Isn't Angioedema: The Great Mimics of Neck Swelling

We’ve all been there: a patient rolls into the ED with a neck that looks like it’s about to burst. The immediate mental algorithm is a race to rule out the "killers"—Ludwig’s angina, a ruptured carotid, or anaphylaxis. Once the airway is secured and the CT scan rules out a focal abscess or a hematoma, we often settle into the "angioedema" bucket. We look for ACE inhibitors, check C4 levels, and wonder if it's a weird idiopathic hereditary fluke. 

But every now and then, a case comes along that reminds us that "swelling" is a nonspecific sign and that our diagnostic buckets have holes in them.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Pivot

A male in his 40s presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. He wasn't febrile, and he wasn't tachycardic, but his neck was erythematous and non-tender. The initial CT was concerning for diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space. Because it looked like a deep neck space infection, ENT was called. No abscess. No structural abnormality.

The focus then shifted to angioedema. He had no history of hereditary angioedema, no new medications, and no triggers. However, the "angioedema" didn't quite fit the pattern. While the neck was swollen, the patient also had mild myalgias and a subtle weakness in his hip flexors. When you look closer—really look—you find the clues: periungual erythema and a faint rash on the lateral thigh.

The diagnostic pivot happened with a single lab value: a creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L.

This wasn't a histamine or bradykinin problem; it was an inflammatory myopathy. The patient was eventually diagnosed with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. The "angioedema" was actually pseudo-angioedema—massive inflammatory edema arising from muscle inflammation and vascular leakage, rather than the classic pathways of allergic or hereditary swelling.

## Why This Matters: The Mimicry

Dermatomyositis is a classic "great mimic." We usually look for the "textbook" signs: Gottron’s papules on the knuckles or a heliotrope rash on the eyelids. But this case highlights a more sinister and rare presentation. In some patients, particularly those with specific autoantibodies like SAE1 or MDA-5, the cutaneous manifestation isn't just a rash—it's profound edema.

When this happens in the neck, it can be catastrophic. This patient didn't just have a "fat neck"; he had severe pharyngeal dysphagia and micro-aspiration leading to peribronchial consolidation. The swelling wasn't just cosmetic; it was a functional failure of the muscles of deglutition and airway protection.

## The Clinical Rigor: Beyond the Swelling

If you find yourself staring at a patient with "idiopathic" angioedema that isn't responding to standard therapies, you need to broaden your lens. 

First, look at the periphery. Check the nail folds for microhemorrhages or telangiectasias. Look for the "Holster sign" (erythema on the lateral thighs) or the "Shawl sign" (rash across the shoulders). Second, check the strength. A patient who tells you they are "just feeling a bit tired" might actually have 4/5 power in their hip flexors.

Finally, don't be afraid of the CK. If the swelling is atypical or accompanied by any systemic or cutaneous signs, a CK level is a low-cost, high-yield screen that can shift the diagnosis from "mystery edema" to a treatable, systemic autoimmune disease.

## The Long Game: Malignancy and Management

Once the diagnosis of dermatomyositis is made, the job isn't over. These patients are at significant risk for occult malignancy. Based on the IMACS guidelines, this patient was high-risk due to his sex, the presence of dysphagia, and the SAE1 antibody. This requires an aggressive screening workup: CTs, PET scans, and endoscopies.

Treatment is intense because the stakes are high. This patient required "pulse" methylprednisolone (1g daily for 3 days), IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab to bring the inflammation under control and restore his ability to swallow.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudo-angioedema is a thing:** Dermatomyositis can present as severe facial and neck swelling that mimics histamine or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
*   **Look beyond the neck:** When faced with unexplained swelling, perform a full skin check (nails, thighs, chest) and a proximal muscle strength exam.
*   **The CK is your friend:** A massively elevated CK in the setting of "angioedema" should immediately raise suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy.
*   **Screen for cancer:** Dermatomyositis—especially with SAE1 antibodies and dysphagia—is a red flag for underlying malignancy.
*   **Airway and Swallowing:** Be mindful that neck swelling in myositis isn't just skin-deep; it often involves the pharyngeal muscles, leading to aspiration and dysphagia.
