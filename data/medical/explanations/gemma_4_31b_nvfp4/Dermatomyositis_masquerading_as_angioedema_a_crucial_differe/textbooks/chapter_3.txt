Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## The Diagnostic Workup of Dermatomyositis

Confirming a diagnosis of dermatomyositis requires a multidisciplinary approach that integrates serological testing, advanced imaging, histopathology, and functional assessments of systemic involvement. In this case, the diagnostic challenge began with a presentation of neck swelling that mimicked angioedema. However, the presence of proximal muscle weakness and subtle cutaneous signs—such as periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages—prompted a shift in reasoning toward an inflammatory myopathy. This was first supported by a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L and elevated transaminases (AST and ALT), which are markers of muscle breakdown.

Serological screening for autoimmune diseases typically begins with an anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test. In this patient, a high-titer speckled ANA (1:1280) indicated a systemic autoimmune process. To refine the diagnosis, a myositis-specific antibody panel was employed. This panel was strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier-1 activating enzyme (SAE1) antibodies. Anti-SAE1 antibodies target an enzyme involved in "sumoylation," a post-translational modification of proteins. Clinically, SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is associated with a high prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease (ILD), as well as an increased risk of malignancy, necessitating the comprehensive cancer screening (PET scans and endoscopies) performed in this case.

Imaging plays a critical role in identifying the distribution and activity of muscle inflammation. While MRI of the thighs in this patient confirmed active myositis in the obturator and iliopsoas muscles, an FDG-PET scan provided a more comprehensive systemic view. The PET scan revealed diffuse, abnormal FDG accumulation not only in the limbs but prominently in the head and neck, as well as the chest wall and abdomen. This finding is crucial because it demonstrates that the patient's "pseudo-angioedema" was actually a manifestation of severe muscle and soft tissue inflammation in the cervical region, rather than a histamine- or bradykinin-mediated event.

The gold standard for confirming dermatomyositis is a muscle biopsy. The histopathological hallmark of this disease is a perifascicular distribution of inflammation. In this patient, the biopsy revealed necrotic and regenerative myofibres concentrated at the periphery of the muscle fascicles. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry showed the deposition of the membrane attack complex (complement component C5b-9) in the perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This finding is diagnostic of the complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies where inflammation may be more centrally located within the fascicle.

Finally, because dermatomyositis often affects multiple organ systems, functional evaluations are necessary to guide management. Pulmonary function tests in this patient revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, and a CT chest showed non-cavitating nodules and peribronchial consolidation. To evaluate the severity of the patient's dysphagia, a video fluoroscopic barium swallow was performed, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue in the pyriform fossae and penetration of fluids into the vocal cords. This explained the patient's risk for aspiration pneumonitis and the subsequent need for nasogastric tube feeding during the acute phase of treatment.
