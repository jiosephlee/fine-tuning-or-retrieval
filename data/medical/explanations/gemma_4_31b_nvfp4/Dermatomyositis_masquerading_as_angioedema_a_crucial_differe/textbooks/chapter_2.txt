Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: Pivoting from Localized to Systemic Disease

In clinical practice, a "narrow" initial presentation can often lead to premature diagnostic closure. In this case, the patient presented with severe neck swelling, which initially directed the clinical focus toward a localized ENT or immunological emergency. The initial suspicion of Ludwig’s angina was reasonable given the CT findings of diffuse neck edema and inflammation in the retropharyngeal space. However, when the ENT evaluation failed to find a structural or infective cause, the clinical reasoning had to pivot from a localized focus to a systemic search.

The first step in this pivot occurred during the evaluation for angioedema. While the patient had significant soft tissue swelling, several "red flags" suggested that this was not a typical histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated event. Specifically, the patient lacked the common triggers for acquired angioedema—he was not taking ACE inhibitors or DPP-4 inhibitors—and had no personal or family history of hereditary angioedema. Furthermore, the presence of generalized facial erythema, including the nasolabial folds, was atypical for simple angioedema and suggested a more complex inflammatory or dermatological process.

When a patient presents with an unexplained "pseudo-angioedema," a comprehensive physical examination is essential to uncover systemic clues. In this case, the pivot to a systemic diagnosis was driven by two critical findings: subtle cutaneous changes and proximal muscle weakness. A detailed skin and nail examination revealed periungual erythema and nailfold microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy. While the classic "heliotrope rash" and Gottron’s papules of dermatomyositis were absent, the discovery of a rash on the lateral thigh and a reduction in hip flexor power (4/5) shifted the differential toward an inflammatory myopathy. Assessing proximal muscle strength—specifically the hip flexors and shoulder girdle—is a high-yield maneuver when systemic autoimmune disease is suspected.

Once the clinical suspicion shifted toward myopathy, targeted laboratory testing provided the definitive evidence. The request for a creatine kinase (CK) level is a critical diagnostic step in any patient with suspected muscle inflammation. In this patient, the CK was massively elevated at 16,184 U/L, signaling significant muscle fiber necrosis. This was supported by elevations in aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). It is a common clinical pitfall to attribute elevated ALT and AST solely to liver dysfunction; however, in the context of high CK and proximal weakness, these enzymes serve as markers of muscle breakdown.

Ultimately, this case demonstrates that subcutaneous soft tissue edema can be a manifestation of underlying muscle inflammation rather than a primary vascular leak. The "angioedema" in this patient was actually the result of severe myositis affecting the strap muscles of the neck, leading to secondary edema of the overlying tissues. By transitioning from a localized ENT perspective to a systemic autoimmune perspective—prompted by a thorough skin exam, a proximal muscle assessment, and a CK level—the clinicians were able to diagnose a rare presentation of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis.
