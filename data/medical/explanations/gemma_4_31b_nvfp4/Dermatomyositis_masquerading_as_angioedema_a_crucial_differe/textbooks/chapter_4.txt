Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 4: ## Pathophysiology of anti-SAE1 Positive Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by a systemic autoimmune attack targeting the microvasculature of the skin and muscle. The primary pathophysiological mechanism involves the activation of the complement cascade, leading to the deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) within the endomysial capillaries. This capillary destruction results in microvascular ischemia and subsequent myofibre necrosis. A hallmark of this process is perifascicular atrophy, where the muscle fibres at the periphery of a fascicle undergo degeneration and necrosis due to their precarious blood supply. In the provided case, the muscle biopsy confirmed this pattern, showing necrotic, vacuolated myofibres in a perifascicular distribution with C5b-9 deposition in the capillaries.

The specific autoantibody found in this patient targets the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1). This enzyme is a critical component of the SUMO pathway, which governs "sumoylation"—a post-translational modification process similar to ubiquitination. In sumoylation, Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) proteins are covalently attached to target proteins to regulate nuclear-cytoplasmic transport, transcriptional regulation, and protein stability. While the precise mechanism by which anti-SAE1 antibodies trigger the inflammatory cascade of dermatomyositis is not fully elucidated, the presence of these antibodies serves as a significant biomarker for a specific clinical phenotype.

Patients positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies often exhibit a distinct constellation of systemic involvements. There is a high prevalence of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and dysphagia, both of which were present in this patient. The involvement of the pharyngeal and esophageal muscles leads to impaired bolus propulsion and a failure of the protective airway reflexes. This pharyngeal muscle weakness results in micro-aspiration, where small amounts of food or secretions enter the trachea and lower respiratory tract. This explains the "patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation" and "aspiration pneumonitis" seen on the patient's chest CT, which are distinct from the parenchymal changes seen in classic ILD.

Cutaneous manifestations are a diagnostic cornerstone of dermatomyositis. While the "heliotrope rash" (violaceous eruption on the upper eyelids) and "Gottron’s papules" (erythematous papules over the knuckles) are classic signs, DM can present with more varied patterns. The "shawl sign" refers to a rash across the shoulders and upper back, while the "Holster sign"—present in this patient—manifests as erythema on the lateral thighs, mimicking the area where a firearm holster would rest. These skin changes, along with the periungual telangiectasias and microhaemorrhages observed on nailfold capillaroscopy in this case, reflect the underlying systemic vasculopathy and capillary fragility characteristic of the disease.
