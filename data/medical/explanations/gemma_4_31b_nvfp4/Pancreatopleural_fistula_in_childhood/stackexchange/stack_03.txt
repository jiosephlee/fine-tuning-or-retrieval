Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] From a pathophysiological perspective, what are the primary anatomical pathways that allow pancreatic secretions to travel from the retroperitoneal space into the pleural cavity?

From a pathophysiological perspective, the development of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) requires a disruption of the pancreatic duct or the rupture of a pancreatic pseudocyst, typically occurring in the posterior aspect of the pancreas. When such a rupture occurs posteriorly, pancreatic secretions leak into the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneal cavity.

Because the retroperitoneum provides a potential space that communicates with the mediastinum, these secretions follow the path of least resistance, tracking superiorly. The primary anatomical pathways that allow this transit from the abdomen into the thoracic cavity are the natural diaphragmatic hiatuses—specifically the **oesophageal hiatus** and the **aortic hiatus**. Once the fluid enters the mediastinum, it can breach the pleura, resulting in the accumulation of amylase-rich fluid within the pleural space.

In the presented case, this pathway is clearly demonstrated by imaging. The CT scan showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas, and the MRCP specifically confirmed that the cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extended posteriorly in the retroperitoneum and tracked superiorly through the **oesophageal hiatus** into the right pleural cavity. This explains how a primary abdominal injury—in this case, blunt trauma from a bicycle handle—can manifest as a "pulmonary mask" of recurrent, blood-stained pleural effusions.
