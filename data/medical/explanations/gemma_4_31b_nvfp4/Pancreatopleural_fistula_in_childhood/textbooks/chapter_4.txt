Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich Pleural Effusions

When a clinician encounters a pleural effusion with significantly elevated amylase levels—particularly levels $\ge 1000$ IU/L—pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a primary consideration. However, amylase-rich fluid is not pathognomonic for PPF, and several thoracic and abdominal pathologies can mimic this presentation. Distinguishing between these entities requires a combination of biochemical analysis, detailed patient history, and targeted imaging.

A critical diagnostic tool in narrowing the differential is amylase typing. Amylase exists in two primary forms: pancreatic-type and salivary-type. In PPF, the pleural fluid contains pancreatic amylase, which leaks from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst into the pleural space. In contrast, other conditions, such as certain malignancies or esophageal ruptures, may result in the accumulation of salivary-type amylase. While the case report focuses on the absolute elevation of amylase and lipase, performing an amylase type can definitively distinguish a pancreatic source from non-pancreatic sources of amylase.

In this case, the patient’s history of contact with a relative with pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s disease) and a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid initially suggested a tubercular effusion. ADA is a sensitive marker for TB but lacks specificity, as it can be elevated in various inflammatory or infectious pleural processes. The diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis was ruled out in this patient through the use of GeneXpert MTB/RI, which is a highly specific molecular test for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* DNA. This highlights the importance of utilizing specific molecular diagnostics over nonspecific markers like ADA when a patient presents with a complex history.

Esophageal perforation, specifically Boerhaave’s syndrome, is another significant mimic that can produce amylase-rich pleural fluid due to the leakage of saliva and esophageal secretions. Clinically, Boerhaave’s syndrome is typically characterized by a sudden onset of severe retrosternal chest pain, often following forceful vomiting or retching. In this case, the absence of such a history and the fact that the child was passing stools normally and lacked acute vomiting symptoms allowed the clinicians to rule out esophageal rupture.

Malignancies, including primary lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors, can also present with pleural effusions and elevated amylase. To differentiate these from PPF, clinicians must evaluate for systemic "red flags" such as unexplained weight loss, chronic fever, or lymphadenopathy. In this patient, the absence of constitutional symptoms, a normal peripheral blood smear, and negative pleural fluid cytology effectively excluded malignancy.

Finally, other thoracic-abdominal communications must be considered. Hepatic hydrothorax, caused by the movement of peritoneal fluid through diaphragmatic defects in patients with cirrhosis, can cause effusions, but this was ruled out by normal liver function tests and imaging. Similarly, an acquired diaphragmatic hernia could allow abdominal contents to enter the chest; however, the CT and MRCP in this case showed no displaced abdominal organs, but rather a specific fistulous tract tracking from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus. By systematically ruling out these mimics through imaging and biochemical markers, the diagnosis of PPF was confirmed.
