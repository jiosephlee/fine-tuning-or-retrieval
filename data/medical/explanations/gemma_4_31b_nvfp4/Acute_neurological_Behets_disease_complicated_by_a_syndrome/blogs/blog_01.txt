Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Normal Brain MRI: When to Keep Searching for Neuro-Behçet's

We’ve all been there: a patient presents with a concerning constellation of neurological symptoms and systemic inflammation, but the brain MRI comes back "unremarkable." In many clinical pathways, a clean scan is a sigh of relief—a signal to start looking elsewhere or to downgrade the suspicion of a primary CNS process. However, relying too heavily on neuroimaging can be a dangerous diagnostic pitfall. 

This case reminds us that the "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," particularly when dealing with the great masquerader of the inflammatory world: Behçet’s disease.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Puzzle

A man in his 60s presented to the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, and general malaise. He was also experiencing persistent hiccups and arthralgia. On physical exam, the clinicians found oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash on his face and trunk. He was tachycardic and febrile, but clinically euvolemic.

The initial labs were a mixed bag of "red flags": a CRP of 180 mg/L, an ESR of 70 mm/hour, and a marked hyponatraemia of 124 mmol/L. To add to the complexity, his creatine kinase (CK) was significantly elevated at 1349 U/L. 

Given the fever and headache, the team suspected CNS inflammation or infection. They ordered a brain MRI, which was completely normal. No parenchymal lesions, no hypothalamic involvement, nothing. In many settings, this might have led the team to pivot away from a CNS-centric diagnosis, but the patient's condition continued to evolve.

## The Turning Point: Looking Beyond the Scan

The diagnosis shifted when a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on day five. Combined with the oral aphthae and skin rashes, the clinical picture screamed Behçet’s disease (BD). 

While the MRI was clean, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) told a different story: mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis. With negative cultures and viral PCRs, this was a classic presentation of aseptic meningitis. The diagnosis was acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring concurrently with the systemic diagnosis of BD—a rare occurrence, as neurological involvement typically lags years behind the initial mucocutaneous presentation.

## The Endocrine Twist: SIADH and the "Hidden" Signs

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of this case was the concurrent Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient had low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and inappropriately elevated ADH. 

We often associate SIADH with malignancies or specific medications, but here it was driven by CNS inflammation. The case highlights two subtle clinical clues that should make you think about severe hyponatremia in the setting of neuro-inflammation:
1. **Persistent Hiccups:** An underappreciated but recognized sign of severe hyponatremia secondary to meningitis.
2. **Rhabdomyolysis:** The elevated CK in this patient was likely a metabolic consequence of the profound hyponatremia, rather than a primary muscle inflammatory process.

## The Treatment Surprise

Standard teaching suggests that corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. Remarkably, the colchicine resolved the mucocutaneous lesions, the aseptic meningitis, and the SIADH without the need for steroids or other immunosuppressants. While spontaneous recovery is possible, this suggests colchicine may be a viable option in early-phase neuro-BD when steroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **MRI is not the gold standard for meningitis:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out neuro-Behçet's. Acute neuro-BD can present as an isolated aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially in the early stages.
*   **Concurrent Presentation:** While neuro-BD usually follows systemic BD by several years, be alert for simultaneous presentation. If you see the "triple threat" of oral, genital, and skin lesions paired with a headache, think Behçet's.
*   **The SIADH Connection:** CNS inflammation (potentially mediated by IL-6) can trigger SIADH. If a patient with a suspected inflammatory CNS process develops hyponatremia, don't just treat the sodium—look for the inflammatory driver.
*   **The "Odd" Symptoms:** Keep hiccups and unexplained CK elevation on your radar as potential markers for severe hyponatremia in the neuro-inflammatory patient.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Don't let a "normal" MRI stop your workup** if the clinical suspicion for CNS inflammation remains high.
*   **Behçet’s is a multisystem chameleon;** always perform a thorough skin and mucosal exam in patients with unexplained fever and neurological symptoms.
*   **SIADH can be a manifestation of acute neuro-BD,** and its resolution often mirrors the treatment of the underlying inflammation.
*   **Colchicine may have a role** in treating early-phase neuro-BD, though corticosteroids remain the standard for more severe parenchymal disease.
