Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Breaking the Timeline: Concurrent Systemic and Neurological Behçet's

We are taught that Behçet’s Disease (BD) follows a certain chronological script: first the oral ulcers, then perhaps the genital lesions or uveitis, and years later—usually around the four-year mark—neurological involvement may emerge. But what happens when the script is thrown out the window and the patient presents with systemic and neurological manifestations simultaneously?

This case presents a diagnostic challenge that serves as a reminder: don't let a "normal" MRI or a typical disease timeline lure you into a false sense of security.

## The Presentation: A Multi-System Puzzle

A man in his 60s presented to the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, and general malaise. On the surface, it looked like a generic "sick" patient—fever, arthralgia, and a loss of appetite. However, the physical exam provided the first clues: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk.

The laboratory work added a twist of complexity. The patient was profoundly hyponatremic (Sodium 124 mmol/L). He also had a surprisingly high creatine kinase (CK 1349 U/L) and persistent hiccups. While these might seem like disparate findings, the hiccups and rhabdomyolysis were actually red flags for the severity of his hyponatremia, which was eventually diagnosed as a Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH).

## The Diagnostic Trap: The "Normal" MRI

When a patient presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, our minds immediately jump to the "big hitters": viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV), bacterial meningitis, or perhaps a CNS malignancy. In this case, the clinicians did the right thing—they cast a wide net. CSF was tapped, cultures were run, and a brain MRI was ordered.

Here is the crux of the case: **the brain MRI was completely unremarkable.**

In many neurological emergencies, a normal MRI is a relief. In neuro-Behçet's, it can be a distraction. Acute neuro-BD often presents as a self-limiting aseptic meningitis. This patient’s CSF revealed a lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, confirming CNS inflammation despite the lack of parenchymal lesions on imaging. Combined with the subsequent appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer, the diagnosis of definite systemic BD and concurrent acute neuro-BD was established.

## The Connection: Inflammation and SIADH

The most intriguing part of this case is the link between the neuro-inflammation and the endocrine dysfunction. While SIADH in BD patients is usually associated with drug triggers like cyclophosphamide, this patient had no such triggers. 

The hypothesized mechanism here is the cytokine storm. Pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically Interleukin-6 (IL-6)—are often elevated in the CSF during acute neuro-BD. IL-6 is known to stimulate the release of ADH. This suggests that the SIADH wasn't a separate comorbid entity, but a direct physiological consequence of the inflammatory process occurring in the CNS.

## An Unexpected Recovery

Standard of care for acute neuro-BD typically involves high-dose corticosteroids or other potent immunosuppressants. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. 

Colchicine is a staple for the mucocutaneous manifestations of BD, but it is generally considered ineffective for neurological involvement when used alone. Surprisingly, this patient’s fever, vertigo, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia all resolved within two weeks of starting colchicine. While spontaneous recovery is always a possibility, this suggests that in some early-phase cases of neuro-BD, aggressive steroid therapy might not be the only path to resolution.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Ignore the "Typical" Timeline:** While neuro-BD usually lags behind systemic onset by years, concurrent presentation is possible and should remain on your differential when mucocutaneous lesions overlap with CNS symptoms.
*   **MRI is Not Everything:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-BD. If the clinical suspicion is high, CSF analysis for aseptic meningitis is the critical next step.
*   **Look for SIADH Clues:** In the setting of CNS inflammation, persistent hiccups or unexplained rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) can be subtle clinical markers of severe hyponatremia/SIADH.
*   **The IL-6 Link:** Remember that systemic inflammation and specific cytokines (like IL-6) can drive ADH secretion, bridging the gap between an autoimmune inflammatory state and an endocrine emergency.

## Take-Home Points

*   **High Index of Suspicion:** Fever + Headache + Oral/Genital Ulcers = Think Neuro-Behçet's, regardless of the patient's history.
*   **Diagnostic Rigor:** Use CSF analysis to catch aseptic meningitis when imaging is negative.
*   **SIADH Awareness:** Consider SIADH as a potential complication of CNS inflammation in BD patients.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** While steroids are gold-standard, the response to colchicine in this case highlights the importance of monitoring clinical response in early-stage disease.
