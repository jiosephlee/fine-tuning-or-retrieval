Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hiccups and Hyponatremia: The Unlikely Clues to Neuroinflammation

We’ve all seen the "vague" presentation: a patient arrives with fever, headache, and general malaise. Usually, we're scrubbing the differential for the usual suspects—meningitis, encephalitis, or a systemic inflammatory flare. But what happens when the patient also has persistent hiccups and a crashing sodium level?

Most of us would treat the hyponatremia and maybe order a CT head, but we might overlook the systemic connection. This case reminds us that some of the most "annoying" or non-specific symptoms—like hiccups—can actually be the breadcrumbs leading us to a complex neuro-endocrine disaster.

## The Diagnostic Puzzle

Imagine a man in his 60s presenting with a constellation of symptoms: fever, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. On the surface, it looks like a systemic inflammatory process. However, the lab work throws a curveball: a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L. He's also reporting persistent hiccups. 

While we often associate hiccups with GI issues or diaphragm irritation, in the context of CNS inflammation, they can be a red flag for severe hyponatremia secondary to the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). In this case, the patient was euvolemic, had low serum osmolality, and inappropriately high urine osmolality and sodium—the classic SIADH triad.

## The "Hidden" Complication: Rhabdomyolysis

Here is a clinical pearl that often gets missed: profound hyponatremia can actually trigger rhabdomyolysis. In this patient, the creatine kinase (CK) was elevated to 1349 U/L. While we might first suspect the fever or the arthralgia as the cause of muscle breakdown, severe electrolyte imbalances can destabilize muscle cell membranes. If you see an unexplained jump in CK in a patient with severe SIADH, don't just look at the muscles—look at the sodium.

## When the MRI Lies

The search for the source of the SIADH led to the CNS. Here’s the catch: the brain MRI was completely normal. In many of us, a clean MRI is a "relief" that leads us to stop looking for a CNS cause. But in acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease (Neuro-BD), particularly when it presents as aseptic meningitis, the MRI can be deceptively normal in the early stages.

The diagnosis was hammered home by the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, which showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, and the subsequent appearance of genital ulcers and a folliculitis-like rash. This patient didn't just have a "brain thing"; he had systemic Behçet’s Disease presenting concurrently with neurological involvement—a rarity, as Neuro-BD typically lags years behind the initial diagnosis.

## The IL-6 Connection

How does an inflammatory vasculitis in the brain lead to water retention? The suspected culprit is Interleukin-6 (IL-6). In Neuro-BD, the CSF is often flooded with IL-6. This pro-inflammatory cytokine is known to stimulate the release of ADH, effectively telling the kidneys to hold onto water despite the body being hypo-osmolar. This creates a direct bridge between the inflammatory flare in the brain and the electrolyte crash in the blood.

## A Surprising Resolution

Standard of care for Neuro-BD usually involves heavy-hitting corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. However, this patient was managed with colchicine monotherapy. While colchicine is the gold standard for the mucocutaneous manifestations of Behçet’s, it's rarely used alone for the neurological form. In this case, it worked. The fever broke, the ulcers healed, and the sodium normalized as the neuro-inflammation subsided.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Hiccups as a Clue:** Persistent hiccups in a patient with CNS symptoms can be a clinical marker for severe SIADH and profound hyponatremia.
*   **The Sodium-Muscle Link:** Keep rhabdomyolysis on your radar as a potential complication of severe hyponatremia.
*   **MRI $\neq$ Diagnosis:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute Neuro-Behçet’s or other aseptic meningitis syndromes; rely on CSF analysis and clinical markers.
*   **Systemic Thinking:** When you see a "brain" problem and an "endocrine" problem (like SIADH) occurring together, look for a unifying inflammatory process, such as Behçet’s, sarcoidosis, or malignancy.
