Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Monotherapy in Neuro-BD: Challenging the Steroid Paradigm

Imagine a patient walking into your ED with a confusing constellation of symptoms: fever, a pounding headache, oral ulcers, and... persistent hiccups. On the labs, you see a sodium of 124 and a CK of 1300. It looks like a multisystem disaster. You’re thinking meningitis, systemic vasculitis, or perhaps a bizarre endocrine storm. 

What makes this case a true "zebra" isn't just the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD), but the fact that the neurological involvement and a profound endocrine complication were managed and resolved without a single milligram of corticosteroids. In the world of neuro-BD, where steroids are the undisputed gold standard, this is a provocative result.

## The Diagnostic Puzzle: More Than Just Ulcers

Behçet’s is classically known for the "triple symptom complex" of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and uveitis. However, this patient presented with a more aggressive onset. He had the mucocutaneous hallmarks—oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and genital ulcers—which landed him a "definite BD" diagnosis based on international criteria. 

But the real challenge was the neurological presentation. Neuro-BD usually lags years behind the initial diagnosis, but here it hit simultaneously. The patient had aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the CSF), yet his brain MRI was completely normal. This is a critical clinical reminder: in the early stages of neuro-BD, imaging can be deceptive. A normal MRI does not rule out CNS inflammation.

## The Endocrine Twist: The SIADH Connection

The most surprising element of this case was the development of the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). While SIADH is often associated with medications like cyclophosphamide in BD patients, this patient had no such triggers. Instead, the SIADH was a direct result of the CNS inflammation.

The pathophysiology likely centers on Interleukin-6 (IL-6). In neuro-BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to a surge of IL-6 in the CSF. This pro-inflammatory cytokine is known to stimulate the release of ADH, leading to water retention and hyponatraemia. 

Interestingly, the patient's persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349) were likely not primary symptoms of Behçet’s, but rather manifestations of severe hyponatraemia. It’s a reminder that when a patient with CNS inflammation starts hiccuping, you should check their sodium.

## Challenging the Steroid Paradigm

If you look at any textbook, the treatment for acute neuro-BD is high-dose corticosteroids. Colchicine is generally reserved for the skin and joints and is widely considered ineffective for CNS involvement. 

In this case, the team opted for colchicine monotherapy. The patient’s fever, vertigo, and mucocutaneous lesions resolved rapidly. Even more impressively, as the CNS inflammation subsided, the ADH levels plummeted, and the sodium normalized.

Why did this work? Colchicine doesn't just treat ulcers; it inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. By dampening the IL-6 drive, colchicine may have simultaneously treated the aseptic meningitis and reversed the SIADH. While we can't rule out spontaneous recovery, this case suggests that in mild or early-stage neuro-BD, colchicine might be a viable, steroid-sparing alternative.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Normal MRI $\neq$ Normal Brain:** In acute neuro-Behçet's, the MRI can be unremarkable despite significant CSF pleocytosis and clinical meningitis.
*   **Hiccups and Hyponatremia:** Persistent hiccups in the setting of CNS inflammation should raise a high index of suspicion for SIADH.
*   **The IL-6 Link:** Neuro-inflammation can drive ADH secretion via IL-6; treating the inflammation often resolves the electrolyte imbalance.
*   **Rhabdomyolysis:** Severe hyponatremia can manifest as muscle breakdown; don't overlook the CK when treating SIADH.
*   **Colchicine Potential:** While steroids are the standard for neuro-BD, colchicine may be effective in early, non-parenchymal cases by suppressing the cytokine storm.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Recognize the Pattern:** Fever + oral/genital ulcers + neurological symptoms = think Behçet’s.
*   **Look Beyond the MRI:** Rely on CSF analysis and clinical findings if you suspect neuro-BD.
*   **Monitor Electrolytes:** Be alert for SIADH in patients with neuro-inflammatory conditions.
*   **Consider Alternatives:** In specific cases, colchicine may provide an anti-inflammatory bridge to recovery without the systemic burden of steroids.
