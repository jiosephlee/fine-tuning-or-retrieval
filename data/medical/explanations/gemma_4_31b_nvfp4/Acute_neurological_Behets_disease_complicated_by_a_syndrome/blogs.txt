Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# The Normal Brain MRI: When to Keep Searching for Neuro-Behçet's

We’ve all been there: a patient presents with a concerning constellation of neurological symptoms and systemic inflammation, but the brain MRI comes back "unremarkable." In many clinical pathways, a clean scan is a sigh of relief—a signal to start looking elsewhere or to downgrade the suspicion of a primary CNS process. However, relying too heavily on neuroimaging can be a dangerous diagnostic pitfall. 

This case reminds us that the "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," particularly when dealing with the great masquerader of the inflammatory world: Behçet’s disease.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Puzzle

A man in his 60s presented to the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, and general malaise. He was also experiencing persistent hiccups and arthralgia. On physical exam, the clinicians found oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash on his face and trunk. He was tachycardic and febrile, but clinically euvolemic.

The initial labs were a mixed bag of "red flags": a CRP of 180 mg/L, an ESR of 70 mm/hour, and a marked hyponatraemia of 124 mmol/L. To add to the complexity, his creatine kinase (CK) was significantly elevated at 1349 U/L. 

Given the fever and headache, the team suspected CNS inflammation or infection. They ordered a brain MRI, which was completely normal. No parenchymal lesions, no hypothalamic involvement, nothing. In many settings, this might have led the team to pivot away from a CNS-centric diagnosis, but the patient's condition continued to evolve.

## The Turning Point: Looking Beyond the Scan

The diagnosis shifted when a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on day five. Combined with the oral aphthae and skin rashes, the clinical picture screamed Behçet’s disease (BD). 

While the MRI was clean, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) told a different story: mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis. With negative cultures and viral PCRs, this was a classic presentation of aseptic meningitis. The diagnosis was acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) occurring concurrently with the systemic diagnosis of BD—a rare occurrence, as neurological involvement typically lags years behind the initial mucocutaneous presentation.

## The Endocrine Twist: SIADH and the "Hidden" Signs

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of this case was the concurrent Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). The patient had low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and inappropriately elevated ADH. 

We often associate SIADH with malignancies or specific medications, but here it was driven by CNS inflammation. The case highlights two subtle clinical clues that should make you think about severe hyponatremia in the setting of neuro-inflammation:
1. **Persistent Hiccups:** An underappreciated but recognized sign of severe hyponatremia secondary to meningitis.
2. **Rhabdomyolysis:** The elevated CK in this patient was likely a metabolic consequence of the profound hyponatremia, rather than a primary muscle inflammatory process.

## The Treatment Surprise

Standard teaching suggests that corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. Remarkably, the colchicine resolved the mucocutaneous lesions, the aseptic meningitis, and the SIADH without the need for steroids or other immunosuppressants. While spontaneous recovery is possible, this suggests colchicine may be a viable option in early-phase neuro-BD when steroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **MRI is not the gold standard for meningitis:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out neuro-Behçet's. Acute neuro-BD can present as an isolated aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially in the early stages.
*   **Concurrent Presentation:** While neuro-BD usually follows systemic BD by several years, be alert for simultaneous presentation. If you see the "triple threat" of oral, genital, and skin lesions paired with a headache, think Behçet's.
*   **The SIADH Connection:** CNS inflammation (potentially mediated by IL-6) can trigger SIADH. If a patient with a suspected inflammatory CNS process develops hyponatremia, don't just treat the sodium—look for the inflammatory driver.
*   **The "Odd" Symptoms:** Keep hiccups and unexplained CK elevation on your radar as potential markers for severe hyponatremia in the neuro-inflammatory patient.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Don't let a "normal" MRI stop your workup** if the clinical suspicion for CNS inflammation remains high.
*   **Behçet’s is a multisystem chameleon;** always perform a thorough skin and mucosal exam in patients with unexplained fever and neurological symptoms.
*   **SIADH can be a manifestation of acute neuro-BD,** and its resolution often mirrors the treatment of the underlying inflammation.
*   **Colchicine may have a role** in treating early-phase neuro-BD, though corticosteroids remain the standard for more severe parenchymal disease.



# Hiccups and Hyponatremia: The Unlikely Clues to Neuroinflammation

We’ve all seen the "vague" presentation: a patient arrives with fever, headache, and general malaise. Usually, we're scrubbing the differential for the usual suspects—meningitis, encephalitis, or a systemic inflammatory flare. But what happens when the patient also has persistent hiccups and a crashing sodium level?

Most of us would treat the hyponatremia and maybe order a CT head, but we might overlook the systemic connection. This case reminds us that some of the most "annoying" or non-specific symptoms—like hiccups—can actually be the breadcrumbs leading us to a complex neuro-endocrine disaster.

## The Diagnostic Puzzle

Imagine a man in his 60s presenting with a constellation of symptoms: fever, arthralgia, and oral aphthae. On the surface, it looks like a systemic inflammatory process. However, the lab work throws a curveball: a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L. He's also reporting persistent hiccups. 

While we often associate hiccups with GI issues or diaphragm irritation, in the context of CNS inflammation, they can be a red flag for severe hyponatremia secondary to the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). In this case, the patient was euvolemic, had low serum osmolality, and inappropriately high urine osmolality and sodium—the classic SIADH triad.

## The "Hidden" Complication: Rhabdomyolysis

Here is a clinical pearl that often gets missed: profound hyponatremia can actually trigger rhabdomyolysis. In this patient, the creatine kinase (CK) was elevated to 1349 U/L. While we might first suspect the fever or the arthralgia as the cause of muscle breakdown, severe electrolyte imbalances can destabilize muscle cell membranes. If you see an unexplained jump in CK in a patient with severe SIADH, don't just look at the muscles—look at the sodium.

## When the MRI Lies

The search for the source of the SIADH led to the CNS. Here’s the catch: the brain MRI was completely normal. In many of us, a clean MRI is a "relief" that leads us to stop looking for a CNS cause. But in acute Neuro-Behçet’s Disease (Neuro-BD), particularly when it presents as aseptic meningitis, the MRI can be deceptively normal in the early stages.

The diagnosis was hammered home by the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, which showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, and the subsequent appearance of genital ulcers and a folliculitis-like rash. This patient didn't just have a "brain thing"; he had systemic Behçet’s Disease presenting concurrently with neurological involvement—a rarity, as Neuro-BD typically lags years behind the initial diagnosis.

## The IL-6 Connection

How does an inflammatory vasculitis in the brain lead to water retention? The suspected culprit is Interleukin-6 (IL-6). In Neuro-BD, the CSF is often flooded with IL-6. This pro-inflammatory cytokine is known to stimulate the release of ADH, effectively telling the kidneys to hold onto water despite the body being hypo-osmolar. This creates a direct bridge between the inflammatory flare in the brain and the electrolyte crash in the blood.

## A Surprising Resolution

Standard of care for Neuro-BD usually involves heavy-hitting corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. However, this patient was managed with colchicine monotherapy. While colchicine is the gold standard for the mucocutaneous manifestations of Behçet’s, it's rarely used alone for the neurological form. In this case, it worked. The fever broke, the ulcers healed, and the sodium normalized as the neuro-inflammation subsided.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Hiccups as a Clue:** Persistent hiccups in a patient with CNS symptoms can be a clinical marker for severe SIADH and profound hyponatremia.
*   **The Sodium-Muscle Link:** Keep rhabdomyolysis on your radar as a potential complication of severe hyponatremia.
*   **MRI $\neq$ Diagnosis:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute Neuro-Behçet’s or other aseptic meningitis syndromes; rely on CSF analysis and clinical markers.
*   **Systemic Thinking:** When you see a "brain" problem and an "endocrine" problem (like SIADH) occurring together, look for a unifying inflammatory process, such as Behçet’s, sarcoidosis, or malignancy.



# Cytokines and Water: The Pathophysiologic Link Between Neuro-BD and SIADH

Imagine a patient presenting to your ED with a triad of fever, headache, and hyponatremia. Now, add in a few oral ulcers and a strange, persistent case of hiccups. Your brain likely jumps to the usual suspects: meningitis, encephalitis, or perhaps a systemic autoimmune flare. But when the brain MRI comes back completely clean, the diagnostic puzzle gets significantly more interesting.

This is the essence of a rare but teaching-rich case of acute neuro-Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) complicated by the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH). While we often think of Behçet’s as a disease of "mouth and genital sores," this case pushes us to look at the intersection of neuro-inflammation and endocrine dysfunction.

## The Case: More Than Just Ulcers

A man in his 60s presented with a three-day history of fever, headache, malaise, and arthralgia. On exam, he had the classic mucocutaneous hallmarks of Behçet’s: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes. However, the laboratory data revealed a complication that shifted the focus to the CNS: a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L.

The diagnostic workup for the hyponatremia was textbook for SIADH—low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and high urinary sodium—all driven by an inappropriately elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL). The "where" of the inflammation was the challenge. The brain MRI was unremarkable, which is a critical trap in neuro-BD. In the early phases, neuro-BD can present as an aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, meaning a normal MRI does not rule out CNS involvement. In this patient, CSF analysis provided the answer, showing lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein.

## The Mechanism: From IL-6 to ADH

The core clinical question here is: why does an inflammatory disease like Behçet's cause the body to hold onto water?

The link lies in the proinflammatory cytokine storm. In acute neuro-BD, there is a significant increase in the production of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) within the central nervous system. While we often think of cytokines as merely markers of inflammation, IL-6 is biologically active in the hypothalamus and the posterior pituitary.

Research suggests that IL-6 can directly stimulate the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). In this patient, the neuro-inflammatory process likely triggered a surge of IL-6, which in turn acted as a stimulus for the secretion of ADH, overriding the usual osmotic triggers. This creates a state of water intoxication (SIADH) purely through the mechanism of neuro-inflammation, without the need for a tumor or a drug trigger.

## The Hidden Clues: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis

This case offers two fantastic clinical pearls regarding the presentation of severe hyponatremia. First, the patient had persistent hiccups. While we often dismiss hiccups as a nuisance, in the context of CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, they can be a subtle red flag for severe electrolyte derangement.

Second, the patient had a markedly elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L). While we might initially suspect an inflammatory myopathy given his arthralgias, rhabdomyolysis is actually a recognized, though frequently overlooked, complication of profound hyponatremia. When you see an unexplained jump in CK in a hyponatremic patient, think of the electrolyte shift before you jump to a primary muscle disease.

## A Surprising Twist in Treatment

The standard of care for acute neuro-BD is typically aggressive corticosteroids. However, this patient was managed with colchicine monotherapy. Colchicine is widely used for the skin and mucosal manifestations of BD, but its use as a sole agent for neuro-BD is rare. 

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil activation and suppressing the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. By dampening the IL-6 response, colchicine not only treated the systemic inflammation and the aseptic meningitis but also removed the stimulus for ADH secretion, allowing the serum sodium to normalize without the need for vasopressin antagonists or hypertonic saline.

## Clinical Take-Haves

*   **The MRI Trap:** A normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro-Behçet’s disease; look for aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis if clinical suspicion remains high.
*   **Cytokine-Driven SIADH:** Neuro-inflammation (specifically via IL-6) can drive inappropriate ADH secretion, leading to severe hyponatremia.
*   **The "Odd" Symptoms:** Persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) can be clinical markers of severe hyponatremia in the setting of CNS inflammation.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** While steroids are the gold standard for neuro-BD, colchicine may be a viable option for milder, meningitis-like presentations by targeting the underlying cytokine drive.



# Colchicine Monotherapy in Neuro-BD: Challenging the Steroid Paradigm

Imagine a patient walking into your ED with a confusing constellation of symptoms: fever, a pounding headache, oral ulcers, and... persistent hiccups. On the labs, you see a sodium of 124 and a CK of 1300. It looks like a multisystem disaster. You’re thinking meningitis, systemic vasculitis, or perhaps a bizarre endocrine storm. 

What makes this case a true "zebra" isn't just the diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD), but the fact that the neurological involvement and a profound endocrine complication were managed and resolved without a single milligram of corticosteroids. In the world of neuro-BD, where steroids are the undisputed gold standard, this is a provocative result.

## The Diagnostic Puzzle: More Than Just Ulcers

Behçet’s is classically known for the "triple symptom complex" of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and uveitis. However, this patient presented with a more aggressive onset. He had the mucocutaneous hallmarks—oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and genital ulcers—which landed him a "definite BD" diagnosis based on international criteria. 

But the real challenge was the neurological presentation. Neuro-BD usually lags years behind the initial diagnosis, but here it hit simultaneously. The patient had aseptic meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in the CSF), yet his brain MRI was completely normal. This is a critical clinical reminder: in the early stages of neuro-BD, imaging can be deceptive. A normal MRI does not rule out CNS inflammation.

## The Endocrine Twist: The SIADH Connection

The most surprising element of this case was the development of the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH). While SIADH is often associated with medications like cyclophosphamide in BD patients, this patient had no such triggers. Instead, the SIADH was a direct result of the CNS inflammation.

The pathophysiology likely centers on Interleukin-6 (IL-6). In neuro-BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to a surge of IL-6 in the CSF. This pro-inflammatory cytokine is known to stimulate the release of ADH, leading to water retention and hyponatraemia. 

Interestingly, the patient's persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349) were likely not primary symptoms of Behçet’s, but rather manifestations of severe hyponatraemia. It’s a reminder that when a patient with CNS inflammation starts hiccuping, you should check their sodium.

## Challenging the Steroid Paradigm

If you look at any textbook, the treatment for acute neuro-BD is high-dose corticosteroids. Colchicine is generally reserved for the skin and joints and is widely considered ineffective for CNS involvement. 

In this case, the team opted for colchicine monotherapy. The patient’s fever, vertigo, and mucocutaneous lesions resolved rapidly. Even more impressively, as the CNS inflammation subsided, the ADH levels plummeted, and the sodium normalized.

Why did this work? Colchicine doesn't just treat ulcers; it inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. By dampening the IL-6 drive, colchicine may have simultaneously treated the aseptic meningitis and reversed the SIADH. While we can't rule out spontaneous recovery, this case suggests that in mild or early-stage neuro-BD, colchicine might be a viable, steroid-sparing alternative.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Normal MRI $\neq$ Normal Brain:** In acute neuro-Behçet's, the MRI can be unremarkable despite significant CSF pleocytosis and clinical meningitis.
*   **Hiccups and Hyponatremia:** Persistent hiccups in the setting of CNS inflammation should raise a high index of suspicion for SIADH.
*   **The IL-6 Link:** Neuro-inflammation can drive ADH secretion via IL-6; treating the inflammation often resolves the electrolyte imbalance.
*   **Rhabdomyolysis:** Severe hyponatremia can manifest as muscle breakdown; don't overlook the CK when treating SIADH.
*   **Colchicine Potential:** While steroids are the standard for neuro-BD, colchicine may be effective in early, non-parenchymal cases by suppressing the cytokine storm.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Recognize the Pattern:** Fever + oral/genital ulcers + neurological symptoms = think Behçet’s.
*   **Look Beyond the MRI:** Rely on CSF analysis and clinical findings if you suspect neuro-BD.
*   **Monitor Electrolytes:** Be alert for SIADH in patients with neuro-inflammatory conditions.
*   **Consider Alternatives:** In specific cases, colchicine may provide an anti-inflammatory bridge to recovery without the systemic burden of steroids.



# Breaking the Timeline: Concurrent Systemic and Neurological Behçet's

We are taught that Behçet’s Disease (BD) follows a certain chronological script: first the oral ulcers, then perhaps the genital lesions or uveitis, and years later—usually around the four-year mark—neurological involvement may emerge. But what happens when the script is thrown out the window and the patient presents with systemic and neurological manifestations simultaneously?

This case presents a diagnostic challenge that serves as a reminder: don't let a "normal" MRI or a typical disease timeline lure you into a false sense of security.

## The Presentation: A Multi-System Puzzle

A man in his 60s presented to the ED with a three-day history of fever, headache, and general malaise. On the surface, it looked like a generic "sick" patient—fever, arthralgia, and a loss of appetite. However, the physical exam provided the first clues: oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk.

The laboratory work added a twist of complexity. The patient was profoundly hyponatremic (Sodium 124 mmol/L). He also had a surprisingly high creatine kinase (CK 1349 U/L) and persistent hiccups. While these might seem like disparate findings, the hiccups and rhabdomyolysis were actually red flags for the severity of his hyponatremia, which was eventually diagnosed as a Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH).

## The Diagnostic Trap: The "Normal" MRI

When a patient presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, our minds immediately jump to the "big hitters": viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV), bacterial meningitis, or perhaps a CNS malignancy. In this case, the clinicians did the right thing—they cast a wide net. CSF was tapped, cultures were run, and a brain MRI was ordered.

Here is the crux of the case: **the brain MRI was completely unremarkable.**

In many neurological emergencies, a normal MRI is a relief. In neuro-Behçet's, it can be a distraction. Acute neuro-BD often presents as a self-limiting aseptic meningitis. This patient’s CSF revealed a lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, confirming CNS inflammation despite the lack of parenchymal lesions on imaging. Combined with the subsequent appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer, the diagnosis of definite systemic BD and concurrent acute neuro-BD was established.

## The Connection: Inflammation and SIADH

The most intriguing part of this case is the link between the neuro-inflammation and the endocrine dysfunction. While SIADH in BD patients is usually associated with drug triggers like cyclophosphamide, this patient had no such triggers. 

The hypothesized mechanism here is the cytokine storm. Pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically Interleukin-6 (IL-6)—are often elevated in the CSF during acute neuro-BD. IL-6 is known to stimulate the release of ADH. This suggests that the SIADH wasn't a separate comorbid entity, but a direct physiological consequence of the inflammatory process occurring in the CNS.

## An Unexpected Recovery

Standard of care for acute neuro-BD typically involves high-dose corticosteroids or other potent immunosuppressants. However, this patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy. 

Colchicine is a staple for the mucocutaneous manifestations of BD, but it is generally considered ineffective for neurological involvement when used alone. Surprisingly, this patient’s fever, vertigo, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia all resolved within two weeks of starting colchicine. While spontaneous recovery is always a possibility, this suggests that in some early-phase cases of neuro-BD, aggressive steroid therapy might not be the only path to resolution.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Ignore the "Typical" Timeline:** While neuro-BD usually lags behind systemic onset by years, concurrent presentation is possible and should remain on your differential when mucocutaneous lesions overlap with CNS symptoms.
*   **MRI is Not Everything:** A normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro-BD. If the clinical suspicion is high, CSF analysis for aseptic meningitis is the critical next step.
*   **Look for SIADH Clues:** In the setting of CNS inflammation, persistent hiccups or unexplained rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK) can be subtle clinical markers of severe hyponatremia/SIADH.
*   **The IL-6 Link:** Remember that systemic inflammation and specific cytokines (like IL-6) can drive ADH secretion, bridging the gap between an autoimmune inflammatory state and an endocrine emergency.

## Take-Home Points

*   **High Index of Suspicion:** Fever + Headache + Oral/Genital Ulcers = Think Neuro-Behçet's, regardless of the patient's history.
*   **Diagnostic Rigor:** Use CSF analysis to catch aseptic meningitis when imaging is negative.
*   **SIADH Awareness:** Consider SIADH as a potential complication of CNS inflammation in BD patients.
*   **Treatment Flexibility:** While steroids are gold-standard, the response to colchicine in this case highlights the importance of monitoring clinical response in early-stage disease.
