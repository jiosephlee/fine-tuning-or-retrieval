Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Pathophysiology] Can normal serum C3 and C4 levels rule out a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS)? Explain the pathophysiology behind this finding.

No, normal serum C3 and C4 levels cannot rule out a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). 

In the presented case, the patient demonstrated classic hallmarks of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)—including microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury—yet her initial C3 and C4 levels remained within normal limits. This is a common clinical finding and reflects the underlying pathophysiology of the disease.

CM-HUS is characterized by dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, typically due to genetic mutations or autoantibodies affecting regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, and B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Unlike certain immune-complex mediated glomerulonephritides or systemic lupus erythematosus, where massive systemic consumption of complement leads to detectable hypocomplementemia, the complement activation in CM-HUS is often localized to the endothelial surface. 

In these patients, the pathology is not necessarily a systemic deficiency of complement proteins, but rather a failure to inhibit the alternative pathway's "tick-over" mechanism on the vascular endothelium. This results in the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) and subsequent endothelial damage, leading to microthrombi and organ failure. Because the consumption of C3 and C4 occurs locally and may be balanced by ongoing hepatic synthesis, serum levels frequently remain within the reference range.

Clinicians must recognize that CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. As seen in this case, the diagnosis was reached by ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (via ADAMTS13 activity >10%) and Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (via negative stool PCR), and was confirmed by the patient's clinical response to eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the terminal complement component C5. Relying on standard C3 and C4 levels would have resulted in a missed diagnosis and delayed the administration of life-saving complement blockade.
