Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the hallmark histological findings on a renal biopsy that distinguish thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from other causes of acute kidney injury, such as isolated acute tubular necrosis?

The hallmark histological finding that distinguishes thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from isolated acute tubular necrosis (ATN) is the presence of **microvascular thrombi**, specifically fibrin thrombi within the glomerular capillaries. 

In cases of isolated ATN—which is a common consequence of sepsis-induced hypotension and systemic inflammation—the primary pathology is confined to the tubular epithelium, characterized by cell swelling, loss of the brush border, and tubular necrosis. However, as demonstrated in this case, the renal biopsy revealed "ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi," a finding that is pathognomonic for TMA. These thrombi cause mechanical obstruction of the glomerular capillaries, leading to downstream ischemia and the "ischaemic" appearance of the glomeruli.

It is clinically critical to recognize that these two entities are not mutually exclusive. In this patient, the biopsy showed "severe acute tubular necrosis" occurring concurrently with the TMA. This is a frequent observation in the setting of septic shock, where the kidney suffers a "double hit": global tubular injury from hemodynamic instability (ATN) and localized microvascular occlusion from complement dysregulation or disseminated intravascular coagulation (TMA).

While ATN is typically a reversible process of tubular injury, the presence of fibrin thrombi and scattered crescent formation indicates a more severe vascular insult. In the context of this patient's clinical presentation—microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and anuria—the histological evidence of glomerular thrombosis shifted the diagnosis from simple sepsis-related AKI to a complement-mediated TMA (CM-HUS), necessitating the transition from supportive care to targeted therapy with eculizumab.
