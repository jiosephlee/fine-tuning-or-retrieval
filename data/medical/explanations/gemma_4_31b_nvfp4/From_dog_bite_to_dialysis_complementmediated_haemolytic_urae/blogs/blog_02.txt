Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# DIC or TMA? When Sepsis Takes a Turn

Imagine a previously healthy woman in her 40s walks into your ED. She was bitten by her dog on the lip a few days ago. Now, she’s septic, hypoglycemic, and mottled. Within days, she's in the ICU with pancytopenia, DIC, and complete anuria requiring dialysis. You start broad-spectrum antibiotics, and the sepsis begins to resolve. You think you're winning. Then, the pivot happens: as the inflammatory markers drop and the patient stabilizes, her hemoglobin plummets, her LDH spikes, and schistocytes appear on the smear. 

This is the classic "diagnostic trap." When a patient is in the throes of septic shock and DIC, we tend to attribute everything—the low platelets, the kidney failure, the bleeding—to the sepsis. But what happens when the sepsis clears and the organ failure doesn't? Or worse, when a new hematologic crisis emerges *after* the initial infection is under control?

In this case, the culprit was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod from the dog's mouth. While this organism is notorious for causing fulminant sepsis and DIC, this patient developed something more insidious: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

## The Sepsis Phase: The Great Masquerader
Initially, this patient presented with the "textbook" version of *C. canimorsus* sepsis: high fever, lactic acidosis, and a coagulopathy that looked exactly like DIC. In the early phase of sepsis, the distinction between DIC and a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is incredibly muddy. Both involve microvascular thrombosis and organ failure. 

In this case, the patient had pancytopenia and AKI. While the team considered HUS early on, the presence of severe leukopenia and high inflammatory markers pointed squarely toward sepsis. This is a common clinical hurdle; the "noise" of sepsis often drowns out the signal of a superimposed TMA.

## The Pivot: Recognizing the Transition
The turning point in this case occurred on the nephrology ward. The patient was clinically stable—the "sepsis" part of the story was resolving. However, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L) and a surge in LDH. 

This is the red flag. In simple DIC, you expect the hematologic abnormalities to mirror the severity of the infection. When you see *isolated* microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and persistent thrombocytopenia appearing as the sepsis *improves*, you must pivot your thinking from "sepsis-induced coagulopathy" to "TMA." 

A peripheral smear revealed 15-20% schistocytes. At this point, the differential expands to TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS. This patient's ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin (ruling out STEC-HUS). The diagnosis was finally cemented with a kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli, confirming a TMA.

## The Therapeutic Paradox
Managing CM-HUS in the wake of an encapsulated bacterial infection creates a genuine clinical dilemma. The gold standard treatment for CM-HUS is Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the terminal complement component C5. 

Here is the catch: the complement system is the primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria, including *Capnocytophaga*. Blocking the complement system while a patient is bacteremic is essentially like taking the locks off the doors while the burglar is still in the house.

The clinical team in this case navigated this by using Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a bridge. PEX provided temporary stabilization and helped return spontaneous diuresis. Eculizumab was only initiated once blood cultures were negative and the patient had received meningococcal vaccinations. This sequence is critical to avoid catastrophic infectious complications.

## Clinical Pearls: Take-Home Points

*   **The Timing Matters:** If hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or emerge *after* the inflammatory phase of sepsis has resolved, suspect a superimposed TMA rather than simple DIC.
*   **Don't Trust the C3/C4:** In CM-HUS, standard complement levels (C3/C4) can be completely normal. A normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out complement-mediated TMA.
*   **TTP First:** In any adult with the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the first priority is to rule out TTP via ADAMTS13 activity, as this requires urgent PEX.
*   **The Capnocytophaga Connection:** While rare, *C. canimorsus* can act as a "second hit" trigger for CM-HUS, even in patients without a known genetic predisposition to complement dysregulation.
*   **Sequence Your Therapy:** When using complement blockers (Eculizumab) in the context of zoonotic sepsis, ensure blood cultures are negative and vaccinations are administered to mitigate the risk of opportunistic encapsulated bacterial infections.
