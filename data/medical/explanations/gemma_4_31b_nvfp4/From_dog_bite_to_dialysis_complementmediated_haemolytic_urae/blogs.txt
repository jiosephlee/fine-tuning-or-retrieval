Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# More Than a Scratch: Recognizing *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*

A dog bite to the lip seems like a routine ED visit—clean it up, give a tetanus shot, maybe some prophylactic antibiotics, and send them home. But every now and then, you get a case that reminds you why we take a detailed history. Imagine a healthy woman in her 40s presenting four days after a minor dog bite with high fever, mottled extremities, and a lab panel that looks like a disaster movie: pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, and profound hypoglycemia. 

This wasn't just a bad infection; it was a descent into septic shock caused by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, which eventually evolved into a life-threatening complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

## The Red Flags of *Capnocytophaga*

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, gram-negative rod that lives in the mouths of dogs and cats. While most people who get licked or bitten by a dog are fine, in rare cases, this organism triggers a fulminant systemic response. 

In this case, the hallmarks were present early: rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and a striking pancytopenia. One of the more unusual findings in this patient was refractory hypoglycemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), likely a metabolic consequence of the overwhelming septic state. If you see a patient with a history of animal exposure who presents with "unexplained" pancytopenia and shock, *Capnocytophaga* should be at the top of your differential.

## The Diagnostic Trap

The biggest challenge with *C. canimorsus* is that it is fastidious. It grows slowly in culture, which means the "culture negative" result on day one or two is not an exclusion criterion. In this case, it took seven days for the blood cultures to finally confirm the diagnosis. 

This is a critical clinical point: if the clinical suspicion is high, you cannot wait for the microbiology lab to catch up. This patient was started on broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam immediately, which is the right move. The mortality rate for this pathogen can approach 30%, and early antibiotic coverage is the primary driver of survival.

## When Sepsis Becomes Something Else: The Shift to TMA

The most surprising twist in this case occurred after the initial septic phase. As the inflammatory markers improved and the antibiotics worked, the patient didn't fully recover. Instead, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. 

While the team initially suspected autoimmune hemolytic anemia (due to a positive Coombs test, which later turned out to be a false positive from penicillin), a peripheral smear revealed 15-20% schistocytes. This shifted the diagnosis from simple DIC to a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). 

The diagnostic workup for TMA in the ICU can be a whirlwind. The team had to rule out TTP (ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, ruling it out) and STEC-HUS (stool PCR was negative). A renal biopsy eventually confirmed fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli, leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). 

It appears that the *C. canimorsus* infection acted as the "second hit" trigger, activating the alternative complement pathway and causing systemic endothelial damage. This is a rare but documented phenomenon where the very system meant to clear the bacteria—the complement system—ends up destroying the patient's own vasculature.

## Therapeutic Dilemmas: Eculizumab and Infection

Treating CM-HUS involves eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the terminal complement component 5 (C5). However, there is a significant catch: C5 is essential for fighting encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. 

Starting a complement blocker while a patient is actively bacteremic with *Capnocytophaga* is a dangerous game. The clinical team in this case managed this by waiting until blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, while simultaneously providing meningococcal vaccinations and prophylactic antibiotics. This balanced approach allowed the patient to achieve hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **The "Animal History" is Vital:** Any patient presenting with sepsis and a history of dog/cat bites or scratches should be screened for *Capnocytophaga*.
*   **Pancytopenia is a Clue:** While DIC causes low platelets, a profound pancytopenia in the setting of zoonotic sepsis is a classic red flag for *C. canimorsus*.
*   **Don't Trust Early Cultures:** *Capnocytophaga* is slow-growing. Start broad-spectrum coverage (like Pip/Tazo) based on clinical suspicion, not just lab confirmation.
*   **TMA can hide behind DIC:** If a patient is recovering from sepsis but develops new-onset microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and worsening renal failure, think beyond DIC and consider HUS/TTP.
*   **Eculizumab Caution:** Complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms. Ensure the infection is controlled and vaccinations are administered before starting C5 inhibitors.



# DIC or TMA? When Sepsis Takes a Turn

Imagine a previously healthy woman in her 40s walks into your ED. She was bitten by her dog on the lip a few days ago. Now, she’s septic, hypoglycemic, and mottled. Within days, she's in the ICU with pancytopenia, DIC, and complete anuria requiring dialysis. You start broad-spectrum antibiotics, and the sepsis begins to resolve. You think you're winning. Then, the pivot happens: as the inflammatory markers drop and the patient stabilizes, her hemoglobin plummets, her LDH spikes, and schistocytes appear on the smear. 

This is the classic "diagnostic trap." When a patient is in the throes of septic shock and DIC, we tend to attribute everything—the low platelets, the kidney failure, the bleeding—to the sepsis. But what happens when the sepsis clears and the organ failure doesn't? Or worse, when a new hematologic crisis emerges *after* the initial infection is under control?

In this case, the culprit was *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a fastidious gram-negative rod from the dog's mouth. While this organism is notorious for causing fulminant sepsis and DIC, this patient developed something more insidious: Complement-Mediated Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (CM-HUS).

## The Sepsis Phase: The Great Masquerader
Initially, this patient presented with the "textbook" version of *C. canimorsus* sepsis: high fever, lactic acidosis, and a coagulopathy that looked exactly like DIC. In the early phase of sepsis, the distinction between DIC and a Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA) is incredibly muddy. Both involve microvascular thrombosis and organ failure. 

In this case, the patient had pancytopenia and AKI. While the team considered HUS early on, the presence of severe leukopenia and high inflammatory markers pointed squarely toward sepsis. This is a common clinical hurdle; the "noise" of sepsis often drowns out the signal of a superimposed TMA.

## The Pivot: Recognizing the Transition
The turning point in this case occurred on the nephrology ward. The patient was clinically stable—the "sepsis" part of the story was resolving. However, she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L) and a surge in LDH. 

This is the red flag. In simple DIC, you expect the hematologic abnormalities to mirror the severity of the infection. When you see *isolated* microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and persistent thrombocytopenia appearing as the sepsis *improves*, you must pivot your thinking from "sepsis-induced coagulopathy" to "TMA." 

A peripheral smear revealed 15-20% schistocytes. At this point, the differential expands to TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS. This patient's ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (ruling out TTP) and stool PCR was negative for Shiga toxin (ruling out STEC-HUS). The diagnosis was finally cemented with a kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli, confirming a TMA.

## The Therapeutic Paradox
Managing CM-HUS in the wake of an encapsulated bacterial infection creates a genuine clinical dilemma. The gold standard treatment for CM-HUS is Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks the terminal complement component C5. 

Here is the catch: the complement system is the primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria, including *Capnocytophaga*. Blocking the complement system while a patient is bacteremic is essentially like taking the locks off the doors while the burglar is still in the house.

The clinical team in this case navigated this by using Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a bridge. PEX provided temporary stabilization and helped return spontaneous diuresis. Eculizumab was only initiated once blood cultures were negative and the patient had received meningococcal vaccinations. This sequence is critical to avoid catastrophic infectious complications.

## Clinical Pearls: Take-Home Points

*   **The Timing Matters:** If hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or emerge *after* the inflammatory phase of sepsis has resolved, suspect a superimposed TMA rather than simple DIC.
*   **Don't Trust the C3/C4:** In CM-HUS, standard complement levels (C3/C4) can be completely normal. A normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out complement-mediated TMA.
*   **TTP First:** In any adult with the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the first priority is to rule out TTP via ADAMTS13 activity, as this requires urgent PEX.
*   **The Capnocytophaga Connection:** While rare, *C. canimorsus* can act as a "second hit" trigger for CM-HUS, even in patients without a known genetic predisposition to complement dysregulation.
*   **Sequence Your Therapy:** When using complement blockers (Eculizumab) in the context of zoonotic sepsis, ensure blood cultures are negative and vaccinations are administered to mitigate the risk of opportunistic encapsulated bacterial infections.



# The TMA Algorithm: TTP, STEC-HUS, and the 'Atypical' Rest

Imagine a patient presents to your ED after a dog bite. They're septic, pancytopenic, and crashing. You suspect *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a classic "zebra" that is actually a horse in the context of dog bites. You treat the sepsis, the blood cultures clear, and the patient seems to be turning a corner. Then, suddenly, the hemoglobin drops, the platelets plummet, and the kidneys—which were already struggling—completely shut down. 

You’re no longer just dealing with sepsis and DIC; you’ve entered the world of Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA).

TMA is a clinical nightmare because the presentation—microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI)—is a common final pathway for several very different diseases. This case of a woman in her 40s who went from a lip bite to dialysis is a masterclass in how to navigate the TMA diagnostic algorithm.

## The Fog of War: DIC vs. TMA
In the early stages of severe sepsis, the lines between Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) and TMA are incredibly blurry. Both involve microthrombi and consumption of platelets. In this case, the patient initially presented with the classic *C. canimorsus* package: septic shock, profound leukopenia, and coagulopathy. 

The "pivot" happened after the initial septic phase improved. When a patient develops isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia *after* the inflammatory markers have subsided and the infection is controlled, you have to stop thinking about DIC and start thinking about a primary TMA process.

## The Diagnostic Gauntlet
Once you suspect TMA, the goal is to rapidly categorize the patient. The stakes are high because the treatments are vastly different.

First, you have to rule out **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)**. TTP is the most urgent "must-not-miss" because it's rapidly fatal without plasma exchange. The PLASMIC score is a great tool for triage, but the gold standard is ADAMTS13 activity. In this case, the patient had a PLASMIC score of 4/7 (low probability) and an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%. Since TTP usually requires activity <10%, this was a definitive "no."

Next is **Shiga-toxin producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS)**. This is usually associated with a prodromal diarrheal illness. A stool PCR for Shiga toxin is the quickest way to rule this out. In our patient, the PCR was negative.

That leaves us with the "atypical" rest: **Complement-Mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**.

## The Complement Pitfall
Here is where many clinicians get tripped up: they check C3 and C4 levels, see they are normal, and rule out CM-HUS. **Do not do this.** 

Complement levels (C3/C4) can be completely normal in CM-HUS. The pathology isn't necessarily a systemic deficiency of complement, but rather a localized dysregulation on the endothelial surface. In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed via renal biopsy, which showed the classic hallmarks of TMA: fibrin thrombi in the glomeruli and ischemic changes.

## The Treatment Dilemma
The therapeutic approach to CM-HUS is a balancing act. Eculizumab (a C5 inhibitor) is the gold standard, but it carries a massive caveat: it shuts down the terminal complement pathway, which is exactly what the body needs to fight encapsulated bacteria—including *Capnocytophaga*.

The clinical team in this case handled this perfectly by using Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a bridge. PEX doesn't "cure" CM-HUS, but it can temporarily stabilize the patient by removing inflammatory mediators. They waited until the blood cultures were negative before initiating Eculizumab, ensuring the patient wasn't immunocompromised while still battling a lethal zoonotic infection.

## Clinical Pearls
*   **The Sepsis Transition:** If a patient recovers from sepsis but then develops new-onset MAHA and AKI, think TMA, not just "residual organ failure."
*   **The ADAMTS13 Threshold:** TTP is a diagnosis of ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). If activity is normal, stop treating for TTP and move down the algorithm.
*   **C3/C4 are Unreliable:** Normal complement levels do NOT rule out Complement-Mediated HUS. Biopsy and exclusion of TTP/STEC are the keys.
*   **Eculizumab Timing:** Be cautious with complement blockade during active bacteremia with encapsulated organisms. Clear the infection first, vaccinate for Neisseria, then block the complement.
*   **The Trigger:** CM-HUS often requires a "second hit." While many patients have a genetic predisposition, a severe infection like *C. canimorsus* can trigger the cascade even in previously healthy adults.

**Take-home message:** When the blood smear shows schistocytes and the kidneys fail, the clock is ticking. Use the PLASMIC score for TTP, PCR for STEC-HUS, and remember that "atypical" HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion that often requires a biopsy for certainty.



# The Eculizumab Tightrope: Complement Blockade in the Setting of Sepsis

A dog bite usually prompts a tetanus shot and some concerns about wound infection. It does not usually lead to the ICU, complete anuria, and a lifelong prescription for a monoclonal antibody. But as this case reminds us, when the complement system is involved, the clinical trajectory can take a sharp, unexpected turn.

## The Case: More Than Just Sepsis

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented with a rapidly evolving clinical picture four days after being bitten on the lip by her dog. By the time she reached the emergency department, she was in septic shock with mottled extremities, severe pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI). The diagnosis was quickly narrowed to *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a fastidious, gram-negative rod common in the mouths of dogs and cats.

The initial phase was a classic "sepsis storm": disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), metabolic acidosis, and multiorgan failure. However, after an initial response to broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam), the case took a strange turn. Just as the inflammatory markers were improving and the sepsis seemed to be receding, the patient developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia. 

Peripheral smears revealed schistocytes (15-20%), and the patient became completely anuric. This wasn't DIC anymore—which usually resolves as the primary infection is treated—this was a new-onset Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA). After ruling out TTP (ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%) and STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR), a kidney biopsy confirmed the presence of fibrin thrombi and ischemic glomeruli. The diagnosis was complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), triggered by the *C. canimorsus* infection.

## The Clinical Paradox: The C5 Dilemma

This case brings us to a terrifying therapeutic tightrope. To save the patient's kidneys and stop the hemolysis, the team needed to block the terminal complement pathway using eculizumab (a C5 inhibitor). 

Here is the problem: The complement system is not just the "bad guy" causing endothelial damage in HUS; it is also the primary defense mechanism against encapsulated bacteria. The Membrane Attack Complex (MAC), which forms downstream of C5, is essential for lysing pathogens like *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. 

If you give eculizumab while a patient has active *C. canimorsus* bacteremia, you effectively strip the immune system of its best weapon against that specific organism. You risk turning a controlled infection into an uncontrolled, fulminant disaster.

## Navigating the Tightrope

How do we manage a patient who needs a C5 inhibitor to survive, but needs C5 to fight the trigger? The strategy used in this case provides a roadmap for this high-stakes balancing act:

First, prioritize the clearance of the primary infection. Eculizumab was not initiated immediately. The team waited until blood cultures were confirmed negative, ensuring that the "fire" of the bacteremia was out before removing the "water" (complement) used to put it out.

Second, address the vulnerability. Because C5 blockade massively increases the risk of invasive meningococcal disease, vaccination is mandatory. The patient received Menveo and Bexsero. Since the vaccines couldn't be administered two weeks prior to the first dose of eculizumab, the team added a layer of antimicrobial prophylaxis (ampicillin) to bridge the gap.

Finally, use bridging therapy. Plasma exchange (PEX) was used as a temporary measure. While PEX is not a definitive cure for CM-HUS (unlike in TTP), it can help remove circulating inflammatory mediators and provide a temporary window of stabilization while the team waited for the "safe" moment to start the C5 inhibitor.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The "Sepsis Mask":** CM-HUS can be obscured by the early phase of sepsis and DIC. If a patient shows "isolated" hemolysis or worsening renal failure *after* the initial septic shock has resolved, think TMA.
*   **TTP vs. HUS:** In any adult with the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, ADAMTS13 activity is the gold standard to rule out TTP. A level >10% effectively points you away from TTP and toward HUS or other TMAs.
*   **C3/C4 are Unreliable:** Do not rely on standard C3 and C4 levels to rule out CM-HUS. These can remain within normal limits even while the alternative pathway is causing devastating endothelial damage.
*   **The Eculizumab Rule:** Never start a C5 inhibitor in the setting of active, uncontrolled bacteremia with encapsulated organisms unless the benefit of immediate blockade outweighs the risk of lethal sepsis. Wait for negative cultures, vaccinate, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis.

**Take-home messages:**
*   *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a rare but potent trigger for CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults.
*   The timing of eculizumab is critical: too late, and you lose the kidney; too early (during active bacteremia), and you risk overwhelming infection.
*   The "safe" sequence: Clear the infection $\rightarrow$ Vaccinate $\rightarrow$ Prophylactic antibiotics $\rightarrow$ Initiate complement blockade.
