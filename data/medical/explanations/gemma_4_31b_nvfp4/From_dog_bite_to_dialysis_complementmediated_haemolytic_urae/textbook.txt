Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Initial Presentation: Sepsis and the Role of Capnocytophaga canimorsus

The early clinical course of this patient illustrates the rapid and devastating progression possible with *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. The patient, a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, presented four days after a dog bite to the upper lip. While the injury initially seemed minor, the subsequent development of high fever (>40 °C), malaise, and mottled, cool lower extremities signaled a transition from a localized zoonotic exposure to fulminant sepsis. A particularly striking feature of her presentation was refractory hypoglycemia, which, in the context of severe sepsis, often reflects a combination of depleted glycogen stores and impaired gluconeogenesis due to liver dysfunction and increased metabolic demand.

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Transmission to humans occurs through bites, scratches, or even close contact (such as licking an open wound). From a diagnostic standpoint, *C. canimorsus* is "fastidious," meaning it has complex nutritional requirements and grows slowly in standard culture media. This often leads to a dangerous diagnostic lag where the patient’s clinical condition deteriorates significantly before blood cultures provide a definitive identification. In this case, despite an early clinical suspicion, the bacteremia was only confirmed seven days after admission.

The patient's presentation exemplifies septic shock and subsequent multiorgan failure. On admission, her metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27) and elevated lactate (5.7 mmol/L) indicated severe tissue hypoxia and hypoperfusion. The mottled extremities and distal cyanosis suggested a failure of the microcirculation, a hallmark of severe sepsis. Her condition was further complicated by the intersection of sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). The laboratory findings of pancytopenia (a simultaneous drop in red cells, white cells, and platelets) and coagulopathy are characteristic of the consumptive process of DIC, where systemic activation of coagulation leads to the widespread deposition of fibrin and the exhaustion of clotting factors and platelets. This systemic failure quickly extended to the kidneys, manifesting as acute kidney injury (AKI) and eventually progressing to complete anuria.

Given the severity of the presentation and the clear zoonotic history, immediate empiric antimicrobial therapy was critical. The medical team initiated broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam. This choice was strategic, as this combination provides the necessary coverage for common dog-associated pathogens, including *Capnocytophaga* species, while addressing other potential aerobic and anaerobic organisms that might enter the bloodstream following a mucosal bite. This early intervention, combined with intensive care support, was essential to stabilize the patient during the acute septic phase before the subsequent emergence of complement-mediated complications.

## Pathophysiology: From Infectious Trigger to Complement Dysregulation

To understand the development of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) in this patient, it is first necessary to understand the alternative complement pathway. Unlike the classical pathway, which is triggered by antibody-antigen complexes, the alternative pathway is part of the innate immune system and is constitutively active at low levels through a process called "tick-over." This pathway serves as a critical first line of defense against pathogens. Its activity is strictly controlled by regulatory proteins—specifically Factors H, I, and B—which act as "brakes" to prevent the complement system from attacking the body's own healthy cells. When these regulators function correctly, complement activation is localized to the surface of the invading pathogen.

CM-HUS occurs when there is a failure in this regulatory mechanism, leading to uncontrolled activation of the alternative pathway. This dysregulation results in the excessive formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on host endothelial cells. The subsequent endothelial damage triggers a pro-thrombotic state, resulting in the formation of microthrombi within small vessels. In this case, this process manifested as thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), characterized by the clinical triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), with fibrin thrombi clearly visible on the patient's renal biopsy.

In many adults, CM-HUS follows a "second hit" model. The first hit is typically a genetic predisposition—such as a mutation in the genes encoding Factor H or I—that renders the complement system hypersensitive or under-regulated. The second hit is an external trigger, such as an infection, pregnancy, or autoimmune flare, which initiates the complement cascade. While 50% to 70% of patients harbor such genetic variants, this case is notable because extended genetic testing identified no pathogenic variants. This suggests that in some immunocompetent adults, a severe systemic trigger can be sufficient to drive uncontrolled complement activation even in the absence of a known hereditary defect.

The role of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* in this process illustrates a clinical paradox. *C. canimorsus* is generally complement-sensitive, meaning the host's complement system is the primary tool used to eliminate the bacteria from the bloodstream. However, the very process of fighting the infection—massive complement activation to clear the bacteraemia—can spiral out of control if the regulatory system is overwhelmed. In this patient, the systemic inflammatory response to the sepsis likely acted as the "second hit," shifting the complement system from a targeted host defense mechanism into a systemic driver of endothelial damage.

This pathophysiological sequence explains the timing of the patient's clinical decline. While the initial phase of her illness was dominated by classic septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), the isolated drop in haemoglobin and persistent thrombocytopenia emerged as the sepsis began to improve. This indicates that while the bacteria were being cleared, the resulting complement dysregulation persisted, evolving into a secondary TMA that required targeted complement blockade with eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component C5—to halt the formation of the membrane attack complex and allow for renal recovery.

## The Diagnostic Shift: Distinguishing DIC from TMA

In the early stages of severe sepsis, such as the *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection seen in this case, the clinical presentation often involves a complex overlap of coagulopathy and organ failure. Initially, this patient presented with pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, and a systemic inflammatory response, which was correctly interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). DIC is a consumptive coagulopathy where widespread activation of the clotting cascade leads to the simultaneous formation of microthrombi and a depletion of platelets and clotting factors, typically occurring during the acute, peak phase of sepsis.

The critical diagnostic challenge arises when the patient begins to recover from the initial septic insult but develops a new or persistent hematological derangement. In this case, the "diagnostic shift" occurred when the patient's inflammatory profile improved and coagulation parameters normalized, yet she developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L) and persistent thrombocytopenia. This divergence—where the systemic coagulopathy of DIC resolves but microvascular damage persists or emerges—is a primary clinical red flag for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

To confirm a TMA, clinicians must identify microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA). This is characterized by the mechanical destruction of red blood cells as they pass through narrowed, thrombus-filled capillaries. The primary laboratory markers for this process include an increase in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), reflecting cell lysis, and a decrease in haptoglobin, which binds free hemoglobin released into the plasma. In this patient, the rise in LDH and the fact that haptoglobin became undetectable were hallmark indicators of ongoing hemolysis.

The definitive evidence for MAHA is found on the peripheral blood smear. Unlike the systemic clotting seen in DIC, TMA is characterized by the presence of schistocytes—fragmented red blood cells shaped like helmets or triangles—which result from the shearing of erythrocytes against fibrin strands in the microvasculature. The identification of 15%–20% schistocytes in this patient's smear shifted the diagnostic focus from sepsis-related DIC to a TMA.

A common diagnostic pitfall during this process is the interpretation of the Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT), or Coombs test. In this case, an initial positive DAT for IgG suggested autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), leading to an unsuccessful trial of high-dose corticosteroids. However, the DAT can yield false positives in patients who have received recent blood transfusions or those treated with certain antibiotics, such as penicillin. Once the patient failed to respond to steroids and a repeat DAT was negative, the clinical team could confidently rule out AIHA and focus on the mechanical hemolysis characteristic of TMA. This transition highlights the necessity of integrating laboratory trends and morphology over a single test result when navigating the complex recovery phase of critical illness.

## Differential Diagnosis and Confirmatory Workup

The diagnostic challenge in this case arose when the patient’s clinical picture evolved from septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) into a distinct thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The hallmark triad of TMA—microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI)—overlaps across several critical conditions. To arrive at a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), the clinical team had to systematically exclude other primary TMAs using a combination of scoring systems, biochemical assays, and histology.

The first priority in any adult presenting with TMA is to rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), a medical emergency characterized by severe deficiency of the ADAMTS13 enzyme. To triage the likelihood of TTP, the PLASMIC score is often utilized; in this patient, a score of 4/7 indicated a low pretest probability. This was confirmed by laboratory analysis showing ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5%. Since TTP is typically defined by activity levels below 10%, this result effectively ruled out TTP and shifted the focus toward other forms of HUS.

Simultaneously, the team needed to exclude Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*-associated HUS (STEC-HUS), the most common form of HUS. While the patient had experienced a severe infection, the lack of a prodromal enteric illness (diarrhea) and a negative stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxin argued strongly against STEC-HUS. This left CM-HUS (formerly "atypical HUS") as the primary consideration, particularly given the patient's persistent hematological derangements and dialysis-dependent renal failure.

A kidney biopsy was performed to provide histological confirmation of the TMA. The biopsy revealed severe acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, with some scattered crescent formation. These findings are characteristic of TMA, where microthrombi obstruct small vessels and cause downstream ischemic damage to the tubular epithelium. While the biopsy confirmed the presence of a TMA, it cannot distinguish between different etiologies, necessitating further specialized complement studies.

Interpreting complement levels in CM-HUS can be misleading. In this case, the standard C3 and C4 levels were within normal limits. It is a critical clinical lesson that normal C3/C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS; the dysregulation of the alternative pathway may not result in a systemic drop in complement components sufficient to fall below the reference range. To further investigate, functional assays and genetic testing were employed. The quantitative AH50 test, which measures the activity of the alternative pathway, was markedly reduced (6%). However, because this test was performed after the initiation of plasma exchange and eculizumab, the result likely reflected the treatment effect rather than a baseline deficiency.

Finally, extended genetic testing was performed to identify pathogenic variants in complement regulatory proteins (such as factors H, I, or B). No such variants were identified. In the context of a previously healthy patient with no family history of renal disease, the absence of genetic mutations suggests that the CM-HUS was "infection-triggered." In this scenario, the *C. canimorsus* infection acted as the primary driver of complement dysregulation, triggering an uncontrolled inflammatory cascade on the endothelial surface without the requirement of a pre-existing hereditary defect.

## Therapeutic Management: Complement Blockade and Risk Mitigation

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires a precise understanding of the complement cascade to balance the need for endothelial protection against the risk of systemic infection. In this case, the patient's progression from sepsis-induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) to a confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) necessitated a transition from supportive care to targeted complement inhibition.

The definitive treatment for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the terminal complement component C5. By inhibiting the cleavage of C5, eculizumab prevents the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), the final effector of the complement system. In CM-HUS, the alternative pathway is dysregulated, leading to uncontrolled MAC deposition on endothelial cells, which triggers the microvascular thrombosis and haemolysis seen in this patient. Blocking C5 effectively halts this process, protecting the vasculature and allowing for potential renal recovery.

While awaiting definitive diagnosis and the start of complement blockade, plasma exchange (PEX) was utilized as a bridging therapy. It is important to distinguish the role of PEX in CM-HUS from its role in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). In TTP, PEX is life-saving as it removes autoantibodies against ADAMTS13. In CM-HUS, however, PEX provides only temporary or transient benefits by removing circulating inflammatory mediators and complement fragments. In this patient, while diuresis returned following PEX, sustained haematological remission and renal stabilization were only achieved after the initiation of eculizumab.

The initiation of eculizumab in the setting of an active infection presents a significant clinical dilemma. Terminal complement components are critical for the host's defense against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* species and *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Blocking the C5 pathway during active bacteraemia can impair the immune system's ability to clear the pathogen, potentially worsening the infection. To mitigate this risk, the clinical team deferred the administration of eculizumab until blood cultures had confirmed the clearance of *C. canimorsus*, ensuring that the primary infection was under control before suppressing the terminal complement pathway.

Because eculizumab profoundly increases the risk of invasive meningococcal disease, strict prophylaxis is mandatory. This includes vaccination against *Neisseria meningitidis*. In this case, the patient received both Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). Because these vaccines require time to induce an immune response and were not administered two weeks prior to the start of therapy, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was administered to provide immediate coverage.

The timing of eculizumab initiation is the primary determinant of the renal outcome in CM-HUS. Delayed treatment is strongly associated with incomplete renal recovery and an increased risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. Although this patient achieved haematological remission and a return of urine output, her kidney function remained reduced (serum creatinine 175 µmol/L). This partial recovery reflects the severity of the initial acute tubular necrosis and the complexity of the overlapping sepsis and TMA phases.

## Clinical Outcome and Broader Implications

The clinical trajectory of this patient underscores the critical relationship between the timing of therapeutic intervention and renal prognosis in complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Upon the initiation of eculizumab and plasma exchange, the patient achieved haematological remission and a return of spontaneous diuresis. However, her renal recovery was only partial, with a residual serum creatinine of 175 $\mu$mol/L. In the context of TMA, the degree of renal recovery is often inversely proportional to the duration of untreated complement activation and the severity of the initial acute kidney injury. Because the CM-HUS in this case was initially obscured by the symptoms of septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), the window for optimal renal salvage was narrowed.

The decision regarding maintenance therapy with eculizumab is complex, particularly when genetic testing does not reveal a clear pathogenic variant. In this patient, maintenance therapy (1200 mg every two weeks) was continued for four months following induction. While the identification of a genetic mutation in complement factors (such as Factor H or I) would strongly favor long-term, potentially lifelong therapy due to a permanent predisposition to relapse, the absence of such variants suggests an infection-triggered event. In these cases, the criteria for discontinuation are based on clinical stability and the stabilization of renal function. The severity of the initial presentation in this patient justified continued blockade to prevent relapse and allow for maximum possible renal recovery.

This case provides a significant educational insight into the pathophysiology of CM-HUS: it can be triggered by a severe infection even in the absence of a detectable genetic predisposition. While CM-HUS often arises from a "two-hit" model—where a genetic defect is the first hit and an infection is the second—this patient demonstrates that certain pathogens, such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, may induce sufficient complement dysregulation to trigger TMA in an otherwise immunocompetent adult. This highlights the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TMA in any patient with severe sepsis who exhibits a failure to recover haematological parameters as the primary infection resolves.

A key takeaway for the clinician is the danger of "diagnostic overshadowing" during the early phase of sepsis. In this patient, the initial pancytopenia and coagulopathy were correctly attributed to DIC and sepsis. However, the subsequent "isolated" drop in haemoglobin, the appearance of schistocytes on a peripheral smear, and persistent thrombocytopenia despite the resolution of the inflammatory profile served as the "red flags" for a superimposed TMA. Clinicians should be alert to these signs, as the transition from DIC to CM-HUS can be subtle, and early initiation of complement blockade is the most important predictor of a favorable outcome.

Ultimately, the management of such a complex case necessitates an integrated multidisciplinary approach. The successful outcome relied on the coordination of Infectious Disease specialists to identify and treat the fastidious *C. canimorsus* organism, Haematologists to differentiate between TTP, DIC, and MAHA, and Nephrologists to manage dialysis and implement targeted complement therapy. For the medical student or resident, this case illustrates that when a patient’s clinical course deviates from the expected recovery of sepsis, a multidisciplinary diagnostic re-evaluation is essential to identify life-threatening complications like CM-HUS.
