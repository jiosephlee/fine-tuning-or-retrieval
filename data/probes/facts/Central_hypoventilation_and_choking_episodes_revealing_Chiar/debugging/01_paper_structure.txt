----- Row 2 -----
section: Abstract
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Summary: A woman in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting solids and liquids, nocturnal choking episodes occurring 3–4 times weekly, and chronic occipital headaches spanning 14 years. Home monitoring during the COVID-19 pandemic incidentally revealed episodes of severe oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) without respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies performed to investigate the choking episodes were normal. Review of a 14-year-old CT scan of the head performed for unrelated trauma revealed a previously overlooked crowded foramen magnum. MRI of the head and neck confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent. Sleep study demonstrated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0) with predominantly central events (72%). This case illustrates that CMI can present with respiratory symptoms that closely mimic acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), emphasising the importance of considering structural brain abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

----- Row 3 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined by caudal displacement of cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Contemporary imaging studies reveal CMI in 1% of the adult population, though symptomatic presentations occur in approximately 0.1% of the general population. The condition demonstrates a modest female predominance of 1.3:1 and typically presents during the third decade of life.

----- Row 4 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Pathophysiology centres on a developmentally small posterior fossa, creating a volumetric mismatch between neural structures and available space. However, contemporary evidence challenges the traditional conceptualisation of CMI as universally pathological, proposing instead a spectrum from normal anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations. Recent volumetric studies demonstrate substantial demographic variability in posterior fossa measurements without consistent symptom correlation. This change in thinking necessitates individualised, symptom-based clinical decision-making, distinguishing asymptomatic anatomical variants from pathological entities requiring surgical intervention.

----- Row 5 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Chiari syndrome represents the symptomatic clinical manifestation of CMI, characterised by a distinctive constellation of neurological findings. The cardinal symptom is paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, present in over 80% of cases. Adults typically present with more severe symptomatology compared with children, especially when concurrent syringomyelia develops. Lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15%–26% of patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

----- Row 6 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Respiratory manifestations represent an increasingly recognised complication of CMI. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. A central apnoea index ≥5 was observed in 6.7% of patients, although pure central sleep apnoea occurred in only one case, potentially reflecting brainstem compression. This respiratory pathology extends beyond sleep disturbance; a case series documented acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxaemia in CMI patients.

----- Row 7 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The mechanisms underlying respiratory dysfunction involve brainstem compression, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow obstruction and disruption of respiratory control centres, leading to intermittent respiratory symptoms. The resulting respiratory symptoms represent an acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes termed ‘Ondine’s curse’, distinct from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome form associated with PHOX2B mutations. Recent guidelines emphasise the importance of this connection by recommending routine polysomnography for CMI patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing. Crucially, posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, underscoring the importance of recognising CMI in unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

----- Row 8 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: A woman in her early 40s presented to our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that occurred unpredictably during meals and sleep, affecting both solids and liquids equally. She described the episodes as sudden and distressing, often interrupting meals or awakening her from sleep with violent coughing and gasping, though they never resulted in complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times per week, occasionally two times in one night. Over the years, she had undergone extensive evaluations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which were reported as normal. Despite the lack of diagnostic findings, her symptoms persisted. The social impact was significant; she avoided restaurants and gatherings due to fear of public choking episodes. This ongoing anxiety and embarrassment led to increasing social withdrawal and emotional distress.

----- Row 9 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: A more detailed clinical history revealed that her symptoms began 14 years earlier, following a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness. Shortly afterwards, she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches. These were described as constant, pressure-like and often localised to a coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches were not exacerbated by coughing, straining or other Valsalva manoeuvres. Several physicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she underwent multiple local scalp injections, which offered only transient relief. The most distinctive feature was marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. She ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, though this decision conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress.

section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The temporal relationship between our patient’s motor vehicle accident and subsequent symptom onset warrants careful consideration. Chiari malformation type I has been documented as both a congenital condition and as a sequela of head trauma and other neurological injuries. Contemporary aetiological classifications2022 recognise this spectrum, distinguishing between primary Chiari malformations (congenital) and secondary or acquired forms resulting from trauma or increased intracranial pressure.

section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: In our case, the CT scan performed immediately after the accident demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum. Without pre-trauma imaging, we cannot determine whether the trauma caused or merely revealed this finding. The immediate onset of occipital headaches post-accident, followed by the progressive development of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over 14 years, could reflect either: (1) trauma-induced tonsillar herniation or (2) trauma that exacerbated or unmasked a previously asymptomatic congenital variant.

----- Row 35 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: This case exemplifies the complex respiratory manifestations of CMI and enhances our understanding of acquired central hypoventilation patterns in structural brainstem pathology. The distinction between voluntary and automatic breathing proves crucial in understanding CMI respiratory pathophysiology. Voluntary breathing, mediated by cortical pathways, typically remains intact in CMI patients. In contrast, automatic breathing, controlled by brainstem centres and essential during sleep, becomes compromised due to tonsillar compression of medullary structures. Our patient preserved voluntary ventilation yet lost automatic respiratory drive, a phenotype reminiscent of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, although via a different pathophysiological mechanism. The airport episode, where a momentary distraction precipitated profound desaturation without dyspnoea, vividly illustrates this selective failure of automatic control: the patient literally ‘forgets to breathe’.

----- Row 36 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: We hypothesise that a primary brainstem dysfunction is the cause of the patient’s alarming absence of dyspnoea during episodes of significant oxygen desaturation. This proposed brainstem pathology likely interrupts the critical afferent neural pathways that must ascend to the cortex to produce conscious sensation. The experience of breathlessness is a form of interoception, which depends on the processing of these afferent signals by key brain regions, including the anterior insular cortex. By disrupting these signals during their transit through the brainstem, the patient’s cortex would not receive the necessary input to generate the feeling of air hunger, thus explaining the dangerous lack of awareness of their physiological distress.

----- Row 37 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The marked discrepancy between our patient’s severe symptoms and relatively mild polysomnographic findings (AHI 6.0) warrants explanation. Due to practical limitations, we used type III rather than type I polysomnography, which lacks EEG monitoring. This absence prevented accurate calculation of total sleep time, likely leading to significant underestimation of respiratory events. Without EEG data, the AHI is calculated using total recording time, which includes periods of wakefulness, rather than true sleep time alone. This methodological limitation means the reported AHI of 6.0 likely underestimates the true severity of sleep-disordered breathing.

section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Oropharyngeal dysphagia, affecting both solids and liquids equally, and associated with choking and coughing, was the most distressing symptom in our patient. A prospective evaluation using validated swallowing assessments found dysfunction in 36% of patients, with neurogenic patterns predominating. The equal difficulty with all consistencies distinguishes neurogenic from mechanical dysphagia. The patient’s most distressing symptom was initially and repeatedly investigated from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective, with normal findings; nevertheless, her diagnosis was delayed for several years. This delay in diagnosis highlights an important clinical lesson: dysphagia or choking without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin. The prevalence of CMI in a retrospective study of children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, which is considerably higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence reported in the general paediatric population. This suggests that persistent dysphagia can be a key indicator of underlying CMI.

section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The patient’s report of pain in the vertex area and relief with hair cutting represents an interesting but understudied phenomenon. Occipital neuralgia occurs in association with CMI and can cause scalp hypersensitivity; diagnostic criteria require ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. However, the association between occipital neuralgia and CMI is rarely reported, with no conclusive data establishing a causative link.

----- Row 43 -----
section: Discussion
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: An alternative consideration is the recently described ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which presents with scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia and features that do not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications such as migraine, cluster, tension-type or nummular headache. The hypothesised mechanism may involve small fibre pathways originating in the scalp, with patients responding variably to greater occipital nerve blocks. In our patient, the vertex symptoms may represent: (1) occipital neuralgia related to CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally; or (3) an entirely separate primary headache disorder. Systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations is indeed lacking, as evidenced by the absence of any published instrument to assess specific symptoms in CMI patients prior to the development of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given this paucity of evidence, we present this finding as an interesting clinical observation rather than a definitive diagnostic feature.
