paragraph: Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

----- Row 1 -----
section: Title/Authors
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

----- Row 2 -----
section: Abstract
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Summary: A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented with rapidly progressive sepsis 4 days after a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia and acute kidney injury, ultimately progressing to complete anuria and dialysis dependence. Blood cultures confirmed Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Despite an initial clinical improvement with antibiotics and supportive care, she developed sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory and renal biopsy findings confirmed the presence of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) was diagnosed. Following plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab therapy, haematological remission was achieved, and her kidney function partially recovered. This case highlights C. canimorsus infection as a rare trigger of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and outlines the patient’s diagnostic workup, the challenges of differential diagnosis and the complexity of both the clinical course and therapeutic approach in case of TMA following sepsis.

----- Row 3 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Human infections are rare, and transmission can occur via bites, scratches or even close contact. Clinical presentations are highly variable, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite broad antibiotic susceptibility, mortality can approach 30%.

----- Row 4 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: C. canimorsus is the Capnocytophaga species most frequently linked in invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Diagnosis can be delayed due to its fastidious nature and slow growth in cultures, prompting increasing use of molecular methods, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

----- Row 5 -----
section: Background
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: In our case, C. canimorsus infection presented with septic shock, severe leukopaenia and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. Although the initial clinical course was dominated by sepsis-related multiorgan involvement, the later development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) suggested a superimposed TMA, highlighting how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase.

----- Row 6 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: A previously healthy woman in her late 40s, with two prior uncomplicated pregnancies, presented to the emergency department with a rapidly worsening clinical condition. Four days prior to admission, she had been bitten by her dog on the upper lip and received tetanus vaccination as prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the day of the incident; however, antibiotic treatment was not initiated, as she was unable to collect her prescription. She developed a high fever (>40 °C), malaise, reduced oral intake and later sought medical attention when numbness and discolouration of her feet appeared. At primary-care emergency, she was found to have hypoglycaemia, refractory to oral glucose and glucagon, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, prompting urgent transfer to our emergency department.

----- Row 9 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Given her constellation of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy with AKI and lactic acidosis—her condition was interpreted as DIC secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). A surgical cause of her mottled extremities—including necrotising fasciitis—was ruled out after clinical and radiological evaluation. Due to the severity of her presentation, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was started to cover dog-associated pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. Notably, C. canimorsus was suspected from the onset and was 7 days later confirmed by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring.

----- Row 10 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Given the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology was consulted. The differential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered but seemed unlikely due to leukopaenia, inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin and non-compensatory reticulocytes. Flow cytometry was performed, and bone marrow biopsy planned; however, normal results—with no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—led to cancellation of the biopsy. Clinically, the presentation was consistent with sepsis. While awaiting results, supportive thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions were administered.

Aggressive fluid resuscitation and concurrent administration of loop diuretics were attempted without success. She subsequently developed septic cardiomyopathy with fluid overload, requiring the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune tests (antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)) were negative.

paragraph: After initial improvement, the clinical picture changed when she developed a sudden isolated drop in haemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L over 6 days) without evidence of bleeding or haemodynamic instability. Platelets remained persistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable and LDH levels rose, indicating ongoing haemolysis (figure 1).

----- Row 16 -----
section: Case presentation
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) was positive for IgG, raising suspicion of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was started alongside routine prophylaxis, and several blood transfusions were given over 2 days. When the haemolytic process failed to respond to corticosteroid therapy and platelets remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further evaluation was warranted. A peripheral blood smear revealed 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with MAHA suggestive of TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT was likely a false positive from penicillin or prior transfusions; repeat testing was negative.

paragraph: Complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity were sent for analysis. Locally available standard complement tests (C3/C4) were within normal limits, and functional assays of the classical, alternative and lectin pathways were likewise normal. ADAMTS13 activity was reported shortly after, at 43.5%, effectively ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP typically presents with activity below 10%. While awaiting these results, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as bridging therapy prior to the first dose of eculizumab.

----- Row 23 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Following the complex medical management, the patient is in good general condition with adequate urine output. Kidney function remains reduced (serum creatine 175 µmol/L), and she will be followed in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She continues maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg intravenous every 2 weeks), for 4 months post-induction. Extended complement genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants; the results were communicated verbally by the reference laboratory, with the formal written report pending at the time of submission. Based on this information, complement blockade is being continued due to the severity of her disease, with planned reassessment for discontinuation if renal function stabilises. Identification of pathogenic complement gene abnormalities would favour longer term therapy with close monitoring for relapse. However, no such variants were identified in this patient.

paragraph: Differential diagnosis of TMA

----- Row 25 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

----- Row 26 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: The initial priority in adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI is to exclude thrombotic TTP, a condition defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scores like PLASMIC can help triage while awaiting the results, but diagnostic confirmation is laboratory based. Our patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability of TTP), and ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, making TTP unlikely and guiding further diagnostic workup.

----- Row 27 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: STEC-HUS is another important consideration. The patient tested negative for Shiga toxin by PCR. Thus, the absence of prodromal enteric illness and negative microbiological testing argues against STEC-HUS.

----- Row 28 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Ultimately, the constellation of persistent haemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-dependent AKI and kidney biopsy findings consistent with TMA supported a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). The diagnosis was further strengthened by the exclusion of STEC by PCR and her rapid clinical improvement after initiating eculizumab therapy. Her insignificant medical history weakens the hypothesis of a pre-existing complement defect requiring a ‘second hit’ and supports infection-triggered CM-HUS.

----- Row 29 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion and complement levels (C3/C4) may be normal during the disease course. Importantly, normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM-HUS, as levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course. Notably, haemolysis appeared only after sepsis began improving, suggesting infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than pre-existing disease.

----- Row 30 -----

subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: In our patient, basic complement tests (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were obtained immediately before the first PEX session. The extended complement testing was performed 8 days after the final PEX session and 4 days after the first dose of eculizumab. The observed reduction in the alternative pathway is most consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement depletion or activation.

paragraph: Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

----- Row 32 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: CM-HUS (historically termed ‘atypical HUS’) arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, often in genetically predisposed individuals. Between 50% and 70% of patients harbour genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory proteins, such as factors H, I and B or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). However, disease penetrance is incomplete, and clinical manifestation typically requires an external trigger, most commonly infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disease or certain medications.

----- Row 33 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Although rarely reported, C. canimorsus can activate complement pathways, particularly in susceptible hosts. Notably, C. canimorsus displays variable serum susceptibility, with many isolates being complement sensitive while some capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance, which means that complement activity is an important component of host defence. This creates a clinical paradox: while complement activation clears the pathogen, it may also trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA in predisposed individuals.

----- Row 34 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: This case adds to a small but growing body of literature linking C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. A particularly illustrative report by Bjørkto et al described a patient with C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS harbouring a complement factor B variant, successfully treated with eculizumab. In contrast to the previously reported case, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants were identified in our patient, supporting infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement predisposition.

paragraph: Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

----- Row 36 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: Management of CM-HUS in the context of active infection presents unique challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component 5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and markedly improves haematological and renal outcomes, particularly when initiated early. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and consequently higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

----- Row 37 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: In contrast, PEX is lifesaving in TTP, but provides only temporary benefit in CM-HUS, mainly by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators or autoantibodies, without correcting the underlying complement system dysfunction. In our patient, diuresis returned after the final PEX sessions but before eculizumab, reflecting only transient stabilisation. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade.

----- Row 38 -----
section: Outcome and follow-up
subsection: No Subsection
paragraph: A key therapeutic dilemma is using complement blockade during active bacteraemia, as terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—raising concerns about eculizumab-associated infectious risks. In this case, eculizumab was initiated after blood cultures became negative, minimising this risk of uncontrolled infection. Vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis and close monitoring remain essential when using complement inhibitors.
