# From “Right to Control” to Fraudulent Inducement: How the Fifth Circuit Reframed Social-Media Pump-and-Dump Prosecutions

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision reviving the federal indictment in the alleged social-media “pump and dump” scheme is important for a reason that extends well beyond stock-promotion influencers. The court drew a sharp line between two different theories of fraud: an impermissible “right to control” theory, which the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, and a classic fraudulent-inducement theory, which the Supreme Court recently reaffirmed in *Kousisis v. United States*. That move matters because the government increasingly prosecutes online deception through the broad federal fraud statutes, and defendants have responded by arguing that the cases are really about misleading market participants or depriving them of information, not about taking money or property.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that framing. In the court’s view, the indictment did not merely allege that defendants lied to followers about market conditions or about the defendants’ own trading positions. It alleged something more concrete: that the defendants used false statements to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money. That distinction is now doing a lot of work in federal fraud doctrine, and this case shows how quickly *Kousisis* has become the government’s favorite answer to post-*Ciminelli* defense arguments.

## The court’s core move

The district court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that the government was really alleging the deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information,” which *Ciminelli* says is not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit said that was the wrong lens. The indictment, the panel emphasized, did not charge the defendants merely with hiding information from followers. It charged them with using false and misleading social-media posts to induce those followers to purchase securities.

That is a fraudulent-inducement theory. As the Fifth Circuit explained, fraudulent inducement involves using a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property. The victim may get something in return, even something of equivalent value, but that does not negate fraud if the object of the scheme was to obtain the victim’s money or property through deceit. The panel leaned heavily on *Kousisis*, which expressly held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off.

So the key analytical point is this: the government was not required to plead a “right to control” deprivation because it did not frame the case that way. It alleged that the followers were induced to buy securities. In the court’s telling, that means the scheme targeted money, not merely information.

## Why the distinction is doctrinally significant

The opinion’s real significance lies in how it narrows the practical impact of *Ciminelli*. *Ciminelli* was widely read as a warning against federal fraud prosecutions that are really about deception unconnected to a recognized property interest. The Supreme Court said the right-to-control theory was not enough because the federal fraud statutes protect money and property, not an abstract interest in making informed economic decisions.

Defendants in cases like this hoped to use that holding to challenge fraud charges built around misinformation in markets: inflated claims, omitted risks, hidden positions, and manipulated online hype. The Fifth Circuit’s response is that these are not just information cases if the lie is used to induce purchases. Once the government alleges that the misrepresentation was designed to make the victim part with money, the fraud statutes are satisfied even if the victim receives a security, a service, or some other item in return.

That means *Ciminelli* is not the broad anti-fraud retrenchment some predicted. It remains a strong rejection of “information as property” theories, but it does not bar the government from prosecuting deceptive sales pitches, sham contracts, or misleading inducements so long as the object is obtaining money or property. In that respect, the Fifth Circuit’s opinion fits neatly with *Kousisis* rather than trying to stretch *Ciminelli* beyond its facts.

## The intent question: profit without “harm”

The defendants also argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud because it said they were trying to profit, not to injure anyone. The Fifth Circuit rejected that too, and again the Supreme Court’s recent guidance did most of the work.

The panel read *Kousisis* to foreclose the notion that fraud requires proof of intended net economic harm. A defendant can intend to deceive a victim into parting with money even if the victim receives something of value in return. In that sense, the fraud consists in the dishonest procurement itself, not in proving that the victim was left worse off at the end of the transaction.

That is an important clarification for fraud cases involving market transactions, where the defense often argues that the victim got the stock, the service, or the contract bargain they paid for. The Fifth Circuit treated that as beside the point. If the object of the scheme was to use false statements to cause purchases, then the intent-to-defraud element is satisfied. The court also noted the indictment’s allegation that the defendants “had money in mind,” which underscores the commercial nature of the alleged scheme.

## How this may affect future prosecution strategies

This decision will likely encourage prosecutors to plead pump-and-dump and other market-manipulation cases as straightforward inducement cases rather than as disputes over informational rights. That is likely the safest post-*Ciminelli* path. Instead of arguing that victims were deprived of accurate information needed to make investment decisions, prosecutors will emphasize that the false statements were the means by which the defendants got victims to buy securities.

That framing may also matter in a wider range of online fraud cases. Influencers, promoters, and “finfluencers” increasingly operate in a gray zone between speech and salesmanship. If prosecutors can show that false claims were used to induce transfers of money, the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning suggests the federal fraud statutes will reach the conduct even if the victims received something tangible in return or even if the defendants’ immediate goal was self-enrichment rather than direct victim impoverishment.

At the same time, the decision leaves open some of the harder boundary questions. There is still a meaningful difference between deception that distorts someone’s decision-making and deception that actually induces a transfer of money or property. The former may be too close to *Ciminelli* if the government cannot identify a traditional property interest at stake. The latter is what the Fifth Circuit found here. Future litigation will likely turn on how concretely the indictment connects the misrepresentation to the transfer of money or property.

## A note of caution

The opinion is doctrinally tidy, but it also illustrates how flexible federal fraud law remains after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. The line between “withholding important information” and “fraudulently inducing a purchase” is not always bright in practice, especially in securities markets where information is the currency of the transaction. The Fifth Circuit avoided that problem by grounding its analysis in the indictment’s allegations of false posts, induced purchases, and the defendants’ profit from the resulting price movement.

That grounding matters. The court did not say that any market manipulation is fraud simply because investors were misled. It said the indictment alleged a concrete scheme to get followers to part with money by means of false statements. That is a classic fraud theory, and the panel was on firmer ground for saying so than the district court was in collapsing the case into a right-to-control dispute.

## The bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s decision is best read as a post-*Kousisis* clarification of how federal fraud doctrine applies to digital-era securities schemes. The indictment survived not because the court expanded fraud law, but because it refused to treat a purported information-theory case as something it was not. In the panel’s view, the government alleged a scheme to deceive followers into buying securities, thereby obtaining their money. That is fraudulent inducement, not a forbidden right-to-control theory.

For future defendants, the lesson is clear: *Ciminelli* remains a useful limit on amorphous fraud theories, but it will not save a case where the indictment alleges that lies were used to make victims hand over money. For prosecutors, the lesson is equally clear: if the facts support it, plead the transfer of money or property directly and avoid casting the case as one about information alone.



# Property Without Loss? The Fifth Circuit’s Broad Reading of “Intent to Defraud” After *Kousisis*

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in the *Constantinescu* “pump and dump” case is important for a reason that extends well beyond securities prosecutions: it pushes federal fraud law further away from any requirement that the government prove a defendant intended to leave victims economically worse off. Under the panel’s reading, it is enough that defendants schemed to obtain money or property by deception—even if the victims received something of market value in return and even if the defendants’ objective was profit rather than victim impoverishment.

That matters because federal fraud doctrine has spent the last several years sorting through the Supreme Court’s effort to cabin the “right to control” theory in *Ciminelli* while preserving classic fraudulent-inducement cases. The Fifth Circuit reads *Kousisis* as strongly confirming that a fraud prosecution survives so long as the defendant uses misrepresentations to induce a transfer of money or property. In the securities context, that means the government does not need to allege that the scheme was designed to make investors “worse off” in a net sense. It is enough that the defendants allegedly lied to induce purchases of securities and then profited from the resulting trades.

## The court’s basic move

The district court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that it did not allege deprivation of a traditional property interest. The panel reverses by drawing a sharp line between two different ideas: depriving victims of valuable economic information, which *Ciminelli* rejects as a standalone theory, and inducing victims to part with money or property through deceit, which *Kousisis* expressly preserves.

That distinction does most of the work in the opinion. The indictment, as the panel reads it, does not say merely that the defendants misled followers so those followers could make better-informed investment decisions. It says the defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities. That is enough, in the court’s view, to plead a scheme to defraud because the object of the scheme was the followers’ money, not just their decision-making autonomy.

The opinion is also notable for how little friction it sees between the defendants’ theory and the victims’ receipt of securities in exchange. The panel relies on *Kousisis* for the proposition that fraud does not require proof that the victim suffered net pecuniary loss. If the defendants obtained money through material deception, the fraud is complete even if the victims received something with value in return. That is the key doctrinal bridge the court uses to treat a pump-and-dump operation as ordinary fraudulent inducement rather than a more ambiguous “information harm” case.

## “Intent to defraud” without intent to cause net loss

The more consequential part of the opinion may be its treatment of intent. The defendants argued that the indictment alleged only an intent to profit, not an intent to harm. The panel rejects that as a distinction without a difference.

This is where the Fifth Circuit reads *Kousisis* expansively. The Supreme Court there held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. The Fifth Circuit takes that principle and applies it cleanly to the indictment before it: because the alleged scheme was to induce purchases by false pretenses and then dump the shares for profit, the defendants had “money in mind,” and that is enough.

That formulation matters. It suggests that, for purposes of federal fraud statutes, “intent to defraud” is satisfied by a plan to obtain money or property through deception, even if the defendant’s mental state is wholly profit-driven and even if the victim’s bargain may have included something of market value. In other words, the law does not require a mirror-image loss-and-gain structure. The harm element is not tied to economic asymmetry in the transaction; it is tied to the deceptive acquisition of property.

## The tension at the heart of the opinion

There is, however, a real tension running through the case. On one hand, the court insists—correctly, under *Ciminelli*—that federal fraud law cannot be based on a mere deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information.” On the other, it treats a set of alleged misrepresentations that distorted how followers bought securities as sufficient, even though the victims were allegedly left holding securities that may have had some value.

That leaves the doctrine in a somewhat expanded posture. The “traditional property interest” requirement remains formally intact, but the practical threshold for pleading fraud is low once the indictment alleges that deception induced the transfer of money. In cases like this one, the market value of the thing received does not do much work at the pleading stage. The court’s focus is on the transfer itself: if the defendant induced the victim to part with money on false pretenses, the property interest is satisfied.

One could say the panel’s view is faithful to longstanding fraud doctrine. But one could also say it moves the law closer to treating any deceptive inducement to trade as fraud, so long as the transaction involved money changing hands. That may be sensible in a pump-and-dump case, where the deceit is not incidental but central to the scheme. Still, the reasoning has broader implications. It can be deployed in other settings where defendants provide something of value but allegedly manipulate the bargain through falsehoods.

## Downstream effects

The immediate effect of the decision is to make it harder to dismiss securities-fraud indictments that are framed as deceptive inducement cases. Prosecutors need not plead or prove that the target investors were guaranteed to lose money, or that the defendants’ scheme was designed to leave them economically worse off. A pleaded plan to use false statements to induce purchases will usually be enough, especially at the indictment stage where the court takes the allegations as true.

More broadly, the opinion strengthens the government’s hand in cases involving online influencers, touting, and market manipulation. That is not surprising; the alleged facts here are especially ugly, with the defendants supposedly exploiting large social-media followings to move prices and cash out. But the opinion’s doctrinal language is capable of traveling. If fraud is complete once money is induced through deception, then the line between classic fraud and aggressive but lawful salesmanship becomes even more dependent on how concrete the misrepresentation is and how directly it is tied to the transfer.

That suggests two downstream questions for future cases. First, courts will need to police the boundary between fraudulent inducement and nonactionable nondisclosure or puffery. Second, defense arguments may shift away from “no economic loss” and toward whether the alleged statements were material misrepresentations at all, or whether the indictment truly alleges a scheme to obtain property rather than merely manipulate information flows. Those questions may become the real battleground now that the loss-based argument has largely been foreclosed.

## A broader reading of fraud, but not a limitless one

The Fifth Circuit does not hold that every misleading financial post is fraud, nor does it erase the property requirement announced in *Ciminelli*. But it does embrace a broad conception of fraud injury: once deception is used to induce a transfer of money or property, the government has alleged enough—even if the victim receives something of value and even if the defendant’s goal was simply to profit.

That is a powerful doctrinal statement. In practical terms, it aligns the fraud statutes with what most people intuitively think pump-and-dump schemes are: not merely the theft of information, but a con by which investors are tricked into buying while the schemer exits. Still, the opinion’s logic also underscores how elastic federal fraud law remains after *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. The traditional property requirement is still there, but in many inducement cases it may function more like a pleading label than a meaningful limit.

For now, the Fifth Circuit has made clear that in securities-fraud cases, the government does not need to allege that defendants aimed to impoverish their victims. It needs to allege that they used deception to get the victims’ money. After this decision, that may be enough to carry the day.



# Section 1348 Grows Up: What the Fifth Circuit’s Opinion Says About Securities Fraud as a Mail-and-Wire-Fraud Cousin

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision reversing dismissal of the “pump and dump” indictment is important for a reason that goes beyond the defendants’ alleged social-media stock scheme. It tells us something larger about how federal securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 is going to be read in the post-*Ciminelli* and post-*Kousisis* era: not as a narrow, securities-specific provision with its own distinctive limits, but as a statute that borrows heavily from the familiar mail- and wire-fraud universe.

That matters because § 1348 sits at the intersection of two currents in fraud doctrine. On one side is the Supreme Court’s insistence that federal fraud statutes protect money and property, not freestanding interests in accurate information or honest markets. On the other is the government’s continuing effort to use broad fraud provisions to police deceptive conduct that moves through modern markets, including online influencer-driven trading. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion comes down firmly on the government’s side, and it does so by embracing a methodology that treats § 1348 as a cousin of mail and wire fraud rather than a statute with meaningfully narrower securities-specific contours.

## The court’s basic move

The indictment charged the defendants with operating a classic pump-and-dump scheme: buy a security, post false or misleading claims to inflate demand, sell into the inflated price, and pocket the difference. The district court dismissed the indictment on the theory that it alleged only a deprivation of potentially valuable economic information, not a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. Its reasoning is straightforward: the indictment does not merely allege that the defendants lied to deprive their followers of better information. It alleges that the lies induced followers to part with money to buy securities. That, the court says, is a property deprivation. The panel therefore characterizes the case as one of fraudulent inducement, not a forbidden right-to-control theory. And once the case is framed that way, the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Kousisis* does a lot of work. Under *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes can reach schemes to obtain money or property by deception even if the defendant does not seek to leave the victim economically worse off overall.

The court’s treatment of intent follows the same logic. The defendants argued that they intended to enrich themselves, not to harm their followers. The panel answers that fraud law does not require a separate purpose to inflict net economic loss. If the object of the scheme was to obtain money through falsehoods, that is enough. In the court’s view, the indictment alleges just that.

## The methodology: § 1348 as mail-and-wire fraud in securities clothing

The most interesting part of the opinion is not the outcome, but the interpretive posture. The panel explicitly notes that § 1348 borrows “key concepts” from the mail and wire fraud statutes, and that courts therefore often analyze securities fraud using those precedents. That is a familiar move in Fifth Circuit law, but the opinion takes it seriously in a way that deserves attention.

This is not merely a case of citing analogies for convenience. The court’s analysis tracks the modern fraud cases closely: what counts as a scheme to defraud, what counts as intent to defraud, and what kind of property interest must be implicated. The result is a reading of § 1348 that effectively imports the Supreme Court’s mail-and-wire-fraud property framework into the securities context.

There is real doctrinal logic to that approach. Section 1348 uses terms—“scheme or artifice to defraud,” “money or property,” and “in connection with” a security—that are deeply familiar from the fraud statutes. If Congress chose language that echoes those provisions, it is unsurprising that courts would interpret them in parallel. That parallelism also promotes coherence: it would be odd if identical deceptive conduct were criminal under one fraud statute but not another simply because the victim was buying a security.

At the same time, there is a structural counterargument. Section 1348 was enacted as part of Sarbanes-Oxley against the backdrop of securities-market scandals. One could read that as a sign Congress wanted a securities-specific anti-fraud provision, not a wholesale transplantation of mail-fraud doctrine. If that is right, then the statute might have been intended to reach some forms of market deception more flexibly—or, depending on one’s view, to be cabined by securities-law context rather than stretched to match general fraud law. The Fifth Circuit does not really engage that possibility. Instead, it assumes that “borrowing” is the right interpretive frame and then works within it.

## Why the court’s Ciminelli/Kousisis distinction matters

The district court’s error, in the Fifth Circuit’s account, was treating the indictment as if it alleged only a deprivation of information. That is the line *Ciminelli* drew: the federal fraud statutes do not criminalize a scheme to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information, standing alone.

The Fifth Circuit’s answer is that the indictment alleges more. The defendants did not just manipulate what their followers knew; they allegedly induced them to spend money on securities through misstatements about trading positions and price prospects. That makes the case a fraudulent-inducement case, which *Kousisis* says is different from a right-to-control case.

That distinction is doctrinally significant because it preserves a large space for fraud prosecutions involving lies that affect purchasing decisions. After *Ciminelli*, defense lawyers have argued that many fraud cases are really about information asymmetry, not property. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is a reminder that many such cases will survive if the government can allege—and later prove—that the misstatements were the means by which victims were induced to transfer money or property.

The panel’s point about economic harm is also important. It relies on *Kousisis* for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not require a showing that the victim was left net worse off. That matters in markets, where defendants often claim that buyers received something of value in return. The court rejects the notion that a fraudulent scheme is excused because the victim got securities that had some market value. In the fraud framework the panel adopts, the key question is not whether the victim got something, but whether the money or property transfer was procured by deceit.

## The downstream effects

The likely immediate effect is to make dismissal harder in securities-fraud cases that are pleaded as classic deception-to-trade schemes. If the indictment alleges that defendants lied to induce others to buy or sell securities, the Fifth Circuit’s opinion gives the government a clear path past a “no property” challenge.

But the broader effect may be doctrinal. This decision reinforces a trend in which § 1348 operates as a flexible anti-fraud backstop rather than a tightly bounded securities-specific offense. That has consequences for both prosecutorial discretion and defense strategy. Prosecutors will likely feel emboldened to charge social-media driven manipulation, influencer fraud, and other modern market schemes under § 1348 without needing to prove a complicated, standalone theory of market integrity injury. Defendants, meanwhile, will have less room to argue that their conduct is merely deceptive chatter about future prices rather than a property fraud.

There is, however, a limiting principle buried in the opinion. The court repeatedly emphasizes that the indictment alleged more than deceptive information flow; it alleged induced purchases and the transfer of money. That means the opinion does not authorize a prosecution based solely on bad advice, puffery, or optimistic predictions about securities prices. The government still needs the basic fraud elements: material misrepresentation, deceit, and an object of obtaining money or property. The opinion is broad, but not boundless.

## A final thought

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is best read as part of the post-*Ciminelli* repair job on federal fraud law. The Supreme Court narrowed one overexpansive theory and then, in *Kousisis*, made clear that traditional fraud doctrine still reaches schemes that use lies to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit has now applied that framework to § 1348, and in doing so it has made securities fraud look even more like its mail-and-wire-fraud relatives.

That may be the right answer as a matter of doctrine and enforcement. But it also means § 1348 will continue to function less like a specialized securities provision and more like a general anti-fraud statute with a securities label. For better or worse, that is where the law appears to be headed.



# Finfluencers, Market Manipulation, and Criminal Law: The Emerging Case Against Online Trading Hype

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision to reinstate the securities-fraud indictment against a group of social-media stock promoters is notable for more than just the alleged size of the scheme — the government says the defendants profited $114 million. The opinion matters because it places a legal marker on a rapidly growing category of enforcement: cases against so-called “finfluencers” who cultivate large online followings, tout securities, and allegedly hide their own trading positions while encouraging retail investors to buy.

In practical terms, the court has made it easier for prosecutors to plead these cases as classic fraud cases rather than as mere disputes over misleading commentary or market hype. That distinction is crucial. A lot of modern financial speech lives in a gray zone between opinion, salesmanship, and deception. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion says that when the government alleges more than a failure to disclose valuable information — when it alleges a plan to induce followers to part with money through falsehoods about the promoter’s own trades and the securities’ prospects — the fraud statutes can reach it.

## The court’s core move: this is not just a “right to control” case

The district court had dismissed the indictment on the theory that it did not allege a traditional property fraud. That framing reflected an important recent Supreme Court boundary: in *Ciminelli v. United States*, the Court rejected the idea that depriving a victim of “valuable economic information” alone is enough to constitute fraud. In other words, the government cannot turn every misleading business practice into a federal fraud case merely by saying the victim lost the ability to make a fully informed decision.

But the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment here alleged something more concrete. According to the indictment, the defendants bought securities, pumped them on social media with false and misleading claims, induced followers to purchase, and then dumped their own holdings at a profit. That, the court held, is not just a deprivation of information; it is a scheme to induce people to hand over money to buy securities.

That distinction tracks the Supreme Court’s later decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, which the Fifth Circuit relied on heavily. *Kousisis* makes clear that the fraud statutes can cover a fraudulent-inducement theory: a defendant uses lies to get the victim to part with money or property, even if the victim receives something of value in return and even if the defendant’s objective is profit rather than causing a net loss. The Fifth Circuit took that principle and applied it to the pump-and-dump context. The result is a relatively clean doctrinal answer: the indictment alleges a scheme to obtain money by deception, which is enough.

## Why the intent-to-defraud analysis is especially important

The defendants argued that the indictment failed because it alleged an intent to profit, not an intent to harm. That argument has a certain intuitive appeal in the online-trading context. Many influencers genuinely think they are making bullish calls, and many securities promotions are accompanied by some mixture of optimism, self-interest, and theater. But the court treated “profit motive” and “fraudulent intent” as analytically distinct.

That is the right move doctrinally. Fraud law has never required a defendant to act out of malice or to seek to leave the victim worse off in a net sense. What matters is whether the defendant intended to deceive and intended to obtain money or property through that deception. The Fifth Circuit emphasized that the indictment alleges the defendants posted false and misleading information so that others would buy the securities. That is enough at the pleading stage to allege an intent to defraud.

The opinion is also careful not to collapse all misleading market commentary into criminal conduct. The court does not say that every undisclosed position, every optimistic statement, or every self-interested stock tip is fraud. What it says is that where the government alleges that a promoter falsely induced followers to buy securities and then secretly sold into the very demand he helped create, the indictment plausibly alleges a fraudulent scheme.

## The pleading burden just got easier for prosecutors

One of the most important downstream effects of the decision is procedural. The Fifth Circuit is reviewing only the sufficiency of the indictment, not the truth of the government’s allegations. That may sound obvious, but it matters a lot in cases like this. Securities-fraud prosecutions often turn on the details of what was said, what was omitted, what the defendant knew, and how the audience understood the promotion. Defendants regularly try to knock cases out early by arguing that the allegations sound more like aggressive sales tactics than criminal deception.

This opinion makes that route harder in the online-promotion setting. If prosecutors plead that the defendant lied about his own trading position, misstated the prospects of the stock, induced followers to buy, and profited by selling into the resulting demand, the indictment will likely survive a motion to dismiss. The court’s message is that these allegations describe a familiar fraud theory, not a novel attempt to criminalize information asymmetry.

That does not mean the government wins on the merits. But it does mean that defendants in future cases may have to fight these cases with evidence, not just with theory.

## The broader enforcement campaign against online stock hype

This opinion fits into a larger regulatory and enforcement response to social-media-driven market manipulation. Retail trading, meme stocks, and influencer-driven stock promotion have created new opportunities for both legitimate commentary and deceptive promotion. The enforcement challenge is that the same platforms that reward candor and charisma also reward hype, concealment, and velocity.

The government’s theory in cases like this is straightforward: if an online personality leverages trust and audience reach to create artificial demand while hiding his own position, the conduct is not merely puffery. It is a modern version of a classic pump-and-dump. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion gives that theory a cleaner doctrinal path by anchoring it in ordinary fraud principles: deception, inducement, and obtaining money or property.

That said, the opinion’s logic will likely be watched closely by defense lawyers and First Amendment skeptics. The danger in any fraud case involving speech is overbreadth. Financial commentary often involves exaggeration, selective disclosure, and self-interested advocacy. The line between “I love this stock” and “I am secretly dumping on my followers” is everything. The court did not erase that line; it simply held that the indictment, as pleaded, crosses it.

## My read: the court’s reasoning is strong, but the practical line remains tricky

Doctrinally, the Fifth Circuit’s analysis is persuasive. *Ciminelli* foreclosed the idea that mere informational deprivation is enough. *Kousisis* clarified that fraudulent inducement to part with money or property is still fraud even if the victim gets something back and even if the defendant’s aim is profit rather than impoverishment. Under those premises, an indictment alleging a social-media pump-and-dump scheme should survive.

The harder question is how far this logic will travel. The opinion is strongest where the defendant allegedly misrepresents concrete facts: his own position, his own intent, or the mechanics of the promotion. It is less clear how it applies when the government’s theory rests on more diffuse misstatements about a company’s prospects, a stock’s “fair value,” or the speaker’s enthusiasm. Those cases will invite fights over materiality, reliance, intent, and whether the alleged falsehoods are statements of fact or opinion.

The practical takeaway is that online financial influencers are now squarely in the crosshairs of traditional securities-fraud law. The court has signaled that the medium does not change the analysis: if the scheme is to use falsehoods to get followers to buy and then profit from the resulting market movement, prosecutors have a viable fraud case.

## What comes next

Expect this decision to be cited in future prosecutions involving influencers, newsletter writers, chat-room promoters, and other market personalities who blur the line between commentary and inducement. It will likely embolden the government to plead pump-and-dump cases more aggressively, especially where there is evidence of undisclosed trading positions, coordinated posting, or rapid selling after promotion.

At the same time, the case may push the defense bar to sharpen a different line of argument: not that fraud law can never reach online trading promotion, but that the government must prove actual deceit with precision. The factual distinctions will matter. Was the speaker’s claim merely optimistic, or was it false? Was the position disclosure incomplete, or affirmatively misleading? Was the audience misled into buying, or simply persuaded by exuberant analysis? Those questions will likely define the next phase of litigation.

For now, the Fifth Circuit has done what appellate courts often do in this space: it has translated a new technological phenomenon into old legal categories. The result is a reminder that “finfluencer” may be a modern label, but securities fraud is still securities fraud.



# An Indictment-Friendly Opinion: What the Fifth Circuit Signals About Pretrial Challenges to Fraud Charges

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision to revive the securities-fraud indictment against eight defendants accused of running a social-media “pump and dump” scheme is notable for two reasons at once. Substantively, it follows the Supreme Court’s recent fraud cases in rejecting an overly cramped reading of property and harm. But procedurally, the opinion is just as important: it reinforces that a motion to dismiss an indictment is not the place to litigate the factual merits of a fraud case when the government has pleaded the statutory elements.

That procedural point has consequences well beyond this prosecution. Fraud charges often turn on close questions about inducement, materiality, victim injury, and the defendant’s object. Defense lawyers routinely try to reframe those issues as threshold defects in the indictment. The Fifth Circuit’s answer here is that, so long as the indictment tracks the elements and alleges facts that, if true, amount to fraud, the case should go forward. In other words, the court is signaling a strong reluctance to resolve the theory-of-the-case fight before trial.

## The posture drives the analysis

The opinion begins where it should: with the limited nature of indictment review. Courts must take the indictment’s allegations as true and ask only whether an offense is stated. The panel quotes longstanding Fifth Circuit law requiring an indictment to enumerate each prima facie element, fairly inform the defendant of the charge, and protect against double jeopardy. That may sound formal, but it does real work here.

The defendants’ basic argument was that the indictment, at bottom, alleged only that they deprived followers of “valuable economic information,” which after *Ciminelli* is not enough. The district court accepted that framing and dismissed the charges. The Fifth Circuit rejected that approach, emphasizing that the indictment did not merely allege information deprivation. It alleged that the defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money or property, while the defendants secretly sold into the inflated price.

That distinction matters because a dismissal motion should not turn into a mini-trial about how to characterize the scheme. The government’s version is, of course, contested. But at the pleading stage, the question is not whether the defendants can ultimately show they were engaged in aggressive but lawful market promotion, or whether purchasers got something of value in return. The question is whether the indictment alleges conduct that, if proven, fits the statutory definitions. The Fifth Circuit says yes.

## The opinion reads *Kousisis* as a pleading case as much as a substantive one

The most important doctrinal move in the opinion is its heavy reliance on *Kousisis v. United States*. The Supreme Court there held that fraud liability does not require proof of net economic loss, and that a defendant can commit fraud by using a material misstatement to obtain money or property, even if the victim receives something of value in return. The Fifth Circuit uses that logic to dispose of the defendants’ two central objections.

First, on the “property” point, the panel draws a line between the invalid right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli* and a fraudulent-inducement theory. In the court’s view, the indictment alleges the latter: the defendants allegedly tricked followers into buying securities, causing them to part with money. That is enough. The court is careful to say that *Ciminelli* would bar a theory based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, but that is not what the indictment charges.

Second, on intent, the defendants argued that they sought only to enrich themselves, not to harm anyone. The court responds that *Kousisis* forecloses any rule requiring proof that the defendant intended the victim to suffer a net loss. What matters is whether the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by false pretenses. If the object of the scheme was to induce purchases through deception, then the intent-to-defraud element is adequately alleged even if the defendant hoped to profit from later trading rather than to leave victims worse off in some simple balance-sheet sense.

That is an important point because it reflects a broader trend in fraud doctrine: courts are increasingly reluctant to let defendants avoid liability by recasting a plainly deceptive scheme as one aimed only at self-enrichment. Fraud law has never required the government to prove sadism, and the Fifth Circuit rightly refuses to read such a requirement into the indictment stage.

## The court’s procedural message is the real headline

What makes this opinion especially interesting is that the panel could have written a narrower, more technical decision about securities fraud. Instead, it chose language that reinforces a general principle: close questions about materiality, inducement, and injury usually belong to the factfinder, not to pretrial dismissal.

That matters in fraud cases because the defense often argues that the alleged misrepresentation was immaterial, that investors were sophisticated, that the market was volatile, or that the victims got what they paid for. Those are serious defenses, but they are usually fact-intensive defenses. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion suggests that, where the indictment alleges false statements, inducement to purchase, and defendant profit from the resulting price movement, the court should not resolve those disputes by recharacterizing the case at the pleading stage.

In that sense, the opinion is not just pro-government. It is pro-jury. It treats the indictment as a charging document, not a preview of the verdict.

## Downstream effects: more room for securities-fraud prosecutions, less room for early dismissals

The immediate consequence is obvious: the government gets to proceed with a high-profile crypto-and-social-media-adjacent securities-fraud case that a district court had thrown out. But the broader effect is doctrinal.

First, the opinion gives prosecutors additional support for charging pump-and-dump cases under fraud statutes even when the alleged deceit is embedded in social-media influence rather than in a traditional brokerage or issuer setting. The court’s reasoning does not depend on any special feature of the internet, but it does make clear that online influencers are not insulated from fraud liability simply because their followers were chasing returns rather than buying a physical good.

Second, the decision narrows the practical force of defense motions that try to use *Ciminelli* as a universal anti-fraud lever. The Fifth Circuit reads *Ciminelli* as targeting a specific theory—the deprivation of economic information as property—not as a broad invitation to dismiss any case in which the victim arguably received some value or retained some market opportunity. *Kousisis* then fills in the other side of the equation by rejecting a net-loss requirement. Put together, those cases leave defendants with less room to argue that a scheme is outside fraud law merely because it is financially complex or because the victim’s loss is not easily reduced to a classic out-of-pocket loss.

Third, the opinion may make district courts more cautious about dismissing indictments in securities and wire fraud cases based on how they characterize the transaction. If the indictment alleges a material falsehood, inducement to part with money, and defendant gain from the scheme, that may be enough even if the defense can offer a plausible alternative narrative.

## A measured but important signal

The Fifth Circuit did not say the government will win. It said the government should get to prove its case. That is a modest holding in one sense, but an important one in another. Fraud doctrine often swings on the temptation to collapse the merits into the pleadings. This opinion resists that temptation.

That matters because the line between aggressive market talk and fraudulent inducement can be difficult to police in modern securities cases, especially where influencers, social media, and rapid trading intersect. The court’s approach suggests that those hard lines should be drawn on a fuller record, not by narrowing the indictment into something less than what the grand jury alleged.

For defendants, that means fewer opportunities to knock out fraud charges early by rebranding the government’s theory as mere information deprivation. For prosecutors, it means a clearer path to surviving dismissal when they plead a traditional fraud theory: false statements, inducement to part with money, and intent to obtain that money through deception.

That is the real lesson of the case. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is about securities fraud, but it is also about the institutional role of the indictment. At least in this circuit, a well-pleaded fraud charge is likely to live long enough to be tested where it usually should be tested: at trial, not on a motion to dismiss.
