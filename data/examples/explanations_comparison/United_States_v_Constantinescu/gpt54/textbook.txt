Case Brief: United_States_v_Constantinescu

### Case Caption and Citation

This appeal was decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The case was styled *United States of America v. Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight*. It came to the Fifth Circuit from the United States District Court for the Southern District of Texas and arose from the district court’s dismissal of a superseding indictment charging a social-media-driven “pump and dump” securities-fraud scheme. The indictment charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud, 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and substantive securities fraud, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Knight pleaded guilty, while the remaining defendants were appellees. The panel consisted of Circuit Judges Higginson, Willett, and Engelhardt, and Judge Engelhardt authored the opinion.

The Fifth Circuit reviewed de novo whether the indictment sufficiently alleged an offense, with the central question being whether the superseding indictment adequately alleged a scheme and intent to defraud in light of *Ciminelli* and, after the district court’s ruling, the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis*. The court reversed the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

### Facts

The government alleged that Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight participated in a “pump and dump” securities-fraud scheme. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants were prominent social-media personalities with large followings across various platforms. They held themselves out as skilled stock traders and regularly posted about their trading activity. The indictment further alleged that the defendants misrepresented their trading positions and the future prospects or price potential of certain securities in order to influence their followers’ investment decisions.

The alleged scheme followed a classic pump-and-dump pattern. The government claimed that the defendants first purchased a security, then “pumped” up its price by posting false and misleading information about the security on social media so that their followers would buy it and artificially increase its market price, and then “dumped” the stock by secretly selling their own holdings at a profit. The indictment alleged that the defendants profited by approximately $114 million from the scheme.

The superseding indictment charged the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and multiple substantive counts of securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Knight pleaded guilty, while the remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion, reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest and instead alleged only a deprivation of potentially valuable economic information. The government appealed. On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo and was required to accept the indictment’s allegations as true.

## Issue(s)

Whether the superseding indictment sufficiently stated an offense under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 by alleging that the defendants engaged in a “pump and dump” scheme in which they used material misrepresentations on social media to induce followers to purchase securities and thereby part with money, or whether the indictment impermissibly alleged only that the defendants deprived victims of valuable economic information, which is not itself a traditional property interest. Relatedly, the court considered whether, in light of *Ciminelli v. United States*, the indictment rested on a prohibited right-to-control theory or instead properly alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory aimed at obtaining money or property.

The Fifth Circuit also addressed whether the indictment adequately alleged an “intent to defraud” where the defendants allegedly sought to profit from the scheme, even if the victims may have received securities of market value and the indictment did not allege that the defendants intended to cause net pecuniary loss. More broadly, the court examined whether a fraud indictment must plead an intent to inflict traditional economic harm on the victims, or whether it is enough to allege that obtaining the victims’ money or property by deceit was an object of the scheme.

### Holding

The Fifth Circuit held that the superseding indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud under the securities-fraud statute. The indictment alleged that defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby causing them to part with money or property; accordingly, it charged a valid fraudulent-inducement theory rather than the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). The court further held that the indictment adequately alleged intent to defraud because it charged that defendants acted to obtain money through deception, and the fraud statutes do not require the government to allege that defendants intended to leave victims economically worse off or to cause a net pecuniary loss.

Accordingly, the court held that the indictment stated offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and related provisions, reversed the district court’s dismissal, and remanded for further proceedings.

### Reasoning

The Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo and began with the familiar rule that, at the pleading stage, a court must accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true and ask only whether those allegations state an offense. An indictment is sufficient if it alleges each material element of the offense, fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and provides a basis for a double-jeopardy defense in any later prosecution. Applying that standard, the court looked to the elements of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and explained that, although there is limited Fifth Circuit precedent construing the statute, § 1348 borrows key concepts from the mail- and wire-fraud statutes. Accordingly, those cases supply the framework for determining whether the indictment alleges a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” Under Fifth Circuit precedent, a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive another in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the person deceived, and intent to defraud requires an intent to deceive and to cause some harm from the deceit.

The court then addressed the defendants’ primary argument: that the indictment failed because it did not allege deprivation of a traditional property interest, but instead alleged only that they deprived followers of potentially valuable economic information. The court rejected that characterization by distinguishing the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). In Ciminelli, the Court held that the federal fraud statutes do not reach a “right-to-control” theory, under which the government attempts to treat the deprivation of information needed to make discretionary economic decisions as the relevant property interest. The Fifth Circuit emphasized Ciminelli’s central point that the fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests and that the “right to valuable economic information” is not itself such an interest. But the court concluded that the indictment here did not rest on a right-to-control theory. Instead, it alleged that defendants used false and misleading social-media posts to induce followers to purchase securities—that is, to part with money or property. That allegation, the court explained, fits a fraudulent-inducement theory: a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a transaction requiring the transfer of money or property. The court further relied on Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), which clarified that fraudulent inducement is not merely a repackaging of the right-to-control theory and remains a valid property-fraud theory because it directly targets money or property. Because the indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced investors to buy securities, it properly alleged a scheme to defraud within the meaning of § 1348.

The court next turned to intent. The defendants argued that the indictment alleged only an intent to profit from the scheme, not an intent to harm victims, and therefore failed to allege intent to defraud. The court rejected that argument in light of Kousisis, which held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off or intended an actual net loss. Under Kousisis, it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property through materially false pretenses, even if the defendant also provided something of value in return. The Fifth Circuit read the indictment as alleging precisely that: defendants posted false and misleading statements about securities so that others would buy them, enabling defendants to profit when the price rose and they later sold. That allegation showed that defendants “had money in mind,” and the court treated the distinction between intending to profit and intending to harm as “a distinction without a difference” where the scheme’s object was to obtain investors’ money by deceit.

The court also rejected the defendants’ suggestion that the indictment was insufficient because the alleged victims may have received securities of market value in return for their purchases. Kousisis foreclosed that argument by making clear that fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return. Thus, the absence of a net pecuniary loss does not negate fraudulent intent or defeat the charge. The Fifth Circuit also relied on its own precedent, including United States v. Baker, to explain that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement under which the victim’s loss must perfectly match the defendant’s gain. What matters is that the defendant’s gain came from inducing the victim to part with money or property through deceit.

On that basis, the court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged both required elements: a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud. The allegations that defendants falsely represented their trading positions and the prospects of the securities, induced followers to buy, and then secretly sold their own holdings for a profit adequately charged a property-based fraud, not merely a scheme to manipulate information. Because the district court dismissed the indictment on the erroneous premise that the government had alleged only a deprivation of economic information rather than money or property, the Fifth Circuit held that dismissal was improper. The court therefore reversed and remanded for further proceedings.

### Rule(s) of Law

An indictment is sufficient when it alleges each material element of the charged offense, fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and provides protection against double jeopardy. On a motion to dismiss an indictment, the court accepts the indictment’s factual allegations as true and asks only whether the indictment states an offense. *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023); *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996).

Section 1348 securities fraud, like the mail- and wire-fraud statutes from which it borrows key concepts, requires both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. A “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive another in order to obtain something of value, such as money or property, and “intent to defraud” requires an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Because § 1348 is construed in light of mail- and wire-fraud doctrine, courts may look to cases interpreting those statutes when analyzing securities-fraud charges.

The federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not a freestanding right to accurate or valuable economic information. Under *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), a “right-to-control” theory based solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information cannot sustain a fraud conviction because that interest is not traditional property. By contrast, a fraudulent-inducement theory remains valid where the defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property. As clarified in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), fraudulent inducement is not a repackaging of the prohibited right-to-control theory because it targets the victim’s money or property directly.

The fraud statutes do not require the government to allege or prove that the defendant intended to cause, or actually caused, net pecuniary loss to the victim. It is enough that obtaining the victim’s money or property through deception was an object of the scheme. Accordingly, a defendant may act with intent to defraud by using materially false pretenses to induce victims to part with money, even if the victims receive something of value in return.

### Concurrence / Dissent

No judge wrote separately. The opinion was authored by Judge Engelhardt for a unanimous panel, and the decision reflects a straightforward panel disposition without any concurrence or dissent.

### Significance / Impact

This decision is a significant Fifth Circuit application of *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* in the securities-fraud context. It clarifies that a social-media-driven “pump and dump” scheme may be charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 when the indictment alleges that defendants used false or misleading statements to induce investors to part with money by purchasing securities. The court emphasized that the proper focus is not whether defendants sought to deprive victims of information alone, but whether money or property was the object of the deception. In doing so, the court rejected the argument that a securities-fraud prosecution becomes an impermissible right-to-control case whenever misinformation influences investment decisions.

The opinion is also important because it reinforces that § 1348 is interpreted using mail- and wire-fraud principles. Relying on Fifth Circuit precedent and the Supreme Court’s recent fraud decisions, the court treated the securities-fraud statute as encompassing traditional fraudulent-inducement theories, under which a defendant may be liable for tricking victims into transactions requiring them to surrender money or property. That framework matters for charging practice after *Ciminelli*: prosecutors must allege a concrete deprivation of money or property, not merely the withholding of economically useful information. Here, the indictment’s allegations that defendants posted false statements so that followers would buy securities, thereby enabling defendants to profit from the resulting price inflation, were enough to state an offense.

Doctrinally, the case helps draw the line between impermissible informational harm and actionable property fraud in modern market-manipulation cases. It makes clear that defendants cannot avoid fraud liability simply by characterizing a pump-and-dump scheme as one aimed only at influencing investors’ choices or extracting trading profits without intending net economic loss to victims. Consistent with *Kousisis*, the court held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that victims were left economically worse off; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain money or property by materially false pretenses. Practically, the decision supports continued use of § 1348 against securities manipulation conducted through social media and influencer-style trading platforms, while signaling to defense counsel that post-*Ciminelli* challenges will fail where the indictment alleges fraudulent inducement to purchase securities rather than mere deprivation of information.
