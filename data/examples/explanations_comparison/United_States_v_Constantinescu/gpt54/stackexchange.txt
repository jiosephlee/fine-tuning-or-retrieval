Law Stack Exchange Q&A: United_States_v_Constantinescu

### How could the government appeal the district court’s pretrial dismissal of a criminal indictment?
Question:
I’m confused about the procedural posture here. The district court dismissed the superseding indictment before trial, and then the United States appealed to the Fifth Circuit. In criminal cases I thought government appeals were limited because of double jeopardy concerns. What is the source of appellate jurisdiction for this kind of government appeal, and why doesn’t the Double Jeopardy Clause block it at this stage?
Answer:
The government’s appellate jurisdiction here comes from **18 U.S.C. § 3731**, which authorizes the United States to appeal from an order **“dismissing an indictment”** so long as the appeal is **not barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause**.

That is exactly what happened here: the district court dismissed the superseding indictment **before trial**, and the Fifth Circuit notes that the government **“timely appealed.”** The posture matters because **jeopardy had not yet attached**. In a jury trial, jeopardy ordinarily attaches when the jury is sworn; in a bench trial, when the first witness is sworn. A pretrial dismissal of the indictment therefore does **not** implicate the Double Jeopardy Clause in the way an acquittal after jeopardy has attached would.

So the basic logic is:

1. **§ 3731 permits government appeals** from pretrial dismissals of indictments.
2. **Double jeopardy does not block the appeal** because the case was dismissed **before trial**, so the defendants had not yet been placed in jeopardy.
3. If the government loses on appeal, the remedy is simply reinstatement of the indictment and further proceedings—not a second prosecution after acquittal.

Nothing in the opinion suggests the district court entered an acquittal or that trial had begun; the opinion treats the order as a straightforward **dismissal of the indictment**, which is the kind of order § 3731 is designed to make appealable by the government.

### Why does the Fifth Circuit review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo and assume all allegations are true?
Question:
The opinion says review is de novo and that the court must take the indictment’s allegations as true to decide whether an offense has been stated. I’m having trouble understanding why the appellate court does not look at whether the government can actually prove the pump-and-dump scheme. Is this just a Rule 12 pleading question, and how is that different from reviewing evidentiary sufficiency after a conviction?
Answer:
Because this appeal is about the **sufficiency of the indictment**, not the **sufficiency of the evidence**.

The Fifth Circuit says the standard explicitly:

> “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. … [T]he court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”  
> *United States v. Fontenot*, quoted in Part II.

That is a **Rule 12 / pleading** inquiry. The question at this stage is only whether the grand jury charging document alleges all the elements of the offense with enough specificity to:

1. state an offense,
2. inform the defendant of the charge, and
3. protect against double jeopardy.

The court restates that standard right away:

> “An indictment must (1) enumerate each prima facie element … (2) fairly inform the defendant … and (3) provide … a double jeopardy defense.”  
> “In sum, to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense.”

So the Fifth Circuit is **not** deciding whether the government can actually prove the pump-and-dump scheme at trial. It is deciding only whether the indictment, **assuming its factual allegations are true**, alleges conduct that would violate § 1348.

That is why the court focuses on the allegations that defendants:

- “pump” the price by posting false and misleading information,
- induce followers to buy securities,
- and then “dump” by selling for profit.

Those allegations, if true, state securities fraud. Whether the government can prove them is a **trial issue**.

### How that differs from evidentiary sufficiency after conviction

After a conviction, the question is different: the court asks whether the **evidence** was sufficient for a rational jury to find the defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. At that stage, the court does **not** assume the government’s allegations are true; it reviews the trial record.

So the distinction is:

- **Indictment sufficiency:** “Did the grand jury charge a crime on the face of the indictment?”
- **Evidentiary sufficiency:** “Did the government prove that crime at trial?”

### Why the court says it must assume the allegations are true

That is built into the nature of a charging instrument. If appellate courts re-litigated proof at the indictment stage, they would effectively convert a pretrial motion to dismiss into a mini-trial on the merits. The Fifth Circuit does not do that here. It asks only whether the indictment alleges a legally cognizable fraud scheme and intent to defraud. In Part III, the court answers yes, because the indictment alleges more than deprivation of “valuable economic information”; it alleges a scheme to obtain victims’ money by fraudulent inducement.

So yes: this is fundamentally a **Rule 12 pleading question**, and it is different from post-trial review of evidentiary sufficiency. The court is testing the **legal adequacy of the charge**, not the **truth of the charge**.

### Why is 'standing' not really discussed in a criminal fraud case like this?
Question:
One thing that confuses me is that the opinion focuses on whether the indictment alleges deprivation of 'money or property,' but it never really talks about standing. If the alleged victims are the defendants’ social-media followers, do those followers need some kind of standing or legally recognized injury for the prosecution to go forward? Or is 'standing' basically the wrong concept in a criminal case, with the real question being whether the statute protects the kind of property interest the government says was targeted?
Answer:
No—“standing” is basically the wrong concept here.

In a **criminal prosecution**, the question is not whether the alleged victims have Article III standing in the way a civil plaintiff would. The prosecution is brought by the **United States**, and the issue on appeal is whether the **indictment alleges the elements of the offense**. That is exactly how the Fifth Circuit frames the case:

- “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated” (Part II)
- an indictment must allege each “material element” of the offense (Part II)

So the real question is not whether the followers had standing, but whether the alleged conduct falls within the **fraud statute’s protected interests**—here, whether the indictment alleges a scheme to obtain **money or property** through deception.

That is why the opinion spends its time on **Ciminelli** and **Kousisis**, not standing. The court asks whether the indictment alleges:
1. a **scheme to defraud**, and
2. an **intent to defraud**,

and whether the object of the scheme was to obtain **money or property**. See Part III, especially:

- “the indictment alleges defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property”
- “Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here ‘protects money and property’”
- “the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm”

So if the “victims” are social-media followers, they do **not** need some separate standing analysis for the indictment to proceed. What matters is whether the government has alleged a fraud on a legally protected property interest. The Fifth Circuit says it has.

In short: **standing is a civil-law lens; this is an elements-of-the-offense question.**

### Why does the court interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348 by borrowing mail and wire fraud doctrine?
Question:
The opinion says there is scant Fifth Circuit caselaw on the securities fraud statute, so courts rely on mail and wire fraud cases because § 1348 'borrows key concepts' from those statutes. I’m not sure how strong that move is as a matter of statutory interpretation. Is the court saying the text is materially similar enough that terms like 'scheme to defraud' and 'intent to defraud' carry over automatically, or is this more of an analogy? Why is it legitimate to import those doctrines instead of reading § 1348 more independently?
Answer:
The court is doing more than making a loose analogy, but less than saying § 1348 is a carbon copy of the mail- and wire-fraud statutes.

The key move is that § 1348 uses the same core fraud language — especially “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” — and the Fifth Circuit treats those phrases as terms that have already been given content in the federal fraud cases. That is why the opinion says § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that “courts have given the terms similar treatment.” The court is not saying the statutes are identical in every respect; it is saying that, because Congress chose familiar fraud terminology, prior fraud doctrine is the best guide to what those terms mean here.

You can see that in the opinion’s doctrinal setup:

- It states that, although there is “scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit,” § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.”  
- On that basis, it uses Fifth Circuit wire/mail fraud precedents to define the elements: a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud.”  
- It then quotes those cases for the operative standards: a scheme to defraud requires a “material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value,” and intent to defraud requires “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”

So the interpretive logic is basically this: **same words, same legal meaning, absent a reason to think Congress meant otherwise.** That is a standard statutory move, not just policy-driven analogy.

Why is that legitimate here?

1. **Textual overlap.** Section 1348 uses fraud terminology that has an established meaning in federal criminal law. When Congress adopts familiar words, courts often read them against the background of existing doctrine.

2. **Structural similarity.** The opinion emphasizes that § 1348 is a fraud statute, and fraud doctrine naturally centers on deceit to obtain money or property. That makes mail/wire fraud precedent especially relevant.

3. **Fifth Circuit precedent.** The court cites *Greenlaw* for the proposition that § 1348 “borrows key concepts” from mail and wire fraud and that courts therefore treat the statutes similarly. So this is not the panel inventing the approach; it is following circuit authority.

4. **Supreme Court guidance.** The opinion also relies on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to confirm the boundaries of fraud doctrine. That matters because it shows the court is not importing mail/wire fraud law indiscriminately; it is using the Supreme Court’s modern fraud cases to decide whether the indictment alleges deprivation of money or property, rather than merely valuable information.

So, to answer your last point directly: the court is **not** reading § 1348 “independently” in the sense of creating a bespoke securities-fraud doctrine detached from fraud law generally. It is reading § 1348 as a fraud statute whose key statutory terms carry the settled meaning developed in mail and wire fraud cases, unless the securities-fraud context or the statutory text indicates otherwise.

That is why the opinion can move from § 1348 to mail/wire fraud doctrine without treating it as a stretch. It is an interpretive assumption grounded in shared statutory language, existing Fifth Circuit precedent, and the Supreme Court’s fraud decisions.

### Why did the court say this indictment alleges deprivation of money or property rather than just a loss of valuable economic information?
Question:
The district court seemed to think the defendants only deprived followers of truthful trading information, which sounds like the kind of 'right to control' theory rejected in Ciminelli. But the Fifth Circuit says the indictment goes further because it alleges followers were induced to buy securities and thus parted with money. I’m struggling with the line here. If the misrepresentation affects the victim’s decision-making, why isn’t that still just a dressed-up right-to-control theory? What exactly made this a valid fraudulent-inducement theory instead?
Answer:
The key distinction is **what the fraud was aimed at obtaining**.

Under **Ciminelli**, the government cannot say a defendant committed fraud merely by depriving the victim of **valuable economic information** needed to make a better decision. That is the rejected **“right-to-control” theory**: the supposed property interest is just the victim’s interest in making an informed choice. As the Fifth Circuit summarizes it, that theory treats the victim’s property as the “right to control [his] assets,” but the Supreme Court held that the “right to valuable economic information” is **not** a traditional property interest.

What saves this indictment is that it alleges something more concrete:

- defendants **posted false and misleading information**;
- **so that other investors were induced to purchase the security**; and
- by doing so, the followers **parted with their money**.

That is why the Fifth Circuit says this is a **fraudulent-inducement theory**, not a right-to-control theory. The opinion relies on **Kousisis**, which explains that fraudulent inducement is when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” The fraud is complete when the victim is induced to **transfer money or property under false pretenses**.

So the line is:

- **Not enough:** “We lied, and that made people worse decision-makers.”
- **Enough:** “We lied in order to get people to buy securities and hand over money.”

The court says the indictment falls into the second category. It alleges that the misrepresentations were not just about information quality; they were the means by which defendants caused followers to **purchase securities**, which is an allegation of deprivation of **money/property**, not merely loss of information.

That is also why the court rejects the district court’s framing. The followers were not simply denied truthful market information in the abstract. According to the indictment, they were **induced to spend money** on securities because of the false posts. That is a classic fraudulent-inducement theory, and under **Kousisis** it is not barred by **Ciminelli**.

In short: **decision distortion alone is not enough; induced transfer of money or property is.**

### How does Kousisis let the court find 'intent to defraud' when the defendants allegedly meant to profit, not to cause net loss?
Question:
The opinion repeats the Fifth Circuit formulation that intent to defraud means intent to deceive and intent to cause some harm from the deceit. But then it says, relying on Kousisis, that the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to seek net pecuniary loss to the victim. I’m having trouble reconciling those ideas. If the followers received securities in exchange for their money, what is the 'harm' the court thinks is enough? Did Kousisis effectively redefine the harm requirement, or is the court saying inducement to part with money under false pretenses is itself the harm?
Answer:
The court is **not** saying that “intent to defraud” now means only “intent to profit.” It is saying something narrower: **the government does not have to prove that the defendants meant to leave the victims economically worse off on a net basis**.

That is the key move from **Kousisis**.

### 1) The Fifth Circuit still uses the traditional intent-to-defraud formulation
The opinion repeats that intent to defraud requires:

> “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”  
> (*slip op.* at III, quoting *Evans*)

So the deceit still has to be coupled with harm.

### 2) But after **Kousisis**, “harm” does not have to mean net pecuniary loss
The court then explains that **Kousisis** rejected the argument that fraud liability disappears unless the defendant intended to hurt the victim’s bottom line:

> the fraud statutes “do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss”  
> and a defendant can be liable even if he “provides something . . . of equal value in return.”  
> (*slip op.* at III.B, quoting *Kousisis*)

So the “harm” requirement is satisfied when the defendant **fraudulently induces the victim to part with money or property**. The harm is the **loss of the victim’s money under materially false pretenses**, not necessarily a final net loss after offsetting value received.

### 3) That is why the court says the followers’ receipt of securities does not defeat fraud
The opinion expressly rejects the idea that getting securities in exchange means no fraud:

> “Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants’ followers’ receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges.”  
> (*slip op.* at III.B)

And even more directly:

> “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.”  
> (*slip op.* at III.B, quoting *Kousisis*)

So the court’s theory of harm is: **the victims were induced to hand over money in reliance on false statements about the securities and defendants’ trading positions**.

### 4) So did **Kousisis** redefine the harm requirement?
Not exactly. It **clarified what counts as harm in a fraudulent-inducement case**.

Under this opinion, the harm element is not “the victim ended up worse off in a net accounting sense.” Instead, it is enough that the defendant **deceived the victim into parting with money or property**. That is why the court says the defendants’ claim that they “intended to profit—not harm” is “a distinction without a difference”:

> “As the indictment alleges, defendants’ object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities.”  
> (*slip op.* at III.B)

### Bottom line
The court is **not** collapsing intent to defraud into mere profit motive. Rather, it is treating **inducing the victim to transfer money by false pretenses** as the relevant harm. In other words: **the fraud lies in getting the money through deception, even if the victim receives something in return and even if the defendant’s goal was to make a profit rather than to impose a net loss.**

### Why is the fraud 'complete' once the victim is induced to part with money, even if the victim receives something of equal market value?
Question:
The opinion quotes Kousisis for the proposition that fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property by materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. That feels counterintuitive to me in the pump-and-dump setting. If a follower buys a stock at the market price and gets the shares they paid for, why is that enough property deprivation for federal fraud? Is the idea that the law protects the victim’s control over the payment itself, so the fraudulent inducement taints the transfer even if there is nominally equivalent value on the other side?
Answer:
Yes — that is essentially the idea, and the opinion makes that point pretty explicitly.

The key move is that federal fraud law does **not** require the victim to end up in a net worse economic position. What it requires is that the defendant, by materially false pretenses, **induces the victim to part with money or property**. Once that happens, the fraud is “complete,” even if the victim receives something of roughly equal market value in return.

### How the Fifth Circuit frames it

The court relies on *Kousisis* for the proposition that a defendant commits fraud when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The opinion then quotes *Kousisis* again for the specific point you are asking about:

> “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”  
> — slip op. at IV.B (quoting *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1394 n.5)

So the focus is not on whether the victim got something of equal market value; it is on whether the defendant used deception to cause the victim to **transfer control of money or property**.

### Why market value on the other side does not defeat fraud

The court answers this in the paragraph beginning “Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm…”. It says that the followers’ receipt of market-value securities “does not obviate the fraud charges,” because under *Kousisis* a defendant may be liable even when he “provides something … of equal value in return.”

That is the important doctrinal point: federal fraud statutes do **not** impose a “mirror image” rule requiring the victim’s loss to equal the defendant’s gain, or requiring the victim to be worse off on the balance sheet. The Fifth Circuit says as much by citing *United States v. Baker* and calling the defendant’s profit motive and the victim’s lack of net loss “a distinction without a difference.”

### Applying that to pump-and-dump

In a pump-and-dump, the fraud is not merely that the promoter gives followers “bad information.” The indictment alleges that defendants:

1. falsely touted the securities,
2. induced followers to buy,
3. thereby caused followers to part with money,
4. and then sold their own holdings for profit.

That is enough for fraud because the property deprivation is the **money paid out in reliance on the lie**. The fact that the follower received shares in exchange does not erase the initial fraudulent obtaining of money.

### Bottom line

So your intuition about “control over the payment itself” is basically right. The law protects the victim’s **money/property interest in the transaction induced by fraud**, not just the ultimate net economic outcome. Under the opinion’s reading of *Kousisis*, once the defendant has induced the victim to part with money by materially false pretenses, the fraud is complete—even if the victim received something that, on paper, had equal market value.

### Does the indictment need to identify specific victims or specific trades to adequately allege securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion says an indictment is sufficient if it alleges each material element, fairly informs the defendant, and protects against double jeopardy. Here the alleged scheme involved social-media followers broadly, not necessarily a small set of named victims. I’m confused about how specific the indictment has to be. Does the government need to identify which followers bought which securities based on which posts, or is it enough to allege the general pump-and-dump scheme and the defendants’ use of misleading posts to induce purchases?
Answer:
No. On this opinion’s reasoning, the indictment did **not** need to identify each individual follower, each purchase, or each trade.

The Fifth Circuit applies the ordinary indictment-sufficiency standard: an indictment is sufficient if it **(1) alleges each material element of the offense, (2) fairly informs the defendant of the charge, and (3) protects against double jeopardy**. The court quotes this rule from *Gaytan* and *Guzman-Ocampo* in Part II.

Applying that standard here, the court held that the superseding indictment was enough because it alleged the **scheme** and the **means** of the fraud:

- defendants had large social-media followings;
- they made false and misleading posts about securities and their own trading positions;
- those posts were used “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security”; and
- defendants then dumped the securities for a profit.

See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14, discussed in Parts I, III(A), and III(B).

That is enough because securities fraud under § 1348 is charged at the level of a **scheme to defraud** and **intent to defraud**, not at the level of proving every victim transaction in the indictment itself. The court emphasized that the indictment “properly alleg[es] defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money” by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities. It did **not** require the government to plead a fully itemized list of victims or trades.

So the short answer is: **the government need not identify which follower bought which security based on which post in order to state an offense**. It is enough, at the indictment stage, to allege the general pump-and-dump scheme, the false posts, the inducement of purchases, and the defendants’ profit motive.

One important caveat: the indictment must still be specific enough to give notice and avoid double jeopardy. But on this opinion’s facts, the broad description of the scheme satisfied that requirement.

### If the substantive § 1348 counts are adequately alleged, does that automatically save the § 1349 conspiracy count too?
Question:
The defendants were charged both with substantive securities fraud and conspiracy to commit securities fraud. The Fifth Circuit mainly analyzes whether the indictment alleges a scheme and intent to defraud under § 1348. I’m not sure how that analysis maps onto the conspiracy charge under § 1349. Does the government also have to allege an agreement with some specificity beyond the underlying fraudulent conduct, and does § 1349 require an overt act like the general conspiracy statute does?
Answer:
Not automatically in the abstract — but in *this* opinion, yes, the § 1349 count is effectively saved because the same factual allegations that support the § 1348 counts also describe a coordinated agreement to commit securities fraud.

The key point is that the Fifth Circuit does **not** separately analyze the conspiracy count in isolation. It frames the case as an indictment for both:

- “**conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and multiple counts of securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2**” (Part I), and
- then holds that “**the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud**” (Intro; Part IV).

That matters because the court’s sufficiency analysis is built around the fraudulent scheme alleged in the indictment: defendants allegedly worked together to “pump” the stock with false posts and then “dump” it for profit. Those allegations are not just enough for substantive fraud; they also naturally describe a **concerted plan** among multiple actors. So on the opinion’s own terms, the conspiracy allegation is supported by the same coordinated conduct.

On your specific doctrinal questions:

### 1) Does § 1349 require something beyond the underlying fraud allegations?
The opinion does **not** require any extra, separate pleading theory for the conspiracy count. It does not say the government must allege a special, more detailed agreement apart from the coordinated fraudulent conduct already described in the indictment. Instead, the court treats the alleged multi-defendant pump-and-dump scheme as sufficient factual support for the charges as a whole.

### 2) Does § 1349 require an overt act?
The opinion does **not** discuss an overt-act requirement at all. So, as far as this decision is concerned, there is no overt-act discussion that the government must satisfy to keep the § 1349 count alive.

### Bottom line
Under this opinion, once the indictment adequately alleges the fraudulent scheme and intent for § 1348, the **same allegations also support the § 1349 conspiracy charge**, because the indictment alleges coordinated conduct by multiple defendants. But the court does **not** hold that any valid substantive fraud count automatically saves a conspiracy count in every case; it simply finds that this indictment, as written, was sufficient overall.

### Is there any due process or vagueness problem with applying § 1348 to social-media 'pump and dump' activity through mail/wire fraud concepts?
Question:
A broader constitutional issue I’m wondering about is fair notice. The court reads § 1348 using mail and wire fraud precedents and then applies that framework to online stock promotion and secret selling. Could a defendant argue that this stretches the statute too far and creates a vagueness or due process problem, especially after the Supreme Court has warned against expansive fraud theories like the right-to-control theory in Ciminelli? Why does the Fifth Circuit seem comfortable that this application of the statute gives constitutionally adequate notice?
Answer:
Not on this record, at least as the Fifth Circuit sees it.

The short answer is that the court does **not** treat this as an open-ended expansion of § 1348. It treats the indictment as alleging a **garden-variety fraudulent-inducement scheme**: defendants allegedly used false social-media posts to induce followers to **buy securities with money**, then secretly sold into the inflated market. That is important, because the court is careful to distinguish this from the kind of theory the Supreme Court rejected in **Ciminelli**.

### Why the court thought notice was adequate

The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that § 1348 “**borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes**,” and that courts therefore give those terms “similar treatment.” It cites **United States v. Greenlaw** for that proposition. In other words, a defendant is on notice that § 1348 is read through the established fraud case law, not as some brand-new, undefined offense.

From there, the court says securities fraud requires:

1. a **scheme to defraud**, and  
2. an **intent to defraud**,

with the scheme requiring a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain money or property, and intent requiring deception plus contemplated harm. That is a familiar fraud standard, not a vague one.

### Why Ciminelli does not create a vagueness problem here

The defendants’ fair-notice argument would be strongest if the government were using a **right-to-control** theory—i.e., claiming fraud based only on depriving victims of “valuable economic information.” The court explicitly says that is **not** what the indictment alleges.

Instead, the indictment alleges that defendants:

- posted false and misleading information,
- induced followers to **purchase securities**,
- thereby caused them to part with **money or property**, and
- then dumped their own shares for profit.

That fits the Supreme Court’s approved **fraudulent-inducement theory**. The court quotes **Kousisis** for the proposition that fraud exists when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property. So the Fifth Circuit’s view is: this is not an unforeseeable extension of § 1348; it is the ordinary application of a settled fraud theory to modern social-media conduct.

### The due process point

A vagueness or due process problem usually arises when a statute fails to give ordinary people fair notice of what conduct is prohibited, or invites arbitrary enforcement. The Fifth Circuit’s answer is essentially that **this conduct is already within the core of fraud doctrine**:

- false statements,
- inducement to part with money,
- secret profit by the speaker.

Because the indictment alleges those classic elements, the defendants had constitutionally adequate notice that their alleged conduct fell within § 1348 as construed through fraud precedent.

So the court is comfortable not because it thinks the statute is boundless, but because it thinks the case fits comfortably within the **traditional fraud paradigm**—and therefore does not present the kind of novel, expansive theory that would trigger the concerns raised in **Ciminelli**.

### Why did the court treat the followers’ purchase money as the relevant property interest instead of the securities market as a whole?
Question:
In securities cases I usually think about harm to the integrity of the market or to counterparties generally. This opinion focuses very specifically on the defendants’ followers being induced to purchase securities. Why did the court frame the protected property interest that way? Is it because, after Ciminelli and Kousisis, the safest theory is to identify a concrete transfer of money from particular investors rather than rely on a more abstract market-manipulation theory?
Answer:
The court framed the protected property interest as the followers’ money because that is the concrete property the indictment says defendants sought to obtain.

Under § 1348, the government must allege a scheme to defraud, or a scheme “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses . . . money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The opinion emphasizes that the indictment alleges defendants used false statements “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” thereby “part[ing] with their money or property.” That is the property interest the fraud statutes protect.

The court specifically contrasts that with the “right-to-control” theory rejected in Ciminelli. Ciminelli held that depriving someone of “potentially valuable economic information” or the abstract ability to make an informed decision is not a traditional property interest. The district court had viewed the case that way, but the Fifth Circuit said the indictment “does not mention the right-to-control theory.” Instead, it alleges a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: lying to get victims to hand over money. The court quotes Kousisis for the proposition that this theory “protects money and property,” and that a fraud can exist even if the victim receives something of value in return.

So yes—your instinct is right. After Ciminelli, the opinion avoids any abstract “market integrity” or “deprivation of information” framing and instead grounds the prosecution in a concrete transfer of money from identified victims. But the key point is not just litigation strategy; it is doctrinal. The fraud statutes protect property, and the indictment alleges a scheme to obtain the followers’ money by inducing them to buy securities on false pretenses.

That is why the opinion says the indictment alleges defendants “schemed to defraud their followers of money,” not merely to distort the market. The market manipulation is the means; the property loss is the victims’ purchase money.

### How does the Fifth and Sixth Amendment indictment standard shape what the appellate court can and cannot decide here?
Question:
The opinion says an indictment must allege each material element, fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and provide protection against future prosecutions. I know this connects to constitutional indictment rights, but I’m unclear on the practical effect. When the Fifth Circuit reverses the dismissal, is it saying only that the grand jury charged a legally valid offense, not that the defendants actually committed fraud? How do the Fifth Amendment grand jury requirement and the Sixth Amendment notice function explain the court’s narrow focus on the face of the indictment?
Answer:
Yes — the Fifth Circuit is deciding only whether the **indictment itself** is legally sufficient, not whether the defendants actually committed securities fraud.

That is the key practical effect of the Fifth and Sixth Amendment indictment requirements.

### 1) What the court is reviewing
The opinion says an indictment must:

- “enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense,”
- “fairly inform the defendant of the charges,” and
- protect against future prosecution.  
  *United States v. Gaytan*, quoted in Part II.

And the court reiterates the standard from *Fontenot* that it must **“take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated.”**

So the appellate court is not weighing evidence, credibility, or whether the scheme really happened. It asks only: **If the indictment’s facts are assumed true, do they charge a crime?**

### 2) Why that is a Fifth Amendment question
The Fifth Amendment grand jury requirement means a defendant may be tried only for offenses that a grand jury actually charged. That limits the court to the **face of the indictment**: the court cannot rewrite the charge or look outside it to decide whether the government can prove the case.

That is why the opinion focuses on whether the indictment alleges the elements of securities fraud — a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” — and whether it alleges deprivation of **money or property**, rather than some non-property interest.

### 3) Why that is also a Sixth Amendment notice question
The Sixth Amendment notice function is why the indictment must “fairly inform the defendant of the charges.” In practical terms, the defendant must be able to read the indictment and know what conduct is alleged so he can prepare a defense. The court’s inquiry is therefore limited to whether the indictment gives adequate notice of the charged theory.

Here, the Fifth Circuit says the indictment does that because it alleges the defendants:

- bought securities,
- posted “false and misleading information,”
- induced followers to buy,
- and then profited from the resulting price increase.

That is enough notice of a fraudulent-inducement theory.

### 4) What the reversal does — and does not — mean
So when the Fifth Circuit reverses the dismissal, it is saying:

- **the grand jury charged a legally valid offense**, and
- the indictment alleges enough facts to satisfy the constitutional charging and notice functions.

It is **not** saying:

- that the defendants are guilty,
- that the government will prove its case,
- or that the facts alleged are true.

Indeed, the opinion repeatedly frames the issue as a sufficiency question, not a merits determination: the court asks whether the indictment “sufficiently alleges” a scheme and intent to defraud, and concludes only that dismissal was improper.

### Bottom line
The Fifth and Sixth Amendments make the indictment a **charging instrument**, not a trial record. That is why the court stays on the indictment’s face: it decides only whether the grand jury alleged every material element and gave constitutionally adequate notice — not whether the defendants actually defrauded anyone.
