Case Brief: United_States_v_Constantinescu

Case Caption and Citation

United States of America v. Edward Constantinescu; Perry “PJ” Matlock; John Rybarczyk; Gary Deel; Stefan Hrvatin; Tom Cooperman; Mitchell Hennessey; Daniel Knight, No. ___. Before the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit (panel: Judges Higginson, Willett, and Engelhardt). The appeal arose from the United States District Court for the Southern District of Texas (USDC Nos. 4:22‑CR‑612‑1 through ‑8). The Government, plaintiff–appellant, appealed the district court’s order dismissing a superseding indictment for alleged failure to state an offense. The panel, in an opinion authored by Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt, reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

Facts

The government charged eight individuals—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—with participating in a coordinated securities “pump and dump” scheme. Each defendant maintained a large social‑media following and publicly held himself out as a skilled stock trader, regularly posting about trading activity and investment opportunities on various platforms.

According to the superseding indictment, the defendants’ scheme operated in three steps: (1) defendants purchased shares of targeted securities; (2) they “pumped” the securities’ prices by posting false and misleading statements to their social‑media followers—misrepresenting their own trading positions and making fraudulent price predictions or price‑target claims that induced followers and other investors to buy; and (3) after the induced buying artificially inflated the securities’ market prices, defendants “dumped” their holdings by secretly selling for substantial profits. The indictment alleges that these misrepresentations caused others to purchase the touted securities and thereby inflated prices, enabling defendants to realize approximately $114 million in proceeds from the scheme. The alleged victims were the defendants’ followers and other investors who relied on the social‑media posts.

The superseding indictment charged the remaining defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, multiple counts of securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and aiding and abetting, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2. Daniel Knight pleaded guilty; the other defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court granted the motion, concluding that the indictment failed to allege that defendants had schemed to deprive victims of a traditional property interest and instead characterized the allegations as an attempt to withhold valuable economic information. The government timely appealed the dismissal.

Issue(s)

1. Whether the superseding indictment sufficiently alleges a “scheme to defraud” in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 by alleging that defendants induced their social‑media followers to purchase securities—thereby obtaining money or property—rather than alleging only the deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli). See 18 U.S.C. § 1348; Ciminelli, 598 U.S. 306 (2023).

2. Whether the indictment adequately alleges the requisite “intent to defraud” under § 1348 given that defendants allegedly acted to profit (by “pump and dump” sales) and that victims may have received market‑value securities—i.e., whether a fraudulent‑inducement theory that seeks to obtain a victim’s money or property is sufficient even if the defendant did not seek to leave the victim net‑worse‑off, in light of the Supreme Court’s clarification in Kousisis v. United States. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025).

Holding

The court held that the indictment was legally sufficient in both respects raised by the defendants. First, the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The allegations describe a fraudulent‑inducement theory—defendants made material misrepresentations on social media to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money or property. That theory is distinct from the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli, which sought to criminalize the deprivation of valuable economic information alone; here the indictment alleges deprivation of a traditional property interest (money/property) through fraudulent inducement, and thus does not run afoul of Ciminelli. See Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023); Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025).

Second, the indictment sufficiently alleged the requisite intent to defraud. Under Kousisis, the fraud statutes do not require that a defendant intend to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss; it is enough that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme. The indictment’s allegations that defendants “pumped” securities through false and misleading social‑media posts and then “dumped” them for profit sufficiently allege an intent to deceive and to obtain money or property by those deceits. See Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388–92; United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 (5th Cir. 2023).

Result: Because the indictment adequately alleges both a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud, the court reversed the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

Reasoning

The court began by restating the governing standard: it reviews de novo the sufficiency of an indictment and must accept the indictment’s allegations as true, asking only whether the indictment alleges each material element of the offense so as to fairly inform the defendants and preserve double-jeopardy protections. The court then framed the elements of § 1348’s securities-fraud offense by reference to this circuit’s precedents and to the mail-and-wire-fraud caselaw from which courts frequently borrow. Under that framework, the government must allege (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” A “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (e.g., money or property), while “intent to defraud” requires an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Although the defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception, they challenged whether the indictment adequately alleged a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent.

Addressing the scheme-to-defraud challenge, the court rejected the district court’s view that the indictment rested on the “right‑to‑control” theory disapproved in Ciminelli. The court explained that Ciminelli forbids convictions based solely on a theory that a defendant deprived a victim of valuable economic information—because the “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. But the indictment here did not invoke a right‑to‑control theory; instead, it alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme in which defendants posted false and misleading statements to induce their followers to buy securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money or property. The panel relied on the Supreme Court’s subsequent treatment of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in Kousisis, which confirmed that fraudulent inducement “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory” and that it “protects money and property.” Because the fraudulent‑inducement theory targets victims’ money or property—traditional interests under the fraud statutes—the indictment’s allegations that followers were induced to purchase securities placed this case outside Ciminelli’s prohibition.

The court next addressed defendants’ contention that the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud because the defendants sought only to profit and did not intend to leave victims worse off. Relying principally on Kousisis, the court explained that the fraud statutes do not require that a scheme aim to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. Kousisis held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes where he “schemes to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Thus, even if victims received market‑value securities or some benefit in return, inducing them to part with money under materially false pretenses is sufficient to allege the intent to obtain money or property that the statute protects. The court observed that the indictment specifically alleges defendants posted false and misleading information “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” and then secretly sold their holdings for profit. Those allegations, the panel concluded, show that obtaining money was an object of the scheme and that defendants “had money in mind,” satisfying the intent‑to‑defraud element under Kousisis.

In applying the precedents to the indictment, the court noted support from this circuit’s authorities: Greenlaw and Evans for the § 1348 elements drawn from mail and wire fraud doctrine, and Baker for the principle that fraud liability does not require a mirror‑image showing that the victim’s loss exactly matches the defendant’s gain. The court also cited a Seventh Circuit decision applying Kousisis in a similar context as corroborative authority. Finally, applying the indictment‑sufficiency standard, the court concluded that the indictment properly alleged each material element—enumerating a scheme to induce purchases by means of material misrepresentations and alleging intent to obtain money through the fraudulent purchases—thus fairly informing the defendants of the charges and protecting against double jeopardy. For those reasons, the court held the district court erred in dismissing the indictment.

Rule(s) of Law

An indictment’s sufficiency is reviewed de novo, with the court accepting the indictment’s allegations as true; to be adequate an indictment must (1) allege each material element of the charged offense, (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and (3) provide a double-jeopardy defense to future prosecutions. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023); United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011); United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996).

The securities-fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, is violated when a defendant engages in (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 (5th Cir. 2023). Because § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” courts frequently look to mail- and wire-fraud precedent in construing these elements. Id. (citing United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018)).

A “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation or omission made with the purpose of deceiving another in order to obtain money or property. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th at 339 (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 711–12). The “intent to defraud” encompasses both an intent to deceive and an intent that the deceit cause some harmful consequence; historically, courts have required that obtaining the victim’s money or property be an object of the fraudulent scheme. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th at 339 (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712); see Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025).

The Supreme Court has clarified the limits of cognizable “property” interests under these fraud statutes. In Ciminelli, the Court held that depriving a victim of valuable economic information—the so‑called right‑to‑control theory—is not a traditional property interest and cannot alone support fraud liability. Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 316 (2023). By contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory—using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property—protects money and property and is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398.

Kousisis further clarified that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim worse off financially: a defendant violates the statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property even if the victim receives something of equal value or is not left with a net pecuniary loss. Id. at 1391–92. In short, fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to part with money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net economic loss. See id. at 1394 n.5; United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019).

Concurrence / Dissent

There are no separate concurring or dissenting opinions in this appeal. The panel rendered a unanimous decision reversing the district court’s dismissal and remanding for further proceedings. Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt authored the court’s opinion, which was joined by Judges Stephen A. Higginson and Matthew J. Kacsmaryk Willett.

Significance / Impact

This decision clarifies how courts should apply 18 U.S.C. § 1348 in the wake of the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli and subsequent guidance in Kousisis. The Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging a fraudulent-inducement theory—i.e., that defendants made material misrepresentations to induce others to purchase securities and thereby parted with money or property—adequately pleads a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite intent under § 1348. The court distinguished Ciminelli’s rejection of the right‑to‑control theory (which treated valuable economic information as a protected property interest) from the fraudulent‑inducement theory at issue here, which targets the victim’s money or property and thus remains a valid basis for fraud prosecutions. See Ciminelli, 598 U.S. 306 (2023); Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025).

Doctrinally, the opinion reinforces that § 1348 is to be read in light of mail‑ and wire‑fraud principles, and that courts may look to that jurisprudence when assessing the elements of a securities‑fraud indictment. The Fifth Circuit reaffirmed that securities fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a scheme to defraud—typically involving a material misrepresentation intended to deceive—and (2) an intent to defraud, understood as an intent to deceive and to obtain money or property as an object of the scheme. The panel relied on the Supreme Court’s explanation in Kousisis that the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property” and is not a repackaging of the disallowed right‑to‑control theory. See Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023); Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388–98.

Practically, the opinion has immediate implications for prosecutions and defenses in “pump‑and‑dump” and social‑media influencer cases. Prosecutors may rely on fraudulent‑inducement allegations—claims that influencers posted false or misleading information to induce followers to buy securities—to allege deprivation of money or property under § 1348. The court emphasized that neither an allegation of a victim’s net pecuniary loss nor an intent to cause such loss is necessary; it is sufficient that obtaining money or property was an object of the deceptive scheme. Kousisis confirms that a fraud is complete when the defendant induces a party to hand over money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the victim receives marketable securities or some other value in return.

The decision also provides guidance to district courts considering motions to dismiss indictments post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis. Reviewing sufficiency de novo, courts must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and fairly construe whether those allegations state each material element of the charged offense. District courts should take care to differentiate a prohibited right‑to‑control theory from properly pleaded fraudulent‑inducement allegations that target traditional property (money) and to deny dismissal where the indictment adequately alleges a scheme and intent to obtain money or property by deception.

Finally, by reversing the dismissal and remanding for further proceedings, the Fifth Circuit signaled that federal fraud statutes remain robust tools for addressing contemporary market manipulation conducted via social media. The opinion confirms that well‑pleaded allegations that influencers “pumped” securities prices through deceptive posts and “dumped” positions to realize profits can sustain securities‑fraud charges under § 1348, subject of course to proof at trial.
