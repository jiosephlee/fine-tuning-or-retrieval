# From Ciminelli to Kousisis: The Fifth Circuit Rehabilitates Fraudulent-Inducement in Pump-and-Dump Prosecutions

## Why this decision matters

The Fifth Circuit’s unpublished opinion reversing the district court’s dismissal of a pump-and-dump indictment marks an important calibration of federal securities-fraud prosecutions in the wake of two recent Supreme Court decisions: Ciminelli (which narrowed the reach of the federal fraud statutes by rejecting a broad “right-to-control” theory) and Kousisis (which clarified that fraudulent-inducement theories remain viable so long as the object of the fraud is money or property). In a fact pattern prosecutors have long relied on—social-media influencers using false or misleading posts to inflate share prices and then sell—the Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging that influencers “induced their followers to purchase securities” by false statements adequately pleads a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The opinion thus provides a road map for DOJ and U.S. attorneys’ offices seeking to charge market-manipulation schemes that operate through deceptive solicitations rather than more formal corporate corruption or misappropriation.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The panel framed the issue as whether the indictment pled the necessary elements of securities fraud: a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit accepted the indictment’s allegations as true and focused its analysis on two lines from the Supreme Court.

First, the panel acknowledged Ciminelli’s limit: the federal fraud statutes protect only “traditional property interests,” and a theory that the defendant merely deprives a victim of “valuable economic information” (the so-called right-to-control theory) cannot support a fraud conviction. But the panel concluded the indictment here did not rest on that infirm theory. Instead, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants posted material misrepresentations on social media that caused followers to hand over money (by buying securities). That is precisely the kind of “money or property” the fraud statutes protect, the court said.

Second, the panel relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ argument that the indictment fails because the defendants intended only to enrich themselves and not to leave victims economically worse off. Kousisis holds that the government need not show that the defendant sought to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that allegations that defendants “pumped” securities through lies so they could “dump” into the inflated market sufficiently allege both the scheme and the requisite intent.

Taken together, the panel read Ciminelli and Kousisis as complementary: Ciminelli cuts down prosecutions that merely claim a loss of informational control, but Kousisis preserves prosecutions where misrepresentations trick victims into parting with a tangible asset—cash or securities.

## Analytical perspective: a sensible boundary, but the edges remain fuzzy

The Fifth Circuit’s approach is doctrinally tidy and practical. It accepts Ciminelli’s central concern—federal fraud statutes should not criminalize the loss of access to information alone—while preserving prosecutors’ ability to pursue more familiar fraudulent-inducement cases where victims are induced to transfer money or property. That is both legally defensible and, from an enforcement perspective, sensible: pumping-and-dumping has traditionally been prosecuted as an economic fraud where misrepresentations induce purchases, producing concrete transfers (money for securities) that result in gain to the fraudster.

But the opinion also highlights continuing doctrinal friction over where the line between “information” and “property” will be drawn in real cases. The panel leans on the phrasing in Kousisis that the fraudulent-inducement theory “protects money and property” and treats the followers’ purchases as transfers of property even where, as here, the victims received marketable securities in return. That is consistent with existing precedents—fraud has long been actionable even where the fraudster provides something of equal nominal value in return. Yet hard cases remain. For instance, what if the alleged misrepresentation merely altered the timing of a trade or induced a trader to pick one security over another without an immediate payment flow to the fraudster? Or what if the primary “transfer” is a coordinated market response rather than an identifiable handing over of cash to the defendant? The Fifth Circuit’s opinion draws a clear line for the facts pleaded here—followers directly bought the shares defendants secretly planned to sell—but lower courts will continue to sort close cases.

Another open question is proof at trial. The opinion resolves pleading sufficiency, not evidentiary sufficiency. Prosecutors will still need to prove that defendants intentionally deceived followers and that the purchase transactions were causally linked to the misrepresentations. Kousisis does ease an evidentiary burden by removing the need to show net victim loss, but questions about reliance, causation, and scienter will be central, particularly when social-media markets are volatile and many other market signals may influence trading.

## Doctrinal implications and the future of market-manipulation prosecutions

The Fifth Circuit’s ruling signals three important doctrinal points for future securities- and wire-fraud prosecutions:

1. Fraudulent-inducement survives Ciminelli. Courts should treat fraudulent inducement—misrepresentation that induces a victim to hand over money or property—as distinct from the right-to-control or mere-information theory struck down in Ciminelli. Where the allegation is that victims were tricked into buying securities (paying money), the indictment can allege “money or property” as the object of the fraud.

2. Net pecuniary loss to the victim is not required. Kousisis removes a potential defense argument that a defendant’s provision of something of comparable value defeats the fraud charge. The question is whether the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by deceit—not whether the victim ended up worse off after the dust settled.

3. The contours of “property” remain contextual. While cash and marketable securities are comfortably within the statute, the distinction between information-as-property and overt transfers remains a live issue. Courts will need to examine transaction structures and alleged causal pathways to determine whether an indictment alleges obtaining money or property rather than merely depriving someone of information or “control” over assets.

For DOJ, the decision will be encouraging. It authorizes charging pump-and-dump schemes under § 1348 where the indictment can allege that social-media statements induced purchases that directly benefitted the defendants. The opinion will likely lead prosecutors to emphasize transactional allegations in charging documents—clear allegations that followers purchased securities after specific false statements and that defendants sold into the artificially inflated market. Likewise, charging teams will be careful to avoid framing their theory as depriving victims of informational control; rather they will stress the tangible transfers (money for securities) and the defendants’ sales.

Regulators and defense counsel will pay close attention to how these cases are pleaded and litigated. Securities regulators (SEC) already pursue market-manipulation claims administratively and civilly; criminal prosecutors will coordinate with civil regulators to marshal evidence of deceptive posts, timing of trades, and resulting transfers. Defense teams, on the other hand, may press Ciminelli’s concerns about criminalizing business and political conduct and will continue to press challenges to causation and scienter, as well as to the sufficiency of allegations tying particular purchases to particular misstatements in highly noisy social-media-driven markets.

## Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s decision does important work: it reconciles the Supreme Court’s guardrails against overbroad “right-to-control” prosecutions with the longstanding reach of fraudulent-inducement theory. For pump-and-dump prosecutions that can allege clear causal links between deceptive communications and victims’ purchases (the transfer of money or property to the market), § 1348 remains a viable tool. For defense counsel and courts, the case highlights the continuing need to distinguish between deceptive schemes that procure tangible transfers and conduct that merely shifts informational advantage. For prosecutors, the lesson is procedural as much as substantive: plead the transactional detail.



## No Net Loss Needed: Profit Motive as Proof of Fraudulent Intent Under §1348

The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision reversing the dismissal of a securities-fraud indictment in the social‑media “pump-and-dump” case reinforces a now-clearer line: criminal liability under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 does not turn on whether victims sustain a net pecuniary loss. Instead, the court follows the Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision to hold that a defendant who fraudulently induces victims to part with money or property — even when the victims receive market‑value consideration or are not left economically worse off — can be charged and convicted so long as the indictment adequately alleges a scheme and intent to obtain money or property by deception. That framing has immediate practical consequences for prosecutors, defense counsel, and trial courts in securities and related fraud cases.

Why this matters

The practical stakes could not be higher in cases that hinge on inducement rather than a classic theft of property. Social‑media influencers who promote thinly traded or microcap securities — and those who follow them — inhabit a landscape where the transactions often involve rapid price swings and where alleged victims end up holding the securities they bought. Defendants in such schemes have a ready defense under older intuitions: if followers received securities at market value and if the promoter also sold shares (so followers are not demonstrably worse off), there is no “money or property” loss for fraud. The Fifth Circuit squarely rejects that formalistic refuge. By applying Kousisis, the panel makes clear that the government may prosecute a fraudulent‑inducement theory when the essential allegation is that the defendant used materially false statements to trick victims into transferring money or property — regardless of net economic harm to victims.

How the court reasoned

The Fifth Circuit began from the basics: § 1348 requires a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The panel accepted the indictment’s factual allegations as true for the sufficiency inquiry and observed that this indictment does not allege a “right‑to‑control” theory like the one the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli (depriving a victim only of valuable economic information). Rather, it alleges a classical fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants posted falsely rosy claims to induce followers to buy particular securities and then sold their shares at inflated prices. Those allegations, the court held, squarely fit within the fraudulent‑inducement paradigm that the Supreme Court in Kousisis affirmed as criminally cognizable under the federal fraud statutes.

On the intent question, the panel confronted the argument that defendants’ motive — to enrich themselves, not to leave followers worse off — negates criminal intent. The court relied on Kousisis for the proposition that the fraud statutes punish schemes to obtain money or property by deception regardless of whether the scheme’s net effect leaves the victim economically harmed. In short, inducing the victim to hand over money at the time of the transaction completes the fraud, even if the defendant later provides something of equal value. The indictment’s allegations that defendants used misrepresentations to induce purchases and that they profited from “pumping” and “dumping” thus adequately plead intent to defraud.

Analytical perspective: the doctrinal tug‑of‑war resolved (for now)

The panel’s opinion stitches together two controlling strands of recent fraud doctrine. Ciminelli shut the door on prosecutions that treat a victim’s loss of informational control as a stand‑alone property deprivation. Kousisis reopened a broad door for fraudulent inducement prosecutions by clarifying that the government need not prove that the defendant intended to cause a victim’s net pecuniary loss. The Fifth Circuit properly distinguishes the theories and keeps both rulings consistent: depriving someone merely of a decision‑critical piece of information is not itself the protected property interest, but tricking somebody into handing over money or property is.

This approach aligns with a long line of mail‑ and wire‑fraud cases that focus on the defendant’s objective — to obtain money or property — and on whether deception caused the victim to surrender something of value. Practically, it avoids a cramped reading of the fraud statutes that would immunize many modern inducement schemes, especially in securities markets that are fluid and where victims often receive marketable assets in return.

Downstream effects: defense strategy, jury instructions, and evidentiary focus

Defense counsel will need to recalibrate. Under the post‑Kousisis/Fifth Circuit framework, an effective defense cannot rest solely on showing that victims ended the transaction with securities that could be sold at market value. Instead, defenders may:

- Emphasize lack of causation: demonstrate that victims’ purchases were not induced by the defendant’s statements (other contemporaneous market information, independent research, or prior positions).
- Attack materiality: argue that the alleged misstatements would not have influenced a reasonable investor’s decision to buy (or were puffery).
- Contest knowledge and intent: present evidence that the defendant genuinely believed the statements or lacked the requisite specific intent to “obtain” money or property by deception.
- Highlight fair consideration: show the defendant provided truthful information or value that defeats the inference of an intent to deprive by fraud.

At trial, the government’s evidentiary focus will likely sharpen toward demonstrating the causal link between the misstatements and specific purchases, the timing and manner in which defendants sold their positions, and communications showing a profit motive (e.g., internal messages, trading records). The indictment in this case, for example, emphasizes the sequence: purchase, pump via social‑media misrepresentations, and secret sale. That sequence — especially when combined with trading profits — will be a central theme for prosecutors.

Jury instructions will become a critical battleground. Courts must craft instructions that faithfully state the law as the Supreme Court and Fifth Circuit have defined it: the government need only prove that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce victims to part with money or property and that the defendant acted with intent to deceive and to obtain money or property. At the same time, judges should take care to avoid misleading juries into thinking mere profit motive alone establishes criminal intent; the government must still prove intent to deceive and that the object of the scheme was to obtain money or property. Trial courts should also ensure instructions explicitly distinguish fraudulent inducement from the disallowed right‑to‑control theory so jurors do not conflate informational deprivation with protected property deprivation.

Policy considerations and open questions

The court’s ruling advances prosecutorial power to reach a broad class of inducement schemes, which may improve enforcement in digital markets where misrepresentations are ubiquitous and harm is diffuse. Yet the decision also raises concerns about over‑criminalization. If the government can prosecute any persuasive (but false) claim that results in purchases, there is a risk of chilling speech, especially in gray areas between puffery, strong prediction, and intentional deception. The requirement that the indictment allege (and the government later prove) intent to deceive and to obtain money or property remains the key safeguard. But proving subjective intent is often circumstantial; trading patterns and timing — persuasive circumstantial evidence — will likely dominate.

Another open question is how courts will parse borderline cases where promoters both make deceptive statements and simultaneously provide some genuine value (research, liquidity, or legitimate price discovery). Kousisis indicates that providing value in return does not negate fraud per se, but it will matter in assessing intent, materiality, and causation. Courts will need to be vigilant in distinguishing aggressive, hyperbolic market commentary from knowingly false statements intended to manipulate prices.

Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is a clear, pragmatic application of Kousisis to the securities‑fraud context: prosecutors need not prove that victims were left worse off to charge fraudulent inducement under § 1348. For defendants in pump‑and‑dump prosecutions, that reality narrows the scope of a traditional “market‑value” defense and shifts litigation toward closer fights over causation, materiality, and intent. For courts and juries, it emphasizes a fact‑driven inquiry into what the defendant sought to obtain and whether deception was used to secure it. The ruling preserves an important line drawn in Ciminelli — informational control alone is not property — while ensuring that the fraud statutes remain capable of reaching schemes that use lies to extract money or property in modern markets.



# Influencers, Hype, and Securities Fraud: When Online Touting Crosses the Line

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal in the “pump-and-dump” social‑media touting prosecution underscores a critical tension at the intersection of securities law, criminal fraud doctrine, and modern social media commerce. At stake is a familiar question in white‑collar criminal law given new urgency by influencer culture and retail‑trading frenzies: when does enthusiastic online stock‑picking become a prosecutable scheme to defraud investors, rather than protected talk, opinion, or mere “puffery”?

The panel’s opinion is important for two reasons. First, it applies recent Supreme Court clarifications about the contours of fraud theories—most notably the limits on the “right‑to‑control” theory in Ciminelli and the affirmation of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory in Kousisis—to a social‑media context. Second, it signals that undercover selling and coordinated hype through social platforms can constitute a classical “pump‑and‑dump” scheme under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 when the indictment alleges that followers were induced, by misrepresentations, to part with money or property.

Understanding how this decision threads those doctrinal needles helps explain where prosecutors can, and cannot, go when policing online touting—and it should also inform how content creators and platforms think about transparency and disclosure.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The defendants were social‑media traders with substantial followings. The superseding indictment alleges they would buy a security, post false and misleading claims about their positions and a security’s price potential to induce followers to buy (thereby inflating the price), and then covertly sell their holdings at a profit—the classic “pump‑and‑dump.” The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it alleged nothing more than depriving followers of potentially valuable economic information—something the Supreme Court in Ciminelli said cannot support a fraud conviction because the fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not a generalized “right to control” one’s assets.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It emphasized two points drawn from recent Supreme Court jurisprudence. First, the indictment here pleads a fraudulent‑inducement theory—defendants allegedly used material misstatements to trick followers into buying securities, i.e., into handing over money or property. The panel explained that Ciminelli does not reach that theory: Ciminelli barred only prosecutions premised solely on depriving victims of valuable economic information (the right‑to‑control theory). By contrast, Kousisis confirmed that fraudulent inducement protects money and property and can form a valid basis for liability even if the fraudster does not intend the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. Second, the court relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ argument that because they sought to profit—not to cause victims a net loss—they lacked the requisite intent to defraud. Under the fraudulent‑inducement framing, the scheme is complete once a victim is induced to part with money or property through materially false pretenses; the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to leave victims worse off overall.

Putting those holdings together, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment sufficiently alleges both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud: the alleged misrepresentations were intended to induce purchases (money or property transfers), and obtaining money was the object of the scheme.

## Where the analysis is strong—and where questions remain

The court’s handling of Ciminelli and Kousisis is careful and, in my view, correct. One of the consistent dangers in applying traditional fraud doctrine to new modes of communication is improperly stretching the concept of “property” so that any manipulation of decision‑relevant information becomes a federal crime. Ciminelli drew that boundary; Kousisis clarified that the boundary does not eliminate long‑recognized fraudulent‑inducement prosecutions in which defendants trick victims into giving up concrete assets.

Here, the government’s theory is classical: induce a purchase through false statements, and the victim parts with money or property. The alleged pump‑and‑dump fits squarely within that mold, and the Fifth Circuit appropriately treated the indictment’s allegations as sufficient at the pleading stage. The court is right to reject a formalistic insistence that prosecutors must show the victim suffered a net loss—Kousisis makes clear that the protection is of the money or property that the victim was induced to transfer, not a guarantee of net loss as a necessary element.

That said, important questions remain about the line between criminal deception and protected speech in a high‑volume, algorithmically amplified communications environment. Several doctrinal and practical tensions merit emphasis.

First, the opinion is careful to limit itself to the indictment’s allegations and to the fraudulent‑inducement theory. But the practical effect of the ruling is to enlarge the vulnerability of social‑media financial commentary to criminal investigation. The core elements remain: material misstatement, intent to induce, and inducement that results in transfer of money or property. Yet in the influencer context, those elements often turn on contested factual questions—what precisely was said, whether statements were objectively material, and whether the audience relied. At trial, proving beyond a reasonable doubt that statements were materially false (as opposed to optimistic or nonactionable opinion) and that the speaker had the requisite mental state will be challenging in many cases. The Fifth Circuit’s ruling does not erase those burdens, but it makes clear the indictment stage is not the place to resolve them.

Second, the opinion does not resolve—but it highlights—the difficult boundary between actionable misstatement and protected puffery or opinion. Courts long have held that vague, aspirational, or unverifiable claims (“this stock is a steal; it’s the best thing out there”) are nonactionable under fraud doctrines and SEC rules. But the indictment here alleges specific misrepresentations about trading positions and price potential—classic covert “scalping” behavior. That is precisely the dangerous mix: when an influencer represents they are buying or holding a position while secretly lining up sales, or when they coordinate false claims with others to create the appearance of market interest, the speech is more akin to a commercial act designed to induce purchases than mere opinion.

Third, the decision stresses covert selling and coordination as touchstones that can convert speech into criminally actionable conduct. “Scalping” analogies are apt: securities law and the SEC have long prohibited market participants from touting securities while secretly selling them. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion signals that those longstanding principles translate into criminal fraud liability when the alleged conduct involves intentional deception aimed at inducing transfers of money or property. Covert selling transforms a content creator from mere commentator into an active market manipulator in the eyes of prosecutors.

Fourth, First Amendment concerns are real but constrained here. The government’s burden remains to plead and prove material falsity and fraudulent intent. Pure opinion, general enthusiasm, or even aggressive marketing that does not conceal relevant facts or make objectively false claims about personal holdings should be protected. But the First Amendment does not protect fraudulent misrepresentations offered as inducements to hand over money. The court’s ruling keeps that balance: protect speech, but not fraud.

## Downstream effects: prosecutions, platforms, and influencers

Practically, the Fifth Circuit’s decision will likely have an immediate effect on charging decisions and on how influencers and platforms respond. Prosecutors now have a clearer path to charging pumping allegations involving social media when the indictment alleges materially false claims that induce purchases and a covert selling plan. We can expect more grand jury investigations into influencer touting campaigns—particularly where the government can document timing between posts and coordinated sell orders, linkable profits, or patterns of coordinated messaging among multiple accounts.

For influencers and commercial content creators, this decision increases the importance of transparency and disclosure. Clear and conspicuous disclosures of positions, trading activity, and any compensation for promotions will be an essential risk‑mitigation tool. Even where disclosure may not eliminate civil liability, at least it diminishes the picture of covert deception that prosecutors will highlight.

For platforms, the ruling raises policy and compliance questions. Platforms may face greater pressure to detect and flag coordinated deceptive hype, to require disclosures for paid promotions involving financial products, and to preserve data that can show communication patterns and timing that are often vital in pump‑and‑dump cases. That will have free‑speech implications for platform policies and content moderation, and it will also implicate privacy and data‑retention tradeoffs.

Finally, at the doctrinal level, we should expect defendants to continue to invoke Ciminelli, Kousisis, and First Amendment challenges at motion practice and on appeal. The key fights will shift from pleading sufficiency to proof: proving material falsity, scienter, and the causal nexus between the alleged misstatements and the victims’ purchases. In social‑media cases, the evidence often comes down to timelines, messages, private chats, and trading records—the sort of circumstantial proof that has long sufficed in fraud prosecutions, but which can be messy and ambiguous.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion appropriately aligns long‑standing fraud doctrines with the realities of digital marketplaces: when influencers make material misrepresentations that are intended to and do induce followers to hand over money or property, those communications can form the core of a fraudulent scheme. At the same time, the decision preserves the important protections of Ciminelli and the First Amendment by anchoring liability to the fraudulent‑inducement theory and to allegations of deception and induced transfers—rather than to the mere manipulation of informational advantage.

As prosecutors, platforms, and creators digest this ruling, the practical upshot is predictable: greater scrutiny of covert selling and coordinated hype, more emphasis on disclosure, and more litigation over the factual line between puffery and deceit. The real work of defining that line, in fact and in law, will come in the trials and appeals that follow.



# Why Charge §1348? The Government’s Criminal Route Around Civil 10b‑5 Doctrines

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court in the Texas “pump‑and‑dump” prosecutions is a useful prism for seeing why prosecutors increasingly prefer 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) when they want to criminalize market manipulation and deceptive trading conduct that also fits under Rule 10b‑5. The opinion—grounded squarely in the post‑Ciminelli and post‑Kousisis landscape—shows what § 1348 imports from the mail and wire fraud doctrines, and what it leaves behind from the distinct doctrinal contours that govern civil 10b‑5 suits. For market participants, social‑media influencers, and defense lawyers, the decision highlights both prosecutorial advantages (a focus on fraudulent inducement and obtaining money or property rather than reliance or materiality contours familiar from Exchange Act litigation) and the lines along which defenses will likely be mounted.

Put simply: the government can charge a coordinated social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” campaign under § 1348 without pleading the elements that have tended to constrain civil 10b‑5 litigation—most importantly, a dependence on the plaintiff’s reliance on a misstatement to purchase or sell, or the duty‑based framework that can limit liability in some civil contexts. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning affirms that a fraudulent‑inducement theory—using false statements to induce investors to hand over money by buying securities—falls within § 1348’s protection, and that courts should not collapse that theory into the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli.

## The court’s reasoning in plain terms

The defendants ran social‑media accounts, posted false and misleading information about small‑cap securities, pumped prices, and then sold into the artificially inflated market. The district court dismissed the indictment because it read the government’s allegations as aiming only to deprive followers of “valuable economic information,” which Ciminelli said is not a traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit reversed.

The opinion rests on two linked points. First, the indictment here alleges a classic “fraudulent‑inducement” scheme: the defendants purportedly used lies to get followers to purchase securities—i.e., to hand over money or property. The Supreme Court in Kousisis recently emphasized that fraudulent inducement is not a repackaging of Ciminelli’s impermissible right‑to‑control theory, but a theory squarely aimed at protecting money and property obtained by deceit. Second, the court relied on Kousisis to reject the argument that the government must allege intent to cause a net economic loss to victims. Under Kousisis, it is enough that the defendant intended to obtain money or property by deceit; actual economic harm to the victim is not a necessary element.

Viewed together, these propositions let the indictment survive: the alleged scheme induced trading and procured money (or its economic equivalent) for the defendants, and the indictment pleaded both deception and an intent to obtain money.

## What prosecutors gain by using § 1348 instead of leaning solely on 10b‑5

There are several practical and doctrinal gains that explain the government’s preference for § 1348 in cases like this.

First, § 1348 borrows the scheme‑and‑intent framing of mail and wire fraud. That framing focuses on whether the defendant engaged in deception with the object of obtaining money or property. That shifts attention away from civil 10b‑5’s elements like reliance (which is often contested in market‑wide misrepresentation cases) and away from complex duties to disclose, scienter debates tied to corporate insider contexts, and private‑suit remedies.

Second, the fraudulent‑inducement theory under § 1348 allows the government to charge conduct that causes market participants to hand over value even if the victim receives some economic equivalent in return (e.g., securities that later turn out to be worthless). Kousisis clarifies that criminal liability does not hinge on leaving the victim worse off overall; it is enough that the defendant intended to obtain money or property by making material misrepresentations that induced the transfer.

Third, § 1348 sits comfortably with conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting charges (18 U.S.C. § 1349 and § 2, respectively). Coordinated social‑media campaigns—where multiple influencers coordinate purchases, timing of posts, and “calls to buy”—map well onto conspiracy and aider/abettor theories. Prosecutors can allege a shared plan to pump securities and a division of roles: some create the narrative, others amplify it, and others time sales to cash out. Conspiratorial liability doesn’t require each conspirator to have personally misled a particular investor; it requires agreement and an overt act in furtherance of the scheme—an evidentiaryly easy fit for coordinated online campaigns.

Finally, criminal charges carry different procedural tools—grand jury investigation, subpoena power, compelled production (subject to Fifth Amendment limits), and the heightened stigma and bargaining leverage of criminal penalties—that bolster enforcement against decentralized social‑media actors.

## Defense angles that still matter

The decision does not make § 1348 a blunt instrument without constraints. Several defense avenues remain salient.

Materiality, scienter, and the contours of the alleged misrepresentations are still contested issues. Although reliance is not an element of fraudulent inducement under the criminal statute, materiality and intent to deceive remain central. Defenders will litigate whether social‑media posts were puffery, opinion, or non‑actionable market commentary rather than actionable false statements of fact designed to induce purchases.

Causation and victim identity can also be contested. Proving that particular investors purchased because of specific posts—rather than for independent reasons—may matter at trial, especially where the government tries to show who was “deceived” and by what message. While Kousisis allows convictions absent net pecuniary loss, the jury still must be convinced beyond a reasonable doubt that defendants intended to obtain money by deceit and acted to advance that goal.

Coordination proof that supports conspiracy or aiding‑and‑abetting liability will also be a focal point. Defendants will challenge whether communications among influencers amounted to an agreement to commit fraud, or whether each was merely engaging in independent commentary or legitimate trading/swapping of ideas. The line between synchronized, unlawful manipulation and parallel, lawful opinion‑sharing will be litigated.

Finally, First Amendment arguments—particularly in the social‑media context—are likely to be pressed. The government must distinguish protected expression (even misleading market talk in some forms) from false statements of fact made with intent to defraud. Those are classic, fact‑intensive disputes that will define many cases.

## Downstream effects and the enforcement landscape

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion reinforces a trend: criminal securities enforcement that looks to the fraud statutes’ mail/wire‑fraud inheritance is a robust avenue for policing deceptive market conduct in decentralized digital markets. Expect more prosecutions against influencers, coordinated chat‑room actors, and cross‑platform campaigns where the government can marshal evidence of an intent to deceive and concrete steps to obtain money. Prosecutors will package core fraudulent facts within conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting theories to capture the networked nature of modern manipulation.

At the same time, courts will continue to police the boundary between protected statements and criminal misrepresentations. Post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis doctrine narrows a right‑to‑control theory but leaves intact fraudulent‑inducement claims that target the obtaining of money or property. That balance preserves room for vigorous enforcement without eliminating important limits on federal fraud statutes—but it also places a premium on careful factual pleadings and careful jury instructions about intent, materiality, and the nature of the statements alleged.

In short, the Fifth Circuit’s reversal is a reminder to market actors that criminal exposure under § 1348 is real and doctrinally anchored when the government can show an intent to obtain money through deception—especially in coordinated, pump‑and‑dump style campaigns that exploit social‑media reach.



# Drafting Indictments After Kousisis: What the Fifth Circuit Expects in Market-Manipulation Cases

## Why this opinion matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision reversing the dismissal of a multi-defendant “pump-and-dump” indictment is one of the first circuit-level applications of Kousisis to social-media-driven market manipulation. Prosecutors and defense counsel will read this opinion for guidance on how to plead—and how to attack—securities-fraud cases that rest on fraudulent inducement rather than the “right-to-control” theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli. At stake are two familiar but often-contested questions: what counts as a legally cognizable “property” interest under the fraud statutes, and what must be alleged about intent to defraud when a scheme results in the defendant’s gain but not necessarily a net loss to victims.

Put simply, the Fifth Circuit says: an indictment that alleges (1) material misrepresentations aimed at inducing followers to buy securities, (2) that the purchases were the means by which defendants obtained money or property (i.e., the object of the fraud), and (3) an intent to deceive and obtain money, is sufficient. You do not have to allege a “right to valuable economic information” theory, and you do not have to allege that victims were left economically worse off.

## The court’s reasoning, in plain terms

The panel framed the indictment against the backdrop of Kousisis and Ciminelli. Ciminelli bars convictions that rest solely on depriving a victim of potentially valuable economic information—the so-called right-to-control theory—because that “right” is not a traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. Kousisis, by contrast, confirmed that the fraudulent-inducement theory is distinct: when a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property, the statutes protect that money or property even if the victim receives something of ostensibly equal value in return.

Applying those principles, the Fifth Circuit rejected the district court’s dismissal. The indictment did not allege only the deprivation of information; it alleged that defendants repeatedly posted false and misleading statements about securities to induce followers to buy them, thereby inflating prices so the defendants could secretly sell at a profit. Those allegations, the court held, sufficiently plead a “scheme to defraud” because the object of the deception was to obtain money or property (the followers’ purchase proceeds and the defendants’ resale profits). Likewise, intent to defraud was adequately alleged by alleging purposeful deception aimed at inducing purchases—Kousisis removes the requirement that prosecutors show the defendant sought to leave victims worse off in the aggregate.

The panel therefore reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

## What this means doctrinally

First, the opinion underscores Kousisis’s practical effect: fraudulent-inducement pleading survives Ciminelli’s narrowing of the fraud statutes. Courts will be receptive to indictments in which the alleged fraud causes victims to transfer money or property as part of a commercial transaction, even where the victim may receive some marketable asset in return.

Second, the decision reaffirms the central elements borrowed from mail- and wire-fraud jurisprudence—material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and the object of obtaining money or property—as the framework for § 1348 prosecutions. That framework is flexible enough to capture modern schemes that deploy social media as the instrument of deception.

Third, the court’s approach narrows the defensive utility of arguing “no cognizable property interest” in many market-manipulation cases. Where allegations describe induced purchases—followers buying a security because of false statements—the prosecution can plausibly claim the “money or property” element is satisfied by the purchase itself. Conversely, Ciminelli will remain powerful in cases that truly only allege the withholding or distortion of information without any alleged transfer of money or property.

Finally, by leaning on Kousisis’ emphasis that the fraud statutes do not require a victim be left economically worse off, the panel signals that the government need not plead (and later prove) net pecuniary loss to secure an indictment under the fraudulent-inducement theory. That follows Kousisis’ rejection of a “mirror-image” requirement tying the defendant’s gain to an equivalent victim loss.

## Practical implications and downstream effects

For prosecutors: This opinion offers a roadmap for drafting indictments in market-manipulation cases tied to influencers or other social-media actors. Plead that the defendants made materially false or misleading public statements, that those statements were designed to induce followers to buy (or sell) securities, and that the induced purchases were the mechanism by which defendants obtained money or property (i.e., they sold out at inflated prices). Explicitly allege the object of the scheme as obtaining money or property and that defendants acted with intent to deceive. Do not conflate this theory with a right-to-control theory that asserts deprivation of information as the sole injury; instead, emphasize the monetary transfers induced by the misstatements.

For defense counsel: The Fifth Circuit’s opinion tightens one defensive line but leaves others intact. A motion to dismiss will be hard to win where the indictment persuasively alleges induced purchases. That said, Ciminelli still limits the government where no transfers of money or property are alleged; motions to dismiss remain viable in purely informational-deprivation cases. At the merits stage, defendable arguments remain about absence of materiality, lack of specific intent to deceive (versus puffery or opinion), and reliance causation in the evidentiary context—even though the indictment stage does not require proof of reliance or net loss.

For courts: The opinion suggests courts will read Kousisis and Ciminelli together rather than allowing one to swallow the other. Indictments will be judged by whether they allege a scheme whose object is money or property and whether the acts alleged fit within traditional fraud paradigms. Courts should ensure that indictments plead each material element without importing factual disputes best resolved at trial.

## Drafting takeaways — how much detail is enough?

The Fifth Circuit’s sufficiency analysis provides practical guidance on the level of pleading detail necessary to survive dismissal:

- Allegations of material misrepresentation: The indictment should identify the nature of the misrepresentations with enough specificity to put defendants on notice—describe the content of posts, their falsehood, and the platforms used. The court treated the generalized allegation that defendants “posted false and misleading information” as adequate at the indictment stage because it tied those statements to the scheme’s object.

- Allegation of the object (obtaining money/property): Do not leave this element implicit. The indictment should allege that the misrepresentations were intended to induce purchases (or sales) and that those induced transactions were the means by which defendants realized profits. Framing the scheme as a “fraudulent-inducement” case—rather than a right-to-control or mere information-manipulation theory—clarifies this point.

- Intent to deceive: Plead purposeful, repeated acts directed at inducing transactions. Allegations that defendants “posted” and “pumped” securities to induce purchases, coupled with alleged proceeds (here, $114 million), help bridge the gap from puffery to fraudulent intent for pleading purposes.

- No need to plead net loss or reliance at the indictment stage: Following Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s application, you need not allege that victims suffered net pecuniary loss, or that victims relied in a formal, individualized way. The government can allege the transfer of money/property resulting from induced purchases as the cognizable injury.

- Protect double-jeopardy and notice functions: Although the opinion allows concise allegations, indictments should still enumerate the material elements (§ 1348’s scheme and intent elements) and provide sufficient factual context so defendants can prepare a defense and avoid later surprise. Specific examples of misleading posts, dates, and transactions bolster the indictment’s notice function and will help resist later challenges.

## Concluding perspective

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal is doctrinally unsurprising but practically consequential. It operationalizes Kousisis in the fast-moving world of social-media market manipulation, confirming that prosecutors can proceed under a fraudulent-inducement theory so long as an indictment alleges that misrepresentations induced victims to transfer money or property. Defense counsel cannot rely on Ciminelli alone to dismiss cases that allege induced purchases; instead, they will need to litigate intent, materiality, and causation in the evidentiary arena.

The opinion preserves a principled boundary: fraud convictions cannot be grounded on information deprivation alone. But within that boundary, courts will accept allegations that social-media hype was deployed not merely to mislead, but to cause followers to hand over money—precisely the classic species of fraud the statutes were designed to reach.



# Ciminelli Overread? The Fifth Circuit Cabins Right-to-Control While Preserving Traditional Fraud

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision reversing a district court’s dismissal of a sprawling “pump-and-dump” indictment matters for two related reasons. First, it narrows the practical reach of Ciminelli v. United States—the Supreme Court’s decision that a fraud conviction cannot be premised solely on depriving someone of “valuable economic information” or a mere “right to control” assets. Second, it reaffirms that traditional fraudulent‑inducement theories remain viable: where the government alleges that misrepresentations trick victims into parting with money or property (here, by buying securities), the indictment can adequately plead a scheme and intent to defraud even if victims ultimately receive marketable securities in return.

For prosecutors, defenses, and courts wrestling with post‑Ciminelli doctrine, the Fifth Circuit opinion is a useful corrective. It prevents Ciminelli from being read to immunize classic pump‑and‑dump schemes merely because the victims got shares (rather than empty promises) and instead pins liability to the object of the fraud—the defendant’s obtaining of money or property through deceit.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The government charged eight social‑media traders with running a pump‑and‑dump: buy shares, amplify misleading bullish statements to induce followers to buy, inflate the price, and then secretly sell for large profits. The district court dismissed the superseding indictment, reasoning that the allegations described defendants depriving followers of “valuable economic information” rather than a traditional property interest—a theory the court treated as foreclosed by Ciminelli.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It accepted that Ciminelli forecloses only a right‑to‑control theory that treats valuable economic information as the protected property. But it distinguished that strand of authority from the fraudulent‑inducement theory alleged here. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory (as the Supreme Court clarified in Kousisis v. United States), a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis holds that this is a paradigmatic fraud—one that protects “money and property”—and is not a mere repackaging of the disallowed right‑to‑control theory.

The Fifth Circuit applied Kousisis and concluded the indictment adequately alleged a scheme to get followers to hand over money by buying securities based on materially false and misleading statements. The panel also disposed of the argument that the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud because defendants allegedly sought profit rather than to harm followers. Relying on Kousisis, the court reiterated that the fraud statutes target schemes to obtain money or property by deceit regardless of whether the victim is left economically worse off after the transaction.

## A close, reasonable reading of Ciminelli and Kousisis

This opinion performs a needed doctrinal calibration. Ciminelli established an important limit: federal fraud statutes do not, standing alone, criminalize schemes directed at withholding or manipulating information that the victim needs to exercise discretionary control over assets. But that principle was never meant to eviscerate the traditional garden‑variety fraud prosecution where the government alleges the defendant induced a victim to hand over money or other property based on false statements.

Kousisis supplied the clarifying work Ciminelli invited. It explicitly recognized the fraudulent‑inducement theory as distinct and legitimate: if the misrepresentation induces the victim to contractually part with money or property, the government is pursuing a classic fraud—even if the defendant’s actions also result in some benefit to the victim or do not leave the victim poorer on net. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion simply implements that framework: isolate Ciminelli’s narrow prohibition, and apply the fraudulent‑inducement doctrine to sustain an indictment that pleads the necessary elements.

That approach is faithful both to statutory text—section 1348 protects schemes “to defraud” and to “obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses… any money or property”—and to the Supreme Court’s recent gloss. It avoids the implausible result of insulating conduct that actively diverts wealth via deceit simply because the victim got something (e.g., shares) in return.

## Doctrinal implications

The Fifth Circuit’s decision has several doctrinal consequences:

- Indictment drafting and pleading: Prosecutors will (and should) be careful to frame allegations under the fraudulent‑inducement rubric when they seek to prosecute pump‑and‑dump or similar market manipulation schemes. Charging documents will emphasize inducement to buy or otherwise transfer money/property and identify the object of the fraud (the defendant’s obtaining money), rather than relying on a right‑to‑control theory.

- Ciminelli’s scope contained, not expanded: Lower courts were understandably anxious about Ciminelli’s potential overbreadth. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion joins a line of cases that decline to treat Ciminelli as a categorical bar to prosecutions that allege deceit leading victims to part with money or property. This limits Ciminelli’s doctrinal reach to its intended core.

- Jury instruction and proof issues remain crucial: Although an indictment can survive, conviction still requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the elements—material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and an object of obtaining money/property. Defendants can press at trial on whether the alleged misstatements were materially deceitful, whether they proximately caused the followers to buy, and whether the government has proof tying defendants’ sells to the deceptive campaign.

- Interaction with securities‑specific regimes: Section 1348 overlaps with, but is distinct from, securities statutes and SEC enforcement. Courts will continue to navigate the relationship between common‑law style fraud prosecutions and statutory securities offenses; the Fifth Circuit’s approach highlights that classic fraud theories retain force even in securities contexts.

## Practical downstream effects and defense strategies

For prosecutors, the decision is a reminder to align theory and pleading: articulate how false social‑media statements induced purchases (money transfers), identify the transfers and the defendant’s gains, and avoid equivocal reliance on “control” over others’ decisions as the predicate property interest.

For defense counsel, the path forward is to press the line carefully: attack causation and materiality (were statements puffery, opinion, or attributable to other market forces?), and emphasize the mechanics of the transactions (did followers actually part with funds as alleged? was the causal chain too attenuated?). Defenses grounded in First Amendment protection for opinionated market commentary will remain salient in appropriate cases, but the Fifth Circuit’s opinion underscores that speech is not a blanket shield where it functions as the instrument of fraudulent inducement.

At trial, defenses can exploit the fact‑intensive nature of inducement: the timing of posts relative to buys/sells, the content and specificity of the representations, and the sophistication and independence of the followers. Those are classic evidentiary battlegrounds where reasonable jurors might differ.

## Broader landscape: limiting, not swallowing, Ciminelli

Taken together, this decision is part of a modest but significant trend in the lower courts: Ciminelli imposes a real constraint, but courts are generally declining to let that constraint undermine the ability to prosecute straightforward fraudulent‑inducement schemes. Kousisis supplied the doctrinal framework to do that squarely, and the Fifth Circuit’s opinion implements that framework in the pump‑and‑dump context.

The result should reassure prosecutors that they can continue to use fraud statutes against deceptive inducements to buy securities, while also making clear that prosecutors must carefully plead and prove an actual taking of money or property rather than a speculative or informational harm. That balance preserves both the statutory protections against classic fraud and the principle—central to Ciminelli—that not every informational manipulation is federal fraud.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit got the balance right. By distinguishing Ciminelli’s narrow rebuke of right‑to‑control theories from the fraudulent‑inducement paradigm recognized in Kousisis, the court preserved a sensible core of fraud doctrine: deceit that induces victims to part with money or property remains a prosecutable wrong. The opinion curtails an overbroad reading of Ciminelli without erasing its salutary limit on prosecutorial overreach. The hard work now shifts to the district court on remand where proof, causation, materiality, and competing First Amendment arguments will determine whether these social‑media traders were merely loud commentators or the architects of a multimillion‑dollar fraud.



# Sentencing and Restitution After a Social-Media Pump-and-Dump: The Coming Fights

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in the social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” prosecution marks an important near‑term victory for prosecutors: a panel has squarely held that an indictment alleging fraudulent inducement of investors to buy securities suffices under the securities‑fraud statute. But the opinion—relying heavily on the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications in Ciminelli and Kousisis—also sets the stage for what may prove to be the sharper, more consequential fights in the months and years after conviction: how to compute loss for Sentencing Guidelines purposes and how to measure and prove restitution. Those downstream disputes will decide whether prosecutors can translate convictions into meaningful penalties and restitution awards in schemes where victims received securities at artificially inflated market prices rather than a literal cash transfer.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning is clear: this is a fraudulent‑inducement case, not a “right‑to‑control” theory, and a defendant can be guilty even if victims do not suffer a net pecuniary loss once all market movements and return flows are accounted for. That framing—“a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses”—echoes the Supreme Court’s signal in Kousisis. But Kousisis and this opinion leave open hard questions for sentencing courts and restitution tribunals: what counts as the victim’s loss, when does a defendant’s gain equate to victim loss, and how to draw the causal and foreseeability lines Congress intended for the Guidelines and MVRA.

## The opinion and the doctrinal background

The panel reversed dismissal because the indictment alleged the classic pump‑and‑dump transactional arc: defendants bought shares, posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy, the induced buying inflated prices, and defendants sold into that inflated market pocketing approximately $114 million. The court rejected the district court’s suggestion that the scheme was merely to deprive followers of “valuable economic information”—a theory foreclosed by Ciminelli—because this indictment alleged that followers were induced to hand over money or property (i.e., to purchase securities). The Fifth Circuit relied on Kousisis to underscore that the fraud statutes protect “money or property” obtained by fraudulent inducement, and that the government need not show that the scheme intended to leave the victim net worse off.

That combination—(1) a fraudulent‑inducement theory that targets the acquisition of money or property, and (2) the proposition that intended or actual net loss is not required for liability—answers a liability threshold question in prosecutors’ favor. But it does not resolve, and in some ways complicates, the ancillary calculations required at sentencing and restitution proceedings.

## Sentencing: the Guidelines “loss” debates to come

For federal sentencing the critical provision is U.S.S.G. §2B1.1, which increases offense level based on “loss.” The Guidelines define loss broadly but with several controlling glosses: intended loss versus actual loss, reasonably foreseeable loss attributable to the defendant’s conduct, and the causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and victims’ losses.

Several tension points flow directly from the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning:

- Intended loss vs. actual loss. The court’s embrace of Kousisis’ “fraud is complete upon induced deprivation” language bolsters prosecutors who will argue that intended loss can be the operative figure under §2B1.1 when a defendant’s scheme was designed to cause investors to pay artificially high prices. But defendants will press the converse: where investors were left holding marketable securities (not a worthless shell) and later sold at a loss or even at no loss, the “actual loss” can be contested and the government may be forced to rely on intended loss—an approach the Guidelines permit but require proof that the defendant had a specific intent to cause that loss or that such loss was reasonably foreseeable.

- The measure of loss in pump‑and‑dump cases. Do courts compute loss as the aggregate amount of money investors spent to buy the shares at the inflated prices (the “transactional purchase amount”), or as the decline in value after the fraud was disclosed (the resale or price dip loss)? Courts have split. Prosecutors will prefer to rely on the purchase price during the manipulated window because it tracks the money that changed hands due to the misrepresentations. Defendants will often argue that victims received stock with market value and that loss should be computed as the post‑disclosure decline (or even zero if they sold out or hedged) or discounted by any gains/proceeds returned. The Fifth Circuit’s focus on “induced deprivation” suggests a forceful argument for using purchase price as a loss proxy, but Guideline commentary and circuit precedent still require careful causal and foreseeability proof tying that purchase to the fraud.

- Aggregation and double‑counting. When followers trade frequently or when multiple co‑conspirators are involved, disputes will arise over which trades are attributable to which defendant, whether purchases made in the open market can be aggregated, and how to treat subsequent market movements not caused by the fraud. These are technical evidentiary fights—reliant on market data, trade sequencing, and expert testimony—but they will determine whether loss hits a five‑, eight‑, or 14‑level enhancement.

In short, Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit give prosecutors license to treat induced purchases as the relevant harm. But successful sentencing enhancements will still require robust proof tying purchases and price inflation to the defendants’ misrepresentations, and courts will need to police the line between intended/foreseeable loss and speculative market effects.

## Restitution and causation: MVRA and foreseeability puzzles

Restitution under the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act (MVRA) or 18 U.S.C. §3663A requires that payments be tied to the victims’ loss that was “caused by the offense.” Here the causal hook can be fraught.

If loss is measured as the full purchase price at the inflated market peak, defendants can be ordered to repay purchasers who bought during that artificial window. But defense counsel will press several defenses:

- Causation: Did the government prove that individual investor purchases were causally (not just temporally) induced by the defendants’ misrepresentations? Proof will demand individualized evidence of reliance or, at minimum, class‑wide proof that the misrepresentations were the proximate cause of the price inflation that led to purchases. Absent demonstrable reliance, courts may be reluctant to award full restitution.

- Foreseeability: Were subsequent trades, wash sales, or market forces that reduced prices foreseeable consequences of the fraud or independent market events? The MVRA’s causation requirement invites argument that restitution should exclude losses caused by unrelated market declines or by victims’ own trading strategies.

- Offset and mitigation: Victims may have realized gains, offsetting losses; they may have sold before the crash, or hedged exposures. MVRA excludes from restitution victims’ gains and requires courts to account for mitigation. Proving net loss on a buyer‑by‑buyer basis will be an evidentiary heavy lift.

Given the Fifth Circuit’s posture—that the fraud is complete once induced purchases occur—courts may be more willing to accept restitution claims tied to purchase amounts. But the MVRA’s causation and offset rules mean restitution awards will not be automatic; expect protracted hearings with market experts, broker records, and sampling methodologies to determine who actually lost and how much.

## Practical takeaways and likely litigation trajectories

Several practical consequences follow from the Fifth Circuit’s opinion:

- Prosecutors will increasingly frame pump‑and‑dump prosecutions around induced purchases and the money defendants “obtained” during the manipulation window, leaning on Kousisis’ language to preempt right‑to‑control defenses.

- Defense teams will shift resource allocation toward aggressive pre‑ and post‑conviction efforts contesting loss causation: hiring market‑structure experts, proving investor sophistication, demonstrating alternative causes for trading volume and price moves, and advocating for narrower calculations tied to actual net losses.

- Sentencing judges will become crucial gatekeepers—deciding whether to credit intended‑loss arguments and how to credit foreseeability and causation—so we should expect a spate of appellate decisions refining how §2B1.1 applies in market‑manipulation contexts.

- Restitution proceedings will be fact‑intensive and slow. Courts and legislatures may face pressure to adopt more administrable restitution schemes or to provide clearer standards for market‑manipulation harms.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal is an important doctrinal reaffirmation that the fraud statutes reach classical fraudulent inducement in the modern social‑media context. But turning liability into a punishment and meaningful remediation will require crossing the slippery shoals of loss measurement, causation, and foreseeability—issues that will shape how much punishment these cases ultimately produce and how victims are made whole. Those fights are coming, and they may matter more than the initial indictments.



# Coordinated Online Trading and the Future of Market-Manipulation Enforcement

## Why this case matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court in the United States v. Constantinescu et al. indictment marks an important step in the federal criminal enforcement of market-manipulation schemes driven by social-media influencers. At issue is whether an indictment that alleges social-media personalities bought securities, promoted them with false or misleading posts to induce followers to buy, and then sold their holdings for profit—so-called “pump-and-dump” activity—adequately pleads a scheme to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The Fifth Circuit concluded it does, rejecting the district court’s narrower view that the indictment alleged only deprivation of “economic information,” a theory precluded by the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli. The panel relied on the fraudulent-inducement theory endorsed in the Supreme Court’s more recent decision in Kousisis, holding that an allegation that defendants tricked investors into handing over money by buying securities suffices to plead a protected property deprivation, even if victims ultimately received the security at market value.

This is not just a district-court procedural victory for prosecutors. The opinion signals that criminal charges tied to coordinated influencer-driven campaigns can survive a sufficiency challenge where the government alleges the classic pump-and-dump narrative: purchase, misrepresentation to induce purchases by others, price inflation, and sale. Given the rise of influential retail-trader communities and the powerful amplification effects of social platforms, the decision will be watched closely by prosecutors, defense counsel, securities regulators, platforms, and market participants.

## The court’s reasoning, in plain terms

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis rests on two connected doctrinal points drawn from the Supreme Court’s recent fraud jurisprudence. First, Ciminelli barred prosecutions premised solely on a “right-to-control” theory—i.e., that a fraudster deprived a victim of the right to make discretionary economic decisions by concealing information. But the Fifth Circuit found the Indictment here alleges a different species of fraudulent conduct: defendants allegedly induced followers to buy securities—to “hand over” money or property—by making material misrepresentations. That is the fraudulent-inducement theory, which the Supreme Court in Kousisis made clear protects traditional money-or-property interests and is not foreclosed by Ciminelli.

Second, the court addressed intent and alleged harm. Defendants argued that because they intended to profit rather than to leave followers worse off, the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud. The panel relied on Kousisis again: federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a defendant sought to produce a net pecuniary loss to the victim. It is enough that deceit was used to obtain money or property. Thus, allegations that influencers pumped interest through false or misleading posts so that followers would buy shares—and that defendants then sold their holdings for large gains—adequately allege both a scheme to defraud and intent to obtain money by fraud.

Taken together, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment pleads the necessary elements of § 1348: a materially deceptive scheme and an intent to obtain money or property as an object of the fraud. The panel therefore reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

## Analytical perspective: where this ruling fits and where questions remain

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is doctrinally conservative in one sense: it adheres to the Supreme Court’s delimiting of fraud theories post-Ciminelli and Kousisis rather than attempting to expand the statutory reach. But it is also prosecutorially significant because it endorses applying long-standing fraudulent-inducement principles to a new factual ecosphere—social media, influencer marketing, and coordinated retail trading.

Several important caveats temper the practical effects. An indictment alleging a pump-and-dump survives sufficiency review when it alleges the classic chain—purchase, false promotion, induced purchases by others, and sale. But surviving an indictment-stage challenge is a low bar. Proof at trial remains a separate, demanding task: the government must prove material misrepresentations, scienter, causation between the misrepresentations and the followers’ purchases, and the defendants’ knowledge and intent regarding those elements. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion does not collapse those burdens; it simply confirms that pleading the elements is possible under § 1348 in this context.

A second question concerns the boundary between protected speech about markets and criminal fraud. Market commentary and advocacy—even if aggressively bullish—are often characterized by opinion, hyperbole, or imperfectly supported claims. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, grounded in the fraudulent-inducement model, implies prosecutors will need to show that influencers made knowingly false or reckless misstatements of fact (not mere puffery or opinion) intended to induce purchases. But where that line falls in practice will be litigated intensely.

## Doctrinal implications and chilling effects

This decision channels enforcement into a familiar doctrinal lane: prosecutors should bring § 1348 counts when they can allege that social-media actors secured transfers of money or property through deceit. That is compatible with SEC enforcement actions—administrative or civil—that target the same factual conduct under anti-fraud provisions (e.g., Rule 10b-5), and it may encourage parallel civil and criminal filings where facts are strong.

Yet the decision could have chilling effects on legitimate retail-trading communities. Average users and small influencers may fear criminal exposure for bullish posts that help move markets even if the intent was not deceitful. The specter of criminal liability might discourage market commentary, information-sharing, or group coordination that is lawful and central to the democratization of markets. Courts and prosecutors can moderate that chilling risk by focusing on objective falsity and scienter—targeting only deliberate or reckless misrepresentations, not ordinary investment opinion or legal conduct such as coordinating to voice support for a company without false statements.

Platforms will also face pressure. Law enforcement may ask for metadata, coordination evidence, or platform records to show communications among promoters and timing of posts. Platforms will need doctrines and policies balancing user privacy, transparency, and cooperation with lawful investigations.

## What this portends for SEC action and criminal prosecutions nationwide

For the SEC, the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion is an alignment rather than a divergence. The civil securities regime has long targeted manipulative and deceptive schemes; criminal enforcement under § 1348 now has clearer footing to pursue charismatic promoters when they cross into fraudulent inducement. We should expect more coordinated civil-criminal enforcement in high-profile influencer schemes, particularly where large amounts of money moved, there is evidence of coordinated timing or messaging, or promoter profits are substantial.

For criminal prosecutors, this case reinforces the viability of charging influencer-led pump-and-dump campaigns under general fraud statutes, provided charging documents plead fraudulent-inducement and intent to obtain money. The decision may embolden U.S. Attorney offices in jurisdictions that have the investigative bandwidth to parse social-media evidence. But given evidentiary hurdles (intent, falsity, causation), prosecutions will likely concentrate on the clearest cases: demonstrable falsehoods, repeat conduct, coordinated messaging, internal communications, or rapid sales timed to alleged “dumps.”

Finally, defense practitioners should press early for particularized pleading and careful parsing of social-media content to distinguish protected opinion from false factual claims. They will also push back against expansive discovery seeking private communications absent sufficient indicia of intent.

## Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal is an important, sensible application of Kousisis to the modern realities of market influence. It preserves prosecutors’ ability to reach classic pump-and-dump schemes even when they are executed through social media, while remaining tethered to statutory limits that exclude mere deprivation of economic information. How the boundary between lawful market advocacy and criminal deceit is applied in future cases will determine whether this decision deters fraud or chills benign market participation. Courts, regulators, and platforms now shoulder the task of translating these doctrinal principles into concrete standards that protect investors without unduly constraining the open exchange of market views.
