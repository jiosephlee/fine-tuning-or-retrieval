Law Stack Exchange Q&A: United_States_v_Constantinescu

### What gives the Fifth Circuit appellate jurisdiction over the government’s appeal from a dismissal of an indictment?
Question:
The district court dismissed the indictment pretrial, and the government appealed. I thought the government’s ability to appeal in criminal cases is limited. Under what statute (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 3731?) and doctrinal framework does the Fifth Circuit have jurisdiction here, and why isn’t double jeopardy a bar when the case was dismissed before trial?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Government’s appeal from a pretrial dismissal of an indictment is brought under the statutory vehicle Congress provided for criminal appeals by the United States—18 U.S.C. § 3731—and the Fifth Circuit here accepted and decided the appeal (the opinion notes that “the government timely appealed” and proceeds to review the dismissal).
- On the merits the court applied ordinary appellate standards: it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo (Op. § II) and evaluates whether the indictment alleges the elements of securities fraud (scheme to defraud and intent to defraud) (Op. § III).
- Double jeopardy was not treated as a bar in this case because the court reversed a pretrial dismissal and remanded for further proceedings; the opinion’s analysis assumes review of the indictment and reinstatement rather than exposure of the defendants to impermissible reprosecution.

Relevant statute and doctrinal framework (as applied in the opinion)
- Statute: The Government’s appeal from dismissal of an indictment is made under 18 U.S.C. § 3731 (Appeal by the United States). That is the ordinary statutory route for the United States to appeal certain pretrial orders in criminal cases. The opinion does not quote § 3731, but it expressly says the government “timely appealed” and then proceeds to appellate review—so the case is being treated as a Government appeal of a pretrial dismissal under the normal statutory framework.
- Standard of review and scope: The court states it “review[s] the sufficiency of an indictment de novo” (Op. § II) and applies the familiar pleading requirements for an indictment (it must allege each material element, fairly inform the defendant, and afford a double‑jeopardy defense (Op. § II, citing Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo)). On the substantive questions the court analyzes whether the indictment adequately alleges the elements of securities fraud—(1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) “intent to defraud” (Op. § III)—drawing on prior Fifth Circuit law and Supreme Court guidance (Ciminelli and Kousisis) to decide whether the indictment alleges deprivation of a protected property interest and the requisite intent (Op. § III.A–B).

Why double jeopardy was not a bar here (as reflected in the opinion)
- The opinion reverses the district court’s dismissal and remands for further proceedings rather than treating the dismissal as an acquittal or a final bar. The court’s analysis focuses on whether the indictment alleged the elements of the offense (scheme and intent) and concludes it did (Op. § III).  
- Although the opinion does not contain an extended discussion of the Double Jeopardy Clause, its disposition is consistent with the settled principle that a pretrial dismissal does not necessarily bar reprosecution in the way an acquittal at trial would; here the appellate court concluded the indictment was legally sufficient and restored the prosecution by reversing and remanding.

Key citations from the opinion you should note
- The Government “timely appealed” the district court’s dismissal (Op. § I).
- Review standard: “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo” and the indictment must allege each material element (Op. § II).
- Elements: securities fraud requires a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” (Op. § III); the court relies on Ciminelli and Kousisis to explain that a fraudulent‑inducement theory (inducing victims to hand over money/property) is distinct from the right‑to‑control theory and can support conviction (Op. § III.A–B).
- Disposition: because the indictment sufficiently alleges scheme and intent, the court “REVERSE[d] the district court’s dismissal of the indictment and REMAND[ed]” (Op. § IV).

If you want to brief this issue: cite 18 U.S.C. § 3731 for the Government’s appellate route, emphasize the de novo review instruction in Op. § II, and focus your argument on the Op. § III discussion applying Ciminelli and Kousisis to show the indictment pleads a fraudulent‑inducement theory that protects money/property rather than mere economic information.

### Why is the sufficiency of an indictment reviewed de novo, and what does ‘taking the allegations as true’ actually mean?
Question:
The panel says it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo and must accept the indictment’s allegations as true. Is this because sufficiency is a pure question of law under Rule 12(b)? Does this mean the court cannot consider exhibits or competing factual inferences (e.g., defendants’ tweets or trading records) at this stage, even if they might show the statements weren’t misleading?
Answer:
Short answer
- The court reviews indictment sufficiency de novo because sufficiency is a legal question: given the allegations in the charging instrument, do they allege each material element of the offense? (Opinion II: “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo.”; quoting Fontenot/Crow/Guzman‑Ocampo on taking allegations as true and alleging each material element.)
- “Taking the allegations as true” means the court treats the facts pleaded in the indictment as true for the purpose of deciding whether the indictment states an offense; it does not permit the court to resolve factual disputes, weigh competing inferences, or accept defense evidence that contradicts the indictment at that stage.

Why de novo review
The opinion frames sufficiency review as a pure question of law: the appellate court asks whether the allegations, if true, meet the statutory elements. See II: “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” The court therefore independently re‑examines whether the indictment pleads each “material element of the offense” (citing Gaytan and Guzman‑Ocampo). That is why the standard is de novo rather than deferential.

What “taking the allegations as true” means in practice
- The court accepts the indictment’s factual allegations and views them in the light most favorable to the government for the limited purpose of testing legal sufficiency. (Opinion II, quoting Fontenot/Crow.)
- It asks: assuming those pleaded facts are true, do they establish the elements of the charged crime (here, scheme to defraud and intent to defraud under § 1348)? The panel does exactly that when it treats the indictment’s allegations about pumping, inducing purchases, and obtaining money as true and then asks whether those allegations fit the fraudulent‑inducement theory (Part III, A–B).

What the court will not do at this stage
- The court will not resolve factual disputes, weigh competing inferences, or accept defense proffered evidence that contradicts the indictment’s allegations. The opinion treats defendants’ contrary characterization (that the indictment alleges only deprivation of information) as an argument about legal sufficiency, not a factual contest to be litigated on the merits. The panel rejects the defendants’ sufficiency argument by construing the indictment’s allegations as alleging fraudulent inducement to obtain money (III.A–B).
- Put another way: if defendants point to tweets, trading records, or other exhibits that, they say, show the posts were not misleading, those materials raise factual issues or affirmative defenses for trial (or for a properly supported evidentiary dismissal), not for a de novo sufficiency review that “takes the allegations … as true.” The opinion does not consider or weigh any such defense evidence; it confines itself to the indictment’s allegations and whether they, if true, allege the statutory elements.

A practical corollary from the opinion
- If the indictment itself incorporates specific conduct (for example, alleges particular misleading statements or specific posts) the court must accept those pleaded factual claims as true for sufficiency. But if the defense seeks to negate those allegations with outside evidence (tweets, records), that is a merits argument for trial (or a different pretrial mechanism), not for the sufficiency inquiry described and applied in this opinion.

Bottom line
De novo review asks a legal question about whether the pleaded facts, assumed true, establish each material element of the offense. It is not a vehicle for the court to resolve factual disputes or credit defense evidence that contradicts the indictment — those issues belong to later proceedings. The Fifth Circuit followed exactly that approach here: it accepted the indictment’s allegations as true and held they adequately pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement scheme to obtain money under § 1348 (Parts II–III).

### How is ‘standing’ relevant, if at all, in a federal criminal fraud prosecution like this one?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly refers to victims (the social-media followers) but does not analyze Article III standing as in civil cases. In criminal prosecutions, is ‘standing’ simply not a requirement the court must address, with subject-matter jurisdiction grounded in the federal criminal statute? If so, what replaces the standing inquiry—just the statutory elements (e.g., that someone’s money or property was the object of the fraud)?
Answer:
Short answer: Standing, as an Article III threshold for who may sue in federal court, is not a feature of ordinary federal criminal prosecutions. The court does not perform a civil-style standing analysis when deciding whether a criminal indictment states an offense. What the court does instead—both in this opinion and generally—is assess whether the indictment alleges the statutory elements that establish federal jurisdiction and the defendant’s criminal liability (here, a “scheme to defraud” plus “intent to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348).

Why: Article III standing limits who can bring or defend civil claims because civil suits vindicate private rights and require a concrete injury to a plaintiff’s legally cognizable interest. Criminal prosecutions are brought by the sovereign and rest on alleging and proving statutory elements and, by extension, the nature of the victim’s interest that the statute protects. The Fifth Circuit opinion reflects that distinction.

How the opinion treats it (specific cites):
- The court frames its task as reviewing whether the indictment alleges each material element of the charged offense and fairly notifies defendants (Part II: citation to Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo). That is the operative inquiry in criminal indictments, not an Article III standing inquiry.
- The opinion analyzes § 1348’s elements—“scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud”—and asks whether the indictment alleges a protected property interest (money/property) was the object of the fraud (Part III). The court reasons that because the indictment alleges defendants induced followers to buy securities (thereby parting with money or property), it pleads the fraudulent‑inducement theory and thus alleges deprivation of a protected property interest (Part III.A).
- The opinion distinguishes Ciminelli (no conviction for depriving a victim only of economic information) and relies on Kousisis to explain that the fraudulent‑inducement theory does protect money/property even if the victim is not left worse off overall (Part III.A–B). The court’s focus is therefore on whether the charged scheme targets money/property (statutory element), not on whether victims have Article III standing.

What replaces a standing inquiry in practice:
- The court asks whether the indictment pleads the elements required by the criminal statute—here, that defendants (1) engaged in a scheme to defraud with a material misrepresentation intended to deceive, and (2) had the intent to obtain money or property (or otherwise to defraud) (Part III, citing Greenlaw, Evans, Kousisis).
- Relatedly, the court examines whether the alleged victim interest falls within the category of “money or property” protected by the fraud statute (that is why Ciminelli mattered): if the indictment only alleged deprivation of non‑property interests (e.g., mere loss of information/control), it would fail; but if it alleges induced transfers of money/property (fraudulent inducement), it suffices (Part III.A).

Takeaway for this case:
- The Fifth Circuit did not perform an Article III standing analysis. It asked whether the indictment adequately alleged that the defendants schemed to induce followers to buy securities—i.e., to obtain their money/property—and that they intended to do so (Part III). Because the indictment alleges those statutory elements, dismissal was improper (Decision, Parts III–IV).

Short practical point for practitioners/students:
- In criminal fraud cases, focus on pleading and proving the statutory elements (what the fraud statute protects) and whether the alleged victim interest is a protected property interest. The civil doctrine of Article III standing does not supplant that element‑based inquiry.

### Why did the court import mail/wire fraud doctrines into 18 U.S.C. § 1348 securities fraud?
Question:
The panel relies on Greenlaw to treat § 1348 as borrowing ‘key concepts’ from mail and wire fraud. What is the interpretive basis for that borrowing (textual similarities, legislative history, or consistent circuit practice)? Are there any limits—i.e., doctrines that apply in mail/wire fraud but not in § 1348—given the different statutory language and context?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit imported mail- and wire-fraud doctrine into 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because § 1348 uses the same core terms and elements—“scheme to defraud,” and obtaining “money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses”—and because the circuit has long treated the statutes as consistent and cross‑referential (see Greenlaw). The panel therefore analyzes § 1348 using the established mail/wire‑fraud framework (scheme + intent), while recognizing limits imposed by the statute’s text and by Supreme Court decisions (notably Ciminelli and Kousisis) that constrain which wire/mail doctrines may be relied on.

Why the court borrows mail/wire fraud doctrines (interpretive basis)
- Textual similarity: The opinion points out that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and that securities fraud requires the familiar two elements “(1) a ‘scheme to defraud’ and (2) enacted with an ‘intent to defraud.’” That language mirrors mail/wire fraud formulations and supplies the primary basis for treating the doctrines alike (Op. § III, first two paragraphs).
- Precedent and circuit practice: The opinion relies on prior Fifth Circuit authority (Greenlaw) and other decisions (Evans, Baker) that have already used mail/wire fraud concepts to interpret § 1348. The panel explicitly says courts “often” rely on mail and wire fraud cases when analyzing securities‑fraud charges (Op. § III, opening ¶), reflecting consistent circuit practice where § 1348 caselaw is “scant.”
- Doctrinal coherence: Because the statutory text focuses on schemes to obtain money or property by deceptive means, the court finds it sensible to apply the well‑developed mail/wire fraud tests for what counts as a “scheme” and what mental state (intent to deceive and to obtain money/property) is required. The opinion deploys those tests to distinguish the fraudulent‑inducement theory from the rejected right‑to‑control theory (Op. § III.A–B).

What doctrines carry over and what are the limits
- Carry‑over doctrines:
  - The two‑element formulation (scheme to defraud + intent to defraud) and related definitions of “scheme” (material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain value) and “intent” (intent to deceive and to cause harm or obtain money/property) are applied to § 1348 (Op. § III).
  - The fraudulent‑inducement theory (using a material misstatement to induce a victim to part with money or property) is explicitly treated as available under § 1348; the opinion says the indictment alleges such a theory and cites Kousisis approvingly (Op. § III.A, citing Kousisis).
  - The principle that actual net economic loss to the victim is not required for fraud liability when the defendant obtains money or property by fraud (Kousisis) is adopted here—i.e., a defendant can be guilty even if the victim ends up with market‑value consideration (Op. § III.B).
- Limits and doctrines that do NOT carry over:
  - Right‑to‑control theory rejected by Ciminelli: The court expressly distinguishes Ciminelli and says that a deprivation consisting only of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory) is not protected property; that theory cannot serve as the basis for a § 1348 conviction (Op. § III.A, discussing Ciminelli).
  - Statutory text controls: The court repeatedly roots its analysis in § 1348’s requirement that the scheme target “money or property in connection with” a security. The importation of mail/wire doctrine is not carte blanche—doctrines inconsistent with § 1348’s text or with Supreme Court limitations (e.g., Ciminelli’s property‑interest constraint) do not apply (Op. § III, passim).
  - Context‑specific mail/wire doctrines that depend on the use of mail or wires (e.g., jurisdictional mailing/wiring requirements) are irrelevant to § 1348 because the statute criminalizes securities fraud without needing a mail/wire predicate; the opinion does not adopt inapposite procedural requirements purely tied to the mail/wire statutory vehicles (the opinion instead focuses on substantive elements).
- Practical effect of the limits: The court uses mail/wire doctrine where it defines the substantive elements (what a “scheme” and “intent” look like), but it rejects doctrinal expansions that would convert non‑property interests (information) into the protected “money or property” the statute requires. The panel thus borrows analytical tools, not free‑standing rules that would override the statute or controlling Supreme Court holdings (Op. § III.A–B).

Support from the opinion (specific citations in the text)
- “§ 1348 ‘borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,’ so ‘courts have given the terms similar treatment’” — Op. § III opening para (quoting Greenlaw).
- Elements articulated as (1) scheme to defraud, (2) intent to defraud — Op. § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).
- Rejection of right‑to‑control theory (Ciminelli) and adoption of fraudulent‑inducement framework (Kousisis) — Op. § III.A (discussion of Ciminelli and Kousisis).
- No requirement that the victim suffer net economic loss — Op. § III.B (discussing Kousisis and Baker).

Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit’s approach is interpretive and incremental: it imports the substantive analytical framework developed for mail/wire fraud because § 1348 uses parallel statutory language and because circuit practice has long treated them similarly, but it hews to § 1348’s text and to Supreme Court limits. So courts will borrow the mail/wire doctrines that define what counts as a “scheme” and the requisite intent, but will not adopt theories that attempt to expand “money or property” beyond what the statute and controlling precedent (Ciminelli, Kousisis) permit.

### Does § 1348(1) itself require that ‘obtaining money or property’ be an object of the scheme, or is that requirement coming from Ciminelli/Kousisis via analogy?
Question:
Section 1348 has two clauses: (1) a scheme to defraud any person in connection with a security, and (2) a scheme to obtain money or property by false pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. The opinion repeatedly emphasizes that ‘obtaining money or property’ must be an object of the fraud. Is the court reading that requirement into clause (1) as well, or is it saying the indictment satisfies clause (2)? How do Ciminelli and Kousisis constrain clause (1), if at all?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit treats “obtaining money or property” as part of what a “scheme to defraud” must aim to do, but it does so by applying the Supreme Court’s Ciminelli/Kousisis line (and mail/wire‑fraud doctrine) to §1348’s general “scheme to defraud” element. The court did not silently graft a new statutory text into §1348(1); rather, it reads the longstanding mail/wire‑fraud concept—that a scheme must be intended to obtain something of value—into the §1348 “scheme” element, and then explains that the indictment pleads the fraudulent‑inducement theory (what Kousisis approves) rather than the right‑to‑control theory (what Ciminelli rejected). See Opinion ¶¶ III, A–B.

What the opinion actually says (and why)
- The statute has two clauses, but the offenses share the same two elements: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The court leans on prior mail/wire‑fraud cases (cited in ¶ III) and explains that the “scheme to defraud” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” (Opinion ¶ III (quoting Greenlaw and Evans).)

- The Supreme Court decisions control how that “something of value” requirement is applied:
  - Ciminelli holds you cannot base fraud solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information (the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory). The Fifth Circuit cites Ciminelli and says the indictment here is unlike Ciminelli because it does not rely on a right‑to‑control theory (Opinion ¶ III.A).
  - Kousisis clarifies and preserves the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory: a defendant who uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into relinquishing money or property has “obtained” money/property even if the victim isn’t left economically worse off. The Fifth Circuit repeatedly quotes and relies on Kousisis to show that obtaining money/property can be the object of the fraud even where the fraudster provides something in return (Opinion ¶¶ III, A–B).

- How those rulings affect §1348(1) versus §1348(2):
  - The court treats the “obtain money or property” concept as governing the scheme element generally (i.e., the same requirement constrains a §1348(1) “scheme to defraud” charge). See ¶ III where the court says securities fraud requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) intent, and then explains that the “scheme to defraud” requires deception “in order to obtain something of value” (citing Evans and Greenlaw).
  - But the court does not say it is ignoring the statutory text distinguishing the two clauses. Instead, it shows the indictment pleads a fraudulent‑inducement theory—that defendants induced followers to buy securities (thus induced them to part with money/property). That pleading fits comfortably within clause (2)’s phrasing and, crucially, supplies the “obtain money or property” object that the court requires the “scheme” element to have under Ciminelli/Kousisis. See ¶ III.A (explaining the indictment alleges they induced purchases) and ¶ III.B (relying on Kousisis to say intent to obtain money suffices even without net loss).

Bottom line for clause (1)
- Ciminelli and Kousisis constrain clause (1) in this way: clause (1)’s “scheme to defraud” must be a deception aimed at obtaining some money or property (not merely depriving the victim of information). Ciminelli forbids a conviction based solely on loss of valuable economic information; Kousisis permits a fraudulent‑inducement theory where the defendant’s object is to obtain money/property even if the victim suffers no net loss. The Fifth Circuit applied that framework and found the indictment adequately alleges that the defendants’ scheme aimed to obtain money (Opinion ¶¶ III.A–B).

### What exactly distinguishes the prohibited ‘right-to-control’ theory from the permitted ‘fraudulent-inducement’ theory?
Question:
The panel says Ciminelli forecloses convictions based on depriving a victim of ‘valuable economic information’ alone, but Kousisis allows ‘fraudulent inducement’—tricking a victim into handing over money or property. In practice, how do courts police the line so the government cannot simply re-label right-to-control cases as ‘inducement’? What features of this indictment (inducing followers to buy securities) make it materially different from right-to-control?
Answer:
Short answer
The forbidden “right‑to‑control” theory condemns schemes that only deprive someone of valuable economic information — i.e., influence over a decision — without alleging that the defendant obtained the victim’s money or property. The permitted “fraudulent‑inducement” theory instead alleges that the defendant used material misstatements to trick victims into a transaction that required them to part with money or property. The Fifth Circuit here found the indictment to allege the latter (inducing followers to buy securities and then selling into the inflated market), not merely the former.

How courts police the line (doctrinally and in practice)
1. The protected interest. Ciminelli says federal fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests,” not a bare “right to valuable economic information.” 598 U.S. at 316 (quoted in the opinion). So courts ask whether the indictment alleges deprivation of money/property (a protected interest) or only deprivation of information/control (not protected).

2. The object of the scheme. Courts look to whether “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was an object of the fraud. The Fifth Circuit describes the governing elements: a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and intent to defraud (intent to deceive and cause harm). It relies on Kousisis and Ciminelli: “a defendant commits securities fraud when he ‘engaged in deception’ and ‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.” Op. § III (quoting Kousisis/Ciminelli).

3. The operative conduct alleged, not labels. Labels like “inducement” or “right to control” won’t decide a case. Courts examine the facts the indictment actually pleads: did the defendant trick victims into parting with money/property (fraudulent inducement), or did the defendant merely withhold or misstate information to influence a discretionary decision while the victim retained their money/property (right‑to‑control)? The opinion emphasizes that Ciminelli barred convictions based “solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information,” but that a different allegation—inducing purchases—falls on the permitted side. Op. § III.A.

Why this indictment falls on the permitted side (specific features the court relied on)
- Express allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13 (quoted in Op. § III.B). That is a classic fraudulent‑inducement allegation: material misstatements used to cause purchases.
- Allegation of the defendants’ profit motive and realized profit: the scheme allowed defendants to “artificially increase [a security’s] price” and then “dump” it, and the indictment claims $114 million in profits. Op. § I & III.B. That shows the object of the scheme was to obtain money/property.
- The Court contrasts this with Ciminelli (lobbyist paid to influence government contracting) and explains the indictment “does not mention the right‑to‑control theory” and instead pleads that victims were induced to buy securities — i.e., to hand over money/property. Op. § III.A.

Other policing tools the opinion and precedents use
- Mens rea and causation: the indictment must plausibly allege intent to obtain money/property by deception (the Fifth Circuit treats the pleading of intent to profit as probative). Op. § III.B (discussing Kousisis on intent).
- The “complete fraud” point from Kousisis: a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to part with money/property under materially false pretenses, even if the victim gets market‑value consideration or is not left worse off. Op. § III.B (quoting Kousisis at 1394 n.5). That prevents defendants from escaping liability by pointing out that victims received securities rather than nothing.
- Pleading specificity: an indictment that only asserts deception about one’s subjective intent or only alleges that the defendant withheld information without alleging induced transactions will risk dismissal under Ciminelli. The court’s analysis shows that concrete allegations that victims were induced to buy are decisive.

Bottom line for line‑drawing
- If the government pleads that the defendant’s misrepresentations caused victims to enter transactions that required them to part with money or property (here: buying the promoted securities), the claim is a fraudulent‑inducement theory (permitted). Op. § III.A–B.
- If the government’s theory depends only on depriving the counterparty of economic information or control (and does not allege the defendant obtained money/property), Ciminelli bars conviction. Op. § III.A.
- Courts police relabeling by looking at the substantive allegations (who parted with money/property, what was obtained, the object of the scheme), not merely the label the prosecutor uses. Op. §§ III.A–B (contrasting Ciminelli and Kousisis and emphasizing the indictment’s specific allegations that followers were induced to purchase securities).

If you’re studying the opinion: focus on the passages in § III.A–B where the court contrasts Ciminelli’s right‑to‑control rejection with Kousisis’s approval of fraudulent inducement and where it quotes the indictment (¶ 13) describing inducement to purchase and the $114M profit allegation. Those are the facts that move this case off the forbidden side of the line.

### How does the court reconcile its earlier ‘intent to cause some harm’ requirement with Kousisis’s statement that net economic loss isn’t required?
Question:
The opinion quotes Fifth Circuit law that intent to defraud includes intent to deceive and to cause some harm, but then relies on Kousisis that a defendant need not seek to make the victim worse off financially. Do courts now treat the ‘harm’ prong as satisfied when the object is to obtain the victim’s money or property by deceit, even if the victim receives equivalent value?
Answer:
Short answer: The court treats the “harm” (or second) element of intent to defraud as satisfied whenever the defendant’s object was to obtain the victim’s money or property by deceit — even if the defendant provided something of “equal value” so the victim is not left economically worse off. The Fifth Circuit reconciles its prior phrasing that intent to defraud requires an intent “to . . . cause some harm” with the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis by reading that “harm” as the induced deprivation of money or property (i.e., obtaining the victim’s money/property), not as a required net pecuniary loss.

Why (doctrine and how the opinion says it)

- Two elements remain: (1) a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation intended to deceive) and (2) intent to defraud. The court repeats its previous statement that intent to defraud requires an intent “to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” (Opinion § III, quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712.)  
- But Kousisis clarifies what “harm” means in the fraudulent‑inducement context. The Supreme Court held that the fraudulent‑inducement theory criminalizes using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property “regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” The Court thus said a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” (Opinion § III.B, citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392.)  
- The Fifth Circuit applies that principle here: because the indictment alleges defendants made misrepresentations to induce followers to purchase securities (i.e., to part with money/property), it sufficiently alleges an intent to defraud. The court explains that a fraud is complete when a defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, “regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.” (Opinion §§ III.A–B; citing Kousisis at 1394 n.5.)

What this means in practice

- The older language “intent . . . to cause some harm” is not discarded; it is read through Kousisis: the “harm” can be the deprivation of money or property obtained by deceit (the object of the fraud), even if the victim later receives something of equal apparent value.  
- So courts will find the intent-to-harm element satisfied where the defendant’s object was to obtain the victim’s money/property by fraud (a classic fraudulent‑inducement scenario), and they will not require proof that the defendant wanted the victim to be net worse off financially.

Limitations/caveats the opinion preserves

- This is not an invitation to criminalize mere deception that does not seek to obtain money or property. The opinion distinguishes Ciminelli (right‑to‑control/valuable information) from fraudulent inducement: Ciminelli forbids convictions based solely on depriving someone of economic information (a non‑traditional property interest). The fraudulent‑inducement theory charged here “protects money and property” and therefore survives Ciminelli (Opinion § III.A).  
- The government still must allege (and later prove) deception and that obtaining the victim’s money/property was an object of the scheme; absent that, the statute is not implicated.

Bottom line: Under this opinion (following Kousisis), the “harm” part of intent to defraud is satisfied when the defendant seeks to obtain money or property by deceit — proof of a victim’s net economic loss is not required.

### Why doesn’t receiving market-value securities defeat the ‘scheme to defraud’ element?
Question:
Defendants argued followers weren’t injured because they received market-value securities. The court says fraud is complete upon deceptive inducement to part with money or property. What is the doctrinal basis for treating the induced transfer (rather than net loss) as the injury, and how does this avoid collapsing into a broad right-to-control theory?
Answer:
Short answer: Because federal fraud law (as the court reads it) protects the induced transfer of money or property itself — not only net pecuniary loss — a scheme that tricks victims into handing over money (or buying securities) is a completed fraud even if the victims receive market‑value securities in return. That is the fraudulent‑inducement theory the court applies, and the opinion explains why that theory is materially different from the right‑to‑control (information‑deprivation) theory rejected in Ciminelli.

Why the induced transfer (not net loss) is the injury
- The court rests its analysis on the fraudulent‑inducement doctrine: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Op. III.A (quoting Kousisis). Under that theory, the relevant harm for the “scheme to defraud” element is the deceptive inducement that causes the victim to part with money or property.
- The opinion follows Kousisis: “a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schem[es] to “obtain” the victim’s “money or property,” regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.’” Op. III.B (quoting Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392). The court therefore treats the act of obtaining the victim’s money/property by fraud as the culpable objective and as a sufficient injury for the indictment.
- The court states explicitly that “[a] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.” Op. III.B (quoting Kousisis at 1394 n.5). That is the doctrinal basis for treating the induced transfer itself as the injury.

Why this does not collapse into a broad right‑to‑control theory
- Ciminelli held that depriving someone of valuable economic information (a “right to control”) is not a traditional property interest and cannot support a fraud conviction. Op. III.A (discussing Ciminelli).
- The court distinguishes that holding: the indictment here does not allege mere deprivation of information; it alleges affirmative inducement to buy securities — i.e., to hand over money/property. The opinion stresses the difference: the fraudulent‑inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory” and “protects money and property.” Op. III.A (quoting Kousisis).
- Put another way, the line is between (1) schemes whose only effect is to withhold information so a victim makes a different discretionary decision (Ciminelli’s forbidden right‑to‑control theory) and (2) schemes that trick victims into parting with money or property (fraudulent inducement). The first seeks to control decisions; the second seeks to obtain money/property. The court treats only the second as within the fraud statutes.

How the market‑value securities point fits
- The defendants’ point (followers received market‑value securities, so no net loss) is foreclosed by Kousisis and the court: a fraudster can “provide something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return” and still commit fraud if he induced the victim’s transfer by deceit. Op. III.B (quoting Kousisis at 1391). Thus receipt of market‑value securities does not defeat the “scheme to defraud” element when the indictment alleges fraudulent inducement to purchase and the defendant’s object was to obtain money.

In short: the opinion treats the fraud as complete when the defendant knowingly and deceptively induces victims to transfer money or property (here, purchase securities). That doctrine protects tangible transfers (money/property) rather than mere informational harms, and so it avoids collapsing into the broad right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli.

### What is the materiality standard under § 1348, and how do misstatements about one’s trading positions qualify?
Question:
The court says a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation. Is ‘materiality’ under § 1348 the same as in mail/wire fraud (capable of influencing a reasonable person) or the securities-law standard from Basic/Matrixx? Why are false statements about holding/selling a stock deemed material in this criminal context?
Answer:
Short answer
Materiality under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 in this opinion is treated the same way courts treat materiality in mail/wire fraud cases — i.e., whether the misrepresentation was capable of influencing a person to part with money or property (the fraudulent‑inducement sense) — not the distinct civil securities “reasonable‑investor” or market‑efficiency formulations from Basic/Matrixx. The Fifth Circuit holds that false statements about one’s trading positions are material in § 1348 prosecutions when those statements are alleged to have been used to induce followers to buy securities (i.e., to part with money or property).

Why — the court’s reasoning (with citations)
- § 1348 borrows mail/wire fraud concepts. The opinion expressly notes that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and that courts “have given the terms similar treatment” (Op. § III, first paragraph). So materiality is analyzed in the same functional way used in fraud prosecutions.
- The “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” The court quotes this formulation when describing the elements of securities fraud under § 1348 (Op. § III, quoting United States v. Evans at ¶ III).
- The opinion relies on the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” The Court contrasts that theory with the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli and emphasizes that the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property” (Op. III.A (citing Kousisis)).
- The court therefore rejects the defendants’ argument that the indictment pleads only a deprivation of “valuable economic information” (the theory the Supreme Court disallowed in Ciminelli). Instead the indictment alleges that defendants used false posts to induce followers to purchase securities — i.e., to give up money/property — which is squarely the kind of material misrepresentation § 1348 proscribes (Op. III.A, ¶¶ quoting Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 and discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis).

Why false statements about holding/selling a stock qualify as material
- Because the indictment alleges those statements were used to induce purchases: it alleges defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” then “dump” for a profit (Indictment ¶ 13–14; Op. I and III.A). A misstatement that is capable of causing a follower to buy (or refrain from selling) thereby causes the follower to part with money/property — the precise harm the fraud statutes guard against.
- The Court emphasizes that the fraud statutes do not require the victim to be left economically worse off; it is enough that the fraud was used to obtain the victim’s money or property (Op. III.B, discussing Kousisis). Thus a claim that a speaker holds (or will sell) stock is material if it is alleged to have influenced followers’ trading decisions and thereby enabled the speaker to obtain money through the pump‑and‑dump.

Short practical takeaway for analysis
- For § 1348 prosecutions, ask whether the alleged misstatement was capable of influencing a person to part with money or property (fraudulent inducement), not whether it meets the civil securities law’s reasonable‑investor/market‑efficiency tests. If the indictment alleges the false statement was used to induce purchases (as here), the statement is material for criminal securities fraud purposes.

### Does criminal § 1348 require proof of reliance or loss causation like private Rule 10b-5 actions?
Question:
The opinion does not mention reliance or loss causation. In criminal securities fraud under § 1348, is the government required to show that a victim relied on the misstatement or that the misstatement caused a pecuniary loss, or are those civil elements inapplicable? If they’re inapplicable, what connects the deceit to the ‘in connection with’ requirement for the purchase or sale?
Answer:
Short answer: No — under § 1348 as the Fifth Circuit explains here (drawing on Ciminelli and Kousisis), the government need not prove the civil Rule 10b‑5 elements of loss causation or a requirement that the victim suffer a net pecuniary loss. What the government must allege and ultimately prove in a criminal fraudulent‑inducement case is (1) a material misrepresentation (a “scheme to defraud”), (2) intent to deceive and to obtain money or property, and (3) that the misrepresentation was used to induce the purchase or sale (i.e., it caused the victim to part with money or property)—which is how the deceit is “in connection with” a security transaction.

Explanation tied to the opinion

- The court frames § 1348 as requiring a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” borrowing concepts from mail/wire fraud case law. The “scheme” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” and the intent element requires an intent to deceive and to cause harm from the deceit. (Op. § III, citing Greenlaw and Evans.)

- The opinion distinguishes Ciminelli (which rejected a right‑to‑control theory that treats deprivation of valuable economic information as a protected property interest). Ciminelli does not help the defendants because the indictment here alleges a fraudulent‑inducement theory — i.e., that defendants used false statements to induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with money or property. The Fifth Circuit cites Kousisis for the point that fraudulent‑inducement “protects money and property” and is “not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” (Op. § III.A, discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis.)

- Crucially, the opinion relies on Kousisis to say the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. A defendant violates the statute where he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Thus, actual loss or net harm to the victim is not an element. (Op. § III.B, discussing Kousisis.)

- What connects the deceit to the “in connection with” requirement is the inducement of the transaction: the indictment alleges defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13. That is the causal link the court treats as satisfying the “in connection with” requirement — the misrepresentation induced the purchase (i.e., the victim parted with money/property), and obtaining that money/property was an object of the fraud. (Op. §§ III.A–B; Indictment ¶ 13.)

Net: Unlike civil Rule 10b‑5 claims that require elements like reliance and loss causation to prove investor loss and proximate cause of damages, a criminal prosecution under § 1348 grounded on a fraudulent‑inducement theory must prove deception, intent to obtain money/property, and that the deception was used to induce the purchase/sale (transaction causation). It does not require proof of the civil elements of reliance in the sense of proving plaintiff’s compensable loss or net pecuniary harm.

### How does the panel justify using an intervening Supreme Court case (Kousisis) that post-dated the district court’s ruling?
Question:
The district court dismissed before Kousisis, but the Fifth Circuit applies Kousisis on appeal. Is this simply the normal rule that intervening Supreme Court decisions apply to cases on direct review? Are there any retroactivity or fair-notice concerns given the criminal context?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel applied Kousisis because it was an intervening Supreme Court decision decided while the case was pending on direct review, and the ordinary rule is that controling Supreme Court authority announced while a case is on direct appeal governs the disposition of that case. The panel explains that Kousisis clarified (rather than upended) the elements of a fraud offense under § 1348 and then tested the indictment against that clarified standard; because the indictment plainly alleges conduct that Kousisis treats as traditional fraudulent inducement (inducing victims to part with money/property), there are no separate retroactivity or fair‑notice problems.

Why that is legally justified (with references to the opinion)
- The opinion itself notes the timing: “After the district court dismissed the indictment, the Supreme Court clarified in Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1398 (2025), that an indictment alleging a fraudulent‑inducement theory, as here, does not run afoul of Ciminelli.” (Opinion, III preface.) The panel therefore applied Kousisis on appeal.
- That is the normal rule: if the Supreme Court announces a controlling rule while a case is on direct review, appellate courts are bound to apply it. The panel follows that practice by reassessing the sufficiency of the indictment under Kousisis’s articulation of the fraudulent‑inducement theory (see Opinion III.A–B where the court quotes and relies on Kousisis at multiple points, e.g., “the fraudulent‑inducement theory … protects money and property,” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1398, and that a defendant may be liable “regardless of whether the fraudster … seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss,” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).
- The panel’s substantive analysis shows how Kousisis is used as a clarification, not as a novel retroactive rule. The court contrasts Ciminelli (which rejected a right‑to‑control theory) with Kousisis’s validation of a fraudulent‑inducement theory, and then holds the indictment “does not run afoul of Ciminelli because it alleges defendants deprived their followers of a protected property interest by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities.” (Opinion III.A.) It therefore applies the clarified doctrine to the alleged facts and concludes the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud (Opinion III.B).

Why retroactivity / fair‑notice is not a live problem here
- This is direct review of the district court’s dismissal on the government’s timely appeal; the ordinary rule is that new Supreme Court precedent controlling the legal question applies to cases then pending on direct review. The panel treated Kousisis as controlling authority for the statutory elements and evaluated the indictment under that controlling interpretation.
- The panel implicitly rejects any due‑process/fair‑notice concern by showing the indictment charged classic fraudulent inducement (alleging false statements to induce purchases so defendants could sell at a profit). The opinion emphasizes that Kousisis confirms liability when a defendant induces a victim to hand over money/property even if the victim does not suffer net pecuniary loss (Opinion III.B: quoting Kousisis 145 S. Ct. at 1392, 1394 n.5). Because the charged conduct (pump‑and‑dump inducing others to buy securities) falls squarely within that clarified fraudulent‑inducement theory, defendants had adequate notice that such conduct could be charged as fraud.
- The panel’s reliance on prior Fifth Circuit law about the elements (see Opinion III opening paragraphs citing Greenlaw and Evans) shows it is applying a line of authority refined by Kousisis, not adopting an unforeseeable new criminal prohibition. The indictment alleged deception and that “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was an object of the fraud (Opinion III, quoting Kousisis 145 S. Ct. at 1390–91), so the application of Kousisis did not criminalize previously lawful conduct without fair notice.

Bottom line: The panel properly applied Kousisis as intervening Supreme Court precedent on direct review, used Kousisis to clarify the applicable fraudulent‑inducement standard, and found the indictment satisfies that standard. Because the alleged pump‑and‑dump scheme clearly involved inducing victims to part with money/property, the panel had no retroactivity or fair‑notice bar to applying Kousisis on appeal.

### What is the constitutional dimension, if any, to the court’s insistence on money-or-property (post-Ciminelli) as the fraud object?
Question:
Ciminelli grounds its rejection of the right-to-control theory in the federal fraud statutes’ protection of ‘traditional property interests.’ Is the Fifth Circuit’s analysis here purely statutory, or is there a constitutional avoidance or due process vagueness concern pushing courts to confine fraud to traditional property?
Answer:
Short answer: Purely statutory. The Fifth Circuit’s insistence that the fraud object be “money or property” is rooted in statutory interpretation—i.e., what the federal mail/wire/securities‑fraud statutes protect—not in a separate due‑process vagueness or constitutional‑avoidance doctrine.

Why (with citations to the opinion)

- The panel frames the issue as one of what § 1348 (and its mail/wire‑fraud analogues) actually prohibit. It repeatedly analyzes whether the indictment alleges a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” under the statutory elements, citing precedent about what those elements require. See § III (collecting elements), and the description that securities fraud requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” (Opinion § III, first paragraph.)

- The opinion treats Ciminelli as a limiting rule of statutory interpretation: Ciminelli held that “the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests” and therefore rejected the right‑to‑control theory as a statutory basis for conviction. The Fifth Circuit says that exact statutory holding twice: “In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information” and that “because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, . . . the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” (Opinion § III.A, quoting Ciminelli.) The court uses that statutory rule to distinguish the case at bar (fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property) from Ciminelli (deprivation only of information).

- The panel’s remediation of the district court’s error is statutory: it reverses because the indictment alleges a “fraudulent‑inducement theory,” which the Supreme Court in Kousisis recognized as protecting “money and property” and not a mere repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory. The court’s analysis relies on Kousisis’ interpretation of the fraud statutes and on the classical requirement that the object of the fraud be “money or property.” (Opinion § III.A–B, citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. 1382.)

- The opinion does not invoke the Due Process Clause, vagueness doctrine, or any constitutional‑avoidance canon. There is no language suggesting the court felt compelled to cabin the statute to avoid constitutional infirmity; instead it treats Ciminelli/Kousisis as definitive statements about the statutory scope of fraud prosecutions and applies them to the indictment’s allegations.

How to read the Court’s language about “traditional property interests”
- When the court repeats that Ciminelli said the statutes “protect only traditional property interests,” it is doing statutory work: identifying what kinds of interests constitute the “money or property” the statutes criminalize. The court’s point is doctrinal—distinguish right‑to‑control (information) from fraudulent inducement (money/property)—not to announce a constitutional floor or express vagueness worries.

Bottom line
- The opinion confines fraud to “money or property” for statutory reasons (what § 1348 and its predecessors cover), following Supreme Court precedents Ciminelli and Kousisis. It does not rely on or discuss due‑process vagueness or constitutional‑avoidance principles.

### Why didn’t the panel address potential First Amendment defenses given that the alleged misstatements were social-media posts?
Question:
The conduct involves public statements about securities. Are fraudulent misrepresentations categorically unprotected so the First Amendment plays no role, or did the panel avoid that issue because a Rule 12 indictment challenge isn’t the right vehicle? At what stage would courts evaluate First Amendment arguments in a § 1348 prosecution?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel didn’t decide a standalone First Amendment question because it concluded the indictment—taken as true for a Rule 12 sufficiency challenge—alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme that falls outside First Amendment protection under controlling Supreme Court law (as interpreted in Ciminelli and Kousisis). Put another way, the panel found there was no need to reach a free‑speech ruling because the indictment alleges unprotected fraud, and a Rule 12 sufficiency review is the wrong vehicle to litigate factual or facial First Amendment defenses that do not plainly appear on the face of the indictment.

Why the panel took that approach (point by point, with citations to the opinion)

- Standard for a Rule 12 sufficiency review. The court begins from the settled Rule 12/indictment sufficiency rules: it must take the indictment allegations as true and decide only whether they state each element of the offense. (Op. § II (citing Rafoi, Fontenot, Gaytan).) That framework limits the panel to whether the charged elements of § 1348 are plausibly alleged, not to resolving contested factual defenses or constitutional claims that depend on evidentiary development.

- Fraudulent‑inducement is not protected speech. The panel relied on Ciminelli and Kousisis to explain that the government charged a fraudulent‑inducement theory (not the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli). Under Kousisis, a fraudulent inducement that uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property protects “money and property,” and therefore is a form of fraud that is outside First Amendment protection. The opinion states that the indictment “alleges defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property,” and so it does not run afoul of Ciminelli. (Op. § III.A (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis).)

- No need to decide a free‑speech question when elements are plainly alleged. Because the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” and intent to obtain money/property (Op. § III intro and § III.B), the panel reversed the dismissal on sufficiency grounds and therefore had no occasion to decide whether any asserted First Amendment defense might be successful on the merits. In short, the alleged conduct fits squarely into the unprotected category of fraudulent conduct, so the court resolved the case under the statute and the fraud precedents rather than addressing a separate constitutional ruling.

When courts typically consider First Amendment defenses in § 1348 prosecutions

- At the Rule 12 stage only where the constitutional defect is apparent on the face of the charging instrument. A motion to dismiss on First Amendment grounds can succeed at Rule 12 if the indictment is fatally deficient as a matter of law (e.g., it charges protected pure opinion with no factual falsity or intent to defraud). But when an indictment alleges material false statements intended to induce payment or contract (a fraudulent‑inducement theory), courts will usually treat that as unprotected conduct and decline to find a facial First Amendment bar. (See Op. § II and § III.A–B.)

- Otherwise, First Amendment issues are often litigated later: on a more developed factual record at summary judgment (civil analog), at trial (through evidentiary rulings and jury instructions), or in criminal contexts via Rule 29 motions for judgment of acquittal or on appeal after trial. The panel’s procedure reflects that posture: because the indictment, as pleaded, alleges unprotected fraudulent inducement, the court did not need to resolve a free‑speech defense that would likely hinge on contested factual questions (what was said, whether it was knowingly false, intent, causation, etc.).

Bottom line: The panel did not ignore the First Amendment; it concluded the pleaded theory—fraudulent inducement to buy securities—alleges unprotected fraudulent conduct under Ciminelli/Kousisis. Given the Rule 12 sufficiency standard, there was no occasion to reach a separate First Amendment ruling at that stage. If defendants want to press a constitutional defense, the proper vehicles are those that can address the factual record and the exact nature of the speech (e.g., a motion to dismiss only if the indictment conclusively shows protected speech, a Rule 29 motion after trial, or appellate review), rather than a facial sufficiency challenge that accepts the indictment’s fraud allegations as true.

### What level of specificity must an indictment include to satisfy the double jeopardy notice function in a complex fraud scheme?
Question:
The panel recites that an indictment must ‘provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.’ For a multi-month ‘pump and dump’ scheme with many posts and trades, what factual particulars (timeframe, securities, statements, co-conspirators) are necessary so that a future prosecution would be barred?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit reaffirmed the ordinary indictment rules: an indictment must plead each material element of the offense and give the defendant fair notice and a double‑jeopardy defense. The opinion does not announce a new, rigid checklist of particulars (dates, every post, every trade) that must be included for a multi‑month pump‑and‑dump; instead it holds that an indictment that (1) alleges the fraudulent‑inducement theory, (2) pleads the scheme and intent to obtain money by causing victims to purchase securities, and (3) supplies enough factual specificity to identify the charged scheme will satisfy the double‑jeopardy/notice function. United States v. Constantinescu, slip op. §§ II–III (5th Cir.).

Relevant legal standard from the opinion
- The sufficiency test: an indictment must (1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges; and (3) provide a double‑jeopardy defense against later prosecutions. United States v. Gaytan, quoted at § II of the opinion.
- For securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the government must allege a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The “scheme” requires a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value, and the “intent” requires an intent to deceive and to cause some harm (or, under fraudulent‑inducement, an intent to obtain money/property — actual net loss is not required). See § III (discussion of Evans, Ciminelli, Kousisis).

What the opinion says about necessary factual specificity
- The opinion rejects a narrow “right‑to‑control” theory (Ciminelli) but accepts a fraudulent‑inducement theory when the indictment alleges the fraudster induced victims to hand over money/property (i.e., to buy securities). Id. § III.A.
- The court sustained the indictment based on allegations that defendants (summarized in Indictment ¶¶ 13–14): had large social‑media followings, held themselves out as traders, purchased securities, posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy (thereby pumping price), and secretly dumped positions to realize profits (alleged $114 million in profits). The Fifth Circuit treated those allegations as sufficient to plead the elements and to give notice. See § I and § III.A–B.

Practical import for what to include in an indictment to satisfy the double‑jeopardy notice function
The opinion shows what is required in principle even if it does not impose a hyper‑technical list. To ensure a later prosecution would be barred, an indictment should at minimum:

- Plead the theory of liability clearly: here, the fraudulent‑inducement theory (allegations that defendants used material misstatements to induce purchases of securities). (See § III.A.)
- Allege the offense elements: identify the scheme, the misrepresentations (their nature), the intent to obtain money/property, and the means by which victims were induced to part with money (e.g., purchase of securities). (See § III intro & § III.B.)
- Provide enough factual particularity to identify the charged scheme in time and scope: the opinion accepted allegations describing a multi‑actor, multi‑post “pump and dump” that covered multiple transactions and alleged a specific profit figure ($114 million). That suggests the court will tolerate some breadth where the indictment ties the defendants to a recognizable scheme rather than abstract wrongdoing. (See § I; reference to Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 and $114 million.)
- Identify actors and roles: naming defendants and alleging their conduct (posting, pumping, dumping) supports notice and a future‑prosecution bar because it ties the alleged scheme to specific people. (See § I.)
- Give temporal and transactional anchors (examples, not necessarily exhaustive): dates or date ranges, the securities or types of securities, representative posts or misstatements, and representative transactions or trades are the kinds of particulars that make clear what conduct is charged and thus what later prosecutions would duplicate. The opinion itself relies on allegations describing the multi‑month scheme, the posting practice, induced purchases, and profits rather than requiring a catalogue of every post or trade. (See § I and § III.)

What the opinion does not require
- The court did not say an indictment must list every post, every trade, or every victim to be sufficient. It accepted a complaint that alleged the scheme and concrete ways the fraud worked (posts induced purchases, followed by secret sales). Thus, absolute itemization of each act is not mandated by this opinion — but sufficient factual anchors are required so the defendant is fairly informed and a future prosecution can be assessed for double jeopardy. (Compare the court’s reliance on Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 with its discussion rejecting the right‑to‑control theory in Ciminelli, § III.A.)

Bottom line for drafting or evaluating an indictment in a complex pump‑and‑dump
- Make the theory explicit (fraudulent inducement), plead each statutory element, and include concrete factual allegations that tie the defendants to a defined scheme (who did what, by what means, to induce purchases, within what timeframe or transaction set, and with what economic result). That level of specificity is what the Fifth Circuit treated as adequate here to satisfy the double‑jeopardy/notice function; the court did not demand a literal inventory of every post and trade so long as the indictment identifies the charged scheme with sufficient factual content. (See §§ II–III and the Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 discussion.)

### How does conspiracy under § 1349 interact with the substantive § 1348 offense when the government proceeds on a fraudulent-inducement theory?
Question:
The indictment charges both § 1349 conspiracy and § 1348 substantive counts. If the fraudulent-inducement theory sets the property/intent contours for § 1348, do those same contours (no net-loss requirement; object to obtain money/property) define the conspiracy’s ‘unlawful objective’? Is proof of actual victim loss or reliance still irrelevant for § 1349?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes. When the government proceeds on a fraudulent‑inducement theory, the unlawful objective of a § 1349 conspiracy is the same sort of § 1348 objective described in the opinion: to obtain money or property by fraud (i.e., by inducing victims to part with money/property through material misrepresentations).
- No, proof of an actual net loss or that victims were economically worse off is not required to plead or prove the conspiracy. The Fifth Circuit (following Kousisis) treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as protecting money/property even when the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss or actual reliance that produces net loss.

Why — doctrinal explanation, tied to the opinion
- Elemental framework. The opinion reiterates that § 1348 requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” (III). An indictment must allege each material element of the offense (II). Conspiracy under § 1349 criminalizes agreement to commit the substantive offense, so the conspiracy’s unlawful objective must be the same substantive offense the government alleges (here, securities fraud under § 1348).
- Fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control. The court distinguishes Ciminelli’s rejected “right‑to‑control” theory from the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Ciminelli held you cannot base fraud on deprivation of valuable economic information alone; by contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory “protects money and property” because it alleges the defendant tricked victims into contracts that required handing over money or property (III.A). The indictment here alleges exactly that: defendants induced followers to buy securities so defendants could “dump” for profit (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14, cited in III.A). Thus the unlawful objective alleged for the conspiracy is to obtain money/property through that fraudulent inducement.
- Net‑loss and reliance. The opinion relies on Kousisis to hold that the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis holds that an offender can “obtain” money or property by inducing its transfer even if the victim later receives something of equal value or suffers no net pecuniary loss (III.B). The Fifth Circuit applied that holding to reject the defendants’ argument that an intent to harm victims’ bottom line is required; the indictment’s allegation that defendants “had money in mind” and induced purchases is sufficient (III.B). By parity, a § 1349 conspiracy charged on the fraudulent‑inducement theory need not allege or prove actual victim net loss or detrimental reliance as an element of the unlawful objective.
- Conspiracy mens rea. Although the opinion does not recite the separate conspiracy mens rea in detail, it treats the conspiracy charge as dependent on alleging a scheme and intent to defraud (summary at top and III). Practically, the government must allege an agreement to pursue the fraudulent‑inducement scheme (i.e., agreement to obtain money/property by fraudulent inducement) and the requisite intent to deceive/obtain (see III).

What to look for in pleadings/trial
- The indictment must allege the conspiracy’s unlawful objective in terms of obtaining money/property by fraud (not merely depriving victims of information). The opinion reverses the dismissal because the indictment did so (III.A).
- The government does not need to allege that victims suffered net pecuniary loss or that the fraudster intended to leave victims worse off; allegations that the conspirators intended to induce purchases and thereby obtain money/property suffice under Kousisis as applied in this opinion (III.B).

Bottom line
If the government proceeds on a fraudulent‑inducement theory, the conspiracy’s unlawful objective is defined by the fraudulent‑inducement contours explained in the opinion (i.e., obtaining money/property by inducing transfers through misrepresentations). Consistent with Kousisis as applied by this Fifth Circuit opinion, actual net loss or a showing that the fraudster intended to harm the victim’s bottom line is not a required element of either the § 1348 substantive offense or a § 1349 conspiracy predicated on that theory.

### What makes the alleged conduct satisfy the ‘in connection with the purchase or sale’ language of § 1348(2)?
Question:
The alleged misstatements were social-media posts about securities and the defendants’ positions. How close must the nexus be between the deceptive statements and the victims’ purchases to meet ‘in connection with’ under § 1348? Is the standard analogous to § 10(b), or is it broader/narrower in criminal prosecutions?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treats the § 1348(2) “in connection with the purchase or sale” language as satisfied when the alleged misrepresentations are the means by which the defendant fraudulently induced the victims to buy the securities — i.e., the tweets/posts were intended to (and are alleged to have) caused the followers to part with money or property by purchasing the stock. The court frames this as the fraudulent‑inducement theory (not the right‑to‑control theory): because the indictment alleges defendants used false social‑media posts to induce purchases (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14), that is enough to place the scheme “in connection with” the purchase or sale under § 1348. The court therefore applies a theory focused on the causal nexus between the deceit and the victims’ purchases rather than requiring a separate traditional property‑interest theory or actual net loss.

Explanation and legal doctrine (with citations to the opinion)
- What doctrine governs: The Fifth Circuit treats § 1348 as borrowing “key concepts” from mail/wire‑fraud jurisprudence and evaluates securities fraud under two elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud” (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and (2) intent to defraud. United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6; opinion §III (quoting Evans). The court relies on the Supreme Court’s distinction between (a) the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory in Ciminelli and (b) the valid “fraudulent‑inducement” theory endorsed in Kousisis. Op. §III.A–B.

- What “in connection with” means here: The indictment alleges defendants bought shares, then “pumped” the price by posting false and misleading social‑media posts so “other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and then “dumped” positions (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14). The panel holds that those allegations describe a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — the misrepresentations were the instrument by which victims were induced to make purchases and thus the scheme was “in connection with” the purchase. Op. §III.A (distinguishing Ciminelli and endorsing Kousisis’s fraudulent‑inducement theory).

- Causation/nexus required: The opinion requires a factual nexus in the indictment between the deception and the inducement to buy — i.e., the deceit must be alleged as intended to and actually inducing purchase of the security (see Indictment ¶ 13 and the court’s description that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security”). The court emphasizes that what matters is that the defendant sought to “obtain” money or property by inducing purchase (Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388–92, quoted at Op. §III.B).

- No separate requirement of net loss or a “traditional property‑interest” victim right to information: The panel rejects the district court’s dismissal reasoning that the indictment only deprived followers of valuable information (a right‑to‑control theory) and so failed to allege deprivation of a protected property interest. Op. §III.A (citing Ciminelli). Instead, by alleging fraudulent inducement to buy (and resulting profits for defendants), the indictment alleges deprivation of money/property. Op. §III.A–B. The court also notes the Supreme Court’s holding that an actual economic loss to the victim is not required for fraudulent‑inducement liability (Kousisis), so the presence of market‑value securities received by followers does not defeat the “in connection with” allegation. Op. §III.B.

Relation to § 10(b)
- The court does not undertake a detailed § 10(b) analysis, but it treats § 1348 as incorporating similar mail/wire‑fraud concepts and focuses on whether the misrepresentations were the means of inducing purchases. In practical terms, that is analogous to the causation/nexus inquiry courts apply under § 10(b) (did the misstatement have the requisite connection to the purchase or sale?), but the panel’s framing is squarely criminal and tied to the fraudulent‑inducement theory: if the misstatements are alleged to have induced the purchases (i.e., they were the instrument to obtain money/property), they satisfy “in connection with.” Op. §III (collecting authorities and relying on Kousisis).

Practical rule of thumb for pleading or evaluating an indictment under § 1348(2)
- To meet “in connection with the purchase or sale,” the indictment should allege (and here the panel accepted such allegations) that:
  1) the defendant made material misrepresentations on social media about the security or the defendant’s positions;
  2) the misrepresentations were intended to and were used to induce followers to purchase the security; and
  3) the inducement caused the followers to part with money or property (i.e., to buy the securities), allowing the defendant to obtain money/property by selling at inflated prices (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; Op. §III.A–B).

Bottom line
Under this opinion, the “in connection with” requirement is satisfied where the alleged social‑media lies are pleaded as the instrument by which investors were induced to buy securities — i.e., when the indictment alleges that the deceptive posts caused or were intended to cause purchases so defendants could obtain money/property. The court treats that as the proper criminal fraudulent‑inducement nexus (Kousisis), rather than the rejected right‑to‑control theory from Ciminelli.

### Why was aiding-and-abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 charged alongside primary § 1348 violations?
Question:
The indictment cites § 2. In coordinated ‘pump and dump’ schemes with multiple speakers and traders, what does the government gain by charging aiding and abetting in addition to principal liability, and does it change the required proof for participants who didn’t personally make the misstatements?
Answer:
Short answer: The government charged 18 U.S.C. § 2 (aiding and abetting) alongside principal § 1348 counts so it can reach defendants who knowingly assisted, encouraged, or facilitated the fraud even if they did not themselves utter the misstatements. Section 2 is an alternative/participation theory — it does not change the substantive elements of securities fraud (a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud), but it changes what the government must prove about a particular defendant’s role: rather than proving the defendant personally made the material misrepresentation, the government need only prove the defendant knowingly and intentionally aided the principal who did.

Why that makes sense here (grounded in the opinion)
- The indictment alleges a coordinated “pump and dump” scheme: multiple social‑media actors “post[ed] false and misleading information” to induce followers to buy securities, then sold their positions (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14, quoted at pp. 1–2 of the opinion). The Court treated these allegations as alleging a single fraudulent scheme and intent to obtain money by inducing purchases (pp. 3–7).
- Given multiple speakers and traders, charging only as principals would require the government to prove each defendant personally made the fraudulent misrepresentations. By pleading § 2 as well, the government can prosecute defendants who “aided, counseled, commanded, induced or procured” the commission of the § 1348 offense — for example, by coordinating posts, sharing scripts, arranging trades, or otherwise facilitating the misstatements and sales.
- The opinion makes clear that the indictment must allege the scheme and intent to defraud (the substantive elements of § 1348) and that those elements are met here (pp. 3–7). Section 2 does not alter those substantive requirements; it supplies the mode of culpability for participants who did not personally utter the misstatements.

What the government must prove for an aider/abettor
- The Court’s analysis of § 1348 emphasizes that securities fraud requires (1) a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and (2) intent to defraud (intent to deceive and cause harm or obtain money/property) (pp. 3–4). Those elements remain the core of the offense the government must prove.
- For a § 2 charge, the government must prove that the defendant (a) took some act in furtherance of the offense (affirmative assistance, encouragement, or facilitation) and (b) did so with the knowledge of the unlawful scheme and with intent to bring it about (i.e., the mental state sufficient for aiding and abetting). In practice, that means proving the defendant knew about the fraudulent scheme alleged in the indictment (the coordinated pumps and sales) and intentionally helped it succeed.
- The opinion treats the indictment’s allegations about coordinated posts and profit motive as sufficient to allege intent to obtain money by fraud (pp. 4–7). Those same allegations, if proved at trial, would support aiding‑and‑abetting liability for those whose role was facilitation rather than direct statement.

Practical effects
- Charging § 2 is largely prosecutorial flexibility and redundancy: it preserves convictions if the evidence shows a defendant played a facilitating role but not that she personally made a fraudulent misrepresentation.
- It does not lower the government’s burden on the substantive elements of fraud. The indictment still must (and here, the Court found it did) allege the scheme and intent to defraud; for each defendant charged under § 2 the government must prove the defendant’s knowing, intentional assistance in that scheme.

Bottom line: The Fifth Circuit’s opinion confirms the government can pursue a fraudulent‑inducement theory against a coordinated group and may charge § 2 to reach facilitators. But § 2 is a theory of participation — it does not dispense with the § 1348 elements the Court requires the indictment to allege and, at trial, the government must prove both the underlying scheme and the defendant’s knowing, intentional role in enabling it (as reflected in the indictment’s allegations about coordinated posts, inducement to buy, and profit motive).

### Must the government identify specific victims or trades in the indictment to allege a ‘scheme to defraud’ of money or property?
Question:
The opinion treats ‘followers’ or ‘other investors’ as the victims induced to purchase. Is it sufficient at the indictment stage to allege a class of victims and a general time period, or must the indictment name specific purchasers, dates, and transactions to show the object was to obtain particular victims’ money or property?
Answer:
Short answer: No. Under this opinion the government need not plead the names of individual purchasers, dates, or every transaction so long as the indictment sufficiently alleges the elements of a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — i.e., a material misrepresentation, an intent to deceive, and an object of obtaining money or property by inducing persons to buy securities. Alleging a class of victims (here, “followers” or “other investors”) and the defendants’ scheme to induce those people to purchase is enough at the indictment stage.

Why: the court applied the usual indictment‑sufficiency rules (take the allegations as true; an indictment must allege each material element and fairly inform the defendant) (see III; Rafoi, Fontenot, Gaytan citations). The Fifth Circuit found the superseding indictment properly alleged that defendants: (1) purchased securities, (2) posted false and misleading social‑media statements to “pump” the price so “other investors were induced to purchase,” and (3) “dumped” their positions to profit — including a $114 million profit allegation (Indictment ¶¶13–14; III.A). Those allegations, the court held, state a fraudulent‑inducement theory that protects money/property (unlike the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli) and therefore adequately alleges a scheme to obtain victims’ money (III.A).

The court also rejected the argument that the government must show the defendants intended to harm victims’ bottom line; Kousisis confirms that an intent to obtain the victim’s money or property — even if the victim receives something of value in return or is not left economically worse off — satisfies the intent element (III.B; discussion of Kousisis). The indictment’s allegation that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” was enough to allege both scheme and intent (III.B).

Practical limits/caveat: an indictment still must allege the material elements and “fairly inform” the defendants (Gaytan). If the government’s theory required victim‑specific allegations to establish an element (for example, if liability turned on a particular investor’s reliance or some statutory element tied to identified victims), then more detail might be necessary. But nothing in this opinion requires naming individual purchasers, transaction dates, or line‑item trades when the indictment otherwise alleges a coherent fraudulent‑inducement scheme directed at a class of victims over a defined period.
