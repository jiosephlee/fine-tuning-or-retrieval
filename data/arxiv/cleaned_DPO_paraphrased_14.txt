\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and develop some reasoning capabilities, yet their behavior remains challenging to direct due to the entirely unsupervised fashion of their training.
To introduce controllability, current approaches rely on gathering human assessments of the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the original LM to reflect these preferences, frequently utilizing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF involves a complicated and frequently unstable process: first, constructing a reward model that captures human preferences, and subsequently applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large LM to maximize this estimated reward while maintaining proximity to the base model.
In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model in RLHF, enabling the derivation of the associated optimal policy in closed form, and thus reducing the conventional RLHF objective to a straightforward classification loss.
Our method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and computationally simple, removing the necessity for language model sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter searches.
Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can adapt LMs to human preferences as effectively as, or better than, alternative approaches. Specifically, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling generation sentiment and achieves equal or improved performance on summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being significantly easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these LMs learn from data produced by people with diverse intentions, values, and levels of expertise. Some of these human goals and skills might not be ideal for imitation; for instance, although it is useful for an AI programming assistant to \textit{recognize} typical coding errors so it can fix them, we still want the model to prioritize the (often infrequent) examples of expert programming found in its training corpus when producing code. Likewise, we may wish our language model to be \textit{informed} about a widespread misconception held by half the population, but we definitely do not want it to repeat this misconception as fact in half of its answers to related questions! Thus, ensuring that the model consistently gives \emph{appropriate responses and exhibits the right behavior}—as opposed to merely showcasing its broad \textit{knowledge and competencies}—is essential for developing AI that is reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While common techniques use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL-based objective often used can, in fact, be solved directly through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which significantly streamlines the preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches teach language models desired behaviors by leveraging carefully collected sets of human preferences that capture what humans perceive as safe and useful conduct. This process of learning preferences takes place after an initial phase of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a large-scale text corpus. The simplest way to incorporate preferences is to apply supervised fine-tuning on human-curated demonstrations exemplifying strong responses. However, the most effective family of techniques centers around reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods first train a reward model using a dataset of human preferences, and then employ reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy so it generates outputs that earn high reward, all while keeping it sufficiently close to the base model. Although RLHF yields models with remarkable abilities in conversation and code generation, this training pipeline is far more involved than standard supervised learning: it necessitates training several language models and repeatedly sampling from the policy during training, resulting in high computational demands.

This work demonstrates how to optimize a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same objective as standard RLHF techniques (maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint), yet remains easy to implement and train. Essentially, DPO updates the model by raising the log probability ratio of preferred versus non-preferred outputs, but crucially incorporates an adaptive, example-specific importance weight to avoid the model collapse seen with simpler probability ratio methods. Similar to other techniques, DPO employs a theoretical preference model—such as the Bradley-Terry model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}—to assess how well a reward function reflects actual preference data. However, whereas conventional methods use the preference model to create a loss for training a reward model and then optimize a policy against that learned reward, DPO applies a variable substitution that enables direct computation of the preference loss based on the policy itself. Using a dataset of human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO can thus train a policy via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function tailored to the preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that enables training language models using preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Our experimental results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current techniques—including PPO-based RLHF—when applied to preference-based tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and conversational modeling, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size have demonstrated the ability to complete certain tasks without any prior examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or with just a handful of prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning them on collections of instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', allows LLMs to generalize to new instructions beyond those seen during tuning and generally boosts their usefulness \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction-tuning has proven successful, gathering \textit{comparative} human feedback on response quality is often simpler than collecting expert-labeled examples. As a result, recent research has focused on refining LLMs using datasets built from human preferences, leading to advances in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these approaches begin by training a neural reward model to match the preference data, often using a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. The language model is then further trained to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related techniques \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely related strategy involves using LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback to generate new synthetic preference data for specific traits such as harmlessness or safety \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying on minimal human oversight in the form of written evaluation rubrics for model outputs. These methodologies reflect the intersection of two main research directions: training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse tasks~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the general study of learning from human preference data \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}.  
While leveraging relative human feedback is attractive, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models presents significant practical obstacles; this work introduces a theoretically grounded alternative for optimizing with relative preferences that avoids RL.

Outside the realm of language, learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of several methodologies. In contextual bandit learning, when preferences or rankings over actions replace rewards, the setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute rewards, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the standard optimal policy concept with the \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, while CDBs typically provide preference feedback online, human preference learning is usually performed on a fixed offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In the same vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) utilizes binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} 'scoring' function, instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. PbRL encompasses a range of algorithms, some of which enable the reuse of offline preference data, but most approaches start by modeling the latent scoring function (the reward model) and then perform policy optimization with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-stage approach that directly learns a policy aligned with the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This approach typically consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by further training a pre-trained LM through supervised learning using carefully curated data tailored to the target task(s) (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: In this stage, the SFT model is provided with prompts $x$, generating answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These response pairs are evaluated by human annotators, who indicate their preference, denoted by $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the selected and non-selected completions from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these human preferences are governed by an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various statistical models can be used to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a standard choice (though broader ranking schemes like Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can be used if multiple answers are ranked). According to the BT model, the distribution over human choices $p^*$ is defined by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of human comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we can define a parameterized reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters using maximum likelihood estimation. By viewing this as a binary classification task, the loss function corresponds to the negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
with $\sigma$ denoting the sigmoid function. In language model applications, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is usually initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with a linear projection layer added on top of the last transformer block to output a scalar reward score \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To stabilize the reward estimates, previous studies typically normalize the rewards such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning phase, the language model receives guidance from the previously trained reward function. Building on earlier research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem can be stated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ tunes how much the policy diverges from the reference distribution $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ begins as $\pisft$. Incorporating the KL divergence penalty is essential, as it restricts the model from straying excessively from the data distribution where the reward model is valid, while also promoting diversity in the outputs and avoiding convergence to only a handful of high-reward responses. Because text generation is inherently discrete, this objective cannot be directly differentiated and is usually handled through reinforcement learning. The prevalent strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and employing PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} for optimization.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization based directly on preferences. In contrast to traditional RLHF approaches, which first train a reward model and then optimize it using RL, our method utilizes a specific form of reward model parameterization that allows us to analytically derive its optimal policy, eliminating the need for an RL loop. 

As we elaborate below, the main idea is to exploit a closed-form correspondence between reward functions and their optimal policies. This lets us reframe the optimization problem: instead of minimizing a loss over reward models, we can define an equivalent loss over policies. 

By applying this change-of-variables technique, we bypass the need to fit a separate reward model while still optimizing with respect to established preference models, such as the Bradley-Terry model. In summary, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the underlying (implicit) reward signal.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard reinforcement learning objective as in previous research, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, for a generic reward function $r$. As established in earlier studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be shown that the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the normalization constant. For the full derivation, see Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when utilizing the MLE-based reward estimator $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, estimating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which complicates the practical use of this formulation. To address this, we can manipulate Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization $Z(\cdot)$. In particular, by taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
Applying this transformation to the actual reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$, we note that the Bradley-Terry model is a function of the difference in rewards between two outputs, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Replacing $r^*(x,y)$ in the preference formula using Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} eliminates the partition function, enabling us to formulate the human preference probability using only the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies the preference relation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A detailed derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived for the Bradley-Terry model, analogous results can be obtained for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the human preference probabilities now expressed in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy objective is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this formulation, we are implicitly learning a reward function through a different parameterization, where the resulting optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, because this approach is mathematically equivalent to fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits useful theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about how the preference data is distributed \cite{bong2022generalized}. Additional discussion of DPO’s theoretical aspects compared to other methods can be found in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To build a mechanistic intuition for DPO, it's helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns more weight when reward predictions are incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{pushes up likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduces likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
with $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ representing the reward signal implicitly specified by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). In essence, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ encourages the model to boost the probability of the preferred completions $y_w$ while lowering the probability of the less preferred completions $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is reweighted by how much higher the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ ranks the less preferred answer, modulated by $\beta$, which reflects how badly the model misorders the candidates, taking into account the KL regularization strength. Our findings highlight the significance of this reweighting: omitting it, as in a simple version of the approach, can cause the language model to collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO workflow proceeds as: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate two completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate pairs according to human preferences to assemble the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the DPO objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the chosen $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and hyperparameter $\beta$.

In real-world scenarios, it is often desirable to leverage existing public preference datasets instead of creating new samples and collecting fresh human preference data. As these datasets are typically constructed with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we instead define $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$: specifically, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy aims to reduce distribution mismatch between the actual, unknown reference distribution and the proxy $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation notes and hyperparameter choices can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we delve deeper into the DPO approach, offering theoretical justification and clarifying its benefits. We also discuss how the strengths of DPO address some of the challenges encountered with actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO achieves policy learning through a unified maximum likelihood objective, avoiding the need to explicitly fit a reward model or employ RL. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} aligns with a Bradley-Terry model, where the rewards are represented as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our optimization focuses directly on the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which corresponds to reward model training described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after a change of variables. This section develops the theoretical framework for this reparameterization, demonstrates that it does not restrict the set of possible learned reward models, and enables the precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, which divides the set of reward functions into equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions that belong to the same family yield identical distributions over preferences.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical optimal policies for the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
The arguments are straightforward, so we postpone the details to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-known ambiguity, referred to as under-specification, present in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is standard practice to introduce extra identifiability constraints to obtain any guarantees on maximum likelihood estimation based on Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within the same equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy. As a result, our ultimate objective is to recover just one representative reward function from the optimal class. We demonstrate the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild conditions, every reward equivalence class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including Bradley-Terry as a special case) can be written using the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ denote an arbitrary reward function with induced optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We now establish that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed in the form specified above. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, the operator $f$ normalizes $r(x, y)$ using the logarithm of the corresponding partition function for $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ still belongs to the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward function), we find that $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, $f$ produces a member of the equivalence class with the required form, showing the reparameterization covers all cases needed in our reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying precisely which reward function within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization: the one that fulfills
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are non-negative and sum to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} describes the normalization constant (partition function) for the optimal policy generated by $r(x, y)$.
The central insight of the DPO approach is that by applying particular constraints to the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) class of preference models, we maintain the expressiveness of the reward model class, yet directly enforce the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} to be analytically solvable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instabilities}

Our framework also allows us to analyze the instability issues present in standard actor-critic approaches utilized in RLHF, including PPO. Adhering to the RLHF methodology, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We can relate this problem to the control as inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we have a parametric policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, and we seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\|\pi^*(y\mid x)]$, with $\pi^*$ representing the optimal policy (as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model}) determined by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through further manipulation, the resulting optimization target is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}

This objective matches the form optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} when applying the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward function $r_{\phi}$. In this context, we can view the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. While this normalization does not change the optimal policy, omitting it can result in a policy gradient with large variance and thus unstable training dynamics. One remedy is to approximate this normalization using a learned value function, though this is often challenging to train effectively. As another workaround, prior research has employed reward normalization via a human completion baseline, which acts as a Monte Carlo estimate (using a single sample) of the normalization constant. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization naturally gives rise to a reward function that is baseline-free.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we experimentally assess how well DPO can learn policies directly from preference data. We begin by examining a controlled text-generation scenario to investigate how effectively DPO balances the objectives of maximizing reward and minimizing KL-divergence from a reference policy, relative to widely-used preference optimization methods like PPO. Following this, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF problems, such as tasks involving summarization and dialogue. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or outperforms strong baselines like PPO-based RLHF, as well as surpassing the best-of-$N$ trajectories sampled with a learned reward. Prior to discussing these outcomes, we outline our experimental framework; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We assess our methods across three distinct open-domain text generation tasks. Across all settings, models learn a policy based on a collection of preference data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ serves as a movie review prompt from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the model's objective is to generate $y$ with positive sentiment. To facilitate objective assessment, we synthetically construct preference pairs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large to the IMDb training set until it converges (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for more). For \textbf{summarization}, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the task is to produce a summary $y$ capturing the central ideas. In line with prior studies, we leverage the Reddit TL;DR summarization corpus \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human-labeled preference data curated by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT baseline uses a model further trained on human-generated post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} within the TRLX RLHF toolkit \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The preference annotations were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on samples from a related SFT model. For the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a user prompt, ranging from scientific questions to personal advice. The model's challenge is to deliver a useful, engaging reply $y$; we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which features 170k human–assistant conversations. Each dialogue concludes with two model-generated responses and a human annotation indicating the preferred reply. Since a pre-trained SFT model is unavailable in this case, we construct the SFT baseline by additional training on the preferred responses from the dataset using a general-purpose language model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we conduct controlled sentiment generation experiments, measuring each algorithm’s trade-off between achieved reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy. This trade-off, or frontier, can be directly computed since we possess the ground-truth reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical scenarios where the actual reward function is unavailable, we rely on the \textit{win rate} of each algorithm when compared to a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 acts as a surrogate for human judgment, evaluating summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of the reference summaries from the test split, while for dialogue, the baseline is the test set's preferred response. Although prior research indicates that LMs can outperform traditional metrics as automatic evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we supplement our approach with a human study—detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}—to validate GPT-4’s reliability. Our findings show that GPT-4’s assessments are highly correlated with those of human evaluators, with human-GPT-4 agreement matching or exceeding the typical agreement between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a range of established strategies for training language models in accordance with human preferences. For the summarization task, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, and for the dialogue task, we employ 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. Additionally, we evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model, and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$—sourced from either the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a standard language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another related supervised approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, where the model is trained to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ and explicitly reduce the likelihood of $y_l$, incorporating an optional scaling factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ for the unlikelihood component. We also include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} utilizing a reward model trained on human preferences, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method that optimizes for the actual reward function in the controlled sentiment scenario. For sentiment-based experiments, we employ two PPO-GT variants: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that applies reward normalization and fine-tunes hyperparameters for enhanced results (these changes are similarly adopted for PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we investigate the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, where $N$ completions are generated from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), and the one with the highest score from a learned reward model is chosen. While this strategy often yields strong performance by separating reward modeling from PPO optimization, it is computationally costly for larger $N$, as it necessitates generating $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates as assessed by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH one-turn dialogue; DPO stands out as the sole approach surpassing chosen summaries within the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured for various sampling temperatures throughout training progression. The advantage provided by DPO over dataset labels remains relatively steady for different sampling temperatures as training advances.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective, commonly employed in RLHF methods, serves to maximize reward while ensuring the policy remains close to a reference policy. Consequently, when comparing different algorithms, it is important to evaluate both the obtained reward and the associated KL divergence; gaining marginally higher reward at the expense of much larger KL is often suboptimal. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-versus-KL tradeoff for several algorithms within the sentiment task. For each algorithm, we conduct several training runs, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seed selection for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training iterations, up to convergence, we assess each policy on a fixed test set, reporting both the mean reward under the actual reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, this refers to the sum of the per-step KL-divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results demonstrate that DPO attains the most favorable reward-KL frontier by a significant margin, yielding the highest reward while keeping KL low. This outcome is striking for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO target the same objective, DPO is substantially more efficient, as its reward/KL profile consistently outperforms that of PPO. Second, DPO achieves a superior reward-KL frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even in the scenario where PPO uses the actual ground-truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, standard automatic metrics like ROUGE may not align well with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO on human preference data leads to more useful summaries. We assess different approaches by generating outputs on the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the mean win rate versus reference outputs. For each method, completions are sampled with temperatures ranging from 0.0 up to 1.0, with the resulting win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO reaches an approximate 61\% win rate at a temperature of 0.0, surpassing PPO's peak performance of around 57\% at its optimal temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a greater maximum win rate than the best of $N$ baseline. It is worth mentioning that we did not perform any substantial tuning of the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO, so these outcomes may not fully reflect its capabilities. Furthermore, DPO displays increased resilience to changes in sampling temperature relative to PPO, whose effectiveness can drop to that of the original GPT-J model when temperatures are high. The Preferred-FT approach does not yield substantial gains over the SFT baseline. We further conduct a direct comparison of DPO and PPO using human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where human raters favored DPO samples generated at temperature 0.25 in 58\% of cases over those from PPO at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess the various approaches using the subset of the test split from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} that contains one round of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 assessments, we use the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rate of each method. Since there is no established SFT model for this benchmark, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions so that outputs remain in-distribution, and then continue training with DPO. We also benchmark against the top-scoring completion out of 128 Preferred-FT samples (our experiments show that the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at 128 completions for this setting; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the base Pythia-2.8B model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the best performance across the optimal temperature for each approach. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained using PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but are unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B. Considering our TL;DR results and that both strategies optimize for the same reward function, we regard Best of 128 as a coarse stand-in for PPO-level performance. On the whole, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient approach that consistently outperforms the preferred completions from the Anthropic HH dataset and achieves performance on par with or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}, DPO also achieves its peak performance after relatively few steps.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers an effective and scalable approach for developing robust and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward method for training language models from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning techniques. Instead of reframing the preference learning task to fit a conventional RL paradigm just to leverage existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, making it possible to train language models to align with human preferences using a basic cross-entropy loss—bypassing both reinforcement learning and any compromise in expressiveness. Remarkably, DPO achieves performance comparable to or surpassing established RLHF approaches such as those utilizing PPO, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment; as a result, DPO significantly lowers the obstacles to training language models using human preference data.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings highlight multiple key avenues for subsequent investigation. One open question is how well the DPO policy extrapolates to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward signal. Although our preliminary analysis indicates that DPO policies can achieve generalization on par with PPO-based methods, a more thorough evaluation is warranted. Another interesting direction is to examine whether self-labeling using the DPO policy can leverage unlabeled prompts as effectively. Additionally, it is important to explore how reward over-optimization presents itself within the direct preference optimization paradigm, and whether the modest drop in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is indicative of this phenomenon. Our current experiments focus on models up to 6B parameters; scaling DPO to substantially larger, state-of-the-art architectures represents a promising research trajectory. On the evaluation side, we note that GPT-4-computed win rates are sensitive to prompt formulation; further research could investigate optimal strategies for eliciting accurate, high-quality judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO holds significant potential for applications extending beyond preference-driven language model training, such as the training of generative models across other data modalities.
