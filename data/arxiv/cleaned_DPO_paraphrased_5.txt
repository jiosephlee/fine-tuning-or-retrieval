\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and some reasoning abilities, it remains challenging to precisely steer their outputs due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training process. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve gathering human annotations that indicate the relative preference between model outputs and then fine-tuning the original unsupervised LM to better reflect these preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable method that first requires training a reward model to approximate human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM so that it maximizes the predicted reward while avoiding excessive divergence from the base model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF, which permits direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby allowing the standard RLHF objective to be addressed through a straightforward classification loss.}  
The approach we develop, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency by removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} conducting LM sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter optimization. Empirical results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs to human preferences as effectively or better than prevailing techniques. In particular, DPO-based fine-tuning surpasses PPO-driven RLHF in controlling sentiment in generated text and achieves comparable or improved quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being significantly easier to implement and train.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on massive datasets, demonstrate unexpectedly advanced abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects diverse aims, values, and expertise. Some of these aims and skills might not be ideal to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to steer the model toward the (possibly uncommon) high-quality coding examples embedded in its training data. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{conscious} of a widespread misconception held by roughly half of the population, yet we certainly do not want the model to present this false belief as accurate in half of its responses on the topic. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad spectrum of \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches typically use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed preferred behaviors into a language model by utilizing carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect the kinds of behaviors considered safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial stage involving large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest method for preference learning is supervised fine-tuning based on human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective approaches employ reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model on human preference data and then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy, encouraging responses that receive high rewards while preventing significant deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding capabilities, its workflow is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the policy during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF algorithms—maximizing rewards while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint—but with a simpler implementation and more straightforward training process. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to less preferred ones, while applying a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting that mitigates model collapse observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to prior approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function corresponds to observed preference data. Yet, unlike traditional methods that use this preference model to create a loss for training a reward model followed by policy optimization, DPO leverages a change of variables to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Consequently, given human preference data over model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring the learning of an explicit, separate reward model or sampling from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated, reinforcement learning-free approach for training language models based on preferences. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably or better than current techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences in applications like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow in scale have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either without any examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or by using just a few examples provided as prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with the intentions of users can be substantially enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' equips large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the examples seen during fine-tuning and generally improves their practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality is often simpler than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, which has led to improvements in tasks like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural network reward function to align with the preference data, often modeled by frameworks such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Following this, the language model is fine-tuned to maximize this reward through reinforcement learning techniques, commonly including REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related avenue of research utilizes LLMs already fine-tuned with human feedback for instruction following to produce additional synthetic preference datasets targeting specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics for the LLM’s evaluations. These strategies unify two main research threads: one focusing on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; this paper proposes a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods proposed. Learning in contextual bandits using preferences or rankings over actions instead of numerical rewards is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When explicit rewards are unavailable, theoretical studies of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} other policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike the CDB setting where preference feedback is received in an online fashion, preference-based learning from humans typically relies on a fixed dataset of offline preference-annotated pairs of actions \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates on binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been developed, including some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but these generally require an initial step of explicitly estimating the latent scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing the policy \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a single-stage policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently discussed in works such as \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally involves three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-existing language model using supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.) in order to produce a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human evaluators who indicate their preference between the two responses, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ are the favored and less favored completions from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown to us. Several methods exist for capturing such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a commonly adopted approach (though more general ranking frameworks like the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be integrated if multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the probability distribution of human preference $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_2)\bigr)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, a parametric reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be learned by maximizing the likelihood. Treating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the setting of language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the pretrained SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by a linear layer added on top of the final transformer output to yield a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the reward estimates, previous studies often normalize the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been learned is employed to guide the language model through feedback. Building on previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ acts as a coefficient that regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model’s policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized to $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial because it restricts the model from straying excessively from the distribution where the reward model remains reliable, helps preserve output diversity, and avoids collapsing into a limited set of high-reward responses. Since language generation involves discrete outputs, this objective is non-differentiable and generally optimized through reinforcement learning techniques. The common strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} reformulates the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizes it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of deploying reinforcement learning techniques on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and then apply RL to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the reward modeling phase and directly trains a language model from preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows for deriving the optimal policy in closed form, bypassing the need for an RL training cycle}.  

As will be detailed shortly, the central idea is to exploit an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling the conversion of a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to forgo building an explicit reward model}{circumvents the requirement to fit a separate, explicit reward model}, while still working within established frameworks for modeling human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously encodes both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) goal as previous studies, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which involves a generic reward function $r$. Following the approach from earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution maximizing the KL-constrained reward objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization term $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is known as the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even if we replace the true reward function $r^*$ with its maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practicality of this formulation. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ along with its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing us to represent the human preference probability solely through the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework obeys the preference model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The detailed derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, similar derivations hold for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as described in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling method (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{By doing so, we effectively eliminate the explicit reward modeling phase and avoid the complexities of reinforcement learning optimization.}{This approach fits an implicit reward through an alternative parameterization where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits favorable theoretical guarantees such as consistency given appropriate assumptions on the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate further on the theoretical aspects of DPO and its relation to other approaches in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ defines the reward implicitly through the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, this gradient update raises the probability of favored outputs $y_w$ while lowering the probability of less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each sample is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly ranks the less preferred outputs higher, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL regularization. Our experiments highlight the significance of this weighting, as a straightforward variant of this approach without the weighting factor can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO process involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and use human feedback to label them, thereby creating the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the given reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target parameter $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than producing new samples and collecting human annotations. Because these datasets are typically gathered using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not present, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, expressed as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional specifics regarding implementation and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper understanding of the DPO approach, present its theoretical justification, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered in actor-critic algorithms commonly employed in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO manages to circumvent both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the process of fitting a direct reward function} and reinforcement learning by utilizing a single maximum likelihood objective to train the policy. It is important to observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized in a manner equivalent to optimizing a reward model as described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once an appropriate change of variables is applied. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the scope of learnable reward functions, and prove that it permits exact identification of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated as follows:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the identical optimal policy when solving the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma reflects a common issue of under-specification inherent in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability constraints in order to obtain reliable guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma highlights that every reward function within the same equivalence class produces the same optimal policy, implying that for our ultimate goal, recovering any representative reward function from this optimal class suffices. The subsequent theorem is demonstrated in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, all reward classes consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed via the reparameterization 
    \[
        r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}
    \]
    for some model $\pi(y \mid x)$ along with a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed in the above reparameterized form. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function of $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original reward $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, which holds for any reward function, we obtain 
\[
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, $f$ maps $r$ onto a member of its equivalence class represented in the desired reparameterized form, ensuring no loss of generality in the reward modeling.
\end{sproof}
Another interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies the particular reward function within each equivalence class that the DPO reparameterization selects, namely, the reward function fulfilling:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y \mid x)\text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures $\pi(y \mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it follows that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$.
The fundamental insight behind the DPO method is that by applying specific constraints to the underdetermined Plackett-Luce (especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, we maintain the entire set of representable reward functions while simultaneously rendering the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly solvable for all input prompts $x$.

\subsection{Instability in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our framework can also be employed to analyze the instability issues present in common actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF workflow and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control as inference framework \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, we address the constrained RL problem defined in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, we aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} derived from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier studies  
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function of the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit large variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One way to incorporate the normalization term is via a learned value function; however, optimizing this can be challenging. Another approach, as done in prior work, normalizes rewards by employing a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reformulation provides a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly based on preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, in comparison to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks like RLHF using PPO and also outperforms selecting the best of $N$ sampled trajectories guided by a learned reward function. Prior to discussing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. In each experiment, the algorithms acquire a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, the input $x$ consists of the beginning of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to generate an output $y$ that conveys a positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, preference pairs are \textit{generated} by employing a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is trained to convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details are provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy must create a summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. We follow previous research by utilizing the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preferences collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The human preference annotations were sourced from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} based on samples from a related, yet differently-trained, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query, which can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for advice on relationships. The policy is expected to produce a response $y$ that is both engaging and helpful. We leverage the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation ends with two responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the response favored by humans. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available in this setting, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred responses to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure the frontier of attained reward against the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in real-world scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy, employing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons are made using reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the preferred responses in the test data serve as the baseline. Although prior work indicates that language models can outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate our use of GPT-4 through a human study described in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4’s evaluations align closely with human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 often matching or exceeding the agreement levels observed between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of existing techniques aimed at training language models to better align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task, as well as 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Moreover, we analyze the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, the latter being fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$, which originates either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach employing pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing the likelihood of $y_l$; an optional weighting factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is applied to the unlikelihood component. Additionally, we include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained using a reward function derived from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that utilizes the ground truth reward function accessible in the controlled sentiment experiments. For the sentiment tasks, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are also applied when running conventional PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ candidate outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the response with the highest score based on a reward model trained on the preference dataset. Although this approach effectively separates reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$, as it requires generating $N$ completions for every input at inference time.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogues; among all methods, only DPO shows improvement over the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. The enhancement provided by DPO relative to the dataset labels remains consistently stable during training at different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the divergence of the policy from a reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; a marginally higher reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-KL tradeoff frontier across several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we perform multiple training trials, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, as well as different random seeds for preferred-FT). This set comprises a total of 22 runs. At intervals of every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy using a collection of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward based on the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes the most effective frontier by a wide margin, achieving the greatest reward while maintaining a low KL divergence. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates significantly higher efficiency, with its reward/KL balance strictly outperforming that of PPO. Secondly, DPO attains a superior frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO is provided with ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO guided by human preferences can yield better summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions from the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test split and calculate the average win rate against the reference completions provided in the test set. The outputs for each method are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models fine-tuned by DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings indicate that DPO achieves roughly a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s best performance of about 57\% at the same temperature setting. Furthermore, DPO attains a higher peak win rate compared to the strongest $N$ baseline. It is worth noting that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, so these outcomes may undervalue DPO’s full capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates greater stability across different sampling temperatures relative to PPO, whose performance tends to decline to that of the original GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the initial SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also present a direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO, revealing that samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split that contains a single round of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations employ the preferred completions from the test set as the benchmark to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions to ensure the completions align with the model’s distribution, and subsequently train it using DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the best result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline stabilizes at 128 completions for this task; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the base Pythia-2.8B, observing that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperature settings for each method. We also test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that improves upon the baseline Pythia-2.8B’s performance. Drawing on our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that enhances performance beyond the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and offers comparable or superior results to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively swiftly.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language models to be trained from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a straightforward cross-entropy loss, without any need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current RLHF techniques, including those utilizing PPO; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several critical questions that warrant further investigation. In particular, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to approaches that learn from explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based approaches, yet more thorough analyses are necessary. For instance, can self-labeling techniques applied to the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts as well? Another aspect to explore is how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the slight performance dip shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is a reflection of this phenomenon. Moreover, although our experiments involve models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models that are several orders of magnitude larger represents a promising avenue for future work. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt used; future studies might focus on identifying the optimal strategies to obtain reliable evaluations from automated systems. Lastly, DPO’s potential applications extend well beyond language model training from human preferences and include training generative models across various other modalities.
