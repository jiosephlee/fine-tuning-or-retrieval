\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely directing their output remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches to achieve such control involve collecting human judgments on the comparative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to conform to these preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF is a complicated and frequently unstable process, which first requires training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM through reinforcement learning to optimize this predicted reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. \rev{In this work, we utilize a relationship between reward functions and optimal policies to demonstrate that this constrained reward maximization can be \emph{exactly optimized} in a single policy training step, effectively reducing to a classification problem over human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits the direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF objective to be addressed through a straightforward classification loss.} Our approach, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{training a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results indicate that DPO can fine-tune language models to better align with human preferences, matching or outperforming current methods. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and equals or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being notably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data encompassing diverse aims, priorities, and skill levels. Some of these aims and skills might not be ideal to replicate; for instance, while we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we also prefer it to favor producing (sometimes rare) high-quality code found in its training examples. Similarly, a language model might need to \textit{know about} a common misconception held by roughly half of the population, but it should definitely not assert that misconception as true in half of the related responses. Put differently, choosing the model’s \emph{appropriate behaviors and outputs} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches often guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective in these methods can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed the desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors considered safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training step conducted on an extensive text corpus. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-generated examples of high-quality responses, the most effective approaches utilize reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference data and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate outputs with high reward while maintaining similarity to the original model. Despite producing models with strong conversational and programming capabilities, the RLHF framework is substantially more intricate than supervised fine-tuning, requiring training multiple language models and incorporating policy sampling within the training loop, which leads to considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model so that it aligns with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF techniques (maximizing rewards under a KL-divergence constraint), yet remains easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to less favored ones, while applying a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid the model collapse that often arises from a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates how well a given reward aligns with observed preference data. However, unlike prior approaches that first define a preference loss to train a reward model and then optimize a policy based on that reward, {\methodac} reparameterizes the preference loss as a direct function of the policy. Consequently, given human preference data on model outputs, {\methodac} can optimize the policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without training a separate explicit reward model or requiring policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function derived from the preference data}.

The primary contribution of this work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Our experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across various tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, employing language models with sizes up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow in size demonstrate the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal examples provided in prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user objectives can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond the original fine-tuning dataset, thereby improving their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has proven successful, collecting \textit{relative} human judgments of response quality tends to be simpler than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, later studies have fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, enhancing their capabilities in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically begin by training a neural reward model to align with the preference data according to a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction utilizes LLMs already fine-tuned for instruction adherence with human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference data focused on specific characteristics like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision through textual rubrics for the model’s annotations. These strategies represent a synthesis of two research streams: one focused on reinforcement learning for training language models across diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preference data \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains practically challenging; this paper proposes a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without the need for reinforcement learning.

Beyond the realm of language, the problem of deriving policies from preference information has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of proposed methods. Learning in contextual bandits by leveraging preferences or rankings over actions instead of explicit rewards is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute reward signals are unavailable, theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy that has an expected probability of winning against \textit{any} competing policy of at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, preference feedback is obtained in an online manner, whereas preference-based learning from humans typically relies on a fixed offline dataset of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of direct reward values \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There exist several PbRL algorithms, some of which can incorporate off-policy preference data, but most approaches first explicitly estimate this latent scoring (reward) function and then optimize it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our method introduces a single-step policy learning strategy that directly optimizes the policy to conform with the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently discussed in works such as \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process typically involves three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and learning a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model through supervised learning, using high-quality datasets tailored to the specific downstream tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is queried with prompts $x$ to generate answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference for one of the responses, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ refer to the favored and less favored completions from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various techniques exist to model such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly used approach (though more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also fit within this framework when multiple ranked answers are available). According to the BT model, the human preference probability $p^*$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison tuples $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating the task as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the setting of language models, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is often initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear output layer atop the final transformer representation to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the learned reward function, prior methods typically normalize the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ r_{\phi}(x, y) \right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been trained is employed to guide the language model through feedback. Building on previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy is allowed to diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuning model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ starts off as $\pisft$. Incorporating this constraint is crucial to avoid the model straying too far from the distribution on which the reward function is reliable, while also preserving output diversity and preventing collapse into producing a narrow set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, the objective is non-differentiable and thus usually optimized through reinforcement learning methods. The common strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it via PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preferences directly. In contrast to earlier RLHF approaches that first train a reward model and then apply reinforcement learning to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly refines the language model using preference data}{exploits a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived analytically without requiring an RL training procedure}. 

As will be detailed shortly, our central idea is to use an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling the conversion of a loss function defined over rewards into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to omit the separate reward modeling stage}{circumvents the need to fit an independent reward model}, while still optimizing with respect to established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Effectively, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as in earlier studies, presented in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, using a general reward function $r$. Following the approach of previous works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution maximizing the KL-constrained reward in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant $Z(x)$ is defined as $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ of the true reward function $r^*$, computing $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making direct use of this form impractical. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can express the reward $r$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the reward differences between two outputs, namely, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, enabling the human preference probability to be written exclusively using the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} employs the Bradley-Terry model, similar derivations can be made for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the likelihood of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward while also eliminating the requirement for reinforcement learning optimization.}{Thus, we implicitly learn a reward through an alternative parameterization whose optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because this method effectively fits a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. In Section~\ref{sec:theory}, we provide a more detailed discussion of the theoretical aspects of DPO compared to related methods.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward estimate is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ encourages the model to increase the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the training samples are weighted according to how much the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the dispreferred outputs relative to the preferred ones, modulated by $\beta$, which reflects the influence of the KL regularization. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it leads to model degradation, as shown in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The standard workflow for DPO involves: 1) Drawing completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, annotating them with human preference labels to create the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ based on the chosen $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and hyperparameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human annotations. Because these preference datasets are typically obtained using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever this is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, that is, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy reduces the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed during DPO. Additional information on implementation details and hyperparameters is available in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
This section offers a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, presents theoretical support, and connects the benefits of DPO to challenges encountered by actor-critic methods applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by utilizing a single maximum likelihood objective to train the policy. It is important to recognize that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework with a reward representation given by $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Under this transformation, optimizing our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ aligns directly with the reward model optimization described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In this section, we will develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no limitations on the variety of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the precise retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class produce the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma reflects a well-recognized issue of under-specification associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to secure guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma confirms that all reward functions within a given equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy. Therefore, when targeting our ultimate objective, it suffices to recover any one reward function from the optimal equivalence class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under fairly general conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (specifically the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed through the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}
    \]
    for some policy $\pi(y \mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that an equivalent reward function can be represented via the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log-partition function of $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, valid for any reward, yields 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, the projection $f$ maps any reward function to an equivalent one of the desired form without any loss of generality.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies the particular reward function within each equivalence class selected by the DPO reparameterization. This reward function satisfies the constraint:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{, by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a proper probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it follows that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO method is that by imposing specific constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce (notably Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, we maintain the full expressive power of representable reward functions while rendering the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Challenges in Actor-Critic Algorithm Stability}

Our proposed framework can also be applied to identify instability issues in common actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We establish links to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained reinforcement learning problem presented in \ref{eq:RL}. Considering a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} defined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[ \underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective matches that optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which utilize a reward equivalent to the DPO formulation for the reward function class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not change the optimal policy solution, omitting it can lead to high variance in the policy gradient, thus destabilizing the training process. One way to handle the normalization term is through a learned value function, but this approach can present optimization challenges. Alternatively, prior work has employed normalization via a human completion baseline, effectively using a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalizing factor. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward structure that eliminates the need for such baseline adjustments.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO’s capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the maximization of reward against the minimization of KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, especially in comparison to popular preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF tasks, such as summarization and conversational agents. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines like RLHF with PPO and also outperforms methods that select the best trajectory out of $N$ samples according to a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the methods learn a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a partial movie review excerpted from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is to generate an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable controlled assessment, we \textit{construct} preference pairs based on outputs scored by a pre-trained sentiment classifier, such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is trained until convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the model must generate a concise summary $y$ capturing the post’s key points. Following previous research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} alongside human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model used here was fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and integrated with the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The human preference labels were obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from examples generated by a separate but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} setting, the input $x$ represents a user query, which can range from scientific inquiries to personal advice requests. The system must generate a relevant and engaging response $y$ to this query. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170,000 exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses produced by a large, though unspecified, language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the preferred reply by a human. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available for this task, we fine-tune an existing language model exclusively on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm’s performance based on the frontier of attained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be determined because the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is accessible. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline is the reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, it is the preferred response recorded in the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show a strong correlation between GPT-4’s evaluations and human judgments, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding the agreement observed among different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple established strategies for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task, and 2-shot prompting using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Additionally, we test the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned through supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (in tasks with controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach employing pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method includes an optional weighting parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood term. We also incorporate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, leveraging a reward function trained from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that learns using the true reward function accessible in the controlled sentiment experiments. For the sentiment tasks, we deploy two versions of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are similarly applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate completions from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and selects the top-scoring response based on a reward model trained on the preference dataset. While this method achieves strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even at moderate values of $N$, as it requires generating $N$ outputs per input during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on the Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue task; among the methods tested, only DPO demonstrates an improvement compared to the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked across different sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels across various sampling temperatures during the training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the policy’s divergence from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of substantially increased KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms in the sentiment analysis context. We perform multiple training iterations per algorithm, varying the hyperparameters that control policy conservativeness for each run (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. At every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy using a test prompt set, calculating the average reward based on the true reward function alongside the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO yields the most efficient frontier, achieving the highest reward while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates superior efficiency, with its reward/KL balance clearly outperforming PPO. Secondly, DPO attains a better frontier than PPO even when PPO has direct access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO in tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often exhibit weak alignment with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO using human preferences yields higher-quality summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions on the test partition of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the average win rate against the reference completions from the test set. For all methods, completions are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT methods each fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings indicate that DPO achieves roughly a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which attains about 57\% at its best temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO surpasses the maximum win rate achieved by the best of $N$ baseline methods. It is worth mentioning that we did not extensively optimize the $\beta$ parameter for DPO, implying that these results might underestimate its full capabilities. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can decline to the level of the original GPT-J model when temperatures are high. Preferred-FT shows only marginal improvement over the SFT baseline. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we provide a direct comparison between DPO and PPO based on human evaluations, revealing that samples from DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training}, which involves a single human-assistant exchange. GPT-4 evaluations utilize the preferred completions from the test set as a benchmark to calculate the win rates for each method. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions ensuring that outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and subsequently perform training with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the best-performing temperatures for each approach. We further evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that improves upon the base Pythia-2.8B model’s performance. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both techniques optimize the same reward signal, we treat Best of 128 as a rough estimate for PPO-level effectiveness. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that enhances performance beyond the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving results comparable or superior to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively swiftly.

\subsection{Confirming GPT-4 Evaluations Against Human Assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the accuracy of GPT-4’s evaluations, we perform a human subject study based on the TL;DR summarization task using two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, labeled as simple, only requests which summary better captures the key information of the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt, described as concise, asks additionally which summary is more succinct; this is motivated by our observation that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt compared to human preferences. Full prompt details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We carry out three pairwise evaluations involving the top-performing (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate (SFT, temperature 0.25) method, ensuring coverage across a spectrum of output qualities. Each is benchmarked against the greedily sampled PPO model at its optimal temperature setting. Results indicate that GPT-4’s judgments align with human raters about as frequently as humans agree with one another, implying GPT-4 serves as a reliable stand-in for human evaluation. (Note that due to a limited pool of human raters, multiple human annotations were collected only for comparisons involving DPO and PPO-1.) In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that better mirror human choices; accordingly, this prompt is employed for the primary analyses reported in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information on the human evaluation study, including the rater interface and volunteer list, is available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Preference-based learning provides an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables learning from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions. This allows training language models to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or exceeding current RLHF methods, including those using PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions for further investigation. In particular, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to methods that learn from an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with PPO-based approaches, but a more thorough analysis is warranted. For instance, is it possible that training with self-labeling using the DPO policy can similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Another area to explore is how reward over-optimization appears in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the minor performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right exemplifies this phenomenon. Moreover, although our experiments cover models up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models remains a promising avenue for future research. Concerning evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by the prompt design; subsequent studies might focus on identifying optimal strategies to obtain reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, the scope of DPO extends beyond training language models on human preferences, with potential applications in training generative models across different data modalities.
