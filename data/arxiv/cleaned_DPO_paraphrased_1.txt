\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their behavior remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve gathering human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, typically via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, requiring first the training of a reward model that captures human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the large unsupervised LM in order to maximize this estimated reward without deviating excessively from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby reducing the standard RLHF procedure to a straightforward classification objective.} The method we introduce, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as well as, or better than, existing techniques. Importantly, fine-tuning with DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs, while matching or enhancing quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, all with significantly simpler implementation and training requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from data produced by humans who have diverse aims, values, and expertise. Not all of these aims and skills are desirable to replicate; for instance, although we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we prefer to steer the model toward exhibiting the (sometimes uncommon) high-quality coding proficiency found within its training examples when it generates code. Likewise, we may want the language model to be \textit{cognizant} of a widespread misconception held by about 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want it to assert this misconception as true in half of all related queries! Put differently, carefully choosing the model's \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and skills} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches typically guide LMs toward aligning with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective commonly used can be precisely optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, substantially simplifying the process of preference learning.

At a broad level, current techniques incorporate preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected sets of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial stage of extensive unsupervised pre-training conducted on a large corpus of text. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-generated examples of high-quality responses, the most effective approaches employ reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference data, then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward scores while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF’s ability to yield models with strong conversational and programming skills, its process is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the model policy during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly align a language model with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches (maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint) while being easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to less preferred ones, integrating a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting to avoid model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio approach. Similar to existing techniques, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to quantify how well a reward function matches observed preference data. However, unlike previous methods that use this model to define a preference loss for training a reward model followed by policy optimization, {\methodac} reformulates the preference loss directly in terms of the policy through a change of variables. Thus, given human preference data over model outputs, {\methodac} can train a policy via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{eliminating the need to learn an explicit reward model or to sample from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, across various preference learning tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, employing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that scale up in size have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks without any examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or by using a few demonstration prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with human intent can be substantially enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-crafted completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often called `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond the fine-tuning data and improves their overall practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been effective, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality is typically simpler than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, resulting in better performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with the preference data under models like the Bradley-Terry preference framework \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then optimize the language model to maximize this reward through reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback on instruction following to synthesize additional preference datasets focused on specific attributes, including safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision via textual rubrics guiding the model’s annotations. This strategy merges two research areas: one centered on training language models with reinforcement learning across various goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another dedicated to general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, fine-tuning large-scale language models with reinforcement learning still presents significant practical difficulties; the present work offers a theoretically sound method to optimize relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Outside the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preference information has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods developed. When contextual bandit algorithms rely on preferences or rankings of actions instead of explicit rewards, this framework is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Due to the lack of absolute reward signals, theoretical treatments of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, in the CDB paradigm, preference feedback is received in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences often involves training on a fixed dataset of offline preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates by learning from binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} latent `scoring' function rather than direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been proposed, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data; these typically proceed by first explicitly modeling the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing the policy accordingly \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to satisfy given preferences in a single stage.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We provide an overview of the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This approach typically involves three key stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning for optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts with fine-tuning a pre-existing language model using supervised learning on carefully curated datasets relevant to the target tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, and others), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators who indicate their preference between the two answers, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the favored and less favored responses out of $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to capture such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a commonly used approach (though more flexible Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be integrated if multiple ranked outputs are available). The BT model posits that the true human preference distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. By treating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss function takes the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model settings, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the final transformer layer, producing a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the learned reward function, prior research often normalizes rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ r_{\phi}(x, y) \right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the language model receives feedback through the learned reward function. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the parameter $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial as it limits the policy's drift from the data distribution where the reward model performs reliably, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse to a few high-reward outputs. Because language generation is inherently discrete, this objective cannot be differentiated directly and is usually optimized via reinforcement learning techniques. The common practice \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. In contrast to earlier RLHF approaches that first learn a reward function and then perform RL to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly optimizes the language model using preference information}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that permits closed-form derivation of the optimal policy without requiring an RL training process}. 

As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, which allows converting a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to omit the explicit step of modeling rewards}{removes the need to fit a separate, explicit reward model} while still optimizing with respect to established human preference models such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as established in earlier studies, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which assumes a general reward function $r$. Building on previous research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it is straightforward to demonstrate that the optimal policy solving the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the normalization constant, or partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Although we might substitute the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practical utility of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can represent the reward $r$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends solely on the difference between rewards of two outputs, given by ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function cancels out, allowing the human preference probability to be formulated entirely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full proof can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived under the Bradley-Terry model, analogous formulations can be obtained for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the probability of human preference data with respect to the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to skip the explicit step of reward modeling and also removes the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In doing so, we implicitly learn a reward via an alternative parameterization whose optimal policy is directly given by $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from certain theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions regarding the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical insights about DPO in comparison to related methods are provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ is defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ encourages an increase in the likelihood of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing the likelihood of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the disfavored outputs, scaled by the factor $\beta$—reflecting the extent to which the KL regularization influences the ordering of outputs. Our findings indicate that this weighting is critical; omitting the weighting term can lead to model deterioration, as demonstrated in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO process involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then use human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a target parameter $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of producing new samples and collecting human judgments. Because these preference datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we instead initialize $\piref$ by performing maximum likelihood estimation on the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ utilized in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, supply theoretical support, and connect the benefits of DPO to challenges encountered in actor-critic algorithms applied to RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by employing a single maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy. It is important to recognize that the optimization objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized in a manner equivalent to the reward model training described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once we apply an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the range of possible reward models that can be learned, and show how it enables the precise identification of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry variant, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class produce the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations are straightforward and thus postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma reflects a common under-specification challenge associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is generally necessary to introduce further identifiability constraints to ensure meaningful guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma asserts that all reward functions in a given equivalence class lead to an identical optimal policy; therefore, for our ultimate goal, recovering any single reward function from this class suffices. We establish the following Theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Provided mild conditions hold, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (specifically, Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as described in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a representative reward from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the stated reparameterization. Define the mapping $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ effectively normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Because this normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original reward $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ by the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Hence, $f$ projects $r$ onto an equivalent reward function of the desired parametric form, implying our reward model reparameterization entails no loss of generality.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as pinpointing which specific reward within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses, namely, the reward function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a legitimate probability distribution (non-negative and sums to one). By referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it follows that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy defined by $r(x, y)$. The crucial observation behind the DPO approach is that we can enforce particular constraints on the inherently underdetermined Plackett-Luce (notably Bradley-Terry) family of preference models so that the range of representable reward functions is preserved, while simultaneously ensuring the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} is explicitly computable for every prefix $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework can also be applied to identify instability problems present in common actor-critic algorithms utilized in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF workflow, concentrating specifically on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}, we consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} derived from the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this objective can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This formulation matches the objective optimized in earlier research 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} which employ the DPO-equivalent reward corresponding to the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not alter the final optimal policy, omitting it can lead to a policy gradient with elevated variance, thus causing instability during training. While it is possible to incorporate this normalization term through a learned value function, optimizing such a function can be challenging. Alternatively, previous approaches have normalized rewards using a baseline derived from human completions, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization factor. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that obviates the need for any baseline adjustments.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to learn policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, compared to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and conversational tasks. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and also outperforms the best trajectory selected from $N$ samples using a learned reward function. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further specifics can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In each experiment, the algorithms acquire a policy based on a preference dataset denoted by $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a movie review prefix drawn from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation, preference pairs in this experiment are \textit{generated} by comparing model outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage, we train GPT-2-large until convergence on training data from the IMDb dataset (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is required to create a concise summary $y$ that captures the post’s key points. Consistent with previous research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model used here is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Notably, the preference dataset was created by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} based on samples from a distinct but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, the input $x$ represents a user’s query, which could range from astrophysics questions to requests for relationship guidance. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170,000 exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two candidate replies produced by a large (yet unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating which response humans favored. Since no pre-existing SFT model is available in this domain, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to establish the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we examine the trade-off curve between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy in the controlled sentiment generation scenario; this curve is obtainable since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is accessible. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we measure algorithm performance by their \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human assessment of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test dataset; for dialogue, it is the preferred response recorded in the test data. While prior work indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators compared to conventional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 for this purpose in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results reveal a strong correlation between GPT-4’s evaluations and human judgments, with the agreement between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding the agreement observed between human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. Additionally, we examine the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ obtained either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised technique considered is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional weighting parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the 'unlikelihood' component. We also explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained with a reward function inferred from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment experiments. For the sentiment evaluation, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these improvements are similarly applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, which generates $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and selects the top-scoring output based on a reward model trained on preference data. Although this method effectively separates reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires generating $N$ completions per input during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left panel.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for the Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue task; among the methods tested, only DPO shows an improvement compared to the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right panel.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures during training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels throughout training for all sampling temperature settings.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The typical RLHF algorithms employ a KL-constrained reward maximization objective that strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the policy’s divergence from a reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; a marginally higher reward coupled with a substantially increased KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier across various algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, multiple training runs are performed with varying hyperparameters governing policy conservativeness (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This hyperparameter sweep comprises a total of 22 runs. At intervals of every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is assessed on a set of test prompts by measuring the average reward based on the true reward function alongside the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the cumulative sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO yields the most efficient frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining low KL values. This observation is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO demonstrates substantially greater efficiency, with its reward-KL balance clearly surpassing that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even in the scenario where PPO has access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
In the following, we assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO based on human preferences leads to higher-quality summaries. We compare several approaches by generating completions on the TL;DR summarization dataset's test split and calculating the average win rate relative to reference completions. Samples from all methods are drawn at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT techniques all fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO attains about a 61\% win rate at zero temperature, surpassing PPO’s peak performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature setting. Additionally, DPO achieves a superior maximum win rate compared to the best $N$ baseline approach. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize the $\beta$ parameter for DPO, suggesting these outcomes might understate its full capability. Furthermore, DPO exhibits greater stability across different sampling temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance deteriorates toward the base GPT-J model level at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the SFT baseline. We also perform direct comparisons between DPO and PPO in human evaluations (see Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}), where DPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time against PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset's test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on a single human-assistant interaction step. GPT-4 evaluations employ the preferred completions from the test set as a benchmark to calculate win rates across different methods. Due to the absence of a standard SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions—ensuring these completions align with the model’s distribution—and subsequently train using DPO. Our comparisons also include the top results from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. We find that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each method. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a recognized repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}; however, no prompt or sampling temperature configuration outperforms the baseline Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both approaches optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that enhances the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 evaluations against human assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the trustworthiness of GPT-4’s evaluations, we perform a human subject study leveraging the outcomes of the TL;DR summarization task alongside two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, described as simple, requests a judgment on which summary better captures the essential information in the post. Alternatively, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt, described as concise, asks not only which summary is more informative but also which is more succinct; this prompt was tested because we observed that, under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more redundant summaries compared to human preferences. Complete prompt details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We carry out three sets of comparisons involving the highest-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temperature 1.0), and a mid-level method (SFT, temperature 0.25), in order to sample a range of output qualities. Each of these is contrasted against the greedily sampled PPO method at its optimal temperature. Our results show that for both prompting approaches, GPT-4’s agreement rate with human judgments is roughly on par with human-to-human agreement, implying that GPT-4 can serve as a credible stand-in for human evaluation. Due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were gathered only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons. Overall, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that align more closely with human preferences, so we adopt it for the principal analyses presented in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information regarding the human study setup, including the user interface shown to raters and a list of participants, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language models to be trained from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit conventional RL frameworks and using existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be optimized to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or surpassing that of current RLHF methods, including those based on PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several critical questions to be addressed in subsequent studies. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that learn from explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities comparable to PPO-based models, but this warrants more thorough investigation. For instance, can self-labeling based on the DPO policy similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Additionally, how does reward over-optimization appear within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the minor performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right represent such an effect? Moreover, while our experiments cover models up to 6 billion parameters, extending DPO to scale with state-of-the-art models that are orders of magnitude larger remains an intriguing avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt used, suggesting that future research should focus on identifying optimal strategies to obtain reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s potential applications extend well beyond fine-tuning language models with human preferences, encompassing the training of generative models across various modalities.
