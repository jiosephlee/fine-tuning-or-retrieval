\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely directing their behavior remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training process. Current approaches to achieve such control involve gathering human judgments about the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, typically via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable approach, which requires first training a reward model that captures human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large unsupervised LM to maximize the predicted reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. 
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we present a novel parameterization of the reward model used in RLHF that allows us to directly derive the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, thereby addressing the conventional RLHF task using only a straightforward classification loss.} 
The approach we propose, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, efficient, and computationally inexpensive, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} generating samples from the LM during fine-tuning, or conducting extensive hyperparameter optimization. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to better align with human preferences compared to existing techniques. Importantly, DPO fine-tuning surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs, and either matches or improves quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop unexpectedly advanced skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data reflecting diverse aims, priorities, and expertise levels. Some of these aims and skills might not be desirable to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to encourage the model to emphasize the (sometimes rare) high-quality coding examples found in its training set. Likewise, we might want the language model to be \textit{conscious} of a widespread false belief held by roughly half of people, but we certainly do not want the model to assert that falsehood as fact in half of the responses about it! Put differently, carefully choosing the model's \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for developing AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques generally guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective used in these approaches can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly simplifying the process of learning from preferences.

At a broad level, current techniques embed the preferred behaviors into a language model by utilizing carefully selected sets of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and beneficial by humans. This phase of preference learning follows an initial period of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on an extensive text corpus. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human-generated responses, the most effective approaches rely on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference data and then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy to generate outputs with high reward while maintaining closeness to the original model. Despite producing models with strong conversational and coding performance, the RLHF framework is significantly more complex than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and incorporating policy sampling during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly tailor a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF techniques (maximizing reward while constraining KL-divergence) but offers a simpler implementation and easier training process. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over disfavored ones, while applying a dynamic, example-specific weighting to avoid the model collapse observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function fits observed preference data. Unlike prior methods that utilize this model to craft a preference loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy against that model, DPO employs a variable transformation to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Consequently, with a dataset of human judgments on model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy through a simple binary cross entropy loss, \rev{without learning an explicit, standalone reward model or sampling from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function aligned with the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models using preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, across preference-learning tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size can perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few-shot prompting examples \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be markedly enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-crafted responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the specific instructions seen during tuning, thus boosting their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, obtaining \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality tends to be easier than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets reflecting human preferences, resulting in improved capabilities in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically begin by training a neural reward model to align with the preference data, often based on a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related algorithms \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction exploits LLMs already fine-tuned with human feedback to generate synthetic preference data targeting specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These techniques represent a merging of two research streams: one focused on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; the present work proposes a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methodologies developed. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In situations lacking absolute reward signals, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with the idea of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves an expected win probability of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike the CDB scenario where preference feedback is obtained sequentially during learning, preference-based learning from humans typically involves training on a fixed dataset containing offline annotations of action preferences \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) methods rely on binary preference data generated by an unknown latent `scoring' function instead of direct reward values \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been proposed, many of which incorporate the reuse of off-policy preference data but commonly require an initial step of explicitly inferring the underlying scoring function (i.e., the reward model), followed by optimizing the policy based on this estimate \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a unified, single-stage approach that directly optimizes policies to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by refining a pre-trained language model through supervised learning using high-quality datasets relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ to generate answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators who indicate their preference between the two answers, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the favored completion and $y_l$ the less favored one among $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown to us. Several methods exist to model these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly used approach (though more comprehensive Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be employed if multiple ranked responses are available). According to the BT model, the probability distribution representing human preference $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{ x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)} \bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can represent the reward function by a parametric model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating this as a binary classification task, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[ \log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)) \bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the context of language models, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by a linear layer appended to the last transformer layer to output a single scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the learned reward, previous studies apply reward normalization, ensuring that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward model is utilized to guide the language model through feedback. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the parameter $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is allowed to diverge from the base reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model is initialized to $\pisft$. Incorporating this constraint is crucial because it restricts the model from straying too far from the distribution where the reward model remains reliable, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse to a limited set of high-reward responses. Since language generation involves discrete outputs, this objective is not differentiable and is generally optimized using reinforcement learning techniques. The common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and then maximizing it through PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly uses preference information. In contrast to earlier RLHF techniques that first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the reward modeling phase and directly trains a language model using preference data}{utilizes a specific reward model parameterization allowing the optimal policy to be derived analytically, removing the need for an RL training loop}. 

As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, which permits converting a loss defined on reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to omit the separate reward modeling step}{circumvents the requirement to fit an explicit, independent reward model}, yet still performs optimization consistent with established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as established in previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, utilizing a general reward function $r$. Following the approaches of earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the normalization constant. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practical use of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of its optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization factor $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference probability model given by Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition functions cancel out, allowing us to represent the human preference likelihood purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry preference framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full derivation is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous formulations can be derived for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data with respect to the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward function while simultaneously eliminating the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In this manner, we implicitly fit a reward via a different parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits beneficial theoretical characteristics, including consistency guarantees under reasonable assumptions about the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical insights regarding DPO, particularly in comparison with related methodologies, are presented in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward estimation is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference distribution $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, this gradient encourages increasing the probability of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering the probability of the less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted based on how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly favors the less preferred completion, modulated by the factor $\beta$, which reflects the influence of the KL regularization. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting scheme, as a simplified variant without it can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, annotate them with human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these preference datasets are typically collected using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. Otherwise, if $\pisft$ is unavailable, we define $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, i.e.,  
${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$.  
This approach reduces the distributional mismatch between the actual, unknown reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, present its theoretical foundations, and connect the benefits of DPO to the limitations found in actor-critic methods commonly applied in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. It is important to observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. By optimizing our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, we effectively perform reward model optimization as described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, with an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no limitations on the set of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation defined over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. We can then present the following pair of lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class produce the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a well-recognized under-specification problem associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is often necessary to enforce extra identifiability conditions to secure reliable guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) results from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma confirms that all reward functions from a given equivalence class lead to an identical optimal policy. Consequently, for our ultimate goal, recovering any reward function within the optimal class suffices. The following theorem is demonstrated in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry special case) can be expressed through the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)},
    \]
    where $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a designated reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ which induces an associated optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ defined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will prove that there exists a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ that admits the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, the transformed reward $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original reward $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally), we find:
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Thus, $f$ yields a representative from the equivalence class of $r$ in the desired form, confirming that no generality is lost by adopting this reparameterization.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies the reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization within each equivalence class, namely the function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (i.e., all probabilities are positive and sum to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it follows that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward $r(x, y)$.
The fundamental insight of the DPO method is that by applying particular constraints to the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce family (and specifically the Bradley-Terry subset) of preference models, we maintain the expressiveness of the reward class while rendering the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also help identify sources of instability in conventional actor-critic algorithms commonly employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure, concentrating on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach allows us to relate it to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained reinforcement learning formulation presented in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and seek to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \parallel \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. By performing some algebraic manipulation, this results in the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[ \underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective corresponds to that optimized in earlier studies  
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} using the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function of the baseline policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy, excluding it can lead to large variance in the policy gradient, causing unstable learning dynamics. One way to incorporate this normalization is by learning a value function, but optimizing it can be challenging. Alternatively, previous works have normalized rewards via a human completion baseline, effectively employing a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of this normalization. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward formulation that eliminates the need for such baselines altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, in comparison to established preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that DPO, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, generally matches or surpasses robust baselines including RLHF with PPO and the strategy of selecting the top trajectory among $N$ samples based on a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the methods learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, the input $x$ consists of the beginning portion of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ that exhibits positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, preference pairs for this task are \textit{generated} by comparing outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on the training subset of the IMDb reviews (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ corresponds to a Reddit forum post, and the policy must generate a concise summary $y$ that captures the post’s key points. Following previous work, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and trained using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The human preference data was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from a different but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task involves $x$ representing a user query, which can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. Here, the policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k conversations between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses created by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the response favored by humans. In this scenario, since no pre-existing SFT model is available, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess how effectively each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in a controlled sentiment generation context, we measure performance based on the trade-off frontier between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible because we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons use the reference summaries from the test set, whereas for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test dataset. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators relative to traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate our reliance on GPT-4 through a human study detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate that GPT-4’s evaluations align closely with human assessments, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding inter-annotator agreement levels.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. Initially, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task, and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Additionally, we analyze the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$, derived either from the SFT model (in scenarios involving controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach based on pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to maximize the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously reducing the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood component. We further evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} with a reward function learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method leveraging the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment context. For the sentiment experiments, we employ two variants of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost performance (these adjustments are also applied to the regular PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top-scoring output based on a reward function trained on the preference dataset. Although this approach effectively separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even for moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ completions per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4-evaluated win rates for the Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; among all methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked across various sampling temperatures during training. The advantage of DPO over the original dataset labels remains relatively consistent throughout training across different temperature settings.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the policy’s divergence from the reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; a marginal increase in reward accompanied by a significantly larger KL divergence may not be advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-KL trade-off curves for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training sessions, varying the hyperparameters controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT), totaling 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a test set, measuring the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL divergence\footnote{This refers to the cumulative sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO achieves the most effective frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining a low KL divergence. This outcome is particularly significant for several reasons: firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO is considerably more efficient, with a reward/KL balance that strictly surpasses PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when the latter has access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on actual preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO guided by human preferences yields improved summaries. To compare different approaches, we generate completions from the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test split and calculate the average win rate relative to reference completions. Completions for all methods were sampled across a range of temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that at a sampling temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, surpassing PPO’s best performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO achieves a superior maximum win rate compared to the best-performing $N$ baseline. It is worth mentioning that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, so these outcomes might underestimate its true capability. Furthermore, DPO shows greater stability across different sampling temperatures than PPO, which tends to degrade to the baseline GPT-J model’s performance at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT yields little improvement over the original SFT model. A direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split, focusing on one round of human-assistant exchange. GPT-4 evaluations employ the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate win rates across methods. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a baseline model on the selected preferred completions—ensuring these completions align with the model's distribution—and subsequently continue training with DPO. Our comparisons also include the top results from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. We observe that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of the best temperature settings for these methods. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we do not identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR findings and considering that both methods optimize the same reward, we treat the Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient technique that enhances the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its optimal performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Corroborating GPT-4 Evaluations with Human Assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the trustworthiness of GPT-4’s evaluations, we perform a human subject study utilizing outcomes from the TL;DR summarization task alongside two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly asks which summary better captures the key information in the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires which summary is more succinct; this prompt is tested because we observe that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries with the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt compared to human preferences. Complete prompt details are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three pairwise evaluations involving the best-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the worst-performing method (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temperature 0.25) to ensure a range of summary quality. Each method is benchmarked against the greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Our findings indicate that GPT-4’s agreement with humans is roughly on par with human inter-rater agreement for both prompts, implying GPT-4 can serve as a reliable stand-in for human judgment (note that multiple human ratings were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons due to a limited number of raters). Generally, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that align more closely with human decisions; consequently, this prompt is employed for the principal analyses in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information on the human evaluation procedure, including the rater interface and volunteer list, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach to developing capable, aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit a traditional RL framework and employing standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be optimized to align with human preferences \textit{directly} by using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or surpassing current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several key questions to be addressed moving forward. How well does the DPO policy perform when faced with out-of-distribution data compared to approaches relying on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities comparable to those of PPO-based methods, yet a more thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, is it possible for training using self-labeling derived from the DPO policy to effectively leverage unlabeled prompts in a similar manner? Furthermore, what are the manifestations of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the modest decline in performance seen in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be attributed to this phenomenon? While our experiments cover models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models presents an intriguing avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; subsequent work might investigate optimal strategies to obtain reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, numerous applications for DPO extend beyond aligning language models with human preferences, including training generative models across diverse modalities.
