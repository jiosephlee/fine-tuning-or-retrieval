#### 3.3.2 The Loss Function: Cross-Entropy

To train this model, we need a loss function. Following our probabilistic framework, we use the negative log-likelihood. The probability of a single observation `(x, y)` where `y \in \{0, 1\}` can be written compactly using the Bernoulli distribution:
$$ p(y|x; w, b) = p(y=1|x)^y \cdot (1 - p(y=1|x))^{1-y} $$
Taking the negative logarithm gives us our loss function for one data point:
$$ L(w, b) = -\log p(y|x; w, b) = -[y \log p(y=1|x) + (1-y) \log(1 - p(y=1|x))] $$
This expression is exactly the **binary cross-entropy** loss function, widely used in machine learning for binary classification. This derivation shows that cross-entropy is not an arbitrary choice; it is precisely the negative log-likelihood for a model that assumes a Bernoulli distribution for the binary outcomes.

### 3.4 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

We have now defined a probabilistic model and a corresponding loss function (the NLL). The final step is to establish a general principle for finding the best parameters `\theta`. This principle is **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)**.

**The Principle of Maximum Likelihood:** Given a dataset `D = \{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_N, y_N)\}` and a parameterized model `p(y|x; \theta)`, the MLE principle states that we should choose the parameters `\theta` that make the observed data most probable.

Assuming the data points are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the likelihood of observing the entire dataset is the product of the likelihoods of each individual data point:
$$ L(\theta; D) = p(D; \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} p(y_i | x_i; \theta) $$
Our goal is to find the parameters `\hat{\theta}_{MLE}` that maximise this function:
$$ \hat{\theta}_{MLE} = \arg\max_{\theta} L(\theta; D) $$

#### 3.4.1 The Log-Likelihood

Working with a product of many small probabilities is numerically unstable and mathematically inconvenient for optimisation (e.g., taking derivatives). We can simplify this problem immensely by working with the **log-likelihood** instead. Since the logarithm is a strictly monotonically increasing function, maximising a function is equivalent to maximising its logarithm.
$$ \ell(\theta; D) = \log L(\theta; D) = \log \prod_{i=1}^{N} p(y_i | x_i; \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p(y_i | x_i; \theta) $$
The MLE estimate is therefore also given by:
$$ \hat{\theta}_{MLE} = \arg\max_{\theta} \ell(\theta; D) $$
This is a profound simplification. The product has been converted into a sum, which is far easier to handle. Notice that the log-likelihood `\ell(\theta)` is simply the negative of the total NLL loss we discussed earlier. Therefore, maximising the likelihood is equivalent to minimising the negative log-likelihood. This provides the formal justification for using gradient descent to minimise the sum of NLL or cross-entropy losses over a dataset.

#### 3.4.2 Properties of MLE

MLE is a cornerstone of statistics and machine learning due to its desirable properties.
1.  **Consistency:** As the number of data points `N` approaches infinity, the MLE estimate `\hat{\theta}_{MLE}` converges to the true underlying parameter value `\theta^*` that generated the data (under certain regularity conditions). This means that with enough data, MLE will find the "correct" answer.
2.  **Invariance to Scaling:** The `argmax` operator is unaffected by positive scaling. This means that `\arg\max_{\theta} \ell(\theta)` is the same as `\arg\max_{\theta} \frac{1}{N}\ell(\theta)`. This is why we can choose to minimise either the sum of the NLL over the dataset or the average NLL—the resulting parameters `\hat{\theta}` will be identical.

### Summary

This chapter has introduced the foundational concepts of probabilistic modelling. We began by defining random variables and their expectations, establishing a direct link between the statistical concept of expectation and the machine learning practice of minimising a loss function. We then used the chain rule of probability to show how complex joint distributions can be factorised into manageable conditional probabilities, a technique that underpins modern generative sequence models.

We applied these ideas to the concrete task of binary classification, deriving the logistic regression model and showing its interpretation as a linear model of log-odds. This led to the crucial insight that the commonly used cross-entropy loss is nothing more than the negative log-likelihood for this model. Finally, we formalised the training process with the principle of Maximum Likelihood Estimation, which provides a robust and theoretically sound justification for finding model parameters by minimising the negative log-likelihood. These principles form the bedrock upon which more advanced probabilistic methods, such as Bayesian inference and variational autoencoders, are built.
