## **Chapter 7: Advanced Optimisation: From Stochastic Gradients to Principled Constraints**

### **7.1 Introduction: Beyond Vanilla Gradient Descent**

In your journey through machine learning, you have become well-acquainted with the workhorse of optimisation: gradient descent. The principle is elegant and intuitive: to minimize a loss function $L(\theta)$ with respect to its parameters $\theta$, we iteratively take steps in the direction of the negative gradient, $-\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta)$. This is akin to walking downhill in a dense fog; the slope directly beneath our feet tells us the steepest way down.

However, the pristine, mathematical landscapes of introductory examples give way to treacherous and vast terrains in modern machine learning. Two primary challenges emerge:

1.  **Scale:** Modern datasets can contain billions of data points. Calculating the true gradient, which requires summing the loss over every single point, for just one parameter update is computationally infeasible. It would be like surveying an entire mountain range to decide on a single footstep.
2.  **Constraints:** Real-world problems are rarely unconstrained. We might want to train a reinforcement learning agent to maximize rewards, but *subject to* the constraint that its behaviour doesn't deviate too wildly from a previously safe policy. Or we might want to build a fair model, minimizing error *subject to* the constraint that its predictions are demographically equitable.

This chapter tackles these two challenges head-on. First, we will explore the world of **stochastic optimisation**, a suite of techniques designed to navigate massive datasets efficiently and stably. Second, we will introduce the powerful idea of **Lagrangian thinking**, a principled mathematical framework for converting constrained optimisation problems into unconstrained ones that we already know how to solve.

### **7.2 Taming the Beast: Stochastic Optimisation for Large-Scale Learning**

The core problem with traditional (or "batch") gradient descent is its reliance on the full dataset to compute the gradient at each step. If our dataset $\mathcal{D}$ has $N$ samples, the loss is $L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} L_i(\theta)$, and the gradient is $\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla_{\theta}L_i(\theta)$. For large $N$, this is prohibitively slow.

#### **7.2.1 Minibatches and the Noisy Gradient**

The foundational idea of stochastic optimisation is to approximate the true gradient using only a small subset of the data.

*   **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD):** In its purest form, SGD uses a single, randomly chosen data point $(x_i, y_i)$ to estimate the gradient at each step: $\hat{g} = \nabla_{\theta}L_i(\theta)$. This estimate is unbiased, meaning $\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}] = \nabla_{\theta}L(\theta)$, but it has very high variance. The resulting parameter updates are extremely noisy, leading to a jagged, erratic path towards the minimum.

*   **Minibatch Gradient Descent:** The practical compromise is to use a small, random batch of $B$ samples (a "minibatch"), where $1 \ll B \ll N$. The gradient estimate is $\hat{g} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \nabla_{\theta}L_i(\theta)$. This approach strikes a beautiful balance:
    1.  **Reduced Variance:** By averaging over $B$ samples, the variance of the gradient estimate is reduced by a factor of $B$ compared to pure SGD, leading to more stable convergence.
    2.  **Computational Efficiency:** Modern hardware like GPUs are optimized for parallel, vectorized operations. Processing a minibatch of size 32, 64, or 256 is often significantly faster than processing single samples 32, 64, or 256 times sequentially.

This "noisy" gradient is both a challenge and a blessing. The noise can help the optimiser escape sharp, undesirable local minima, but it also makes convergence near a good minimum difficult. The following techniques are a toolkit for managing this noise and stabilising the training process.

#### **7.2.2 Stabilising the Descent: A Modern Toolkit**

**1. Adaptive Learning Rates: Adam and AdamW**

A constant learning rate $\eta$ is problematic in a noisy landscape. We desire an optimiser that can adapt, taking larger steps for parameters that need to move quickly and smaller, more cautious steps for others. The **Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation)** optimiser achieves this by maintaining two running averages for each parameter:

*   **First Moment (Momentum):** A running average of the gradients themselves, denoted $m_t$. This acts like momentum, smoothing out the updates and helping the optimiser power through small bumps and plateaus.
    $m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1) \hat{g}_t$
*   **Second Moment (Variance Scaling):** A running average of the squared gradients, denoted $v_t$. This estimates the uncentered variance of the gradients for each parameter.
    $v_t = \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2) \hat{g}_t^2$

The parameter update rule for Adam (simplified) is:
$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon}$

Here, $\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t$ are bias-corrected versions of the moments, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. The intuition is powerful: if a parameter's gradients are consistently large (high $v_t$), its effective learning rate is reduced. If its gradients are small or sparse (low $v_t$), its effective learning rate is increased. This per-parameter adaptation makes Adam robust and often effective with minimal tuning.

**AdamW** improves upon Adam by modifying how weight decay (L2 regularization) is handled. In standard Adam, weight decay is coupled with the gradient, meaning its effect is also scaled by $\sqrt{v_t}$. AdamW decouples the weight decay from the adaptive learning rate, applying it directly to the weights after the Adam step. This often leads to better generalisation.

**2. Learning Rate Schedules**

The optimal learning rate changes during training. Early on, when we are far from a minimum, a large learning rate helps us make rapid progress. Later, as we approach a minimum, a smaller learning rate is needed to fine-tune the parameters without overshooting. A **learning rate schedule** automates this process. Common schedules include:

*   **Step Decay:** Reduce the learning rate by a factor (e.g., 0.1) at specific epochs.
*   **Cosine Annealing:** Smoothly decrease the learning rate from its initial value to zero following the shape of a cosine curve. This is highly effective as it spends more time with smaller learning rates near the end of training.

**3. Gradient Clipping**

Occasionally, a particular minibatch may produce an unusually large gradient, causing a massive parameter update that "explodes" the model, catapulting the parameters far from the optimal region. This is especially common in Recurrent Neural Networks. **Gradient clipping** is a simple and effective defense. We define a threshold $C$, and if the L2-norm of the gradient vector $\|\hat{g}\|$ exceeds this threshold, we rescale the gradient to have norm $C$:

If $\|\hat{g}\| > C$, then $\hat{g} \leftarrow C \frac{\hat{g}}{\|\hat{g}\|}$

This acts as a safety rail, preventing catastrophic updates and dramatically stabilising training without changing the direction of the gradient.

### **7.3 Learning with Rules: An Introduction to Constrained Optimisation**

We now shift our focus from the *how* of optimisation (navigating noisy gradients) to the *what* (optimising with constraints). Consider a common problem in reinforcement learning: we want to update our policy $\pi_{\theta}$ to maximize expected reward $R(\theta)$, but we don't want the new policy to be too different from a trusted reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. A drastic change could lead to unstable or unsafe behaviour.

We can formalize this as:
**Maximise** $R(\theta)$
**Subject to:** $D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{ref}}) \le C$

Here, $D_{KL}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of the difference between two probability distributions, and $C$ is a small constant. How can we solve this using gradient-based methods?

#### **7.3.1 The Lagrangian Formulation**

The method of **Lagrange multipliers** provides a powerful and elegant way to convert a constrained optimisation problem into an unconstrained one. The core idea is to incorporate the constraint directly into the objective function, weighted by a new variable called a Lagrange multiplier, typically denoted by $\lambda$ or, in this context, $\beta$.

We construct a new objective, the **Lagrangian**, which for our problem is:
$J(\theta) = R(\theta) - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{ref}})$

We have transformed our problem into an unconstrained maximisation of $J(\theta)$. But what is the role of $\beta$?

#### **7.3.2 The Multiplier as a Trade-off Knob**

The Lagrange multiplier $\beta$ is not just a mathematical formality; it has a profound and intuitive interpretation: it is the **price** or **penalty** for violating the constraint. It acts as a trade-off knob that allows us to control the balance between our original objective and our constraint.

Let's analyze its effect:

*   **If $\beta \to 0$ (Constraint is "cheap"):** The second term in $J(\theta)$ vanishes. The objective becomes simply to maximise $R(\theta)$. The optimiser will pursue higher rewards without any regard for the KL divergence constraint. The resulting policy might be high-reward but dangerously different from the reference.

*   **If $\beta \to \infty$ (Constraint is "expensive"):** The second term, $-\beta \cdot D_{KL}$, becomes dominant. To maximise $J(\theta)$, the optimiser will be forced to make $D_{KL}$ as close to zero as possible, even if it means sacrificing reward. The resulting policy will be extremely conservative, staying very close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and satisfying the constraint with ease, but potentially missing out on higher-reward strategies.

*   **The "Just Right" $\beta$:** The goal is to find a value of $\beta$ that perfectly balances these two competing desires. A moderate $\beta$ tells the optimiser: "Pursue higher rewards, but I will penalise you proportionally for every bit you increase the KL divergence."

In practice, $\beta$ can be treated as a hyperparameter that we tune. If we find our policy is changing too much (violating the KL constraint), we increase $\beta$. If it's too conservative and not improving its reward, we decrease $\beta$. In more advanced methods (like Trust Region Policy Optimisation or Proximal Policy Optimisation, which you may encounter later), there are algorithms to automatically adjust $\beta$ to ensure the constraint is met on average.

This "Lagrangian thinking" is a cornerstone of advanced optimisation. It allows us to take complex, real-world problems with hard constraints and translate them into the language of unconstrained gradient descent that our powerful stochastic optimisers are built to solve.

### **7.4 Conclusion**

In this chapter, we have moved beyond the simple case of batch gradient descent. We first confronted the challenge of scale by embracing the noisy but efficient world of stochastic optimisation. We saw that while minibatches provide a practical path forward, their inherent noise requires a sophisticated toolkit—including adaptive methods like Adam/AdamW, learning rate schedules, and gradient clipping—to ensure stable and effective training.

We then addressed the challenge of complexity by introducing the Lagrangian framework for constrained optimisation. This powerful perspective allows us to encode rules and constraints directly into our objective function, using Lagrange multipliers as intuitive knobs to manage the trade-off between performance and compliance. These two pillars—stochastic methods for scale and Lagrangian methods for constraints—form the foundation upon which the optimisation of virtually all large-scale, modern machine learning models is built.
