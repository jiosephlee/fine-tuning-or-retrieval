## **Chapter 7: The Probabilistic Foundations of Transformer-Based Sequence Models**

### **7.1 Introduction**

In previous chapters, we explored the Transformer architecture, focusing on the mechanics of self-attention, positional encodings, and the encoder-decoder structure. We have established *how* these models process information in parallel and capture long-range dependencies within sequences. Now, we shift our focus from the architectural "how" to the probabilistic "why." How does a Transformer, at its core, learn to generate coherent text? How do we steer its behavior from simply predicting the next word to following complex instructions?

This chapter delves into the probabilistic principles that govern the training and application of large language models (LLMs). We will treat the Transformer not just as a function approximator, but as a learned probability distribution over sequences. Understanding this perspective is fundamental to grasping advanced techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).

We will dissect three core concepts:
1.  **Sequence Likelihood Factorisation:** The mathematical foundation that allows us to define and optimize a probability distribution over an entire sequence of text.
2.  **Reference vs. Current Policy:** A framework for comparing two different language models, which is essential for controlling model updates during fine-tuning.
3.  **Supervised Teacher Forcing:** The training methodology that provides a stable and effective starting point for more complex alignment procedures.

By the end of this chapter, you will understand how these concepts form a logical progression, taking a pre-trained model and carefully refining it into a helpful and aligned assistant.

### **7.2 The Heart of Generation: Sequence Likelihood Factorisation**

A language model's primary task is to assign a probability to a sequence of tokens. Let's consider a sequence $Y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)$. The joint probability of this entire sequence, $P(Y)$, can be decomposed using the chain rule of probability:

$P(Y) = P(y_1) \cdot P(y_2 | y_1) \cdot P(y_3 | y_1, y_2) \cdots P(y_T | y_1, \dots, y_{T-1})$

This can be written more compactly as:

$P(Y | X; \theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(y_t | y_{<t}, X; \theta)$

where:
*   $X$ is the initial context or prompt (e.g., "Translate this sentence to French:").
*   $y_{<t}$ represents the sequence of previously generated tokens $(y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})$.
*   $\theta$ represents the parameters of our Transformer model.
*   $P(y_t | y_{<t}, X; \theta)$ is the conditional probability of the $t$-th token, given the prompt and all preceding tokens.

This is known as an **autoregressive** model. The Transformer's decoder, with its causal self-attention mask, is perfectly architected to compute these conditional probabilities. The causal mask ensures that the prediction for token $y_t$ can only attend to tokens at positions less than $t$, thereby respecting the conditional dependency required by the chain rule.

In practice, machine learning models are trained by optimizing the logarithm of probabilities, as this turns products into sums, which are more numerically stable and easier to differentiate. The log-likelihood of a sequence is therefore:

$\log P(Y | X; \theta) = \log \left( \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(y_t | y_{<t}, X; \theta) \right) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(y_t | y_{<t}, X; \theta)$

This elegant equation is the cornerstone of training language models. The total log-probability of a sequence is simply the sum of the log-probabilities of each individual token, conditioned on its predecessors. During training (e.g., pre-training or fine-tuning), our objective is to adjust the parameters $\theta$ to maximize this log-likelihood for sequences in our training data. This is known as Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

#### **7.2.1 Training vs. Sampling: The Role of Temperature**

It is crucial to distinguish between the model's training objective and how we sample from it during inference.

*   **During Training:** The objective is fixed: maximize the log-likelihood of the ground-truth data. The model's output logits for the correct next token should be as high as possible. Concepts like sampling temperature are irrelevant at this stage.

*   **During Sampling (Inference):** We use the trained model to generate new sequences. At each step $t$, the model produces a vector of logits over the entire vocabulary. To convert these logits into a probability distribution, we use the softmax function. Here, we can introduce a **temperature** parameter, $T$:

$P(y_t | y_{<t}, X; \theta) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\text{logits}_t}{T}\right)$

Temperature controls the shape of the probability distribution:
*   **$T \to 0$**: The division by a small number exaggerates differences in logits. The probability distribution becomes extremely "peaky," concentrating all probability mass on the most likely token. This leads to deterministic, greedy decoding.
*   **$T = 1$**: This is the standard softmax function, directly reflecting the probabilities the model learned.
*   **$T > 1$**: The logits are flattened, making the distribution more uniform. This increases the chance of sampling less likely tokens, leading to more random, diverse, and sometimes "creative" outputs.

Importantly, **temperature is a sampling-time parameter**. It does not affect the underlying model parameters $\theta$ or the sequence likelihood as calculated during training. It is a tool for controlling the trade-off between determinism and diversity in the generated output.

### **7.3 Policy-Based Perspectives: Reference vs. Current Models**

As we move towards more advanced fine-tuning methods like RLHF, it becomes useful to frame the language model as an **agent** taking actions. In this view, the model is a **policy**, denoted by $\pi$, which defines a distribution over possible actions (the next token) given a state (the current sequence).

$\pi(y_t | y_{<t}, X)$ is simply another name for $P(y_t | y_{<t}, X)$.

In many alignment techniques, we don't just work with one policy. We maintain two:

1.  **The Current Policy ($\pi$)**: This is the model we are actively training. Its parameters, $\theta$, are being updated by gradient descent to optimize some objective (e.g., a reward function).
2.  **The Reference Policy ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$)**: This is a fixed, read-only copy of a model. It typically represents a "safe" or "known-good" state of the model, often the model as it existed right after supervised fine-tuning (more on this in Section 7.4).

A key operation in this paradigm is to compute the ratio of probabilities assigned to a sequence by these two policies. In the log domain, this becomes a difference:

$\log \frac{\pi(Y|X)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(Y|X)} = \log \pi(Y|X) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(Y|X)$

Leveraging the sequence likelihood factorization from Section 7.2, we can decompose this sequence-level difference into a sum of token-level differences:

$\log \pi(Y|X) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(Y|X) = \left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi(y_t | y_{<t}, X) \right) - \left( \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t | y_{<t}, X) \right)$

$= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \log \pi(y_t | y_{<t}, X) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t | y_{<t}, X) \right)$

This decomposition is not just a mathematical curiosity; it is computationally vital. It means we can calculate this important quantity in a single forward pass for each model.

But why is this difference useful? In reinforcement learning, we might reward the current policy $\pi$ for producing outputs that humans prefer. However, if left unconstrained, the model might discover strange, non-sensical sequences that happen to "trick" the reward model. This is known as "reward hacking." The difference, $\log \pi - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}$, acts as a penalty. It measures how far the current policy has diverged from the trusted reference policy. This is often formalized as a KL-divergence penalty, which keeps the current policy $\pi$ from straying too far from the coherent, fluent language distribution of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, thus regularizing the optimization process.

### **7.4 Laying the Groundwork: Supervised Fine-Tuning with Teacher Forcing**

We have established the need for a high-quality reference policy, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. But where does it come from? It is typically the result of a crucial intermediate step called **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**.

SFT takes a general-purpose pre-trained model and adapts it to a specific style or task domain using a high-quality, curated dataset. For instruction-following models, this dataset consists of (prompt, desired_response) pairs written by humans.

The training mechanism used in SFT is **teacher forcing**. Let's consider how we might train a model on the response "The capital is Paris," given the prompt "What is the capital of France?".

1.  **Step 1:** Input: "What is the capital of France?". The model must predict the first token, "The".
2.  **Step 2:** The model must predict the second token, "capital". With teacher forcing, the input for this step is *not* what the model predicted in Step 1. Instead, we "force" the correct token, "The", as the input. The model's task is to predict "capital" given the context "...France? The".
3.  **Step 3:** To predict "is", the model is fed the ground-truth context "...France? The capital", and so on.

At every step, the model is given the ground-truth prefix from the dataset, regardless of its own previous predictions. This has two major benefits:
*   **Stability and Efficiency:** It prevents error propagation. If the model makes a mistake early on, it isn't forced to generate the rest of the sequence based on its own error. Every token prediction is an independent, well-defined supervised learning problem, which makes training much more stable and parallelizable.
*   **Direct Optimization:** The model's parameters are updated to directly maximize the likelihood of the human-written text. The loss function for SFT is simply the negative log-likelihood (or cross-entropy loss) over the tokens in the desired responses.

The SFT phase is critical. It takes a model that is good at predicting text on the internet and teaches it the specific *format* and *style* of being a helpful assistant. The resulting model, $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$, is generally coherent, follows instructions reasonably well, and provides a safe, high-quality distribution over language.

This SFT model is the perfect candidate to become the **reference policy**, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, for the subsequent preference learning stage. It provides a stable anchor, ensuring that as we optimize for human preferences using RL, our model does not forget its fundamental grasp of language.

### **7.5 Chapter Summary**

In this chapter, we journeyed from the fundamental probabilistic definition of a sequence model to the practicalities of preparing a model for advanced alignment.

*   We began with **sequence likelihood factorisation**, showing how the probability of a sequence decomposes into a product of conditional probabilities, which in the log domain becomes a sum of token-level log-probabilities. This is the mathematical objective for training.
*   We then introduced the concept of a language model as a **policy**, distinguishing between a `current` policy being trained and a `reference` policy used for regularization. We saw that the log-probability ratio between them also conveniently decomposes token-by-token.
*   Finally, we identified **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**, which uses the **teacher forcing** algorithm, as the process that creates the ideal reference policy. SFT adapts a pre-trained model to the desired conversational style, providing a robust and stable starting point for further optimization.

These three concepts are not independent; they are sequential building blocks in the modern LLM training pipeline. Understanding their probabilistic underpinnings is essential for anyone looking to go beyond using these models and begin to understand how they are built and refined.
