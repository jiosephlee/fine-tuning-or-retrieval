### **Chapter 8: Aligning Large Language Models with Human Intent**

#### **8.1 Introduction: Beyond Next-Token Prediction**

In your studies thus far, you have encountered large language models (LLMs) primarily as sophisticated sequence predictors. At their core, foundational models trained on vast internet-scale corpora are optimized for a single, simple objective: predicting the next token in a sequence. This process, known as pre-training, endows them with a remarkable grasp of grammar, factual knowledge, and linguistic patterns. However, a model that is an expert at completing text from the internet is not necessarily an expert at being a helpful, harmless, and honest assistant.

The raw output of a pre-trained model might be unhelpful, factually incorrect, or reflect undesirable biases present in its training data. The central challenge, then, is one of **alignment**: how do we steer the behavior of these powerful models to align with human values and intentions? Simply continuing the pre-training objective is insufficient. We need a more targeted approach.

This chapter introduces the dominant multi-stage learning pipeline designed to achieve this alignment. We will explore two critical phases: **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**, which teaches the model the desired style and format of interaction, and **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**, which refines the model's behavior based on nuanced human preferences. By the end of this chapter, you will understand the mechanics, mathematics, and practical challenges of transforming a raw predictive model into a well-aligned conversational agent.

#### **8.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Learning by Demonstration**

The first step in aligning a pre-trained LLM is to teach it the specific task we want it to perform. For a conversational assistant, this means learning to follow instructions and respond helpfully. The most direct way to do this is through imitation learning, which in this context is called Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

**The Goal:** The objective of SFT is to adapt the general-purpose pre-trained model to the specific domain of instruction-following and helpful dialogue. We want to teach it the *format* of a good response.

**The Data:** Unlike the unlabeled text used in pre-training, SFT relies on a smaller, high-quality dataset of curated examples. This dataset consists of prompt-response pairs, where the prompts are representative of what a user might ask, and the responses are high-quality answers written by human labelers.

*   **Prompt:** "Explain the Poisson distribution in simple terms and provide a real-world example."
*   **Response (Human-written):** "The Poisson distribution is a statistical tool used to model the number of times an event occurs within a specified interval of time or space. It's useful when these events happen independently and at a constant average rate. For example, a call center might use the Poisson distribution to model the number of calls they receive per hour. If they know the average rate is 20 calls per hour, they can calculate the probability of receiving exactly 15 calls, or more than 30 calls, in any given hour."

**The Process:**
The SFT process is a standard application of supervised learning. We take the pre-trained model and continue training it on this new, labeled dataset. The model's parameters are updated using gradient descent to maximize the likelihood of generating the human-written response, given the prompt.

Mathematically, let our pre-trained model be a policy $\pi$. Given a dataset $D_{\text{SFT}}$ of prompt-response pairs $(x, y)$, the objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood (or cross-entropy loss) of the target response:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\pi) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim D_{\text{SFT}}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \pi(y_t | x, y_{<t}) \right]
$$

where $y_t$ is the $t$-th token of the response $y$, and $y_{<t}$ are the preceding tokens. This is the same loss function used during pre-training, but applied to a curated, task-specific dataset.

**The Outcome:**
After this phase, we have a model, which we will denote as $\boldsymbol{\pi_{\text{SFT}}}$. This model is significantly more capable of following instructions and engaging in dialogue than the original pre-trained model. It has learned the style, tone, and structure of a helpful assistant.

However, SFT has limitations. It is expensive to create a large and diverse set of high-quality demonstrations. More importantly, it is often easier for a human to *judge* which of two responses is better than to *write* a perfect response from scratch. This insight motivates the next, more complex stage of the pipeline. The SFT model serves as a crucial starting point and a reference policy for the RLHF phase.

#### **8.3 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF): Learning from Preference**

While SFT teaches the model to imitate good behavior, RLHF aims to teach it a more abstract concept of what constitutes "goodness" by learning from human preferences. This allows the model to generate novel, high-quality responses that may be better than any single example seen in the SFT dataset. The RLHF process is typically broken into two stages.

##### **8.3.1 Stage 1: Training a Reward Model**

The first step is to formalize human preferences into a utility function that our reinforcement learning algorithm can optimize. This function is called the **reward model**, denoted $r_\theta$, where $\theta$ are the model's parameters.

**The Data:** To train the reward model, we collect a new dataset of human preferences. For a given prompt $x$, we use our SFT model to generate two or more different responses, say $y_1$ and $y_2$. A human labeler is then asked to compare these responses and indicate which one they prefer. The dataset therefore consists of tuples $(x, y_w, y_l)$, where $y_w$ is the "winning" (preferred) response and $y_l$ is the "losing" (dispreferred) response.

**The Model and Objective:** The reward model is typically a language model (often initialized from $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$) whose final output is a single scalar value representing the "reward" or "goodness" of a response. The goal is to train $r_\theta$ such that the score assigned to the preferred response is higher than the score assigned to the dispreferred one: $r_\theta(x, y_w) > r_\theta(x, y_l)$.

This is framed as a binary classification problem using the **Bradley-Terry model**, which models the probability that $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$ as a logistic function of the difference in their rewards:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l | x) = \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))
$$

where $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$ is the sigmoid function. The loss function for the reward model is the negative log-likelihood of the human preferences:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D_{\text{pref}}} \left[ \log(\sigma(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l))) \right]
$$

By minimizing this loss, we train a model $r_\theta$ that can take any prompt-response pair $(x, y)$ and output a scalar score that predicts how much a human would like that response.

##### **8.3.2 Stage 2: Policy Optimization with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**

With a functioning reward model, we can now treat the problem of generating good responses as a reinforcement learning task.

*   **Environment:** The environment is the user, who provides a prompt $x$.
*   **Agent:** The agent is our language model policy, $\pi_\phi$, which we initialize from $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$.
*   **Action Space:** The vast space of all possible token sequences (responses).
*   **Reward:** For a given prompt $x$ and a generated response $y \sim \pi_\phi(\cdot|x)$, the reward is the score given by our reward model, $r_\theta(x, y)$.

The naive objective would be to simply maximize the expected reward: $\max_\phi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D, y \sim \pi_\phi(y|x)} [r_\theta(x, y)]$. However, this approach is fraught with peril. The policy $\pi_\phi$ could quickly learn to generate responses that "hack" the reward model—finding strange, nonsensical text that happens to receive a high score from our imperfect $r_\theta$. Furthermore, aggressive optimization could cause the model to deviate drastically from coherent language, a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting.

To counteract this, we introduce a constraint that penalizes the policy for moving too far away from our trusted SFT model, $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$. This is achieved using the **Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence**, a measure of how one probability distribution differs from a reference distribution. The final RL objective function becomes:

$$
\text{Objective}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D, y \sim \pi_\phi(y|x)} \left[ r_\theta(x, y) - \beta D_{KL}(\pi_\phi(\cdot|x) || \pi_{\text{SFT}}(\cdot|x)) \right]
$$

Let's break this down:
1.  **$r_\theta(x, y)$:** The reward term, which encourages the policy to generate responses that humans would prefer.
2.  **$D_{KL}(\pi_\phi || \pi_{\text{SFT}})$:** The KL penalty term. It measures the "distance" between the token probabilities produced by our current policy $\pi_\phi$ and our original SFT policy $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$.
3.  **$\beta$:** A hyperparameter that controls the strength of the KL penalty. A low $\beta$ allows for more aggressive optimization of the reward, while a high $\beta$ keeps the policy closer to the SFT model, ensuring linguistic coherence and preventing mode collapse.

This objective is optimized using an RL algorithm like **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO is a policy gradient method well-suited for this task because it uses a "clipped" objective function to prevent excessively large policy updates in a single step, contributing to more stable training in this complex, high-dimensional action space.

#### **8.4 Sources of Complexity and Instability**

The SFT-RLHF pipeline is powerful but notoriously complex and fragile. Understanding its failure modes is critical for practitioners.

*   **Computational Complexity:** The full pipeline requires training and holding in memory at least three large models simultaneously during the RL phase:
    1.  The policy being trained ($\pi_\phi$).
    2.  The reference SFT policy ($\pi_{\text{SFT}}$) for the KL calculation.
    3.  The reward model ($r_\theta$) to provide the reward signal.
    This represents a significant engineering and computational burden.

*   **Reward Model Hacking:** The final aligned model is only as good as its reward model. Since $r_\theta$ is an imperfect proxy for true human preferences, the policy $\pi_\phi$ can learn to exploit its flaws. For example, it might learn that the reward model over-weights verbosity and thus produce unnecessarily long answers.

*   **Distributional Mismatch:** The reward model is trained on responses generated by the SFT model. As the policy $\pi_\phi$ is optimized, it begins to generate responses that are out-of-distribution for the reward model. The reward model's predictions on these novel responses may be unreliable, leading to a faulty optimization signal and unstable training.

*   **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The RL phase is highly sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly the KL coefficient $\beta$ and the PPO learning rate. An improperly tuned $\beta$ can lead to a model that either ignores the reward signal entirely (too high) or devolves into incoherent text (too low).

#### **8.5 Conclusion**

The journey from a raw, pre-trained language model to a helpful and aligned assistant is a sophisticated, multi-stage process. Supervised Fine-Tuning lays the foundation, teaching the model the basic format of interaction through high-quality demonstrations. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback then refines this behavior, moving beyond simple imitation to optimize for a learned model of human preferences.

This pipeline, centered on the RLHF process of training a reward model and then optimizing a policy against it with a KL-divergence constraint, represents the state-of-the-art in model alignment. However, its inherent complexity and instability have spurred research into simpler and more robust alternatives, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which you may encounter in more advanced studies. For now, understanding the SFT and RLHF paradigm provides you with a deep insight into the core principles and challenges of making powerful AI systems work for human benefit.
