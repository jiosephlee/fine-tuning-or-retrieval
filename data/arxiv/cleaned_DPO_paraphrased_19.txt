\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning abilities, but their behavior is challenging to control precisely because their training is entirely unsupervised.
To improve controllability, common approaches gather human feedback on the relative merits of model outputs and then fine-tune the unsupervised LM to better reflect these preferences, typically using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Despite its popularity, RLHF is often complicated and can be unstable, involving first fitting a reward model based on human preference data, and then using reinforcement learning to adjust the LM in a way that maximizes this reward, all while ensuring the model does not deviate too far from its original behavior.
This work proposes a novel formulation of the reward model in RLHF, allowing the optimal policy corresponding to the reward to be recovered in closed form, so the standard RLHF problem can be reduced to optimizing a simple classification objective.
This approach, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and lightweight, removing the necessity to sample from the LM during fine-tuning or engage in extensive hyperparameter searches.
Empirical results indicate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as effectively as, or better than, current approaches. Remarkably, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in sentiment control and matches or improves generation quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being much easier to use and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive corpora have demonstrated unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Yet, these models learn from human-produced data reflecting a diverse array of intentions, objectives, and expertise. Not all of these traits are desirable for imitation; for instance, we may prefer our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent coding errors to address them, but ideally, we want the model to emulate the (potentially scarce) high-level programming proficiency captured in its training set when it generates code. Likewise, it is beneficial for a language model to be \textit{informed} about widespread misconceptions held by half the population, but we would not want it to endorse such misconceptions half the time they are mentioned! Thus, the ability to \emph{select appropriate outputs and conduct} from the model’s broad \textit{range of information and skills} is essential for developing safe, reliable, and manageable AI systems~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Although prevailing approaches generally guide LMs toward human-aligned behavior via reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL-based training goal applied by current techniques can in fact be exactly optimized with a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the process of preference modeling.

Broadly speaking, current approaches imbue language models with desired behaviors by utilizing carefully constructed datasets of human preferences that reflect what users perceive as safe and useful behavior. This preference learning phase follows an initial stage of large-scale, unsupervised pre-training on massive text corpora. The simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-curated examples of strong responses; however, the most effective strategies rely on reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on data reflecting human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then applied to adjust the language model’s policy, steering it to generate answers that receive high reward scores, while staying reasonably close to the original model. Although RLHF leads to models with remarkable dialogue and programming performance, its workflow is much more elaborate than supervised training—requiring the training of several language models and in-training sampling from model policies—which results in high computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for optimizing a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning frameworks. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly optimizes the same goal as conventional RLHF algorithms—maximizing reward with a KL-divergence regularization—but with a more accessible and easy-to-train formulation. Conceptually, DPO updates adjust the relative log-likelihood of favored versus non-favored outputs, incorporating a per-instance, adaptive weighting scheme to avoid the model collapse seen with a naive probability ratio approach. Similar to prior algorithms, DPO utilizes a theoretical preference structure (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess the alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. However, in contrast to previous techniques that employ the preference model to define a loss for training a reward model—then subsequently optimize a policy based on that reward—DPO reparameterizes the preference loss to depend directly on the policy itself. With access to a dataset of human-labeled preferences among model completions, DPO is able to train a policy via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, effectively yielding an optimal policy for an implicit reward shaped by the collected preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that trains language models from preferences without using RL. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of established approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, in preference-based learning tasks like sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, on models with sizes up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of increasing size have demonstrated the ability to solve some problems in a zero-shot setting \citep{radford2019language} or by using few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with user instructions are notably enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets composed of instructions and human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to generalize to instructions not present in the original tuning set, thereby improving their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction-tuning has proven successful, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality tends to be more feasible than gathering expert-labeled examples. As a result, subsequent research has focused on further fine-tuning LLMs with datasets reflecting human preferences, leading to improved performance in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction compliance \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches first train a neural network-based reward model to align with datasets of preferences, often using preference models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Then, the language model is further trained to maximize this reward, typically employing reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related algorithms \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related avenue of research involves using instruction-tuned LLMs, refined with human feedback, to produce synthetic preference datasets focused on targeted criteria like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on light supervision from humans in the form of text-based rubrics for annotating outputs. These approaches bring together two main research threads: one concerned with applying reinforcement learning to train language models for various tasks~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another focused on generic techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
While using relative human preferences is attractive, the process of fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning continues to pose significant practical obstacles; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language-related applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various methods. When preferences or rankings over actions—rather than numerical rewards—are used for contextual bandit learning, this is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since CDBs lack absolute reward signals, their theoretical analysis replaces the standard optimal policy notion with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy whose expected win rate is at least 50\% when compared to \textit{every} other policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike in CDBs, where preference feedback is provided in an online fashion, preference-based policy learning from humans often relies on a fixed dataset of offline action pairs with preference labels \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns using binary preferences from an \textit{unknown} underlying 'scoring' function, instead of direct reward feedback \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several PbRL algorithms have been developed, including those that can leverage off-policy preference data, but they typically involve a two-step procedure: first, estimating the hidden scoring or reward function, then optimizing the policy with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a one-step policy optimization method that directly trains a policy to adhere to observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF framework outlined by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently by \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process typically consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by using supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-trained language model on carefully curated datasets relevant to the target task (such as summarization or dialogue), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During this second stage, the SFT model is given input prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These response pairs are shown to human annotators, who indicate a preference for one of the two, represented as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the chosen and rejected answers, respectively, from $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences originate from an unknown underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is inaccessible. There are several methods to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model commonly used (though more general ranking frameworks like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable if multiple ordered responses are available). The BT model posits that the human preference probability $p^*$ is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Viewing this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. Within LMs, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized with the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, and a linear layer is attached to the top transformer block to yield a scalar reward score \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, earlier studies standardize the output so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for each $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the acquired reward function serves as feedback for the language model. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ determines the regularization strength from the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial SFT model $\pisft$. The language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is commonly initialized to $\pisft$ in practice. Incorporating this regularization is crucial for keeping the model's outputs close to the data distribution where the reward model is trustworthy, as well as for preserving diversity in generations and avoiding mode collapse on single high-reward outputs. Since text generation involves discrete outputs, the objective cannot be differentiated directly and is generally optimized via reinforcement learning techniques. The prevailing strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and employs PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} to maximize it.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when deploying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that works directly from preferences. In contrast to conventional RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method utilizes a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows us to extract its optimal policy in closed form—eliminating the need for an RL training loop. 

As we elaborate below, the core idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies. This lets us convert a loss defined over reward functions into a loss defined over policies. By adopting this change-of-variables perspective, we circumvent the need to train a separate, explicit reward model, while still optimizing with respect to established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, our policy network jointly encapsulates both the language model and the (implicit) reward structure.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective, as formulated in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, under a generic reward function $r$, similar to previous research. As established in prior works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the optimal solution to the reward maximization problem with a KL constraint, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the normalization constant. Detailed derivations can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation1}. Even if we use $r_{\phi}$, the MLE-based estimate of the actual reward function $r^*$, calculating $Z(x)$ directly remains computationally costly~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making this form difficult to leverage in real-world applications. Nevertheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be rearranged to represent the reward in terms of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the still-unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and simplifying, we arrive at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the true reward $r^*$ and the corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model requires only reward differences between two options, specifically ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. If we plug in the form of $r^*(x, y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model given in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function cancels out, leading to an expression for the human preference probability that depends solely on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Therefore, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework must satisfy the following preference relationship:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The full derivation is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry framework, a similar approach can be applied to more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preference now expressed in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parametrized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
By doing this, we infer an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where $\pi_\theta$ itself acts as the optimal policy. Additionally, this approach is mathematically equivalent to learning a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, and consequently benefits from properties like consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} will elaborate on these theoretical guarantees of DPO and how they relate to previous methods.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, examining the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is instructive. The gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{increases if reward order is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{pushes up probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{pushes down probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward assigned by the language model and the reference model is defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). In essence, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to boost the probability of preferred responses $y_w$ and suppress the probability of less preferred responses $y_l$. Crucially, this effect is modulated by how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ favors the dispreferred output, multiplied by $\beta$—that is, the weight reflects how much the implicit reward model’s ranking diverges from the correct preference, accounting for the KL constraint’s influence. Experimental results demonstrate this weighting is vital; omitting the weighting factor, as in a simple baseline, can lead the language model to collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO workflow proceeds as follows: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, and annotate them using human preferences to build an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$.  
In real-world applications, it is preferable to leverage existing public preference datasets rather than collect new samples and annotations. Since these datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when it is available. If $\pisft$ is missing, we instead define $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred responses ${(x, y_w)}$, formally, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the practical $\piref$ used by DPO. Additional implementation and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, supply theoretical justification, and discuss how DPO addresses certain drawbacks found in actor-critic methods commonly applied in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO achieves policy learning using a single maximum likelihood objective, thus avoiding the need to separately fit a reward function or carry out RL. Observe that the optimization objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} aligns with a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is given by $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. The parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized in a manner that is equivalent to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after applying a change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical framework for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the set of reward models that can be learned, and show that it enables the exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation on reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this forms an equivalence relation, which divides the set of all reward functions into equivalence classes. We can then formulate the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry preference model, any two reward functions belonging to the same family generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the same optimal policy in the context of the constrained RL formulation.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights the well-established issue of under-specification present in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. To address this ambiguity, it is common to introduce identifiability conditions to obtain meaningful MLE solutions for Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function from a given equivalence class leads to an identical optimal policy; therefore, for our final goal, we only need to recover any representative reward function from that optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under fairly general assumptions, all reward function classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically the Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which produces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the above reparameterization. We introduce the projection operator $f$ defined by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ effectively standardizes the reward function by subtracting the log partition function (a function of $x$ alone) associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term only depends on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ using the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}—which is valid for any reward function—we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, $f$ outputs a member of the equivalence class of $r$ in the desired form, so the proposed reparameterization covers all reward functions of interest without loss of generality.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying the precise reward function within each equivalence class that is chosen by the DPO reparameterization, namely, the function that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, following Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (non-negative probabilities summing to one).
Moreover, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} characterizes the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$.
The central idea of the DPO algorithm is to introduce specific constraints to the otherwise under-identified Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) class of preference models, ensuring the class of representable reward models is preserved, while also making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} computationally tractable for any given prompt $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instability}

Our framework can also help identify instability issues present in standard actor-critic methods commonly used for RLHF, like PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure and particularly examine the RL fine-tuning phase as described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. Connections can be established with the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, which applies to the constrained RL setting in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \| \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, with $\pi^*$ being the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} defined by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some mathematical manipulation, this leads to the following objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}

This formulation is equivalent to the objectives used in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that employ the DPO-equivalent reward function for the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not change the optimal solution, its absence can result in the policy gradient exhibiting high variance, leading to instability during training. One way to address this is by using a learned value function to approximate the normalization, although this approach can be challenging to optimize. Another common strategy in previous work is to normalize rewards using a baseline derived from a human-generated completion, which effectively acts as a Monte Carlo estimate using a single sample for the normalization term. In comparison, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines entirely.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess DPO's capability to learn policies directly from preference data. We begin with a controlled text-generation scenario, investigating how effectively DPO balances reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization relative to popular preference optimization methods like PPO. We then test DPO on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF tasks, including summarization and conversational benchmarks. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO typically matches or surpasses strong baselines such as RLHF with PPO and outperforms the strategy of selecting the best out of $N$ generated samples according to a trained reward model. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline our experimental methodology; further details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We evaluate our methods on three distinct open-domain text generation tasks. Across all experiments, the methods learn a policy using a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ corresponds to a movie review prefix sampled from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy's goal is to generate a continuation $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. To enable systematic evaluation, we \textit{generate} preference pairs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier that satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on the IMDB train set reviews (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for more information). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post and the policy must produce a summary $y$ capturing the post’s main ideas. We adopt the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preferences collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is obtained by fine-tuning on human-written Reddit post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF toolkit. The preference data was collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a comparable SFT model trained on similar data. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ represents a user prompt, which can range from technical inquiries to personal advice requests. The policy must generate a helpful and engaging reply $y$. For this, we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k conversations between a person and an AI assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses from a large (but unspecified) language model, along with a preference annotation identifying the human-preferred answer. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists for this domain, we fine-tune a publicly available language model exclusively on preferred completions to build the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we first examine their performance in the controlled sentiment generation scenario. Here, we assess each method based on its trade-off curve between achieved reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this is feasible since we have access to the exact reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we measure algorithm performance through their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. For this, we use GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judges to assess summary quality and response helpfulness in both summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks. In the summarization task, the baseline is the reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline corresponds to the preferred responses in the test data. Although prior work indicates that LMs can outperform standard metrics as automatic evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we supplement our study with human evaluations in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments} to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation. Our results show that GPT-4's assessments are highly aligned with human judgments, with human-GPT-4 agreement matching or exceeding the agreement observed among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a range of established methods for aligning language models with human preferences. As a basic strategy, we apply zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization tasks and use 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We further include the \textbf{SFT} model as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is trained using supervised learning on the preferred output $y_w$, taken from either the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general-purpose LM (for single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised approach we test is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while explicitly reducing the likelihood of $y_l$, making use of a tunable coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood loss term. We also evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} with a reward model learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that uses the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment experiments. For sentiment tasks, we utilize two PPO-GT versions: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version with reward normalization and further hyperparameter optimization (we apply these tweaks to standard PPO runs as well). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} method, which samples $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and selects the top candidate based on scores assigned by a reward model trained on preference data. While this technique often performs strongly by separating the reward model quality from PPO training, it is not computationally feasible for even moderate $N$, since generating $N$ completions for each query at test time is costly.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue as measured by GPT-4; among the methods tested, only DPO surpasses the performance of the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates for varying sampling temperatures tracked throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms dataset labels across different sampling temperatures as training progresses.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-regularized reward maximization objective commonly employed in standard RLHF approaches seeks to maximize reward while simultaneously preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is essential to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; securing marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially increased KL is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} displays the reward-KL tradeoff frontier for several algorithms on the sentiment task. For each method, we conduct several training sessions, adjusting the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in every run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and varying random seeds for preferred-FT), amounting to 22 total runs. After every 100 training updates, up to convergence, we evaluate each resulting policy over a collection of test prompts, measuring both the mean reward using the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the cumulative per-step KL divergences across the sequence.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO yields the most favorable reward-KL frontier, reaching the highest rewards while maintaining low KL. This outcome is particularly significant for several reasons. First, although both DPO and PPO target the same objective, DPO demonstrates far greater efficiency; DPO’s reward/KL frontier surpasses that of PPO in all cases. Second, DPO achieves a superior frontier even compared to PPO with access to the true reward function (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess DPO’s fine-tuning effectiveness on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak correlation with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier research has demonstrated that leveraging PPO for fine-tuning LMs on human preference data can yield more satisfactory summaries. To compare various approaches, we sample outputs on the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test split and compute the mean win rate against the gold reference completions. Samples from all models are generated across temperature values from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. At a sampling temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate of about 61\%, outperforming PPO, which reaches approximately 57\% at its best (also at 0.0). DPO further surpasses the best-of-$N$ baseline in terms of maximum win rate. It’s important to mention that we did not extensively tune DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these figures might be a conservative estimate of its full capability. Additionally, DPO demonstrates far greater stability with respect to sampling temperature when compared to PPO, whose win rate can fall to the level of the base GPT-J model as temperature rises. Preferred-FT brings only marginal improvement over the SFT baseline. A direct human evaluation comparing DPO and PPO is described in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO samples generated at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over those from PPO at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on the portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split containing just one round of human-assistant exchange. To determine the win rates, GPT-4 assessments use the preferred completions from the test set as the benchmark for each method. As there is no established SFT baseline for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model using selected completions so that outputs are in-distribution, and then continue training with DPO. We further compare to the strongest result among 128 Preferred-FT completions (since we observe the Best of $N$ baseline levels off at $N=128$ for this task; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompt version of the base Pythia-2.8B model, noting that DPO matches or surpasses other methods at their optimal sampling temperatures. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} following instructions from a recognized implementation \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but cannot identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the original Pythia-2.8B model. Given our findings on TL;DR and the fact that both techniques maximize the same reward, we treat the Best of 128 as an approximate indicator for PPO-level outcomes. In summary, DPO is the only method that is computationally efficient while surpassing the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving results comparable to or better than the much costlier Best of 128 baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} also demonstrates that DPO attains its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 Judgments Against Human Evaluations}
\label{sec:human-judgments}

In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally requests a judgment on which summary is more concise. We include this latter prompt because GPT-4, when using \textbf{GPT-4 (S)}, tends to favor summaries that are longer and more repetitive compared to human preferences. Full prompt wordings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We carry out three separate comparisons: one with the best-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), one with the lowest-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and one with an intermediate method (SFT, temperature 0.25). \section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing effective and aligned language models. In this work, we introduced DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge to a traditional RL framework just to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a correspondence between reward functions and language model policies. This allows the model to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising on generality. DPO achieves results on par with, or superior to, current RLHF algorithms—including those using PPO—while requiring minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the difficulty of training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings highlight several significant avenues for future exploration. One key question is how well the DPO policy generalizes to out-of-distribution data in comparison to approaches that learn directly from an explicit reward signal. While our preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies can achieve generalization on par with PPO-based methods, further systematic investigation is required. For instance, could leveraging self-labeling through the DPO policy enable effective utilization of unlabeled prompts? Another aspect to explore is the manifestation of reward over-optimization within direct preference optimization, and whether the modest reduction in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right serves as evidence of this issue. Additionally, our experiments are limited to models with up to 6B parameters; scaling DPO methods to much larger, state-of-the-art models represents a promising direction. On the evaluation side, we observe that GPT-4-computed win rates are sensitive to the phrasing of the evaluation prompt, suggesting future work could focus on optimizing prompt design to elicit more reliable automated judgments. Lastly, the potential applications of DPO extend far beyond human preference-based language model training, opening opportunities for generative modeling in various other domains.
