\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning abilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training process. Current approaches to achieve such steerability rely on gathering human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF involves a complicated and sometimes unstable pipeline: first training a reward model that captures human preferences, and then fine-tuning the large LM via reinforcement learning to optimize this learned reward while preventing significant deviation from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward function within RLHF, which permits direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF challenge to be addressed simply by minimizing a classification loss.} The proposed method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} performing LM sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our evaluations demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences at least as effectively as existing techniques. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling sentiment in generated text and matches or enhances response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects a diverse range of aims, values, and expertise. Not all of these aims and skills are desirable to replicate; for instance, although we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, when it comes to producing code, we prefer to steer the model toward the (possibly infrequent) high-quality coding examples found in its training set. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{cognizant} of a widespread misconception held by roughly 50\% of people, yet we do not want the model to assert this misconception as true in half of the queries concerning it! Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is vital for creating AI systems that are reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches often guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly used can be precisely optimized by employing a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches embed desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated collections of human preferences that reflect the kinds of behaviors considered safe and useful by people. This stage of learning preferences follows an initial phase of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest method for learning preferences involves supervised fine-tuning on human-provided examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective techniques employ reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then applied to adjust the language model’s policy to generate responses that receive high rewards while remaining close to the original model’s behavior. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding skills, its workflow is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the policy during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly tailor a language model according to human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly maximizes the same goal as traditional RLHF methods (i.e., reward maximization subject to a KL-divergence constraint), yet remains easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of favored responses over less favored ones, while integrating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight that avoids the model degradation commonly observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior approaches, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) which evaluates how well a reward function corresponds to observed preference data. Nevertheless, unlike existing techniques that utilize the preference model to create a loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy against this learned reward, {\methodac} employs a variable transformation to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Consequently, with a dataset comprising human preferences over model outputs, {\methodac} can optimize the policy through a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{bypassing the need to explicitly learn a separate reward model or perform policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function derived from the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} matches or surpasses the performance of current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow larger in scale can perform certain tasks without prior examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal examples provided in prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to extend their generalization capabilities to instructions that were not part of the tuning dataset, thereby boosting overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, collecting \textit{relative} human judgments regarding response quality is often simpler than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, which has led to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with the preference dataset using a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward through reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research avenue exploits LLMs fine-tuned for instruction following with human feedback to produce synthetic preference data targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These techniques merge two research streams: one focused on reinforcement learning for training language models across diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of employing relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; this paper introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit frameworks and reinforcement learning, leading to the development of multiple techniques. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in a contextual bandit framework, this is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute reward signals are unavailable in CDBs, theoretical treatment replaces the idea of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves an expected winning probability of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. A key difference is that in CDB scenarios, preference feedback is received in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences usually involves training on a fixed offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates by learning from binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} latent `scoring' function instead of direct reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Numerous PbRL algorithms have been proposed, many of which can incorporate off-policy preference data, but commonly rely on first estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) and then optimizing the policy based on it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified, single-stage method that directly learns a policy to fulfill preference constraints.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (as well as in subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This approach typically consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by refining a pre-trained language model through supervised learning using high-quality datasets tailored to the specific downstream task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These response pairs are then shown to human annotators, who indicate their preference by choosing one answer over the other, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the favored and unfavored outputs within $(y_1, y_2)$ respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to capture such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly employed option (though more general models like the Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also compatible when multiple ranked responses are available). According to the BT model, the probability of human preference $p^*$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. When framed as a binary classification task, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\left(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\right) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model applications, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized based on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by a linear layer placed atop the last transformer layer that outputs a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward signal, previous work often normalizes the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ holds for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the previously trained reward function acts as feedback for the language model. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the parameter $\beta$ regulates the extent to which the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ diverges from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$.  
In practical scenarios, the policy of the language model $\pi_\theta$ is typically initialized to $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial as it restricts the model from straying excessively from the data distribution on which the reward model provides reliable evaluations, while also preserving diversity in generated outputs and avoiding collapse to a few high-scoring responses. Because language generation is inherently discrete, this objective is not differentiable and is therefore commonly optimized through reinforcement learning techniques. The prevalent strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of implementing reinforcement learning algorithms on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly uses preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques, which first train a reward model and then apply reinforcement learning to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the need for a separate reward modeling phase and directly tunes the language model based on preference information}{utilizes a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows us to derive the optimal policy in closed form, without relying on an RL training loop}. 

As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined on reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to omit the explicit step of reward modeling}{removes the requirement to fit a distinct reward model}, yet still permits optimization according to established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as previous studies, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, under a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem stated in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization term, or partition function, is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing a maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ to approximate the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, complicating the practical use of this form. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can represent the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization factor $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the difference in rewards between two outputs, expressed as ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the form from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} results in the partition function cancelling out, allowing the human preference probability to be written solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Therefore, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
For the full derivation, see Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived under the Bradley-Terry model, similar results can be obtained for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the likelihood of human preference data based on the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to eliminate the explicit step of modeling the reward, while also removing the necessity for reinforcement learning-based optimization.}{Here, we implicitly fit a reward by employing an alternative parameterization where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits beneficial theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We expand on the theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of the favored outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of the less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the influence of each example is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the reward of the dispreferred completion relative to the preferred one, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting the intensity of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, since omitting it leads to deterioration of the language model’s performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate two completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then obtain human preference labels to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference model $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a target $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these datasets are typically created using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever it is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not available, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, expressed as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \pi(y_w \mid x) \right]}$. This strategy helps reduce the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed during DPO training. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, present its theoretical foundations, and connect the benefits of DPO to challenges encountered in actor-critic methods commonly applied in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the need to fit an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by employing a single maximum likelihood objective to directly learn the policy. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we optimize our parametric policy model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a way that is equivalent to reward model training described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once the variables are appropriately transformed. In this section, we will develop the theoretical foundations of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the set of possible reward models learned, and confirm that it permits exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation defined over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be presented:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class produce an identical optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are simple and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This first lemma highlights a common under-specification problem encountered in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, additional identifiability conditions are typically required to guarantee meaningful maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma establishes that all reward functions within a given equivalence class result in the same optimal policy. Therefore, when optimizing our final objective, it suffices to recover any reward function from the optimal equivalence class. The subsequent theorem is demonstrated in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (specifically the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to show that a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ can be represented using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Because the normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ using the expression on the right side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds for any reward), we find
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, $f$ maps $r$ onto a member of its equivalence class that has the desired reparameterized form, showing that our proposed reward model representation is fully general.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it identifies the specific reward function selected by the DPO reparameterization within each equivalence class, namely the function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{ by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures $\pi(y \mid x)$ is a legitimate probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward function $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO method is that by enforcing particular constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) class of preference models, we retain the expressiveness of the reward function class while making the optimal policy described in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for all inputs $x$.

\subsection{Instability in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework can also be employed to analyze the instability issues present in conventional actor-critic algorithms commonly applied in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure, concentrating specifically on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach can be linked to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. Consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, where the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, with $\pi^*$ representing the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which used a DPO-equivalent reward from the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it can lead to high variance in the policy gradient estimates, causing instability during training. To handle this normalization, one can introduce a learned value function, but this adds complexity to the optimization. Alternatively, previous approaches normalized rewards via a human completion baseline, effectively using a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate for the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reformulation provides a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, in comparison to popular preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines including RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming the best trajectory among $N$ samples selected via a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ corresponds to the beginning of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the model is tasked with generating a continuation $y$ that expresses a positive sentiment. To facilitate a controlled assessment, we \textit{create} preference pairs from generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we adapt GPT-2-large until convergence on training reviews from the IMDb dataset (additional information in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). Moving to \textbf{summarization}, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the objective is to produce a summary $y$ that captures the main ideas of the post. Consistent with previous research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for RLHF. Notably, the human preference dataset was gathered based on outputs from a different but similarly fine-tuned SFT model as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, the input $x$ represents a user query, which can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for advice on relationships. The policy is expected to generate a response $y$ that is both engaging and helpful. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, consisting of 170,000 exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two model-generated responses from a large, yet unspecified, language model, along with a human preference label indicating the favored reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists for this task, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model exclusively on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we measure performance in the controlled sentiment generation scenario by examining the frontier of attained reward versus the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this frontier can be computed since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in real-world settings where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, using GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response found in the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate the use of GPT-4 for assessment in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4’s evaluations strongly align with human judgments, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding the agreement among different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate various established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we apply zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. Additionally, we assess the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves fine-tuning via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$, sourced either from the SFT model (in tasks like controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method includes an adjustable parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controlling the weight of the unlikelihood component. We also experiment with \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, employing a reward function derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment task. For our sentiment evaluations, we utilize two versions of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and adjusts hyperparameters for enhanced results (these improvements are also applied when using standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we examine the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest-ranked completion according to a reward function trained on the preference data. While this approach achieves strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ outputs for each test query.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; among the methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels regardless of sampling temperature during the training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the policy's divergence from a reference policy. Hence, when evaluating different algorithms, it is crucial to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; a marginal increase in reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward versus KL frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training sessions, each with varying hyperparameters controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This set comprises a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a collection of test prompts, calculating both the average reward under the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of KL divergences at each timestep.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi \mid\mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes the most effective frontier, delivering the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons: firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates significantly greater efficiency, with its reward-KL balance clearly outperforming PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO in terms of the frontier, \emph{even when PPO utilizes ground truth reward information} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often exhibit weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO guided by human preferences yields more accurate summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the average win rate relative to the reference completions. Completions across all methods are sampled using temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings reveal that at temperature 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s best performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a superior maximum win rate compared to the top-performing $N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting these results may be a conservative estimate of DPO’s true capabilities. Furthermore, DPO exhibits greater stability across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose performance tends to decline to the baseline GPT-J model level when temperature increases. Preferred-FT shows minimal gains over the initial SFT model. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO via human evaluations is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

On the single-turn dialogue task, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training} that contains a single human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we use the preferred completions from the test set as the reference to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions—ensuring these completions fall within the model’s distribution—and subsequently perform training with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the best result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the base Pythia-2.8B model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the best-performing temperatures for each method. We further evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we fail to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Given our TL;DR results and that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate proxy for PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient approach that enhances results over the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and delivers comparable or superior performance relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO reaches its optimal performance fairly rapidly.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning problem into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO uncovers a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be trained to align with human preferences \textit{directly} through a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising on generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or exceeding that of current RLHF methods, including those using PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions to be addressed in subsequent research. In particular, how does the DPO policy perform when generalizing beyond the training distribution compared to approaches that learn directly from a defined reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize in a manner comparable to PPO-based models, though a more thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, is it possible to leverage self-labeling with the DPO policy to effectively utilize unlabeled prompts? Another area of interest is understanding how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the minor performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this phenomenon. Furthermore, although we have tested models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models that are significantly larger presents a promising avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluation methods, our observations indicate that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are sensitive to the prompt used; therefore, future studies could focus on optimizing prompt design to obtain more reliable automated evaluations. Lastly, DPO has potential applications extending beyond language models trained on human preferences, such as training generative models across diverse data modalities.
