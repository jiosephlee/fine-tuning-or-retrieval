\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop certain reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging due to their entirely unsupervised training process. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve collecting human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better reflect these preferences, often utilizing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and frequently unstable method, which first involves training a reward model that approximates human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large LM to maximize this reward while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits direct extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF objective to be addressed using only a straightforward classification loss.} Our method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the need for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as well as or better than existing techniques. In particular, DPO fine-tuning outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves output quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop unexpectedly powerful abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from data produced by humans who have diverse intentions, priorities, and expertise. Some of these intentions and skills may not be ideal to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, when generating code, we prefer the model to emphasize the (possibly infrequent) high-quality coding examples found in the training set. Likewise, we might want the language model to \textit{be aware} of a widespread misconception held by half of the population, but certainly not to assert that misconception as true in half of the related queries! Put differently, carefully choosing the model's \emph{preferred responses and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches often use reinforcement learning (RL) to guide LMs towards human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be exactly optimized via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, substantially simplifying the process of preference learning.

At a broad level, current techniques embed desired behaviors into a language model by utilizing carefully selected sets of human preferences that reflect the types of actions humans consider safe and beneficial. This preference learning phase follows an initial stage of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a massive text corpus. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective approaches leverage reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques train a reward model based on a dataset of human preferences, then apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy so it generates responses that receive high rewards while remaining close to the original model’s behavior. Despite RLHF yielding models with remarkable conversational and programming capabilities, the RLHF workflow is substantially more complicated than supervised learning, requiring multiple language models to be trained and sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a method that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches—maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint—while being easier to implement and more straightforward to train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log probability of favored responses over less favored ones, incorporating a dynamic, per-example importance weight that helps avoid the model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that quantifies the alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. However, unlike traditional methods that first define a preference loss to train a reward model and then optimize a policy based on that model, DPO reformulates the preference loss directly as a function of the policy through a change of variables. With a dataset containing human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO thus enables policy optimization using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without needing to learn an explicit, separate reward model or perform policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary innovation is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that DPO performs comparably or better than current approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, in preference learning across various tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with just a few-shot prompting strategy \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intentions can be substantially enhanced by fine-tuning them on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the instruction set used during fine-tuning and generally boosts their practical usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been quite successful, gathering \textit{relative} human evaluations of output quality is often simpler than collecting expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs using datasets of human preferences, which has led to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model that aligns with the preference data under frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then proceed to fine-tune a language model to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research avenue uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback on instruction-following tasks to produce synthetic preference datasets targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics for the model’s annotations. This line of work represents a fusion of two research streams: one focusing on training language models via reinforcement learning for diverse objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of employing relative human preferences, reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; in response, this paper proposes a theoretically-grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, learning policies based on preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple techniques proposed. When contextual bandit learning relies on preferences or action rankings rather than explicit rewards, it is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute rewards, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves an expected win rate of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, preference labels are obtained in an online manner, whereas in learning from human preferences, the data typically consists of a fixed offline dataset of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates by learning from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are several PbRL algorithms available, some of which can leverage off-policy preference samples, but they generally require first estimating the latent scoring function (i.e., the reward model) explicitly before optimizing it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-step policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to comply with the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further expanded in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process typically consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference between them, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the favored completion and $y_l$ the less preferred one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preferences are presumed to arise from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown. Various methods exist for modeling such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a widely used approach (though more flexible Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be integrated if multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the human preference probability distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can define a parametric reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters using maximum likelihood estimation. By casting this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language modeling scenarios, the function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the final transformer block to output a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous research often normalizes the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning (RL) stage, the reward function that has been learned is employed to guide the language model through feedback. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the parameter $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is initialized with $\pisft$. Incorporating this constraint is crucial as it restricts the model from straying excessively from the distribution where the reward model performs reliably, while also preserving diversity in generated outputs and avoiding collapse to a few repetitive, high-reward responses. Given the discrete nature of language output, the objective is non-differentiable and generally optimized using reinforcement learning techniques. The common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it via PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches that first learn a reward function and then apply RL to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the need for reward modeling altogether by directly optimizing the language model with preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived in closed form, removing the necessity of an RL training loop}.  
As will be elaborated upon shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling the conversion of a loss defined on reward functions into one defined on policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{permits us to bypass explicit reward modeling}{removes the requirement to train a separate, explicit reward model}, yet still performs optimization consistent with established human preference frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously encodes the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as in previous studies, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined with a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it is straightforward to demonstrate that the optimal policy solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. For a detailed derivation, please refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ as a proxy for the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, complicating its practical use. Nonetheless, rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} allows us to rewrite the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulations, we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward function $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the reward differences between two outputs, expressed as ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference formulation in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function cancels out, allowing us to represent the human preference probabilities exclusively via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, analogous results can be obtained for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the likelihood of human preference data directly through the optimal policy instead of relying on the reward model, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a policy parameterized by $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective function for the policy can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{By doing so, we avoid explicitly modeling the reward function and eliminate the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In this manner, we implicitly learn a reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits beneficial theoretical guarantees, including consistency properties given appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate on these theoretical aspects of DPO and its relation to prior work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, the loss gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ encourages increasing the probability of the preferred output $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing the probability of the less favored output $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly favors the dispreferred completion, scaled by the factor $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting, as a simplified variant lacking this coefficient may lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The typical DPO process involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate them with human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and a target $\beta$.  
In practical settings, it is preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than producing new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these datasets are typically generated from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, formulated as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the mismatch between the true reference distribution—often inaccessible—and the $\piref$ used in DPO. Additional details about implementation and tuning parameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
This section offers an expanded explanation of the DPO approach, presents its theoretical foundations, and connects the benefits of DPO to the limitations found in actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO circumvents the need for \rev{explicit reward estimation}{directly fitting an explicit reward} and avoids employing reinforcement learning by training the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework with a reward representation defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Under a suitable change of variables, optimizing our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ aligns exactly with the reward model optimization presented in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the space of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the precise retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. We can then formulate the following pair of lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically under the Bradley-Terry preference setup, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class produce the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations are simple and thus are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma reflects a familiar issue of under-specification inherent in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is often necessary to introduce extra identifiability conditions to secure guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma clarifies that every reward function from the same equivalence class corresponds to an identical optimal policy; consequently, our ultimate goal focuses on recovering any representative reward function within that class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, all reward function classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce model family (including the Bradley-Terry special case) can be expressed through the reparameterization 
    \[
        r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}
    \]
    for some model $\pi(y \mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from $r$’s equivalence class admits the above reparameterized form. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function related to $\pi_r$. Since the normalization depends solely on $x$, the transformed function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally for reward functions) yields
\[
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Therefore, the projection $f$ maps $r$ to an equivalent reward function of the proposed canonical form without any loss of generality.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it identifies precisely which reward function within an equivalence class the DPO reparameterization picks — specifically, the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
    \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y \mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y \mid x)$ defines a valid probability distribution (nonnegative and summing to unity).
In light of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, this expression corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy derived from $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO algorithm is that by enforcing particular constraints on the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, we can maintain the set of representable reward functions while simultaneously ensuring that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically tractable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our framework can also be applied to analyze the sources of instability in common actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF workflow, concentrating on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This allows us to relate the problem to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained reinforcement learning problem given by \ref{eq:RL}. Consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, and the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} that corresponds to the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this results in the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} which use a DPO-equivalent reward from the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Within this formulation, the normalization term inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the policy's optimum, omitting it can lead to high variance in the policy gradient estimates, thus destabilizing the training process. To handle this normalization, one may use a learned value function; however, this approach can be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, previous works have addressed normalization by employing a human completion baseline, effectively a Monte Carlo estimate from a single sample to approximate the normalization constant. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that inherently eliminates the need for any baseline adjustments.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, especially when compared to popular preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines including RLHF with PPO, as well as approaches that select the best out of $N$ trajectories based on a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experiments, the algorithms are trained to learn a policy based on a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ represents the beginning of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal of the policy is to produce an output $y$ that expresses a positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation setting, preference pairs are \textit{generated} from model outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, where it holds that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we adapt GPT-2-large until it converges on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional information is provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ consists of posts from Reddit forums, and the policy’s objective is to generate a summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. Consistent with previous studies, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} alongside human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The human preference dataset was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from outputs generated by a separate, yet similarly trained, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query that can range from scientific questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. The policy must produce a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k interactions between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two candidate responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a label indicating which response the human preferred. In this scenario, since no pre-trained SFT model exists, we fine-tune an available language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our study employs two distinct methods for assessment. To examine how well each algorithm performs in optimizing the constrained reward maximization objective, within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm’s performance by plotting the frontier of attained reward against the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this frontier can be determined since we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluation of summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline is formed by reference summaries from the test dataset; for dialogue, the baseline consists of the preferred responses within the test set. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as more reliable automated evaluators than conventional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study to support our decision to use GPT-4 for evaluation, as detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate that GPT-4’s assessments align closely with human judgments, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding the agreement level found among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate multiple established strategies for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we test zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. We also assess the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ sourced either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization tasks) or a generic language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised approach considered is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$, incorporating an optional weighting parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. Additionally, we evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} using a reward signal derived from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment scenario. For sentiment experiments, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that applies reward normalization and hyperparameter tuning to enhance performance (the same adjustments are applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we examine the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, which samples $N$ outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest scoring response according to a reward function trained on the preference dataset. While this technique yields strong results by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires generating $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogues; among the methods tested, only DPO surpasses the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently maintains an advantage over the dataset labels across different temperatures during the training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to optimize reward while preventing the policy from diverging significantly from a baseline policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially larger KL divergence may not be advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL tradeoff frontier across several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. We perform multiple training iterations per algorithm, varying the hyperparameters that control policy conservativeness (target KL values $\in \{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood training, along with different random seeds for preferred-FT). This procedure results in a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a set of test prompts by calculating the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL divergence\footnote{Defined as the sum of the KL divergences at each timestep,} relative to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi \mid\mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO attains the most favorable frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining a low KL divergence. This outcome is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, with its reward/KL tradeoff strictly surpassing that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO is provided access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes models for tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics such as ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that applying PPO fine-tuning based on human preferences leads to improved summary quality. Our evaluation involves generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate compared to reference completions. Completions for all approaches are produced across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models fine-tuned by DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results indicate that DPO achieves roughly a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s top performance of about 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO surpasses the highest win rate observed for the best $N$ baseline method. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO, suggesting the current outcomes might underestimate its true capability. Furthermore, DPO shows greater stability across different sampling temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance tends to decline to that of the original GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT yields little improvement over the baseline SFT. A direct human comparison between DPO and PPO is reported in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO completions sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time against PPO completions sampled at temperature 0.

On the task of single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which involves a single step of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, the preferred completions from the test set serve as the reference to calculate the win rate across methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure they lie within the model’s distribution, and subsequently fine-tune with DPO. Our comparison also includes the top result among 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. We observe that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each method. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify a prompt or temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B. Given our TL;DR findings and the fact that both methods optimize the identical reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. Overall, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient approach that enhances preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering performance comparable or superior to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Corroborating GPT-4 Evaluations with Human Assessments}

We conduct three sets of comparisons involving the highest-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing one (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temperature 0.25), thereby encompassing a range of summary qualities. \section{Discussion}  
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward training method that leverages preferences to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL framework to apply existing RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and preserving generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or surpassing current RLHF methods, including those based on PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several significant questions to be addressed in upcoming studies. In particular, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data when compared to methods that rely on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities comparable to those of PPO-based approaches; however, more thorough investigation is required. For instance, could leveraging self-labeling techniques derived from the DPO policy enable efficient utilization of unlabeled prompts? Another avenue to explore is the phenomenon of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the minor performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this effect. Furthermore, although our experiments include models with up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to considerably larger, cutting-edge models represents an intriguing opportunity for future work. Concerning evaluation procedures, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by the choice of prompt; thus, future research might focus on identifying optimal strategies to obtain reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO holds promise for a variety of applications beyond fine-tuning language models based on human preferences, such as training generative models across different modalities.
