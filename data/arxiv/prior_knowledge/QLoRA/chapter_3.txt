Introduction

Numerical representation lies at the foundation of any computation in machine learning. The choice of numeric format determines how real numbers are encoded in bits, how wide the dynamic range is, how many distinct values can be represented, and how arithmetic operations behave in hardware. This chapter explains the most important numeric formats used in modern ML — standard IEEE floating point types, reduced-precision floats (including FP16, BF16, and FP8 variants), integer and fixed-point schemes used in quantized inference, and how hardware supports these formats through specialized units such as tensor cores and mixed-precision pipelines. Along the way we cover the internal layout of floating-point numbers, the tradeoffs between dynamic range and precision, the primary sources of numerical error, and common rounding modes. The goal is to give you an intuition for why particular formats are chosen in different parts of training and inference, and how to read and reason about research that proposes new numeric formats or quantization schemes.

# IEEE 754 FP32 and FP64 basics (sign, exponent, mantissa)

IEEE 754 is the dominant standard for floating-point arithmetic. It defines formats that encode a real number approximately with a finite number of bits divided into three fields: a sign bit, an exponent field, and a significand (commonly called mantissa) field. Two frequently used IEEE formats are single precision (commonly called FP32) and double precision (FP64).

In both FP32 and FP64 the decoded real value is given by a formula that depends on whether the stored exponent is all zeros, all ones, or something in between. For a normal (non-subnormal, non-special) number the value is

$(-1)^s \times 2^{e - \text{bias}} \times (1.f)$,

where $s$ is the sign bit, $e$ is the unsigned integer value stored in the exponent field, $\text{bias}$ is a constant bias that depends on the format, and $1.f$ denotes the implied leading one followed by the fractional significand bits. The implied leading one is what gives normalized floating point its efficient use of bits.

Concretely:

- FP32 uses 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 fraction bits. The exponent bias is $\text{bias}=127$. The representable exponent range for normalized numbers is from $e-\text{bias} \in [-126, +127]$. Because of the 23 fraction bits, the significand precision is 24 bits (including the implicit 1).

- FP64 uses 1 sign bit, 11 exponent bits, and 52 fraction bits. The bias is $\text{bias}=1023$. The normalized exponent range is $e-\text{bias} \in [-1022, +1023]$ and the significand precision is 53 bits.

There are special cases determined by the exponent pattern:

- If the exponent field is neither all zeros nor all ones, the number is a normalized floating-point number as above.

- If the exponent field is all zeros and the fraction field is zero, the value is signed zero: either $+0$ or $-0$ depending on the sign bit. IEEE distinguishes the two zeroes but they compare equal.

- If the exponent field is all zeros and the fraction field is nonzero, the number is subnormal (denormal). Subnormals represent values very close to zero with reduced precision, and their value is

$(-1)^s \times 2^{1-\text{bias}} \times (0.f)$,

i.e., there is no implicit leading one; instead the leading bit is zero and the effective exponent is fixed at $1-\text{bias}$. Subnormals allow a graceful underflow to zero and extend the dynamic range near zero.

- If the exponent field is all ones and the fraction field is zero, the value is infinity with the appropriate sign, written $\pm\infty$.

- If the exponent field is all ones and the fraction field is nonzero, the value is a NaN (Not a Number), which signals invalid or indeterminate results (for example, $0/0$ yields NaN). There are signaling and quiet NaNs; hardware behavior differs for them.

Two important characteristics define floating formats: precision and dynamic range. Precision is the number of significant bits in the significand (24 bits for FP32, 53 bits for FP64) and determines relative spacing of representable values near a given magnitude. Dynamic range is the ratio between the largest and smallest finite representable magnitudes; it is determined primarily by the exponent width. FP64 trades off storage and bandwidth for much greater precision and a wider dynamic range compared to FP32. In ML, FP32 has historically been the workhorse because it offers sufficient precision for many workloads while keeping memory and compute costs manageable; FP64 is used where much more accurate numeric results are essential (e.g., some scientific computing domains).

# FP16, BF16: differences, dynamic range, and precision

As ML workloads grew larger, researchers and engineers sought ways to reduce memory footprints and increase throughput by reducing numeric precision. Two common reduced-precision floating formats are IEEE half precision (FP16) and the brain-floating variant BF16 (bfloat16). Both use 16 bits per number, but they allocate those bits differently between exponent and significand, which results in different tradeoffs between dynamic range and precision.

IEEE FP16 has 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, and 10 fraction bits. The exponent bias is $\text{bias}=15$. FP16 thus has a significand precision of 11 bits (including the implicit 1) and a normalized exponent range of approximately $[-14, +15]$. This yields limited dynamic range and relatively coarse relative precision. The small significand means that numbers spaced apart by powers of two have comparatively large gaps; for example, when representing values in the range $1$ to $2$ the spacing is $2^{-10}\approx 9.77\times10^{-4}$ per representable increment, whereas FP32 spacing in that same interval is $2^{-23}\approx1.19\times10^{-7}$.

BF16 (bfloat16) is not an IEEE standard format but has become widely used in ML due to its dynamics-friendly design. BF16 uses 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 7 fraction bits. The exponent width and bias are the same as FP32 (bias $=127$ for the 8-bit exponent), but the significand carries only 8 bits of precision (including the implicit 1), i.e., 7 explicit fraction bits. Thus, BF16 has almost the same dynamic range as FP32 but only about one-quarter of its precision. Because BF16 keeps the FP32 exponent width, it can represent very large and very small values similarly to FP32, reducing overflow/underflow issues common with FP16. At the same time, BF16’s reduced mantissa still allows significant memory and bandwidth savings and faster arithmetic in hardware that supports it.

Which format to choose depends on the numeric needs of the algorithm. FP16 provides more precision than extremely low-bit formats and occupies less storage than FP32, but its smaller exponent can cause overflow (e.g., large gradients) or underflow. BF16 provides the large exponent range necessary to carry through the training of deep networks (where activations and gradients can span wide magnitudes) while still halving storage compared to FP32. That is why many ML frameworks use BF16 for accumulating and storing values while relying on accumulators or master-copy weights in FP32 to preserve precision during update steps.

# Reduced formats (FP8 variants) and custom low-bit floats

Research and hardware development have pushed further into very low-bit floating formats such as 8-bit floats (FP8) to further reduce memory and increase arithmetic throughput. Several FP8 variants have been proposed, typically differing in how they allocate bits between exponent and mantissa. Common variants include e4m3 (4-bit exponent, 3-bit significand fraction), e5m2 (5-bit exponent, 2-bit fraction), and even other custom splits. These tiny formats trade off precision and dynamic range in different ways.

For an FP8 e4m3 example, with 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, and 3 fraction bits, the exponent bias might be $\text{bias}=2^{(4-1)}-1=7$ (as in typical bias calculation), yielding a moderate dynamic range with only 4 bits of exponent and just 4 bits of effective significand precision (including implicit 1). The e5m2 variant (1 sign, 5 exponent, 2 fraction) has a wider dynamic range due to the extra exponent bit but even coarser significand precision. One consequence is that e5m2 will better represent very large or very small magnitudes without saturating to infinity or zero, while e4m3 provides slightly higher precision around moderate magnitudes.

At 8 bits, subnormals, rounding behavior, and representation of special values (Inf, NaN) still matter. However, the extremely coarse spacing between representable values makes FP8 sensitive to rounding and quantization error. For many neural network tensors, particularly activations and weights that cluster around zero, FP8 can be practical if combined with techniques such as per-tensor scaling, mixed-precision accumulation (using wider formats for sums), and learned quantization scales. Some hardware vendors provide native FP8 operations (for example, recent NVIDIA architectures introduced support for certain FP8 formats), but because FP8 is not standardized, different vendors or frameworks may use different bit allocations and bias conventions.

Beyond FP8 there are even more exotic custom low-bit floats designed for specific applications, such as posit numbers or tapered floating-point formats, which change how significand and exponent interplay or use variable-length fields. Posits, for example, aim to provide a dynamic allocation between exponent and significand bits based on value magnitude, potentially achieving better accuracy for many distributions at the same bit width. However, nonstandard formats require bespoke hardware support or software emulation and complicate portability and ecosystem tooling.

In practice, when one considers FP8 or other low-bit floats for ML, there are several mitigation strategies to maintain acceptable numeric behavior: use of higher-precision accumulators (e.g., accumulate dot products in FP32), per-layer or per-channel scaling factors to map dynamic ranges into representable ranges of the low-bit format, and calibration procedures or learned scales to reduce quantization error. The choice of exponent/mantissa split is a design decision balancing the need to represent very large/small magnitudes (favoring more exponent bits) against the need to represent small relative differences near typical tensor magnitudes (favoring more mantissa bits).

# Integers and fixed-point representations (Int8, Int4)

Integer and fixed-point numeric representations form the backbone of many quantized inference pipelines where latency and energy efficiency are paramount. Integer formats such as signed 8-bit (Int8) or even 4-bit integers store values as uniform discrete levels. The key to using integers to approximate real-valued tensors is a mapping (scale and optionally zero-point) between the integer code values and real numbers.

A simple affine quantization maps a real value $x$ to an integer code $q$ via

$q = \text{round}\left(\frac{x}{s}\right) + z,$

where $s$ is a scale factor and $z$ is a zero-point. For symmetric quantization often used in ML inference, $z=0$ and the mapping is simply $x \approx s \cdot q$. For asymmetric quantization, $z$ shifts the integer range so that asymmetrically distributed data around a nonzero center can be represented more accurately. The scale $s$ is typically a positive real chosen so that the dynamic range of the real data best matches the integer representable range. For example, for int8 signed codes in $[-128, 127]$ with symmetric quantization, one might choose $s = \max(|x|)/127$ to map the observed maximum magnitude to the maximum representable code.

Fixed-point representations are essentially integers interpreted with an implicit fractional position. A Q-format such as Qm.f denotes a signed fixed-point with m integer bits (including sign) and f fractional bits. The real value represented is $x = q \times 2^{-f}$ (for signed integer $q$ stored in two’s complement). Fixed-point arithmetic is closely related to integer arithmetic but with an implicit scale power-of-two which can simplify hardware implementation since scaling corresponds to bit shifts.

Int8 quantization has become widespread for inference because many neural network computations (notably convolutions and matrix multiplies) are amenable to lower precision if weights and activations are carefully quantized, often with small accuracy loss. Several practical techniques improve quantized int8 performance: per-channel weight scales (each output channel gets its own scale for better matching weight distributions), calibration on representative data to choose scales, clipping to reduce outlier impact, and using higher-precision accumulators (e.g., 32-bit integer accumulators for dot-product sums) to avoid overflow and preserve precision in intermediate sums. After accumulation, results are requantized back to the target integer range, which involves scaling and rounding.

Quantizing to very low bit-widths like Int4 or even binary/ternary representations requires more aggressive techniques. Learned quantization parameters, per-layer/per-channel scaling, fine-tuning the network under quantization-aware training, and careful handling of biases and batch normalization parameters are typical. Int4 can give dramatic memory and compute improvements but often requires retraining or at least calibration and occasionally architecture changes to maintain accuracy.

One important distinction between integer/fixed-point schemes and floating point is that integers have uniform spacing between representable values across the entire representable range (after mapping by scale), while floating-point spacing is relative: spacing grows with magnitude. This is why floating point is often more robust to wide dynamic ranges without manual scaling, whereas integer quantization typically needs scale factors or dynamic range clipping.

# Rounding modes and numerical error sources

All finite formats must round real arithmetic results to the nearest representable value. The IEEE standard specifies several rounding modes and behavior to make floating-point arithmetic reproducible and understandable. The most common rounding mode in ML and general computing is round-to-nearest-even (often shortened to “round-to-nearest”), where ties (exact halfway cases) are broken by choosing the value whose least significant bit is even. This reduces systematic bias in rounding. Other supported modes include round toward zero (truncate), round toward +infinity, and round toward −infinity. Some systems also implement stochastic rounding, which probabilistically rounds up or down based on proximity to representable values; stochastic rounding can be helpful in low-precision training because it prevents small accumulated biases and can preserve small gradient signals that would otherwise be rounded to zero.

Numerical errors arise from several sources. Rounding error is ubiquitous: each arithmetic operation (addition, multiplication, etc.) produces a result that is rounded to the nearest representable value under the chosen mode, introducing a small error. Over many operations, these small errors can accumulate. Cancellation is a particularly pernicious form of error: when subtracting two nearly equal numbers, significant leading digits cancel and the result loses precision relative to each operand. This can amplify relative error and be catastrophic in iterative algorithms. For example, computing $f(x) = 1 - \cos(x)$ for small $x$ in limited precision can be numerically unstable if implemented naively. Algorithmic rearrangement or higher precision can mitigate this.

Underflow and overflow are other important errors. Underflow occurs when a result is too small in magnitude to be represented as a normalized number and might be flushed to zero or represented as a subnormal, with attendant loss of precision. Overflow occurs when a result exceeds the largest representable finite value and becomes $\pm\infty$. Both situations can break computations, though BF16’s larger exponent reduces overflow risk compared to FP16.

Quantization error is the difference between a real value and its quantized representation (e.g., mapping to Int8). This error depends on the chosen scale and zero-point and can bias downstream calculations if not handled carefully. Clipping helps limit the impact of extreme outliers but trades bias for reduced variance. Additionally, operator fusion and different operation orderings (for example, summing in different associativity) produce different rounding paths and thus slightly different results. Mixed-precision approaches (e.g., multiply in BF16 but accumulate in FP32) intentionally use higher precision for accumulation to reduce rounding error in sums of many products.

In practice, you must be aware of the typical sizes of errors and design algorithms that tolerate them or compensate via higher-precision accumulators, selective use of FP32 copies for critical quantities (like master weights during training), careful initialization to avoid catastrophic cancellation, and numerical stabilization techniques (for example, Kahan summation for dot products, log-sum-exp for stable softmax computations). For low-precision training, stochastic rounding or loss-scaling (multiplying small gradients before quantization then dividing back after accumulation) are useful tricks to preserve small gradient signals.

# Hardware support: tensor cores, mixed precision compute, and supported types

Hardware capabilities strongly influence which numeric formats are practical. Modern GPU and accelerator architectures include specialized units — commonly called tensor cores, matrix cores, or systolic arrays — that accelerate dense linear algebra and convolutional operations using mixed-precision arithmetic. These units often accept inputs in a low-precision format but perform internal accumulation in a higher-precision accumulator to maintain numeric stability.

For example, many NVIDIA GPUs that introduced tensor cores supported FP16 input with FP32 accumulation: the hardware multiplies FP16 operands and accumulates the product into a FP32 accumulator. This mixed-precision behavior allows considerable speedup and memory reduction by using 16-bit storage while avoiding severe loss of accuracy in long sums. Later hardware generations added native BF16 support and on some platforms support for FP8 variants. These accelerators typically provide programming primitives (e.g., CUDA WMMA or vendor libraries) that implement mixed-precision matrix multiply-accumulate efficiently.

TPUs and other AI accelerators often expose similar mixed-precision features. For instance, Google’s TPUs provide bfloat16 compute and use 32-bit accumulators for matrix multiplications. Dedicated inference chips from other vendors may offer extremely low-precision integer arithmetic (Int8, Int4) and specialized convolution engines that operate directly on packed low-bit representations efficiently.

When designing systems, it is important to understand three key hardware-related aspects:

1. Supported input/output formats: which precisions are native to the arithmetic units (e.g., FP16, BF16, FP8, Int8). Some hardware provides native support for storage and compute in several formats; other hardware only supports load/store in certain sizes and emulates others.

2. Accumulator precision and reduction behavior: whether the hardware accumulates in FP32 or lower precision, and whether accumulation is exact or rounded at intermediate steps. Accumulators that use higher precision preserve numerical quality when summing many products, which is crucial for deep learning’s large matrix multiplies.

3. Throughput and memory bandwidth tradeoffs: lower-bit formats generally enable higher arithmetic throughput and reduce memory traffic, but you must consider conversion costs, the need for scaling or packing/unpacking, and potential loss of accuracy that could force algorithmic workarounds (e.g., more iterations). Often the bottleneck shifts from compute to memory bandwidth as precision decreases because more values fit in caches, changing performance tradeoffs.

Software frameworks and libraries exploit hardware features to provide mixed-precision training recipes. For example, automatic mixed precision (AMP) in many frameworks allows using FP16/BF16 for forward/backward passes while keeping a master copy of weights in FP32 for parameter updates. Tensor cores typically require that tensors be laid out in certain ways (e.g., particular tile sizes) to achieve peak performance; hence kernel fusion and data layout optimizations in deep learning libraries are matched to hardware characteristics.

Finally, because some of the very low-bit formats (e.g., some FP8 variants) are not standardized, software and hardware vendors must agree on bit allocations and semantics. When using novel formats proposed in research, portability and reproducibility depend on whether hardware and compiler support that format the same way (same bias, handling of subnormals, NaN payloads, etc.).

Conclusion

Understanding numeric representations and floating-point formats is critical to making informed choices in ML system design. IEEE FP32 and FP64 establish the baseline with clear tradeoffs between precision and dynamic range. Reduced formats like FP16 and BF16 enable faster, more memory-efficient computation but shift the design burden to handling lower precision or reduced exponent range. Even more aggressive reductions to FP8 or integer/fixed-point quantization offer dramatic efficiency but require careful scaling, higher-precision accumulation, and sometimes retraining to preserve accuracy. Rounding modes and numerical errors — rounding, cancellation, overflow/underflow, and quantization bias — must be addressed through algorithmic stabilization, mixed-precision strategies, and hardware features such as tensor cores with higher-precision accumulators. Armed with this vocabulary and intuition, you will be able to read papers proposing new numeric formats, evaluate proposed tradeoffs, and understand the practical implications of deploying different numeric representations on modern hardware.
