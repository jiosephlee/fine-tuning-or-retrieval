Introduction

Understanding the statistical properties of weights and activations in pretrained neural networks is foundational for many practical decisions: how to initialize and fine-tune models, how to design efficient quantizers, how to choose scales and zero-points for integer inference, and how to detect and handle pathological cases that degrade performance. This chapter surveys the empirical shapes of weight and activation distributions observed in modern networks, examines the common (and sometimes fragile) assumption of approximate normality, describes practical methods for testing and characterizing distributions, explains the roles of mean-centering and standard deviation when working with weight tensors, and analyzes the presence and consequences of outliers and heavy tails in activations. We also explain why per-block (or per-channel) statistics and small block sizes are often used in practice, and we synthesize these statistical insights into concrete implications for quantizer design, clipping strategies, and scaling choices.

# Empirical distribution shapes of weights in pretrained networks

When you inspect the raw floating-point values of weights in modern pretrained networks (convolutional layers, linear layers, embedding tables, etc.), a few consistent patterns emerge across architectures, tasks, and training recipes. Most weight tensors are clustered around zero: a large fraction of elements have small absolute value. The histogram normally shows a central peak near zero and a decay as values move away from zero. However, the precise shape of that decay varies across networks and layers.

For many layers, especially those that are not heavily regularized or sparsified, the bulk of the weight distribution resembles a bell-shaped curve that is roughly symmetric about zero. This is particularly true for weights after training with common regularization and weight decay. In those cases, the central mass is often well approximated by a Gaussian (normal) distribution, although departures in the tails, skewness, and kurtosis are common.

Nevertheless, many weight tensors show heavier tails than a Gaussian would predict: while the central region fits a normal-like shape, there are more extreme values (outliers) than would be expected under a strict normal model. These heavy tails can result from optimization dynamics (some weights move further to fit rare patterns), from layer-specific constraints (e.g., final fully connected layers that adapt to target class imbalances), or from architectural elements such as residual connections that propagate gradient signals differently. In some architectures, attention matrices and embedding tables show particularly pronounced heavy tails and asymmetries.

Different layer types also have distinct empirical shapes. Convolutional kernel weights, which are often small and numerous, tend to be concentrated and approximately symmetric. Fully connected layers or embeddings that compress or expand representational spaces can show broader variance and stronger tails. Batch-normalization or weight-standardization layers, if present and not fused, can enforce tighter distributions. Finally, weights that are the result of quantization-aware training can be bunched near quantization gridpoints, showing a multimodal histogram that reflects the discrete levels used during training.

In summary, while many weight distributions have a roughly bell-shaped core, it is incorrect to assume universal Gaussianity across all layers; heavy tails, skewness, and occasionally multimodality commonly occur and must be accounted for in downstream processing such as quantization.

# Normality assumptions and methods to test for normality

Why consider normality? The normal distribution is analytically convenient: it is fully described by mean and variance, has well-known tail behavior, and many theoretical analyses (e.g., central limit arguments, propagation of variance through linear operations) rely on or approximate normality. However, treating weights or activations as exactly normal is an approximation. It can be a useful first-order model for reasoning and for designing simple quantizers, but it risks mischaracterizing tail mass and outliers. Therefore, when a pipeline assumes normality (for scale estimation, statistical clipping, or variance propagation), it is essential to test how well the assumption holds in practice.

Several statistical tests and diagnostic tools are commonly used to assess normality. Each has strengths and limitations, and practical use often combines graphical diagnostics with formal tests.

One simple diagnostic is the Q–Q (quantile–quantile) plot against the normal distribution: you sort the data and plot their empirical quantiles against the expected quantiles of a standard normal. If the points lie along a straight line, normality is plausible; systematic curvature indicates skewness or kurtosis differences; deviations at the ends indicate tail mismatches. Q–Q plots are especially valuable because they reveal where the mismatch occurs (center vs tails).

Formal goodness-of-fit tests include the Shapiro–Wilk test, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test, the Anderson–Darling test, and the D’Agostino K-squared test. The Shapiro–Wilk test is powerful for small- to medium-sized samples but can be computationally expensive on very large tensors and tends to reject normality for even small deviations when the sample size is large. The K–S test compares the empirical distribution function to a specified reference (normal) and is sensitive to differences anywhere in the distribution, but it requires specifying parameters or using the Lilliefors variant to account for estimated mean and variance. The Anderson–Darling test gives extra weight to the tails, making it a good choice when tail behavior is of particular concern. D’Agostino’s test evaluates skewness and kurtosis jointly.

In practice, sample size considerations are crucial. Modern weight tensors have millions of elements, which means formal tests can almost always reject strict normality because even tiny deviations become statistically significant. Thus, practitioners should focus on effect size and practical relevance: examine Q–Q plots, compute skewness and kurtosis statistics, and measure tail probabilities beyond thresholds relevant to the application (for example, fraction of values beyond 3 or 4 standard deviations). Robust measures like median absolute deviation (MAD) help characterize spread without being dominated by outliers.

Compute the standardized z-scores by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation:
z = (x - μ) / σ.
Under perfect normality, the probability of |z| > 3 is about 0.0027 and of |z| > 4 is about 6.3e-5. If you observe much larger empirical tail probabilities for these thresholds, the distribution has heavier tails than the Gaussian.

Finally, testing should be layer- and block-specific. Aggregating across many heterogeneous parameters can mask layerwise departures; conversely, very small sample sizes (e.g., thin layers) will have noisy test statistics. A pragmatic workflow is to compute Q–Q plots, skewness, kurtosis, and tail-mass metrics per-layer or per-block, and to flag layers where tail mass or skewness exceed application thresholds.

# Scale (standard deviation) and mean centering of weight tensors

Mean-centering and scale normalization are simple but powerful preprocessing steps for weights, with important implications for quantization and for the numerical stability of inference.

Mean-centering removes any non-zero average in a weight tensor. Many neural network weight tensors are approximately zero-mean because training (with or without batch normalization) often yields symmetric updates; however, some layers—especially those near an output with bias-related terms—can have non-negligible mean. Removing the mean (i.e., subtracting μ from every weight element) can reduce quantization bias, especially for symmetric quantizers that assume distributions centered at zero. If a quantizer uses a symmetric range like [−S, S], a non-zero mean induces an effective offset that reduces the usable dynamic range for symmetric values and may require an asymmetric zero-point to avoid systematic bias.

Scale normalization uses the standard deviation (or other spread measures) to express weights in unitless form. The standard deviation is
σ = sqrt( (1/N) Σ_i (x_i - μ)^2 ).
Using the stdev to form z-scores, z_i = (x_i - μ) / σ, allows comparing distributions across layers and designing layer-agnostic quantizers: a single clipping threshold in units of σ corresponds to different absolute values in different layers. Per-layer scale factors are used extensively in quantization: the scale determines how floating values map to integer levels. Proper estimation of σ is therefore critical. If the scale is underestimated, many values saturate the quantizer; if overestimated, quantization noise increases because integer steps correspond to coarser resolution relative to actual value distribution.

There are several practical choices for measuring scale. Population vs sample variance distinctions matter less for very large tensors, but robust statistics can be preferable when outliers exist. The median absolute deviation (MAD) is defined as MAD = median(|x_i - median(x)|). For a Gaussian distribution, MAD ≈ 0.6745 σ. Using MAD or trimmed standard deviations (ignoring extreme tails) produces scales that are less sensitive to outliers, which can reduce quantization saturation at the expense of slightly higher noise for the central mass.

Mean-centering interacts with bias terms in layers. For a linear layer y = Wx + b, subtracting μ from W and adding μ·x (or absorbing into b if x is constant mean) changes computation. In practice, mean-centering is most straightforward when applied with care to weight matrices whose input activations are zero-mean (for example, when preceding batch-normalization ensures zero-mean activations); otherwise, shifting weights affects output bias. Many deployment flows prefer to fold mean offsets into bias terms or to implement asymmetric quantization that directly handles nonzero means.

Finally, scale estimation should be computed using representative data for activations, but for weights itself you compute μ and σ from the trained tensor. For quantization, per-channel scaling (per-output-channel in convolutions and linear layers) often outperforms a single per-tensor scale because channels can have markedly different variances. That leads directly into the next sections about per-block statistics.

# Outliers and heavy tails in activation distributions

Activations often display more complex distributional behavior than weights. Activations are shaped by input statistics, nonlinearities, normalization layers, and the architecture’s internal computations. A universally observed trait is that activations can be highly non-Gaussian with heavy tails and significant sparsity (many near-zero values with occasional large values). This is especially true after ReLU or similar half-wave rectifiers: the distribution becomes highly asymmetric with a mass at or near zero and a long positive tail.

Heavy tails mean there are more extreme values than a Gaussian model would predict. For example, a small fraction of activations might be 10× or 100× larger than the layer’s typical scale. These outliers can be caused by several mechanisms: local feature detectors that fire strongly on specific inputs, residual additions that accumulate non-Gaussian contributions, attention mechanisms that produce sharp selectivity, and rare input events that trigger large responses.

Outliers and heavy tails matter for quantization because quantizers must represent the dynamic range. A scale chosen to accommodate the maximum activation value will make the quantization step size large relative to the central mass, increasing quantization noise for the majority of activations. Conversely, choosing a smaller scale that fits the bulk of values but clips extreme activations can reduce average quantization error at the cost of saturating a few large activations, which may or may not hurt performance.

Characterizing heavy tails is done via tail-probability metrics: compute P(|x| > τ) for a range of thresholds τ expressed in multiples of σ, or examine the empirical complementary cumulative distribution function (CCDF) on a log scale. Estimates of kurtosis (fourth standardized moment),
kurtosis = E[(x - μ)^4] / σ^4,
provide a single-number indication of tail weight relative to Gaussian (which has kurtosis 3). Excess kurtosis = kurtosis − 3. However, kurtosis is sensitive to extreme outliers and can be noisy for small samples, so it should be used in conjunction with direct tail probability measures.

For activations after ReLU, skewness and the mass at zero are important statistics. Skewness,
skewness = E[(x - μ)^3] / σ^3,
quantifies asymmetry; large positive skew indicates long right tails. For distributions with a discrete spike at zero, measures of sparsity (fraction of near-zero elements) are informative.

In practice, designers use clipping (saturation) strategies to trade off between quantization resolution for the bulk versus preserving outliers. Adaptive clipping methods estimate a clipping threshold such as a percentile (e.g., the 99.9th percentile) or choose a threshold in units of σ derived from a normality assumption. Other approaches apply nonlinear quantizers or logarithmic transforms for activations with multiplicative-like behavior. The crucial point is to quantify how much mass you clip and to validate the end-to-end impact on model performance: a small clipped tail mass (e.g., 0.01%) might still be critical if it corresponds to rare but high-value signals.

# Per-block statistics and why small block sizes are used

“Per-block” statistics refers to computing μ and σ (or other moments) not over an entire tensor but over subgroups such as per-output-channel, per-input-channel, per-layer-block, or small fixed-size blocks of contiguous entries. Per-block normalization and per-block scaling are common because distributions vary significantly within a tensor: different channels often have different variances and means, and aggregating them into a single global statistic leads to inefficient use of dynamic range and degraded quantization performance.

There are multiple reasons to prefer small block sizes for statistics and quantization:

1) Heterogeneity across channels: In convolutional and linear layers, each output channel corresponds to a distinct filter or neuron with its own functional role. Some channels will respond strongly and have larger weight magnitudes or activation variances; others will be weaker. Using a single scale for the whole tensor forces a compromise: either make the scale large enough to accommodate strong channels (losing resolution on weak ones) or make it small and saturate strong channels. Per-channel or per-block scales allow each group to use its own dynamic range, improving effective precision.

2) Reduced quantization error: In a linear quantizer, quantization error scales with the square of the step size. By tailoring scale to smaller groups you reduce the step size for groups with smaller variance, directly reducing mean squared quantization error.

3) Computational tractability and hardware alignment: There is a tradeoff between the granularity of statistics and hardware complexity. Per-scalar quantization would be ideal but impractical. Per-channel is a common sweet spot: it requires one scale and (possibly) zero-point per channel and maps well onto SIMD and tensor-core execution where channels are processed together. When per-channel is still too coarse (e.g., for FPGA or extremely low-bit regimes), small blocks (e.g., groups of 8 or 16 elements) may be used that match vector widths.

4) Training and calibration considerations: When calibrating quantizers with representative data, smaller blocks allow the calibration to capture local activation statistics, which frequently improves accuracy for post-training quantization. Small blocks are also more robust to distribution shift across inputs: a block focused on a specific feature behaves more consistently over the dataset than an aggregated tensor-level statistic that mixes multiple behaviors.

However, small block sizes come with costs. They increase the number of scale parameters to store and can complicate implementation. They also increase sensitivity to estimation noise: for very small blocks, estimates of σ and μ become noisier, which can lead to suboptimal scale choices. A practical compromise is per-output-channel statistics (typical for convolutional and linear layers) or small fixed-size blocks that align with hardware lanes. When estimating per-block statistics, use sufficient calibration data or robust estimators (e.g., clipped variance) to reduce estimation variance.

# Implications of distributional properties for quantizer design

The preceding sections converge on a central application: quantizer design. Quantization maps floating-point values x to a finite set of integer levels. The mapping is governed by a scale factor s and possibly a zero-point z (for affine quantization), so that an integer q corresponds to a real value approximated by s(q - z). Good quantizer design requires matching these parameters to the empirical distribution of the values being quantized.

Symmetric vs asymmetric quantizers. If a distribution is approximately symmetric and zero-centered, symmetric quantizers (z = 0) are simple and efficient since positive and negative ranges are treated equally. For asymmetric distributions and non-zero means, asymmetric quantizers with a learned zero-point reduce bias; however, they require storing and computing an offset which might be slightly more expensive on some hardware. For activations with ReLU and therefore one-sided mass, asymmetric quantization with a zero-point near zero is often appropriate.

Choice of range (clipping). The ideal finite quantizer respects the support of the data. If distributions have heavy tails, using the maximum observed value to set scale leads to large step size and higher quantization noise for the majority of values. A standard engineering tradeoff is to set a clipping threshold T and map all values outside [−T, T] (or [0, T] for nonnegative activations) to the nearest boundary. Clipping trades off saturation error (from clipped extremes) against quantization resolution for the central mass. Methods to choose T include fixed multiples of σ (T = kσ), percentile-based thresholds (e.g., the 99.99th percentile), or optimization-based approaches that minimize a distortion metric such as mean squared error between the original and quantized distributions. Percentile clipping is commonly used in post-training quantization since it does not assume normality and can be estimated robustly from calibration data.

Bit allocation and non-uniform quantization. For heavy-tailed activation distributions, non-uniform quantizers (e.g., log-scale quantizers, Lloyd–Max quantizers, or learned quantization grids) can be more efficient by allocating more representative levels to frequently occurring central values and fewer to extreme tails. Non-uniform quantization typically complicates hardware implementation; nevertheless, certain designs use piecewise-linear or logarithmic encodings to approximate non-uniform spacing while remaining implementable.

Scale estimation: robust vs empirical. As discussed, estimating σ or an equivalent scale parameter can be done with the sample standard deviation, MAD, or trimmed variance. For quantizers that will be used across data shifts, robust measures (MAD or percentile-based) help prevent catastrophic changes in scale due to outliers in the calibration data. In quantization-aware training, the network can adapt weights to the quantizer, making the trained distributions more quantization-friendly (e.g., fewer outliers, rounded values).

Per-channel/proportional quantization. Given channelwise heterogeneity, it is common to maintain a separate scale for each output channel (or per-row in linear layers). This reduces quantization error but requires multiplication of integer outputs by floating-point scales in the next layer or use of scaled-integer math. For integer-only inference, scaling factors are typically quantized into fixed-point multipliers and shifts.

Zero-shot vs calibration-based methods. Post-training quantization often uses a small calibration dataset to estimate activation ranges and hence scales. Calibration methods that use distributional characteristics (percentiles, KL divergence minimization between floating and quantized activation histograms, or MSE minimization) typically outperform naive min-max scaling. Quantization-aware training allows backpropagating through quantization (via straight-through estimators or soft quantizers) and produces weights and activations that are easier to quantize; however, it requires retraining and may not be available for very large models.

Handling outliers specifically. There are targeted techniques for handling outliers in activations, because they are often more detrimental than outliers in weights. One approach is outlier-aware quantization: separate the largest-magnitude values and store them at higher precision while the rest are stored at low precision. Concretely, you can partition activation tensors into a main quantized part and a sparse set of residual high-precision corrections; at inference, corrections are applied only where needed. This incurs a small control overhead but can preserve important rare signals. Another technique is gradual clipping during fine-tuning to encourage the model to reduce reliance on large-magnitude activations.

Design heuristics summarized. From the distributional observations, several practical heuristics emerge: use per-channel (or small-block) scales to handle heterogeneity; test for skewness and use asymmetric quantizers where appropriate; employ percentile or distortion-minimizing clipping thresholds rather than absolute maxima; prefer robust scale estimators (MAD, trimmed σ) when outliers are present; and consider non-uniform or mixed-precision strategies when tails are heavy or critical. Above all, measure the end-to-end accuracy impact and data-dependence: small fractions of clipped mass can sometimes have disproportionately large accuracy effects if they correspond to critical features.

Conclusion

Statistical characterization of weights and activations is more than an academic exercise: it directly informs quantizer choice, scale computation, clipping rules, and calibration strategies that determine the practical trade-offs between model size, speed, and accuracy. While approximate normality holds for many weight cores and can simplify analysis, activations are frequently asymmetric and heavy-tailed, requiring robust and often per-block approaches. Small-block or per-channel statistics strike a practical balance between capturing heterogeneity and keeping implementation manageable. Finally, tools such as Q–Q plots, kurtosis and skewness metrics, percentile tail-mass measurements, and robust scale estimators should be part of any quantization or deployment pipeline to ensure that theoretical assumptions align with the empirical reality of trained models.
