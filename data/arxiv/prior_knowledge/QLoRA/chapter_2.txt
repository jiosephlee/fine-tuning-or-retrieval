Introduction

Deep neural networks are built from elementary operations on vectors and matrices. Understanding how these operations are computed efficiently and how gradients flow through them is essential for both implementing models and reading research papers in machine learning. This chapter develops the linear algebra and automatic differentiation (autodiff) ideas that underlie forward and backward passes in deep networks, with special emphasis on the batched matrix multiplications (general matrix–matrix multiply, GEMM) used extensively in large language models (LLMs), on vectorized computation and broadcasting, on the chain rule as it applies to linear layers and attention mechanisms, and on the efficient computation of gradients via Jacobian/vector–Jacobian products.

We start by explaining batched matrix multiplication and how shapes are organized in practice. Then we treat vectorized computation and broadcasting, since these are how modern frameworks express whole-batch operations without explicit loops. Next we derive backpropagation through linear layers using the chain rule and discuss gradient shapes and how gradients propagate through attention and projection layers found in transformer architectures. We then introduce computation graphs, discuss how forward and backward passes are scheduled and how memory for activations is managed. The chapter closes with a focused discussion of Jacobian/vector–Jacobian products (JVPs) and vector–Jacobian products (VJPs), since these are the primitives used to compute gradients efficiently without forming full Jacobian matrices.

# Batched matrix multiplication (GEMM) and shapes

Matrix multiplication is the core linear algebra operation in neural networks. The basic multiply of two matrices is defined for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$, producing $C = AB \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with entries
$$
C_{ij} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{k} A_{i\ell} B_{\ell j}.
$$
This scalar summation is the unit of work. In deep learning, we repeatedly multiply matrices of large size, and modern hardware (GPUs, TPUs) optimize the general matrix–matrix multiply (GEMM) operation.

In practical training and inference we almost always operate on batches: we process multiple examples in parallel. To avoid writing loops over examples, frameworks provide batched matrix multiplication. There are two common patterns:

1) A batch of independent matrices multiplied by a common matrix: Suppose we have a batch size $B$ and for each example a matrix $X^{(b)} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$. We stack them into a tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times m\times k}$ and multiply each by the same $W \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$. The batched product yields $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times m\times n}$, with
$$
Y_{b} = X_{b} W \quad\text{for } b=1,\dots,B.
$$
This is expressed in many libraries as a batched GEMM where the first dimension indexes the batch.

2) Batched pairwise matrix multiplication: We might have two tensors $A \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times m\times k}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times k\times n}$ and want to compute $C \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times m\times n}$ with $C_{b} = A_{b} B_{b}$ for each batch element. This pattern is common in attention, where each head and each sequence position behaves as an independent small matrix product.

Shapes must match carefully: for each multiplication that reduces along an axis of length $k$, the corresponding dimensions must be equal. When dimension orders differ, tensors are transposed or their strides are manipulated so that the contracted axes align. High-performance implementations also consider memory layout and contiguous strides to use BLAS libraries efficiently.

Large models frequently use a matrix multiply of a 2-D activation matrix by a 2-D weight matrix, but in attention and multi-head contexts we often have 4-D tensors: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$ (batch $B$, sequence length $S$, hidden dimension $H$); for multi-head attention split into $n_h$ heads of head dimension $d$, we reshape to $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times n_h\times d}$ and then perform batched multiplications across heads, often resulting in tensors of shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times n_h\times S\times S}$ for attention scores. Recognizing how these reshape and transpose operations map to GEMM calls is crucial for reading performance-focused papers.

# Vectorized computation and broadcasting

Vectorization means expressing operations over arrays as whole-array computations rather than elementwise loops. Instead of iterating over each example to compute $y^{(b)} = f(x^{(b)})$, we compute $Y = f(X)$ where $X$ stacks all $x^{(b)}$. Vectorization improves performance by enabling single kernel launches and better utilization of parallel hardware.

Broadcasting is a rule that allows tensors of different shapes to participate in arithmetic when one of them can be viewed as having singleton dimensions that are implicitly replicated. A canonical example: if $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$, then adding $b$ to $X$ adds $b$ to every position and every batch element by conceptually expanding $b$ to shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$ without allocating extra memory. The rule that frameworks typically follow is: align shapes on the right and for each axis either sizes are equal or one of them is $1$; axes with size $1$ are broadcast (replicated) to match the other tensor.

Broadcasting and vectorization reduce special-case code and enable expressing computations at the same abstraction level regardless of batch size. However, they also change how gradients are computed: when a broadcasted tensor participates in an operation, the gradient with respect to that tensor is computed by summing over the axes that were implicitly replicated during broadcasting. For instance, if $Y = X + b$ with $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^H$ broadcasted across $B$ and $S$, and if we receive $\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}$, then
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{s=1}^{S} \frac{\partial L}{\partial Y_{b,s,:}}.
$$
Understanding which axes were broadcasted is necessary to reduce gradients correctly.

Another important vectorization pattern is using einsum-style notation for generalized contractions. For example, matrix multiplication of batched tensors can be written as
$$
C_{b,i,j} = \sum_{k} A_{b,i,k} B_{b,k,j},
$$
concise and unambiguous. Many frameworks implement einsum and use it to express sophisticated linear algebra succinctly.

# Chain rule and backpropagation through linear layers

Automatic differentiation relies on the chain rule from calculus. For scalar functions, the chain rule states if $y = f(u)$ and $u = g(x)$, then $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{df}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$. For vector- and matrix-valued functions, the rule generalizes to Jacobian matrices.

Consider a linear layer with input vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}$, weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$, bias $b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$, and output $y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$:
$$
y = Wx + b.
$$
Suppose we have a scalar loss $L$ that depends on $y$. During backpropagation we assume we have computed the gradient of the loss with respect to the output, $g_y = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. We must compute $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}$, and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}$.

Because the layer is linear, the Jacobian of $y$ with respect to $x$ is $W$ itself (viewed as an $m\times k$ Jacobian). Using the chain rule,
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = W^\top \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = W^\top g_y,
$$
which yields a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{k}$. Similarly, viewing each element of $W$ as affecting $y$, the gradient with respect to $W$ is
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = g_y \, x^\top,
$$
an outer product that yields an $m\times k$ matrix. For the bias,
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = g_y,
$$
since the derivative of $y$ with respect to $b$ is the identity.

When we have a batch of inputs $X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times k}$ and compute $Y = X W^\top + b$ (some frameworks use $Y = XW^\top$, others use $Y = WX^\top$ depending on convention), the gradients aggregate over the batch. If forward is $Y_{b} = W X_{b} + b$, and $G_Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times m}$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to $Y$, then
$$
G_X = G_Y W \quad\text{(shapes: }B\times m\text{ times }m\times k\text{ gives }B\times k),
$$
and
$$
G_W = G_Y^\top X \quad\text{or equivalently}\quad G_W = \sum_{b=1}^{B} G_{Y,b}^\top X_b,
$$
depending on whether you treat $W$ transposed. If $b$ was broadcasted across the batch, then
$$
G_b = \sum_{b=1}^{B} G_{Y,b}.
$$

Understanding these shape interactions is essential: the gradient with respect to weights is a contraction of the output gradient with the input, while the gradient with respect to inputs is a contraction of the output gradient with the transposed weight.

The chain rule composes naturally: for a sequence of layers $x \mapsto y \mapsto z$, with $z = f(y)$ and $y = g(x)$, we multiply Jacobians appropriately. In practice, computing full Jacobian matrices is rarely done; instead frameworks use JVPs and VJPs (see later) to compute directional products that yield the needed gradients efficiently.

# Gradient shapes and how gradients propagate through attention and projections

Transformer models, which underpin most modern LLMs, rely heavily on attention and projection layers. These layers are built from linear projections and batched matrix multiplications; thus the gradient shapes follow the same principles as for dense linear layers, but with more axes.

Consider a Transformer’s multi-head attention. Starting from an input activation $X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$, we compute queries, keys, and values via linear projections:
$$
Q = X W_Q + b_Q,\quad K = X W_K + b_K,\quad V = X W_V + b_V.
$$
Typically $W_Q, W_K, W_V\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times H}$ or, more commonly, $W_Q\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times n_h d}$ and then reshaped to separate heads where $n_h$ is number of heads and $d$ is head dimension so that $H = n_h d$.

After reshaping, we have $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times n_h\times S\times d}$ and similarly for $K$ and $V$. Attention scores are computed as scaled dot-products between queries and keys:
$$
\text{scores}_{b,h,i,j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \sum_{t=1}^{d} Q_{b,h,i,t} K_{b,h,j,t},
$$
which is a batched GEMM resulting in a tensor of shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times n_h\times S\times S}$. Softmax is applied across the last axis (keys axis) producing attention weights $A$ of the same shape; then
$$
\text{out}_{b,h,i,t} = \sum_{j=1}^{S} A_{b,h,i,j} V_{b,h,j,t},
$$
a batched matrix multiplication from $A$ and $V$ producing outputs of shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times n_h\times S\times d}$. Finally these outputs are concatenated over heads and linearly projected back to $\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$ by a final weight $W_O$.

Gradients flow through these operations in an orderly way: during backpropagation you receive a gradient with respect to the final output of shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times H}$, which is reshaped to per-head shape and multiplied by $W_O^\top$ to produce gradients on the concatenated head outputs. The gradient with respect to $V$ arises from multiplying $A^\top$ with the gradient into out, while gradients with respect to $A$ come from contracting the gradient into out with $V^\top$. Gradients with respect to $Q$ and $K$ flow through the softmax and the dot product: the derivative of softmax is dense but well-understood; gradients into $Q$ and $K$ are computed via VJP-style operations that exploit batch GEMMs rather than forming explicit Jacobians.

Let us track shapes concretely for a single head to emphasize the pattern. Suppose gradient of loss with respect to the head output is $G_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times d}$. Then

- Gradient w.r.t. $V$ (before projection): $G_V = A^\top G_{\text{out}}$, shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times d}$. Here $A^\top$ is transposed in the sequence axis.

- Gradient w.r.t. $A$: $G_A = G_{\text{out}} V^\top$, yielding shape $\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times S}$.

- Gradient w.r.t. the pre-softmax scores $S$ is obtained by applying the Jacobian of softmax to $G_A$. For each $b,h,i$, if $s$ are the pre-softmax logits and $a=\text{softmax}(s)$, then
  $$
  \frac{\partial L}{\partial s} = \left(\text{diag}(a) - a a^\top\right) \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}.
  $$
  This computation is implemented per query position and head and can be expressed as matrix multiplications with structured matrices (e.g., computing $G_S = G_A - (a \cdot \sum_j G_A_j) \cdot \mathbf{1}^\top$ using broadcasting and sums).

- Finally, gradients w.r.t. $Q$ and $K$ are $G_Q = G_S K$ and $G_K = G_S^\top Q$ (with the appropriate axis ordering and scaling by $1/\sqrt{d}$).

All of these are batched GEMMs; importantly, gradients with respect to the projection matrices $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ are computed by contracting these $G_Q$, $G_K$, $G_V$ with the original inputs $X$: $G_{W_Q} = X^\top G_Q$ (suitably batched), and so on. Thus the shapes of parameter gradients are consistent with the forward weight shapes.

When you have positional embeddings, layer norms, or residual connections, the propagation follows the same mechanical rules: compute local gradients, multiply by Jacobians (often simple linear or elementwise derivatives), and propagate. Keeping careful track of axes and where reductions (sums) occur is essential because mismatches in summed axes are a common source of bugs.

# Computation graph, forward/backward passes, and memory for activations

Automatic differentiation frameworks (autodiff) implement derivatives by constructing a computation graph during the forward pass and then traversing that graph backward to compute gradients. Each node in the graph represents an operation (e.g., matrix multiplication, softmax, elementwise nonlinearity), and edges represent data flow (tensors). During the forward pass the framework records, for each operation, enough information to compute the local backward operation. This recording may include the inputs (activations), outputs, and sometimes auxiliary intermediate values computed in the forward pass.

The backward pass processes nodes in reverse topological order—computing gradients of the loss w.r.t. each intermediate tensor by applying the chain rule and accumulating gradients for tensors that contribute to multiple paths in the graph. For each node, the framework calls a backward function that takes the gradient of the output and returns gradients with respect to the inputs.

Memory usage is a central practical concern. To compute backward gradients, many backward functions require the original inputs or outputs. For example, the backward of a ReLU needs to know the forward input to decide which coordinates were active; the backward of softmax often needs the forward softmax output to apply its Jacobian efficiently. Storing every activation for the entire network and all time steps can therefore be prohibitively expensive, especially for large models or long sequences. Frameworks and practitioners use several strategies to reduce memory:

- Checkpointing (or rematerialization): trade computation for memory by re-computing intermediate activations during the backward pass instead of saving them. The forward pass saves only a subset of activations and recomputes omitted values when needed in backward.

- Gradient checkpointing across layers: store activations only at certain layer boundaries and recompute inner-layer activations on demand.

- In-place operations: careful in-place updates can save memory but must be used compatibly with autodiff, which may require previous values.

- Avoiding storing unnecessary intermediates: some layers can compute backward passes using just the output, not the input, or by recomputing small intermediate values cheaply.

Understanding what needs to be saved for each operation matters. For a linear layer $y = W x + b$, the backward needs $x$ to compute $G_W = g_y x^\top$ (unless $x$ can be recomputed cheaply), and it needs $W$ to compute $G_x = W^\top g_y$ (but $W$ is a parameter and is kept in memory already). For attention, one often needs the softmax outputs $A$ and sometimes $V$ or $K$ to compute gradients. Transformer implementations therefore carefully manage which tensors are kept in GPU/TPU memory and which are recomputed.

Computation graphs can be built eagerly (record operations as they happen) or symbolically (define a graph first and then compile). Eager modes are convenient for dynamic control flow; graph modes can optimize and fuse operations for speed. Both approaches use the same underlying idea for automatic differentiation: store enough forward-stage information to compute the required local backward computations later.

# Jacobian/vector‑Jacobian products and efficient gradient computation

Full Jacobian matrices are often enormous and unnecessary. What autodiff computes in practice are directional products: given a function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, rather than computing the full Jacobian $J_f\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, we often need either a Jacobian–vector product (JVP) or a vector–Jacobian product (VJP). These correspond respectively to forward-mode and reverse-mode autodiff.

A JVP computes $J_f v$ for a given vector $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$ without forming $J_f$. A VJP computes $u^\top J_f$ for a given $u\in\mathbb{R}^m$ (which is the same as $(J_f^\top u)^\top$), and VJPs are what reverse-mode autodiff uses to propagate gradients from outputs back to inputs. If $L$ is a scalar loss depending on $y=f(x)$, the backward pass needs $J_f^\top g_y$, where $g_y = \partial L/\partial y$ is the seed vector. This is a VJP.

Why are VJPs efficient? Because reverse-mode autodiff propagates sensitivities backward, composing VJPs of local operations to accumulate the required gradient in a time proportional to the cost of the forward pass (up to constants), rather than proportional to the input dimension. For functions with scalar outputs (like loss), reverse-mode yields cost proportional to forward evaluation, making it ideal for training networks with many parameters but single scalar loss.

Concrete examples illustrate the mechanics. For a linear map $y = W x$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the Jacobian is $J_f = W$, so

- JVP: $J_f v = W v$ is just a matrix multiplication.

- VJP: $u^\top J_f = u^\top W = (W^\top u)^\top$, so the VJP returns $W^\top u$. This is exactly the backprop multiplication we used earlier.

For elementwise nonlinearities like $\phi:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\phi(x)_i = \sigma(x_i)$, the Jacobian is diagonal with entries $\sigma'(x_i)$. A VJP with seed $u$ simply multiplies elementwise: $J_\phi^\top u = (\sigma'(x) \odot u)$.

In deep networks, we string together JVPs and VJPs. Forward-mode (JVP) would propagate directional derivatives along the forward computation, useful for computing per-example Jacobian–vector products or higher-order forward derivatives. Reverse-mode (VJP) computes gradient w.r.t. parameters efficiently, which is why it’s the default for training.

Efficiency also comes from not constructing dense Jacobians when sparsity or structure exists. Many operations admit specialized backward routines that compute VJPs via matrix multiplications rather than forming the Jacobian. Softmax is an example: the Jacobian is dense and of size $S\times S$ for each position, but the VJP can be computed via a few vector operations:
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s} = a \odot \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} - \left(\sum_j \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j} a_j\right)\mathbf{1}\right),
$$
where $a=\text{softmax}(s)$. This expression uses an inner product and broadcasts to avoid an $S\times S$ matrix multiplication for each entry.

Another important example is the gradient of a batched matrix multiplication. If $C = AB$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}$ and we have seed $G_C$, the VJPs are $G_A = G_C B^\top$ and $G_B = A^\top G_C$, both matrix multiplications that are far cheaper than forming the full Jacobian (which would be $mn \times (mk + kn)$ in size).

In practice, autodiff frameworks implement each primitive operation with an accompanying backward routine that computes VJPs efficiently using matrix multiplications, sums, and elementwise operations. This design avoids forming Jacobians explicitly and scales to extremely high-dimensional problems.

# Putting it all together: practical tips for reading and implementing gradients in LLMs

When you read a paper describing a new architecture or optimization trick, translate each described operation into tensors with concrete shapes and ask: what are the forward computation and the required backward contractions? For every layer, identify the inputs, outputs, and parameters, and then derive the shapes of their gradients. Check whether any broadcasting occurs and where sums will reduce gradient axes.

Remember that backpropagating through linear layers and attention is mostly a sequence of GEMM operations. The heavy computational kernels are forward GEMM calls (e.g., computing $Q,K,V$, attention scores, output projections) and the backward pass comprises a small number of equally sized GEMMs (e.g., $G_V = A^\top G_{\text{out}}$, $G_A = G_{\text{out}} V^\top$, $G_Q = G_S K$, etc.). Profiling and optimizing models usually focus on fusing or scheduling these GEMMs and on minimizing memory by checkpointing or recomputation.

Finally, think in terms of VJPs rather than full Jacobians. If you need to compute gradients w.r.t. parameters given a loss, the reverse-mode strategy computes $J_f^\top g$ for each layer efficiently. If you must compute directional derivatives or per-example gradients, consider JVPs or combinations of JVPs and VJPs; many frameworks provide primitives for both.

Summary

This chapter built the foundations for understanding how linear algebra and automatic differentiation implement forward and backward passes in deep networks. Batched GEMMs and vectorized, broadcasted operations express the computations used in LLMs compactly and efficiently. The chain rule underlies backpropagation; for linear layers and attention, the backward computations reduce to more matrix multiplications and reductions with predictable shapes. Computation graphs record necessary forward information to enable reverse-mode autodiff, and memory management strategies trade computation for storage when needed. At the core of efficient autodiff are JVPs and VJPs which compute needed Jacobian products without forming full Jacobians, making gradient computation tractable at the scales used in modern models. Understanding these patterns will make it much easier to read, implement, and optimize research ideas in deep learning.
