Introduction

As modern neural networks — especially large language models (LLMs) — grow in size, running them efficiently at inference time becomes increasingly important. Quantization reduces model memory footprint and inference latency by representing weights and activations with lower-precision numbers (for example, 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floating point). However, naïvely quantizing every tensor can drastically degrade model quality because some weights or activations are highly sensitive to precision or have heavy-tailed distributions. This chapter surveys practical techniques developed to preserve accuracy while enabling low-precision inference. We focus on inference-centered quantization and "accuracy-preserving tricks" used in practice: per-channel and per-block scaling, methods that re-balance outliers between activations and weights (for example SmoothQuant), state-of-the-art post-training quantization algorithms such as GPTQ targeted at LLMs, grouped/block quantization strategies and their design tradeoffs, hybrid-precision inference where a few tensors remain high precision, and evaluation metrics and protocols for quantized models.

The goal is to provide intuition, explain algorithmic details at a level suitable for reading current research papers, and highlight practical tradeoffs that engineers and researchers consider when deploying quantized LLMs.

# Per-channel and per-block scaling for inference quantization

When we quantize a tensor, the simplest approach is to apply a single scale and zero-point to the entire tensor, mapping its floating-point values to a small set of integers. This is referred to as per-tensor quantization. While simple, per-tensor scaling can be insufficient when different parts of a tensor exhibit very different dynamic ranges. Per-channel and per-block scaling are refinements that let the quantizer adapt to heterogeneity in statistics across channels or sub-blocks.

Per-channel scaling assigns a separate scale (and optionally zero-point) for each output or input channel of a weight matrix (or per row/column depending on layout). For a weight matrix $W$ with shape $(M, N)$, per-channel quantization typically uses a different scale $s_m$ for each row $m$ or each column $n$. The quantized weight for element $W_{m,n}$ becomes
$$
\hat{W}_{m,n} = \mathrm{round}\left(\frac{W_{m,n}}{s_m}\right)
$$
with values clamped to the integer range (for example, $[-127, 127]$ for signed 8-bit). The dequantization at inference multiplies back by $s_m$. Intuitively, per-channel scaling adapts to the magnitude of weights associated with a particular output dimension so small weights are not drowned out by large ones in another channel. This typically reduces quantization error with minimal extra cost: only one scale per channel must be stored. In matrix multiplication, using per-output-channel scales is especially convenient because after dequantizing the integer matrix multiply by appropriate scales, the channel-wise scaling can be fused into bias or following linear transforms.

Per-block scaling generalizes per-channel by splitting a tensor into fixed-size blocks and assigning one scale per block. For instance, a weight matrix could be split into blocks of $k$ consecutive elements along the input dimension, and each block gets a scale. Per-block allows finer-grained adaptation than per-channel but coarser than per-element. It trades off storage and computational complexity: more scales must be stored and applied, but quantization error decreases. Block quantization is particularly helpful when a channel’s elements are not uniform — for example, in huge embedding matrices or the internal layout of transformer weights where a channel can contain elements with varied ranges.

A key implementation detail is how block or channel scales interact with matrix multiplication. If both activations and weights are quantized with per-channel/per-block scales, the output scaling becomes the product of the two relevant scales. Efficient inference kernels must therefore manage scale multiplications carefully to avoid per-element floating-point operations that negate the speed benefits of integer arithmetic. Common strategies include keeping one side (usually activations) in floating point scale, precomputing combined scales for blocks, or using integer arithmetic with compensated scaling followed by a single floating-scale multiply.

Per-channel and per-block scaling are among the most effective and widely used practical techniques because they capture much of the heterogeneity that causes quantization error while keeping storage and computational overhead modest. They also set the stage for more advanced methods that relocate scaling between tensors to reduce the number of outlier values requiring extreme scales.

# SmoothQuant and other methods to move outlier scaling between activations and weights

One recurring challenge in quantizing LLMs is the presence of "outlier" channels or dimensions with unusually large activations compared to others. These outliers force a coarse global quantization scale, causing significant precision loss for the majority of less-extreme values. SmoothQuant and related methods address this by redistributing scaling between activations and weights so that the tensor that will be quantized more coarsely no longer contains the outliers.

The basic idea of SmoothQuant is to move a channel-wise scale from activations into the corresponding weight column so that the activations become more uniform. Consider a linear layer computing $Y = XW$, where $X$ has shape $(B, D_{in})$ and $W$ has shape $(D_{in}, D_{out})$. Suppose some columns of $X$ (i.e., features) have much larger variance. SmoothQuant computes a per-input-channel scaling factor $c_i$ and applies it by scaling $W$ and inverse-scaling $X$:
$$
\tilde{W}_{i,j} = c_i W_{i,j}, \qquad \tilde{X}_{b,i} = \frac{X_{b,i}}{c_i}.
$$
This transformation preserves the product $\tilde{X}\tilde{W} = XW$, so model behavior is unchanged in exact arithmetic. The design chooses $c_i$ so that activations $\tilde{X}$ have a more balanced dynamic range, enabling better activation quantization. Typically $c_i$ is chosen proportional to the ratio of activation and weight scales, for example $c_i = \sqrt{\frac{s_{X,i}}{s_{W,i}}}$ where $s_{X,i}$ and $s_{W,i}$ are per-channel scales measured from calibration data. The square root balances the scaling so neither tensor becomes excessively large or small to quantize.

SmoothQuant can be applied to many linear layers, including self-attention and feed-forward blocks. It is particularly useful when activations are the primary source of outliers, and weights are more amenable to absorbing the scale. Practically, after scaling, weights are quantized (for example to int8) and activations can be quantized or kept in lower-precision formats with far less accuracy loss. A simple extension is to apply this re-scaling only to selected layers or channels determined by a sensitivity analysis: not all layers benefit equally and some might be better left untouched.

There are other related techniques that relocate scale in different ways. For instance, methods inspired by per-channel equalization in convolutional networks iteratively re-balance the ranges of two adjacent linear layers by multiplying one layer’s outputs by a scale and dividing the next layer’s inputs by the same scale. The goal is to equalize ranges so that a single quantizer at the interface causes less relative error. Another family of approaches chooses transforms (not necessarily diagonal scalings) that make weight or activation matrices more compressible — for example, rotating bases or permuting dimensions to concentrate energy into fewer coefficients, enabling coarser quantization on the rest. Those approaches can be more intrusive and costly, but they share the high-level idea of moving or transforming statistics so the quantizer sees more uniform distributions.

Intuitively, these methods exploit the fact that linear layers permit certain exact algebraic transformations (scaling, permutations, orthogonal rotations) that change the numerical distribution of operands without changing the overall computation; quantization-sensitive parts can thus be reshaped to reduce the impact of limited discrete precision. SmoothQuant is among the most practical because it is simple, relies only on diagonal scaling (cheap to implement), and aligns with per-channel quantization infrastructure already common in inference engines.

# GPTQ and post-training quantization for LLMs

Post-training quantization (PTQ) takes a pretrained floating-point model and quantizes its weights and possibly activations without further gradient-based retraining. PTQ is attractive because it avoids full finetuning and can be applied quickly to large models. However, PTQ must carefully manage quantization error to avoid severe degradation of LLM capabilities.

GPTQ (short for "Greedy, Product Quantization" in some papers, though naming varies) is a leading PTQ technique developed specifically for LLMs. Its key insight is that the quantization error introduced in a weight matrix $W$ causes downstream error propagation in the outputs of the layer. GPTQ views quantization as an optimization problem: choose low-bit representations that minimize the error of $XW$ on a calibration dataset of activations $X$. The algorithm commonly used in GPTQ-style methods is a second-order-aware, greedy search that quantizes weights column-by-column (or block-by-block) while compensating for previously quantized columns.

A typical outline of GPTQ-like quantization is:
1. Collect a calibration set: sample a small set of representative input activations $X^{(k)}$ by running a few batches of real data through the model.
2. For a given weight matrix $W$, compute the pre-activation outputs $Y^{(k)} = X^{(k)} W$ and the residuals as quantization choices are made.
3. Proceed column-by-column: when choosing quantized values $\hat{w}_j$ for column $j$, account for the influence of previously quantized columns on the residual output. Use approximate second-order statistics of activations (e.g., the empirical Gram matrix $G = X^\top X$) to select quantized values that minimize the squared output error $\sum_k \|X^{(k)}(W - \hat{W})\|^2$.
4. Update residuals efficiently rather than recomputing from scratch, and iterate.

The second-order information (Gram matrix) is crucial: quantization error on a weight element should be weighted by how much that element is used by the activation covariance. This directs higher precision or smaller quantization error to directions with large activation energy. Because the number of columns and bits makes exhaustive search infeasible, GPTQ uses a greedy or approximate search, sometimes combined with product quantization within blocks, to find a strong solution quickly.

GPTQ variants differ in several implementation choices: whether they quantize weights in 8- or 4-bits, whether activations are simulated at lower precision during calibration, how blocks are defined, and whether dequantization or compensation matrices are stored to correct residual errors. A powerful extension stores small correction matrices or low-rank residuals that are computed during PTQ and applied at inference to recover performance; these corrections add a small overhead but can dramatically restore accuracy for aggressive quantization like 2- or 3-bit.

The main advantage of GPTQ-style PTQ is that it achieves near-fine-tuned performance without retraining. On modern LLMs, GPTQ can produce 4-bit quantized models that maintain perplexity and downstream performance close to the original FP16 model on many benchmarks. The disadvantages are the need for representative calibration data, some extra preprocessing time to run the calibration and optimization, and potentially extra metadata (scales, correction matrices). Also, GPTQ is computationally heavier than simple symmetric rounding and requires careful numerical stability handling when the Gram matrix is ill-conditioned.

# Grouped and block quantization strategies and their tradeoffs

Group and block quantization are general strategies to partition tensors into smaller units and quantize each unit independently. The fundamental tradeoff is between granularity (smaller groups mean better fit to local statistics and lower quantization error) and overhead (more groups means more stored scales and potentially more per-group operations).

A common scheme is to partition columns of weight matrices into groups of size $G$ and quantize each group with its own set of quantization parameters. For example, group size $G=64$ with 4-bit values means we store roughly one scale per group plus the compressed integer values. Group quantization often uses symmetric quantization (zero-point zero) with per-group scale to simplify arithmetic; per-group zero-points are possible but increase complexity.

Block quantization goes further by applying structure within a group, for example splitting groups into sub-blocks and using product quantization or learned codebooks per block. Product quantization approximates each block by the sum of several low-dimensional codewords, which can be very storage-efficient. Block-fine quantization can reach extremely low average bits per parameter while maintaining accuracy, but decoding complexity increases: lookup tables and extra adds are required to reconstruct approximate floats during matrix multiply, which can push computation from pure integer arithmetic into mixed operations.

There are several practical tradeoffs to consider when choosing grouping/blocking strategies. First, memory vs. accuracy: smaller groups reduce quantization error but increase memory overhead from scales and possibly codebooks. Second, speed: if each group requires a separate dequantization during inference, that can impede vectorized, parallel implementation on GPUs or accelerators. Many practical systems align groups to machine-friendly sizes (for instance, powers-of-two multiples of SIMD lanes) to avoid fragmentation. Third, kernel complexity and support: specialized kernels are required to perform fast grouped quantized matrix multiplication; if the deployment environment lacks such kernels, the theoretical memory savings may not translate to speed improvements.

Another dimension is the interaction with activation quantization. If activations are quantized with coarse per-tensor scales but the weight is block-quantized finely, errors can still propagate. Hence, grouping strategies are often co-designed for both weights and activations. This co-design can involve balancing group sizes between different layers based on their sensitivity or even dynamic grouping where group boundaries are adapted per-layer.

Finally, advanced block strategies combine grouping with methods like SmoothQuant and GPTQ. For instance, one can apply SmoothQuant to reduce activation outliers, then use group-wise GPTQ optimization to find best quantized values per block. Empirically, such hybrid methods achieve strong tradeoffs, enabling aggressive quantization (e.g., 3-bit) with acceptable quality loss.

# Hybrid precision inference (keeping some tensors in higher precision)

Hybrid precision means quantizing most of the model to low precision while keeping a subset of tensors at higher precision (for example, FP16 or BF16) to preserve critical numerical properties and application-level accuracy. The rationale is that not all tensors are equally important to model performance; some weights, activations, or intermediate computations are disproportionately sensitive to quantization.

Identifying which tensors to keep in high precision is a key design decision. Common heuristics include: keep layernorm parameters and activations in high precision, keep the final output layer or embedding matrices at higher precision, and preserve attention softmax inputs/outputs in float. More principled approaches measure sensitivity by temporarily quantizing each tensor in isolation on a calibration set and measuring the induced output error or perplexity change. Tensors that cause large degradation when quantized are marked as high precision.

For transformer-based LLMs, typical hybrid strategies keep layer normalization, the residual addition path, and the embedding layers (input or output) in FP16, while quantizing the large weight matrices in the transformer blocks to 8- or 4-bits. The attention softmax and scaling by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}$ often remain in FP16 because the softmax is numerically sensitive and small perturbations can disproportionately affect token probabilities. Some practitioners keep the final logits computation in a higher precision to preserve downstream generation quality.

Hybrid precision also appears in decoding kernels: to keep memory bandwidth low, integer matrix multiplies are used for the bulk of computation, but accumulation is performed in 32-bit integers or FP32, and the final result is converted back to a higher-precision floating format for nonlinearities. This ensures numerical stability in accumulation while still benefiting from compact storage.

The benefits of hybrid precision include a better accuracy/performance tradeoff and simpler engineering than a fully low-precision model that relies on many compensation tricks. The downsides include increased implementation complexity — inference engines must support mixing precisions and efficient data movement between different memory formats — and the potential that the saved memory and speed are limited if too many tensors remain high precision. Therefore hybrid precision is often paired with profiling and sensitivity analysis: quantize aggressively, evaluate quality, and revert only the most sensitive tensors to higher precision until target quality is reached.

# Evaluation of quantized models: perplexity and downstream task performance

Evaluating quantized models requires careful selection of metrics and validation protocols because some performance aspects are subtle. For language models, perplexity on held-out text is a canonical measure that correlates to a model's ability to assign probabilities to sequences. Perplexity is defined as
$$
\mathrm{Perplexity} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N \log p(w_t \mid w_{<t})\right),
$$
where $p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ are the model’s predicted probabilities. A small increase in perplexity after quantization indicates the model’s predictive distribution has diverged from the original; however, perplexity does not always perfectly predict downstream task performance, especially for instruction-following or few-shot tasks.

Therefore, practitioners typically use a combination of metrics. Perplexity is useful for quick calibration and as a proxy during PTQ because it is sensitive to distributional shifts in logits. But for many applications, downstream tasks such as question answering, summarization, translation, or instruction following matter more. Evaluating these tasks involves running the quantized model in generation mode (e.g., greedy sampling, beam search) and measuring task-specific metrics: accuracy, BLEU, ROUGE, F1, or human-evaluated metrics. Many papers report both perplexity and several downstream benchmarks to demonstrate that a quantized model retains practical utility.

A key pitfall in evaluation is mismatched quantization conditions: calibration data must reflect the intended inference patterns. For instance, if quantization is calibrated on short context pairs but deployed on long-context generation, activation statistics may differ and performance can fall. Similarly, evaluation should reflect real decoding algorithms and temperatures used in deployment; some quantization errors manifest more strongly under beam search or sampling.

Another important practical consideration is measuring throughput, latency, and memory usage under realistic conditions. A quantized model that reduces weight memory but requires expensive dequantization per block may not improve latency. Therefore, wall-clock measurements on the target hardware with production kernels are essential. Metrics to report include peak memory, model size on disk, memory bandwidth utilization, throughput (tokens per second), latency (time per token or per sequence), and power consumption if relevant.

When reporting accuracy metrics for quantized LLMs, it is common to present a Pareto frontier or table that shows how different quantization schemes trade off bits (or memory) versus perplexity and downstream task scores. Ablation studies that show the effect of specific tricks (SmoothQuant, GPTQ calibration size, group sizes, hybrid precision choices) are also important to assess the robustness and generality of techniques.

Finally, it is critical to consider reproducibility issues: quantization can be sensitive to random seeds, calibration data, and implementation details. Researchers should clearly document calibration datasets, number of calibration batches, selected hyperparameters (group sizes, scales), and any correction matrices stored. For deployment, stress testing on edge cases and longer-generation sequences helps ensure quality under production conditions.

Conclusion

Quantized inference for large models is a rapidly evolving area combining numerical analysis, optimization, and systems engineering. Per-channel and per-block scaling provide practical, low-overhead ways to adapt quantization to heterogeneous tensor statistics. SmoothQuant and related scaling moves let us relocate outliers to tensors that can better absorb them, improving activation quantization. PTQ techniques such as GPTQ use calibration data and second-order information to produce high-quality quantized weights without retraining. Grouped and block quantization enable aggressive compression but require careful tradeoffs between accuracy, storage, and kernel complexity. Hybrid precision lets practitioners preserve critical numerical fidelity where necessary. Finally, rigorous evaluation using perplexity, diverse downstream tasks, and real-world latency/memory measurements is essential to ensure a quantized model remains useful in practice.

Taken together, these methods make it feasible to run very large models efficiently on commodity hardware while keeping the loss in model quality small. Understanding the underlying principles and tradeoffs enables both researchers and engineers to design quantization pipelines that meet the constraints of specific applications.
