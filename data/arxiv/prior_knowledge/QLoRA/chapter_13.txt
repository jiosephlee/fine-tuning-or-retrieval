Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) trained with next‑token prediction learn a great deal of linguistic knowledge and world knowledge, but they are not automatically aligned to follow a user's instructions, behave politely, or produce outputs that match human preferences. Instruction fine‑tuning and related techniques adapt pretrained LLMs so they reliably follow instructions and behave like chatbots. The methods used fall into two broad families: supervised finetuning with cross‑entropy loss, often using human or synthetic instruction–response pairs, and reinforcement learning methods that incorporate human preferences or learned reward models. This chapter surveys those approaches, explains the data sources typically used, and compares the objectives and their behavioral consequences. We also discuss knowledge distillation and "self‑instruct" methods for scaling supervised instruction data, outline the components of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and present practical considerations for training on multi‑turn dialogues and for prompt formatting.

# Supervised instruction finetuning with cross‑entropy loss

Supervised instruction fine‑tuning adapts a pretrained LLM by continuing gradient‑based training on a dataset of instruction–response pairs. The setup is conceptually simple: given an instruction or prompt \(x\) and a target response \(y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)\) tokenized into a sequence, the model is trained to maximize the conditional probability \(p_\theta(y \mid x)\). In practice we minimize the cross‑entropy (negative log‑likelihood) loss
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = - \sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]
where \(y_{<t}\) denotes the previous tokens of the response. This is the same loss used during standard language model pretraining when conditioned on context, but here the context \(x\) typically contains a clearly defined instruction or a multi‑turn dialogue history.

Intuitively, cross‑entropy learning trains the model to imitate the target responses in the dataset. It directly optimizes for token‑level accuracy: the model learns to place high probability mass on the tokens that appear in the human (or synthetic) responses. Because the loss is decomposed across tokens, the model becomes skilled at generating fluent, locally consistent continuations; if the dataset covers a wide variety of instruction styles and content, the model will learn to generalize and produce reasonable answers to new instructions.

There are important caveats. First, cross‑entropy learning treats the dataset as ground truth: it will try to reproduce whatever behaviors and tones are present in the supervised targets. If the targets are inconsistent or expressive in different styles, the model may produce mixed or unpredictable responses. Second, token‑level loss does not directly measure high‑level properties such as helpfulness, truthfulness, or safety. A model can be very good at matching surface tokens while still being factually incorrect or producing undesirable content. Third, cross‑entropy training relies heavily on dataset quality and coverage. When the supervised examples are scarce or biased, the model's behavior is constrained by those limitations.

Despite these limits, supervised finetuning with cross‑entropy forms the backbone of many production instruction models. It is simple, stable, and effective at steering large pretrained models from raw next‑token prediction toward a conversational, instruction‑following behavior, provided the training data is carefully curated.

# Data sources: human‑created dialogs, model distillation, crowdsourced instruction datasets

The choice of training data is as important as the optimization method. Several common data sources are used for instruction fine‑tuning, each with benefits and trade‑offs.

Human‑created dialogs are gold‑standard data produced by expert annotators or crowdworkers. These datasets typically include rich, multi‑turn dialogues with high‑quality answers, follow‑ups, clarifications, and corrections. Because human raters can be instructed to follow style guidelines (e.g., be helpful, avoid hallucination, deference on uncertain facts), human‑created dialogs lead to models with desirable behavior. The downside is cost and scale: high‑quality human annotation is expensive and time‑consuming, which limits dataset size and diversity.

Model distillation datasets are generated by running existing models (often an ensemble or a larger model) to produce responses for many instructions. Distillation can produce massive datasets at low cost. The idea is to treat the teacher model's outputs as pseudo‑labels and train a smaller or finetuned model to match them. Distillation can capture complex behaviors present in the teacher but also inherits the teacher's mistakes and biases. If the teacher is stronger than the student, distillation is an effective way to transfer ability; if the teacher is flawed, distillation amplifies those flaws.

Crowdsourced instruction datasets occupy a middle ground. Platforms like Mechanical Turk allow researchers to collect many instruction–response pairs at moderate cost. Tasks range from simple question–answer pairs to more elaborate dialogue acts and roleplays. Crowdsourcing can produce diverse datasets quickly, and with careful task design and quality control, yields useful training data. However, crowdsourced workers may not always follow instructions precisely or may produce low‑effort responses, so rigorous filtering and validation are required.

A common strategy is to combine these sources. Researchers may seed training with a smaller set of high‑quality human dialogs, expand coverage with crowdsourced examples, and scale further with model‑generated data. The proportion of each source influences the model: more human data tends to improve alignment with nuanced instruction following, while more model‑generated data increases scale but may reduce quality per example.

# Knowledge distillation and self‑instruct methods

Knowledge distillation aims to transfer behavior from a "teacher" model to a "student" model. In the context of instruction tuning, the teacher might be a larger model, an ensemble, or a model specialized for answering instructions. Distillation proceeds by generating responses from the teacher for many prompts and training the student with cross‑entropy to imitate those responses. At the token level, this is the same objective as supervised finetuning, but the key difference is that the labels are machine‑generated.

Distillation can be implemented in two complementary ways. Hard distillation uses the teacher's generated tokens as the single target sequence; the student minimizes cross‑entropy against this sequence. Soft distillation exposes the student to the teacher's full conditional probability distributions over tokens, minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the teacher and student output distributions. Soft targets often transfer more information because they communicate uncertainty and alternative plausible continuations; however, they require access to the teacher's logits or probabilities, increasing storage and computation costs.

Self‑instruct methods aim to bootstrap instruction datasets using a pretrained model's own capabilities. The canonical self‑instruct workflow starts with a small seed set of human examples, then iteratively generates new instruction–response pairs by sampling tasks, generating responses with the model, and filtering or refining the outputs. The generated data is then used to further finetune the model. Self‑instruct is attractive because it leverages the model to scale instruction data without paying human annotators for every sample.

There are several ways to improve the quality of self‑instructed data. One is to use multiple models or prompting strategies: generate candidate responses with various prompts or sampling temperatures and select the best examples using heuristic filters or a trained verifier. Another is to have humans curate or edit a subset of generated examples, which provides higher quality labels for subsequent iterations. Self‑instruct methods can yield substantial improvements when starting from a capable base model, but they risk reinforcing the model's own errors and biases unless human oversight or reliable automated filtering is used.

Distillation and self‑instruct are powerful for scaling instruction datasets at low marginal cost, but their effectiveness depends on the teacher/model quality and the selection/filtering mechanisms applied to generated examples.

# Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF): components and motivation

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) provides a complementary approach to supervised finetuning. Instead of directly maximizing the probability of target tokens, RLHF optimizes a policy to maximize an objective defined by human preferences. The core motivation is that human evaluators can express preferences over whole responses — for example, preferring one answer because it is simpler, more helpful, or less toxic — properties that are hard to encode in per‑token cross‑entropy loss.

RLHF is typically implemented via three main components: a dataset of preference annotations, a reward model that predicts human preferences, and a policy optimization algorithm that updates the LLM to maximize cumulative reward while maintaining some fidelity to the pretrained language model.

1. Preference data. Human raters are presented with pairs (or more) of model responses to the same prompt and indicate which response they prefer. These comparisons capture holistic judgments about helpfulness, correctness, style, or safety. Preference data is usually collected for a diverse set of prompts and candidate responses generated by the current model or by several models.

2. Reward model. A separate model \(r_\phi(\cdot)\) is trained to predict the human preferences. Given a prompt and a candidate response \(y\), the reward model outputs a scalar score. Training the reward model typically uses a pairwise loss: if humans prefer response \(y^A\) to \(y^B\), the reward model is trained so that \(r_\phi(y^A) > r_\phi(y^B)\). A common loss is the binary cross‑entropy of the predicted preference probability:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\sum_{(A,B)} \log \sigma\big(r_\phi(y^A) - r_\phi(y^B)\big),
\]
where \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid. The reward model distills human judgments into a differentiable surrogate objective the policy can optimize.

3. Policy optimization. With a reward model in place, the policy \( \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \) (the LLM) is updated to maximize expected reward. Because LLMs produce long sequences and high variance can occur in direct policy gradient methods, practitioners often use Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or related RL algorithms that balance reward improvement with constraints that prevent the policy from drifting too far from the pretrained or supervised policy. A typical RLHF objective includes a KL penalty against a reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \), e.g., the pretrained model:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(y \mid x) - \beta \,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big],
\]
where \( \beta \) controls the strength of the penalty. The KL term prevents the policy from producing degenerate high‑reward but incoherent outputs by anchoring it to language patterns learned previously.

RLHF's motivation is that it optimizes for the metric humans care about rather than token‑level likelihood. This allows the model to prefer concise, safe, or otherwise desirable responses even when such properties are not explicitly present in the supervised targets.

# Differences in objectives and their effects on behavior

The objective function used for training shapes model behavior in predictable ways. Cross‑entropy finetuning treats each supervised response as ground truth and minimizes token‑wise error; RLHF optimizes for a scalar reward that captures human preferences over entire outputs. These differences produce distinct behavioral patterns.

Cross‑entropy finetuning encourages faithful imitation of provided outputs. If the dataset contains detailed, high‑quality answers, the model will produce similar outputs. However, CE is susceptible to overfitting dataset idiosyncrasies and can be brittle when encountering instructions outside the training distribution. CE does not inherently distinguish between outputs that are correct but unhelpful and those that are misleading yet plausible; it cannot directly capture trade‑offs like brevity versus comprehensiveness.

RLHF, by optimizing for preference scores, can encourage holistic properties: being helpful, harmless, truthful (as judged by raters), and following style guidelines. Because rewards can encode trade‑offs implicitly, RLHF enables models to prefer responses that humans rank higher, even if they have lower token likelihood under the pretrained model. However, RLHF has its own failure modes. The reward model is an imperfect proxy for human judgment; optimizing the policy against a learned reward can lead to reward hacking, where the model finds ways to achieve high reward that do not correspond to true human preference. The KL penalty mitigates, but does not eliminate, divergence from the prior. RLHF training can also produce overconfident outputs: a model that learns which features the reward model prefers may output plausible‑sounding but false statements if such outputs were consistently favored in the preference data.

Another key difference is sample efficiency and stability. Cross‑entropy finetuning is straightforward and stable: gradients come from a dense token loss and training procedures are well understood. RLHF requires collecting preference data, training a reward model, and running policy optimization, which introduces more hyperparameters, variance, and the potential for instability. On the other hand, RLHF can achieve higher subjective alignment for properties not easily encoded in token targets, making it valuable for chatbot alignment.

Practically, many systems combine both approaches: first perform supervised instruction finetuning to teach the model to respond like a helpful assistant, then apply RLHF to refine behavior according to human preferences. This staged approach leverages the stability of CE and the targeted alignment of RLHF.

# Practical considerations for multi‑turn dialog training and prompt formatting

Training for multi‑turn dialogue introduces additional complexities over single‑turn instruction–response pairs. Dialogue models must maintain context, track conversational state, and produce coherent turn‑taking behavior. Here are key considerations and best practices.

Context windows and truncation. LLMs have finite context windows. For multi‑turn data, the combined length of system prompts, user turns, and model turns must fit within the model's maximum context size. Strategies include summarizing or compressing earlier turns, prioritizing recent history, or using separate long‑term memory modules. Care must be taken when truncating: removing early system instructions or important context can change the semantics of subsequent turns, so truncation policies should ideally retain system and most recent user/model exchanges.

Prompt formatting and role tokens. Dialogue datasets typically include role labels (e.g., "System:", "User:", "Assistant:") and explicit separators. Consistent formatting helps the model learn turn boundaries and role behavior. Using a canonical format for system instructions — e.g., a "system" message describing style and constraints — is important because models often rely on those instructions to determine tone and content. Some training setups use special control tokens marking roles; these tokens can improve learning and make inference prompts shorter and more interpretable.

Teacher forcing and exposure bias. Supervised finetuning commonly uses teacher forcing: at training time the model conditions on the gold dialogue history and is trained to predict the next token. At inference the model conditions on its own sampled outputs. This mismatch can cause exposure bias where the model's errors compound over turns. Techniques to mitigate exposure bias include mixed teacher forcing (occasionally feeding model‑generated tokens during training), scheduled sampling, and incorporating RL objectives that evaluate full dialogues rather than single responses.

Per‑turn vs. full‑dialogue targets. You can train on per‑response targets (predict the assistant's next turn given the history) or on full concatenated response sequences (predict long continuations). Per‑turn targets decompose learning and reduce variance, but optimizing only one turn at a time may cause models to ignore long‑term conversational goals. Training with episode‑level objectives or reward functions that span multiple turns (e.g., coherence across the dialogue) helps align behavior over longer interactions.

Evaluation and human feedback collection. For multi‑turn behavior, evaluation metrics should reflect conversational goals: coherence, consistency, staying on topic, and avoidance of contradictions. Human preference data for RLHF is often collected on per‑turn responses, but it can also be collected for multi‑turn sequences or for entire sessions to better capture long‑term behavior. Designing human evaluation tasks that are clear and unambiguous is critical to produce reliable reward models.

Instruction formatting and system messages. Many deployed chat systems rely on an initial "system" prompt that sets the assistant's role and rules (e.g., "You are a helpful assistant. If you do not know an answer, say so."). In training, including system messages in the context teaches the model to follow these higher‑level instructions. When fine‑tuning, ensure the style and constraints encoded in system prompts during training match what will be used at inference. If you change system prompts at runtime, the model's behavior may shift, so co‑design of system messages and finetuning data is advisable.

Safety and debiasing. For dialogues that may touch on sensitive topics, combine targeted human supervision, filtered datasets, and policy constraints. During RLHF, explicitly collect preference data on edge cases (e.g., harmful instructions) and use reward models that penalize unsafe replies. In supervised datasets, include examples that demonstrate safe refusals and redirections.

Tokenization, decoding, and sampling. The choice of decoding algorithm at inference (greedy, beam search, nucleus sampling) affects multi‑turn behavior. Sampling with temperature and top‑p encourages variety but can reduce coherence; beam search may produce repetitive or overly deterministic replies. When training with RLHF, be aware that the reward model and policy were evaluated under particular decoding settings — mismatches at inference can lead to degraded performance.

Putting these considerations together, successful multi‑turn instruction training often follows this pipeline: curate a mix of high‑quality human dialogs and diverse synthetic examples; format data with consistent role and system tokens; finetune with cross‑entropy per turn; collect human preference comparisons over representative dialogues; train a reward model; and finally run RLHF with a KL constraint to refine behavior. Careful design of truncation rules, prompt formats, and evaluation procedures ensures the model generalizes to real‑world conversational settings.

Conclusion

Instruction fine‑tuning, distillation, and RLHF are complementary tools for adapting LLMs into aligned, conversational agents. Supervised finetuning with cross‑entropy is simple and effective for learning to imitate desired response styles, while distillation and self‑instructing scale up training data using model generations. RLHF brings human preference signals into the training objective, enabling the model to optimize for higher‑level behaviors that are difficult to capture with token loss alone. Each method entails trade‑offs in stability, sample efficiency, and susceptibility to errors. Careful dataset design, role and system prompt engineering, and combined training pipelines that leverage both supervised and reinforcement objectives are practical ways to produce robust multi‑turn dialogue models that behave as helpful and safe chatbots.
