Introduction

Positional encodings are a fundamental ingredient when neural networks must process ordered data, such as sequences of words or tokens. Unlike recurrent architectures that impose order through their connectivity, architectures based on attention or feedforward layers require an explicit mechanism to convey where each token sits in the sequence. Many successful positional encoding schemes—including the sinusoidal encodings used in Transformers—are most naturally understood through the lens of signal processing: sinusoids as basis functions, frequency decomposition, sampling theory, and aliasing. This chapter develops that signal‑processing intuition in depth. We will see how representing position as a superposition of sinusoids gives a frequency decomposition of location, why different frequency bands correspond to different granularities of positional information, how sampling and aliasing affect the fidelity of positional representation, and what happens when we scale or warp the angle inputs to these sinusoids.

# Sinusoids as basis functions and Fourier intuition

Sinusoids—functions of the form $ \sin(\omega t + \phi) $ and $ \cos(\omega t + \phi) $—play a privileged role in signal processing because they are eigenfunctions of linear time‑invariant systems and because they form a complete orthogonal basis (under suitable conditions) for representing a wide class of signals. The Fourier transform formalizes this idea: any reasonably nice function $f(t)$ can be expressed as a superposition (integral or series) of sinusoids of various frequencies,
\[
f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat{f}(\omega) e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega,
\]
or, in the discrete and periodic case,
\[
f[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \hat{f}[k] e^{i 2\pi k n / N}.
\]
Here $\hat{f}$ is the frequency‑domain representation (the Fourier coefficients), and $\omega$ or $2\pi k / N$ indexes frequency. The key conceptual point is that sinusoids are "pure" oscillations: each one carries exactly one frequency. When you represent a signal as a sum of sinusoids, you separate content by how rapidly it changes in time (or across positions).

Three properties of sinusoids are particularly useful for building intuition about positional encodings:

1. Linearity and superposition. If a position encoding is a linear combination of sinusoids, responses to parts of the signal can be understood by considering each frequency component separately. This linear decomposition often simplifies how subsequent layers (which are affine plus nonlinearities) interact with positional information.

2. Orthogonality (in many settings). Different frequency sinusoids are orthogonal under appropriate inner products. Orthogonality means that different frequencies do not interfere when projecting or measuring similarity; this gives a clean separation of information across frequencies.

3. Smoothness and locality tradeoff. Low frequencies vary slowly across time; they encode large‑scale, global structure. High frequencies oscillate quickly and encode fine, local structure. This link between frequency and spatial/temporal scale is central to understanding multi‑scale positional encodings.

To illustrate, imagine a discrete sequence of positions indexed by integer $n$. A low‑frequency sinusoid like $ \sin(2\pi n / 1000) $ changes only slightly between neighboring integer positions; it effectively indicates coarse position over long ranges. A high‑frequency sinusoid like $ \sin(2\pi n / 4) $ flips sign every two positions and thus captures fine alternation. Decomposing a positional signal into such sinusoids yields a frequency spectrum that tells you "how much" of each scale is present.

# Frequency interpretation of positional encodings

When we use sinusoids as components of a positional encoding, we are providing a frequency decomposition of position information. Consider the commonly used sinusoidal positional encoding from the original Transformer formulation. For sequence position $p \in \{0,1,\dots\}$ and dimension index $i \in \{0,\dots,d/2-1\}$, the encoding uses angles
\[
\theta_{p,i} = \frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}},
\]
and defines components
\[
\text{PE}_{p,2i} = \sin(\theta_{p,i}),\qquad \text{PE}_{p,2i+1} = \cos(\theta_{p,i}).
\]
This construction assigns a set of angular frequencies $\omega_i = 1/10000^{2i/d}$ (in units inverse to $p$) and embeds each position as a vector of sinusoids at those frequencies. Interpreting this in Fourier terms, each dimension is a sinusoid with a fixed frequency and zero phase offset across positions. The full vector at position $p$ is a sample of a multi‑frequency periodic (or quasi‑periodic) signal evaluated at $p$.

From the perspective of downstream layers, representing position as a set of sinusoids gives several useful properties. If a model wants to compute relative position information between positions $p$ and $q$, linear combinations of their encoded vectors can produce terms proportional to $\sin(\theta_{p,i})\cos(\theta_{q,i})-\cos(\theta_{p,i})\sin(\theta_{q,i}) = \sin(\theta_{p,i}-\theta_{q,i})$, which depends only on the difference $p-q$. So sinusoidal encodings make relative displacement accessible via inner products and simple linear operations, because phase differences at each frequency translate directly into functions of $p-q$.

More generally, the encoding supplies a bank of frequency "filters". Each dimension highlights a particular rate of change across positions. Low‑indexed dimensions (with large denominators in the angle) correspond to low angular frequencies and thus emphasize slow trends across many positions. High‑indexed dimensions oscillate quickly as a function of $p$ and therefore are sensitive to small shifts in position. In this sense, the vector of sinusoidal components is a multi‑band spectral representation of the discrete position variable.

It is also useful to see that a finite number of sinusoidal components corresponds to a finite frequency bandwidth. If a positional encoding uses frequencies $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_m$, then any linear combination of its components lives in the subspace spanned by those frequencies. The model can therefore represent functions of $p$ whose spectral content lies in that band. If the position‑dependent functions the model needs to represent contain higher frequencies than those provided, they cannot be perfectly reconstructed.

# Aliasing, resolution, and sampling rate analogies for token positions

Sampling theory connects continuous signals and their discrete samples. The classic Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a bandlimited continuous signal of maximum frequency $f_\text{max}$ can be perfectly reconstructed from samples spaced at interval $\Delta t$ if the sampling rate $1/\Delta t$ exceeds $2 f_\text{max}$ (the Nyquist rate). If the sampling rate is too low relative to the signal's highest frequency content, aliasing occurs: higher frequency components masquerade as lower frequencies in the sampled data.

When we view token positions as samples of an underlying continuous position coordinate, the same ideas apply. Consider a hypothetical continuous angular function $g(x)$ over real position $x$ that was sampled at integer tokens $p$. If $g$ contains frequencies above the discrete Nyquist frequency, those high frequencies will alias and cause ambiguous or misleading patterns when observed only at integer positions. In practice, positional encoding schemes often implicitly choose a set of frequencies and then sample them at integer positions. If the set contains frequencies that, relative to the sampling grid of token indices, produce indistinguishable samples for different frequencies, the effective representation suffers from aliasing.

An immediate example: suppose you include both $\sin(2\pi \cdot 0.1 p)$ and $\sin(2\pi \cdot 0.1 p + 2\pi k)$ for integers $k$, they are equivalent because $2\pi k$ is a full period. More generally, if two angular frequencies differ by an integer multiple of $2\pi$ when multiplied by the sampling increment, they produce identical discrete samples. In discrete positional encodings where $p$ takes integer values, frequencies are effectively defined modulo $2\pi$. Frequencies that are too high will wrap around and appear as lower frequencies in the sampled domain, creating aliasing.

Aliasing in positional encodings manifests functionally as ambiguous positional signals: two different positions may produce similar or identical encodings in some frequency components, making it harder for a model to distinguish them using those components. This is tightly related to the concept of resolution. The smallest shift in position that a particular sinusoidal component can reliably detect depends on its local rate of change: high frequencies change more between adjacent positions, providing higher resolution for small displacements; low frequencies change slowly and can only distinguish large offsets.

We can draw an analogy to images sampled by a digital camera. If the camera's sensor spacing is too coarse relative to the fine detail in the scene, high spatial frequencies in the scene alias into lower frequencies in the captured image, causing moiré patterns and loss of detail. For positional encodings, the "sensor" is the sequence of discrete token indices, and the "scene" is the pattern of position‑dependent signals the model needs. If the model must represent very high frequency positional relationships (e.g., distinguishing positions only a fraction of an index apart in some continuous interpretation), but the encoding uses only low frequencies, those fine distinctions are unrecoverable.

Finally, sampling rate analogies help us reason about the maximum unambiguous range of a given frequency component. A sinusoid with angular frequency $\omega$ has period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ in terms of positions. If you want to use that sinusoid to uniquely label positions over a range of length $R$, you need $T$ much larger than $R$ to avoid repeats of the pattern; conversely, if $T$ is small relative to $R$, positions will map to repeating patterns (periodic aliasing) and uniqueness is lost across periods. Thus designers of positional encodings often choose a geometric progression of frequencies so that some components capture very long periods (for distinguishing far apart positions) while others capture short periods (for local discrimination).

# Why low-frequency components capture long-range orders and high-frequency capture locality

The relationship between frequency and spatial scale is arguably the most intuitive link between signal processing and positional encoding. Consider a sinusoid $s_f(p) = \sin(2\pi f p)$ defined over integer positions $p$. The spatial period of this sinusoid is $1/f$: that is, $s_f(p + 1/f) = s_f(p)$ (in continuous terms). For small $f$ (low frequency), the period $1/f$ is large: the sinusoid changes slowly with $p$, so adjacent positions have similar values. For large $f$ (high frequency), the period is small and the sinusoid oscillates rapidly, making adjacent positions likely to have very different values.

From an information perspective, a low‑frequency component does not provide much information about small shifts in position because the change in its value when moving from $p$ to $p+1$ is small. However, it provides stable cues across long distances: since it evolves slowly, one can use phase differences at that frequency to reliably encode or compare positions that are far apart without phase flipping. Conversely, a high‑frequency component exhibits large changes with small shifts in $p$, and so it can encode and help detect local order and fine-grained offsets. This is the reason why frequency banding naturally corresponds to multi‑scale positional granularity.

To make this rigorous, consider the derivative (or discrete difference) of a sinusoidal component with respect to position. For continuous position coordinate $x$, the derivative of $\sin(\omega x)$ is $\omega \cos(\omega x)$. The magnitude of the derivative scales with $\omega$, so higher frequencies produce larger changes per unit shift in $x$. In the discrete case, the finite difference $\Delta s(p) = s(p+1)-s(p)$ behaves similarly: for small angles $\omega$, $\Delta s(p) \approx \omega \cos(\omega p)$, whereas for large $\omega$, the finite difference can be large. Therefore, the sensitivity to local shifts increases with frequency.

An important consequence concerns stability and robustness. Low frequencies being slowly varying makes them robust to small perturbations: noise that perturbs the position by one or two steps will only slightly change the low‑frequency components, which can be desirable when the model should rely on coarse location cues. High frequencies, while offering high local resolution, are also more brittle: a small shift or a missing token can flip phases and confuse components that oscillate rapidly. Thus an ensemble of frequencies gives a balance: the model can use high frequencies to reason about immediate neighbors and use low frequencies to correlate or align distant tokens.

Another angle is the view of basis completeness for representing functions over positions. If you want to represent a function that depends only on coarse order (for example, "early vs. middle vs. late" in a long sequence), low frequencies suffice. If the function depends on parity or exact offsets modulo small integers, high frequencies are necessary. In other words, the spectral content of the target position‑dependent function determines which frequency bands are required. This is why designers include a geometric range of frequencies: to ensure the encoding can support both global ordering and fine local distinctions.

# Consequences of scaling/warping angles on information content

Scaling or warping the angular input to sinusoidal positional encodings changes the effective frequency distribution and thus the information content across positions. Two common manipulations are (1) multiplying the angle $\theta_{p,i}$ by a scalar factor (frequency scaling), and (2) applying a nonlinear warp function $w(\cdot)$ to $p$ before computing the angle (position warping). Both have predictable effects from a signal‑processing standpoint.

First, consider uniform scaling: replace $\theta_{p,i}$ by $\alpha \theta_{p,i}$ for some scalar $\alpha>0$. This multiplies every angular frequency by $\alpha$, shifting the entire frequency palette upward if $\alpha>1$ or downward if $\alpha<1$. Increasing $\alpha$ emphasizes higher frequencies: each sinusoid oscillates faster across positions, improving local discriminability (better resolution for small offsets) but reducing the unambiguous range of each component because periods shrink. Decreasing $\alpha$ makes components more slowly varying, increasing robustness and long‑range distinguishability but losing fine local detail. Uniform scaling thus trades off local sensitivity versus global uniqueness.

A practical implication of scaling is its impact on distinguishability across a fixed sequence length $L$. If $\alpha$ is so large that some components have periods much smaller than 1, those components will vary wildly between consecutive tokens and may be effectively random relative to model weights, making them harder to exploit. If $\alpha$ is so small that the period of a low‑frequency component exceeds the sequence length by a large factor, that component will be nearly constant across the whole sequence, conveying little positional information at all. Designers therefore choose scales that distribute periods across the relevant range of sequence lengths.

Second, consider warping of positions: compute angles as functions of a warped coordinate, e.g., $\theta_{p,i} = \sin(\omega_i w(p))$ where $w(p)$ is some monotonic function such as $\log(p+1)$, $p^\beta$, or a learned warp. Warping changes the spacing of sampling in the continuous coordinate that the sinusoids sample, which in turn redistributes effective frequencies as functions of the original index $p$. For example, a logarithmic warp $w(p)=\log(p+1)$ compresses large values of $p$ into a smaller range of angles, so for a fixed $\omega_i$ the sinusoid changes more slowly at large $p$ and more quickly at small $p$. This nonuniform sensitivity can be used to prioritize local resolution near the beginning of a sequence (where exact position matters more) and coarse resolution for far positions. Warping can therefore implement a desired nonuniformity in positional sensitivity.

However, warping has consequences for linear operations such as relative position computations. The simple trigonometric identities that let us extract $p-q$ from phase differences rely on linearity of the argument: $\sin(\omega p - \omega q) = \sin(\omega(p-q))$. If we replace $\omega p$ by $\omega w(p)$ with nonlinear $w$, we lose that property, and phase differences no longer depend cleanly on $p-q$. This can make it harder for downstream linear layers to compute relative offsets. In some cases that is acceptable—if the model is expected to learn to handle the warp—but it may reduce the efficiency with which simple linear operations can detect relative distances.

Scaling and warping also affect aliasing and periodicity. Scaling frequencies upward can push them past the effective Nyquist limit relative to the discretization, causing aliasing where different scaled frequencies produce indistinguishable samples. Warping can introduce nonuniform aliasing behavior: some regions of the sequence may be represented with higher effective sampling density (less aliasing) and other regions with more aliasing. If a warp is chosen carefully (for instance, monotonic and smooth), the model can take advantage of variable resolution; if chosen badly, it can introduce distortions that obscure distance relationships.

Finally, learned scalings and warps introduce adaptability. If a model learns a multiplicative scaling per layer, it can adjust the band of frequencies that are most useful at that layer, trading off locality and globality dynamically. Learned per‑dimension scalings can reweight different frequency channels, effectively performing spectral filtering. This is a powerful mechanism but must be regularized or parameterized so that the frequencies remain meaningful and do not collapse into degenerate regimes (all very high or all very low).

Conclusion

Thinking about positional encodings through the lens of signal processing provides clear intuitions and practical guidance. Sinusoids form a natural basis, and a bank of sinusoidal components offers a frequency decomposition of positional information. Low frequencies carry long‑range order and are robust to small shifts; high frequencies provide local sensitivity useful for fine distinctions. Sampling and aliasing analogies explain ambiguity and resolution limits for discrete token positions. Finally, scaling and warping of the angles allows one to reshape the information content across frequencies and positions, but at the cost of altering relative‑position semantics and potential aliasing effects. When designing or analyzing positional encodings—whether sinusoidal, learned, or warped—these signal‑processing principles help predict what positional distinctions a model can represent and how robust those representations will be.
