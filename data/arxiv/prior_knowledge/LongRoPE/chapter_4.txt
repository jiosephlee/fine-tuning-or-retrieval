Introduction

Large transformer language models are trained with a fixed maximum context length. Positional encodings — either absolute or relative, learned or sinusoidal — are an essential component that gives the model information about token order. When we ask a model to process sequences longer than those seen during training, the positional inputs provided to the attention layers are out‑of‑distribution: token indices beyond the training range. This mismatch can cause severe degradation in model behavior. Because re‑training a model from scratch with a longer context is expensive, a practical line of research has attacked the problem by modifying how positional information is computed or presented so that the model can accept new, larger token indices without catastrophic failure. This chapter studies those strategies in depth: why out‑of‑distribution positional values are harmful, how simple scaling rules (positional interpolation) work, how Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis suggests frequency‑specific scaling, hybrid/grouped approaches that mix rules across embedding dimensions, the conceptual difference between interpolation and extrapolation in this setting, empirical failure modes observed in practice, and when fine‑tuning is necessary versus when zero‑shot extension suffices.

# Motivation: new token indices and out‑of‑distribution positional values

At training time a transformer receives token position indices $1,2,\dots,L_{\text{train}}$ (or $0$‑based equivalents). The model learns to condition attention patterns, layer computations, and output distributions on these position inputs. If at inference we present positions $1,2,\dots,L_{\text{test}}$ with $L_{\text{test}}>L_{\text{train}}$, the position encodings for tokens beyond $L_{\text{train}}$ take values that the model never saw during parameter updates. For many positional schemes, those unseen values are not mere linear scalings but lie in geometrically different regions of the encoding space: the relative magnitudes and angular relationships among positional vectors change, and attention weights computed from dot products or distance measures change in qualitatively new ways.

To see why this matters, consider a model where positional encodings are added to token embeddings before feeding them into the self‑attention key and query projections. The attention logits between a token at position $i$ and a token at position $j$ depend on inner products like
$\langle W_Q(x_i+\text{pos}_i), W_K(x_j+\text{pos}_j)\rangle$.
If $\text{pos}_i$ or $\text{pos}_j$ moves into a region of embedding space that was never encountered during training, these inner products can grow in magnitude, change sign, or align with directions that the model has never used to route information. The result can be attention patterns that are either too diffuse, overly concentrated on particular positions, or focused on spurious anchors — producing hallucinations, incoherence, or complete loss of context.

Out‑of‑distribution (OOD) positional values cause two interacting problems. First, the local geometry of positions — how positions relate to each other via differences or relative phases — matters for tasks requiring distance awareness, such as copying, long‑range dependencies, or recurrence. Second, the global magnitude and interaction of the positional vectors with projection matrices affect neural activations throughout the network. Both problems can create catastrophic behavior: attention heads that saturate to constant values, activations that explode or vanish, or features that decouple from the token content altogether.

Because retraining is costly, researchers have investigated algorithmic fixes that modify the positional encodings at inference time so that new indices fall into a “friendly” region — ideally one that preserves the model’s learned inductive biases about relative position while avoiding the OOD problems. The rest of this chapter examines these strategies in detail.

# Linear positional interpolation (PI): downscaling position indices

The simplest and most widely used family of approaches is positional interpolation (often abbreviated PI). The core idea is to map a larger index range into the training range by scaling positions. Concretely, given an original position index $p\in\{1,\dots,L_{\text{test}}\}$ and a scale factor $s>1$, we replace $p$ with
$\tilde p = p / s$ (or equivalently multiply the positional positional encoding argument by $1/s$) so that $\tilde p \in [1/s, L_{\text{test}}/s]$. If $s = L_{\text{test}}/L_{\text{train}}$, then the maximum scaled index becomes approximately $L_{\text{train}}$ and the model never sees indices beyond the training maximum.

When positional encodings are computed by a deterministic function $\text{pos}(p)$ (for example sinusoidal encodings or rotary position embeddings), interpolation corresponds to evaluating $\text{pos}(\tilde p)$ rather than $\text{pos}(p)$. If the positional embeddings are learned vectors $\text{pos}_p$ for integers $p\le L_{\text{train}}$, then linear interpolation is implemented by resampling or interpolating the learned table so that fractional indices $\tilde p$ are mapped to vectors obtained by, e.g., linear interpolation between table entries. This is why the term "interpolation" is used: we are computing intermediate positional embeddings between those the model learned.

The intuition behind linear PI is straightforward: compress the long sequence into the coordinate system the model already knows. Relative distances are shrunk uniformly, so patterns depending only on relative ordering and proportional separation are preserved up to scale. For tasks where the model learned to rely on relative position ratios rather than absolute distances, this often works well.

Mathematically, for sinusoidal positional encodings of the form
$\mathrm{pos}(p)_{2k} = \sin(p \omega_k),\quad \mathrm{pos}(p)_{2k+1} = \cos(p \omega_k)$
with frequencies $\omega_k$, scaling the input to $p' = \alpha p$ yields
$\sin(\alpha p \omega_k)$ and $\cos(\alpha p \omega_k)$. Linear PI sets $\alpha = 1/s < 1$. This uniformly compresses the phase advance per token across all frequencies. The model sees slower phase progression in each sinusoidal channel, which corresponds to effectively using fewer “cycles” across the extended context.

Linear PI has advantages: it is simple, parameter‑free, and often produces reasonable zero‑shot generalization to moderately longer contexts. But it has limitations. Because compression is uniform across all frequency channels, high‑frequency channels that encode fine positional detail become underutilized or misaligned relative to the model’s learned expectations. Also, linear PI ensures that the scaled positions are always within the convex hull of training positions, which makes it an interpolation in the mathematical sense, but it does not necessarily preserve the absolute magnitudes that interacting learnable matrices expect. The next section explores more nuanced scaling guided by NTK analysis that addresses these frequency issues.

# NTK‑informed scaling: distributing interpolation across frequencies

Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis provides a theoretical lens into how different frequency components of positional encodings influence a transformer’s behavior, particularly in the linearized (or early‑training or wide‑network) regime. The NTK perspective examines sensitivity of the network output to perturbations in inputs across different spectral components. Applying this to positional encodings suggests that not all frequency channels should be scaled uniformly when extending context; instead, one should consider frequency‑dependent scaling to preserve how the network combines phases across channels.

For sinusoidal positional embeddings with frequencies $\{\omega_k\}$, each channel evolves across position $p$ with phase $p\omega_k$. From an NTK viewpoint, the effect of a small change in phase on model outputs depends on the product of three factors: the frequency amplitude, the projection matrices $W_Q,W_K,W_V$ interacting with the channel, and the kernel weighting that transforms these channel perturbations into output changes. High frequencies produce rapid phase changes across positions; if the model heavily relies on low‑frequency channels for long‑range interactions, simply compressing positions uniformly may underweight these channels relative to what the NTK suggests.

NTK‑informed scaling therefore prescribes a frequency‑dependent scale $\alpha_k$ applied to each channel so that the effective phase becomes $p \alpha_k \omega_k$. The choice of $\{\alpha_k\}$ can be guided by the NTK spectrum: channels with larger influence on the outputs (higher NTK eigenvalues) should be preserved more faithfully, while channels with less influence can be compressed more. One practical heuristic is to apply milder scaling to low frequencies and stronger scaling to high frequencies, reflecting that low frequencies capture global structure and should vary slowly across the extended sequence, whereas high frequencies encode fine local offsets that can be compressed without breaking larger patterns.

Formally, let the original channel compute $(\sin(p\omega_k),\cos(p\omega_k))$. After scaling we produce $(\sin(p\alpha_k\omega_k),\cos(p\alpha_k\omega_k))$. If $\alpha_k$ is made to decrease with $\omega_k$, then high frequency channels see much less phase advance per token than low frequency channels. This mitigates some of the artifacts that arise when high frequency channels go out of phase relative to the model’s learned weights. NTK calculations can quantify the sensitivity of the model output to each channel, enabling principled choices for $\alpha_k$ that preserve the dominant modes of positional influence.

A further insight from NTK is that if the model’s learned kernels emphasize certain linear combinations of sinusoidal channels, it may be beneficial to choose $\alpha_k$ so that these linear combinations retain similar geometrical relationships across positions. In practice, computing the full NTK for a large transformer is intractable, so approximate heuristics (e.g., monotone decreasing schedules for $\alpha_k$, polynomial or exponential decay across channel index) are used. Empirically, NTK‑informed scaling often outperforms uniform scaling at moderate overhead: it reduces high‑frequency noise that causes attention heads to latch on to spurious absolute positions while preserving low frequency structure that supports long‑range coherence.

# Group or hybrid strategies (e.g., dividing dims by frequency and applying different rules)

Group or hybrid strategies generalize frequency‑dependent scaling by partitioning the positional embedding dimensions (or attention subspaces) and applying different rules to each group. The motivation is that different channels — or even different attention heads — serve distinct roles: some encode coarse, slow‑varying global position signals; others encode local, high‑precision offsets. By splitting dimensions and treating them differently, a hybrid strategy can preserve coarse structure while dampening harmful high‑frequency behaviors.

One common grouping approach divides dimensions into “low‑frequency” and “high‑frequency” sets. Low‑frequency dimensions are either left unscaled or scaled weakly, ensuring that their phase progression across the extended context matches training behavior. High‑frequency dimensions are aggressively compressed (or even zeroed out) to avoid producing rapidly varying signals that the model cannot interpret beyond trained ranges. In learned positional tables, one can map high index positions to repeated (tiled) patterns of earlier positions for high‑frequency groups while keeping low‑frequency groups interpolated smoothly.

A more refined hybrid strategy uses different operations per group: for example, apply linear interpolation to one subset of dimensions, apply extrapolative linear scaling (e.g., affine shift) to another, and apply learned mapping (a small adapter) to a third. Consider splitting the embedding into blocks indexed by $b$, each with its own scaling $\alpha^{(b)}$ and perhaps an offset $\beta^{(b)}$, so that the block computes $\sin(p \alpha^{(b)} \omega_{k_b} + \beta^{(b)})$. If attention heads are known to rely on particular blocks, one can design $\alpha^{(b)}$ to preserve those dependencies.

Another hybrid idea operates at the attention‑head level. Because attention heads compute projections into key and query spaces, one can group heads into those that should see unmodified positional phases and those that should use compressed phases. Implementations that support rotary position embeddings (RoPE) can apply RoPE scaling only in a subset of head dimensions. This leverages the modularity of attention heads: heads specialized for local syntactic parsing can use high‑frequency channels kept small, while heads responsible for global discourse keep low frequencies intact.

Hybrid strategies often outperform single‑rule approaches because they balance two competing objectives: preserving the model’s long‑range inductive biases (via low‑frequency channels) and preventing pathological activation patterns from high‑frequency channels. However, they introduce hyperparameters (partition sizes, per‑group scaling schedules) and implementation complexity. Determining optimal groupings often requires empirical tuning or analysis of head roles via probing.

# Extrapolation vs interpolation: definitions and practical differences

In numerical analysis, interpolation constructs values inside the convex hull of known data points; extrapolation predicts values outside that hull. When applied to positional encodings, interpolation means constructing positional vectors for new indices as combinations (often convex or linear) of positional vectors seen during training. Extrapolation means computing vectors that follow some model of the positional function beyond the training range, possibly producing values that lie outside the range of training vectors.

Linear PI as described earlier is technically interpolation if you view the extended positions as mapping into fractional indices within the training range. For sinusoidal positional functions, evaluating $\sin(\alpha p \omega)$ at smaller effective $p$ is interpolation of phases only if $\alpha p$ stays within the training interval; but if scaled values project outside the training phase range in certain channels, the distinction blurs. By contrast, explicit extrapolation strategies might extend the learned table by fitting a parametric model to the learned positions and continuing that model outward. For learned absolute positional embeddings $\text{pos}_p$, extrapolation could mean learning or fitting a linear recurrence $\text{pos}_{p+1} \approx A \text{pos}_p + b$ and using that to generate new positions for $p>L_{\text{train}}$.

Practically, interpolation tends to be safer because it keeps new inputs in the convex combination of what the model has seen; the network’s function approximator usually behaves more smoothly within that region. Extrapolation, while enabling true out‑of‑sample behavior, risks venturing into regions of embedding space where activations and gradients are unpredictable. For sinusoidal encodings, extrapolation is actually natural: the sinusoid is a well‑defined periodic function, so evaluating it at any integer $p$ is meaningful and consistent. For learned absolute embeddings, extrapolation is ill‑posed without a model for how those embeddings evolve beyond the table.

From a performance standpoint, interpolation methods (including linear PI) frequently produce better zero‑shot generalization to modestly longer contexts because they do not surprise the network with new activation regimes. Extrapolative methods can succeed when the positional function is well‑specified and compatible with the model’s learned mechanisms (e.g., when the model has learned to use rotational or Fourier‑basis features that are naturally extrapolative). However, extrapolation may be necessary for very large extensions where compressing the sequence too much would break relative spacing information critical to the task.

Choosing between interpolation and extrapolation also depends on the positional scheme: sinusoidal/rotary embeddings are inherently extrapolatable; learned absolute embeddings are not unless a generative model of embeddings is available. Hybrid schemes sometimes attempt to interpolate some dimensions while extrapolating others to get the best of both worlds.

# Empirical failure modes: crowded positions, catastrophic values, short‑context degradation

While the preceding strategies can improve long‑context performance, empirically they exhibit several failure modes. Understanding these helps to diagnose when a method will work and when it will cause harm.

One common failure mode is crowded positions. When scaling compresses a long sequence into the training index range, many distinct positions map to nearby or identical positional vectors. If the compression is severe, the model may not have enough positional resolution to distinguish tokens that should be treated as distant in the extended context. This is especially problematic for tasks requiring precise positional offsets, such as code completion conditioned on exact line locations, or algorithms that depend on absolute indices. Crowding reduces the effective capacity to represent unique positions and can degrade performance in a way that worsens with compression factor.

Another failure mode is catastrophic values. If position scaling produces positional vectors that, when projected by model weights, create very large dot products or align with directions that trigger saturation in nonlinearities (e.g., tanh or ReLU pre‑activations becoming extremely large), the network can produce numerically unstable activations. This manifests as attention heads producing near‑one‑hot or uniform attention patterns independent of token content, token distributions collapsing or exploding, or gradients that vanish or blow up. These catastrophic values can occur if scaled phases align with eigenvectors of projection matrices or if interpolation artifacts produce large interpolation artifacts in learned tables.

Short‑context degradation is the counterintuitive phenomenon where modifying positional encodings to improve long‑context behavior harms performance on inputs that are within the original training length. For instance, if one applies a global rescaling to positional parameters at inference time, even short sequences will be seen under modified positional geometry, and the model may behave worse on tasks it previously handled well. This is particularly visible when scaling is applied to all dimensions uniformly and the model relied on specific absolute magnitudes or phase progressions during training. Hybrid strategies try to avoid this by keeping at least a subset of dimensions unchanged for short contexts, or by making scaling conditional on sequence length where possible.

Head specialization can exacerbate failure modes. Some attention heads may be robust to positional perturbations and others catastrophic; applying a one‑size‑fits‑all scaling can therefore break the latter. Additionally, models that use relative positional encodings can behave differently: if relative encodings are derived from absolute positions, scaling absolute positions alters relative distances nonlinearly; if relative encodings are implemented directly as learned functions of distance, long distances may produce values never seen during training, creating their own OOD effects.

Empirical mitigation strategies include: using milder scaling; applying group/hybrid schemes so core channels retain original behavior; clipping or normalizing projected positional vectors to avoid extreme magnitudes; and combining scaling with small parameter updates or adapters to recalibrate the model. Careful validation on both short and extended contexts is critical to avoid regressions.

# When fine‑tuning is required vs non‑fine‑tuning methods

A crucial practical consideration is whether an inference‑only modification suffices or whether some form of fine‑tuning is necessary. Non‑fine‑tuning methods, such as linear PI, RoPE rescaling, or group scaling heuristics, are appealing because they require no gradient steps and preserve the original model weights. These methods can produce good zero‑shot performance for moderate extensions of context length, particularly for models that already use sinusoidal-like or rotary positional schemes and whose tasks rely more on relative order than absolute distances.

However, non‑fine‑tuning methods have intrinsic limits. If the extended context requires the model to perform functions that depend on absolute distances larger than training values, or if the model’s downstream layers have strongly coupled expectations about positional magnitudes, simple rescaling will not suffice. Fine‑tuning is required when the positional modification creates systematic shifts in activations that the model cannot compensate for via existing weights. Two forms of fine‑tuning are commonly used:

1. Full fine‑tuning: continue training the entire model on data with longer contexts. This produces the most reliable results but is expensive.

2. Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning: train small adapter modules, low‑rank updates, or only the positional embedding parameters to adapt the model to the new positional regime. Examples include learning per‑frequency scaling coefficients, small MLPs that map old positional encodings to corrected ones, or training attention biases that correct for altered dot products.

Empirical findings show that small amounts of fine‑tuning or adapter training dramatically expand the range where context extension works well. A typical pipeline is to first apply a non‑fine‑tuning interpolation to avoid catastrophic initial behavior, then fine‑tune a small adapter on a corpus with extended contexts so the model can learn to reweight frequency channels and stabilize activations. Fine‑tuning helps resolve issues like crowded positions by letting the model learn to differentiate repeated positional codes, and it can correct catastrophic values by adjusting downstream weights to the new geometry.

When deciding whether to fine‑tune, consider these factors: the desired extension factor, the positional encoding type, the computational cost budget, and the risk of degrading short‑context performance. For small to moderate extensions (e.g., up to 2×), inference‑only PI often suffices. For large extensions (e.g., 4×–16×) or when precise positional resolution is required, at least light fine‑tuning or adapters are usually necessary to regain reliability.

Conclusion

Extending a transformer’s context window by manipulating positional encodings is a pragmatic and active area of research with clear trade‑offs. Simple linear positional interpolation offers a low‑cost, broadly applicable method that works well for modest extensions. NTK‑informed and frequency‑dependent scalings refine this by preserving the channels that matter most for model outputs, while group and hybrid strategies allow more granular control tailored to the model’s internal roles. The distinction between interpolation and extrapolation is fundamental: interpolation tends to be safer but limited in scope, whereas extrapolation can be powerful but riskier. Empirically, a variety of failure modes — crowded positions, catastrophic activations, and degradation on original short contexts — appear and must be guarded against, often via hybrid rules, clipping, or small‑scale fine‑tuning. Ultimately, the choice of strategy depends on the positional scheme used by the model, the scale of extension desired, and practical constraints around compute and risk tolerance. Understanding these methods and their mathematical underpinnings helps practitioners choose and adapt context‑extension techniques appropriately and interpret results when applying models to longer sequences than they were trained on.
