Introduction

Working with long contexts—documents, books, or other extended sequences—is central to many modern natural language processing tasks, including long‑form generation, document question answering, summarization, and retrieval‑augmented generation. This chapter surveys the datasets commonly used in long‑context research, the practical decisions required to convert raw corpora into training and evaluation data, and the evaluation protocols that yield fair and reproducible measurements. The goal is to give you a mental toolbox: how to prepare corpora so models see realistic long sequences during training, how to design validation and test sets that actually measure a model's ability to use long context, how synthetic retrieval tasks can surface retrieval behavior over many kilobytes of context, and how choices like sliding window stride and sample selection affect metrics and comparability across papers.

We begin with common long‑document corpora used in the literature and what each brings to the table. Then we move to data chunking strategies and tokenization practices that determine the effective context seen by models. We next examine how to construct validation and test splits that contain sufficiently long samples to challenge long‑context models. After that we describe synthetic "passkey" retrieval tasks and other synthetic benchmarks designed to measure retrieval capabilities across long context. We then examine sliding window stride and overlap choices and show how they influence evaluation numbers. Finally, we cover practical reproducibility habits—random seeds, deterministic sampling, and how to report your processing decisions so that others can replicate your results.

# Common long‑document corpora: PG19, The Pile (Books3, Pile‑Books), RedPajama, Proof‑pile

Empirical research on long contexts relies on corpora that contain documents longer than the 512–2048 token range typical of older transformer benchmarks. Several established corpora provide long contiguous text: PG19, The Pile (and its subsets like Books3 and Pile‑Books), RedPajama, and Proof‑pile. Each corpus has a different provenance, license, and genre distribution, so understanding their properties helps you select appropriate data for training or evaluation.

PG19 is a dataset composed of books from Project Gutenberg, curated to have long contiguous narrative passages. The documents in PG19 are generally public domain books and tend to be coherent across many pages, which is why PG19 is frequently used to study language models' ability to capture long‑range narrative coherence and entity tracking. Because PG19 comes from public domain works, it is a reliable source for large contiguous blocks of text without copyrighted concerns—this is why many papers use it for training and for testing, especially when focusing on properties like authorship style or long‑distance dependencies.

The Pile is a large, diverse, open dataset collected by EleutherAI, designed as a comprehensive training corpus for large language models. Within The Pile, there are subsections specifically consisting of books: Books3 and Pile‑Books. Books3 is a collection of contemporary books scraped from the web (subject to licensing considerations), and Pile‑Books is a curated subset containing longer book content. These book subsets provide long contiguous passages similar to PG19 but with a different distribution of modern styles and topics. Because The Pile mixes many domains (code, web text, academic writing), researchers often extract the Books portions when they need long coherent documents for modeling continuity or long‑range coreference.

RedPajama is a family of datasets intended to replicate the training material used by certain proprietary models; it is an open‑source effort to match other high quality datasets. RedPajama contains web text and other long documents and is useful for training models that should reflect diverse contemporary usage. The exact composition of RedPajama may overlap with portions of The Pile and other web sources but is packaged for ease of use when training large models.

Proof‑pile is a curated subset of documents that tend to have dense, structured, and technical content—proofs, mathematics, and long technical expositions. This corpus is particularly useful when evaluating models on long technical reasoning or when you want to stress the model's ability to maintain symbolic or logical coherence across long spans. Because technical texts often require precise preservation of earlier claims, Proof‑pile can be a demanding testbed for long‑context retention.

When choosing among these corpora, think about the genre and what aspect of long context you want to study. Narrative books emphasize discourse coherence and character tracking; technical corpora emphasize logical consistency and reference to earlier equations; web text may emphasize topical drift and frequent entity introduction. Licensing and privacy constraints also matter: use public domain sources (e.g., PG19) when you want completely safe release and evaluation, and be cautious with scraped books when publishing outputs.

# Data chunking strategies, minimum length requirements, and bookending tokens

Turning a corpus of long documents into training examples for transformer models requires choosing how to chunk the text into sequences that will be fed to the model. Because transformers typically operate on fixed‑length contexts (e.g., 2K, 8K, 32K tokens), chunking determines how much contiguous context a training example provides and how often document boundaries occur inside examples. Several common chunking strategies are used: sliding windows (with overlap), non‑overlapping fixed chunks, and variable‑length sampling that respects natural boundaries such as paragraphs or chapter markers.

A straightforward approach is fixed‑length non‑overlapping chunking: tokenize the document and split it into consecutive chunks of length $L$ tokens, where $L$ matches the model context size (or a fixed training length). This is simple and fast, but it can sever natural discourse boundaries and may produce many examples that start or end in the middle of sentences. To mitigate this, many pipelines apply sentence or paragraph boundary detection and align chunk boundaries to natural separators when possible. Aligning to boundaries helps the model see more coherent examples and reduces edge artifacts in training.

Sliding window chunking takes a fixed chunk length $L$ but generates chunks by moving a window over the tokenized document with a stride $s$ where $s<L$. If $s$ is small, you'll produce many overlapping chunks; if $s=L$ you recover the non‑overlapping case. Sliding windows are useful for both training and evaluation: they provide many contexts that include each token in different surrounding windows, which can reduce bias from arbitrary chunk boundaries. However, they increase dataset size and computational cost proportionally to $L/s$ on average. Choosing $s$ is a tradeoff between diversity of contexts and computation; we'll return to stride choice later in the chapter.

Minimum length requirements are also important. If your goal is to train or evaluate models on long‑context capability, you should enforce a minimum document length threshold when sampling examples. For example, if you want training examples that span at least 8k tokens of coherent content, you must skip documents shorter than some threshold (e.g., $8{,}192$ tokens) or only sample from multiple concatenated documents. Minimum length thresholds prevent your dataset from being dominated by short fragments that do not require long‑distance reasoning. For evaluation, minimum length thresholds are even more crucial: a test set containing mostly short passages will not reveal a model's performance degradation at long context lengths.

Bookending tokens are special tokens or markers you add to the beginning and end of chunks to indicate they came from a document boundary or to preserve structure. Typical practice is to add an explicit beginning‑of‑document token (e.g., ) and an end‑of‑document token (e.g., ) or custom tokens that mark chapter or paragraph boundaries. Bookending serves several purposes. First, it gives the model a clear signal when context resets, which is helpful when training on concatenated documents or when mixing very long and short documents. Second, bookending enables downstream evaluation that can detect whether the model implicitly learns document scopes (e.g., does it respect the end token and stop attending beyond it?). Third, markers for structural boundaries (chapter, section, paragraph) can be useful features to condition on since many long document tasks depend on hierarchical structure.

When implementing bookending, be consistent: use tokens that are part of your tokenizer's vocabulary or define special tokens and ensure they are present during both training and evaluation. Also consider whether to include bookending tokens inside sliding windows; typically you want bookend tokens to appear in windows that include a start or end of document so the model can learn their semantics. In short, chunking strategy, minimum length, and bookending interact to shape the effective learning signals models receive about long‑range structure—make these choices deliberately and record them.

# Creating validation/test splits with sufficiently long samples

A rigorous evaluation of long‑context ability requires validation and test splits that actually contain long sequences. The common pitfall is to construct a split by randomly sampling documents and then tokenizing into chunks without filtering by length; unless the corpus is dominated by long documents, this will produce many short evaluation items insensitive to context length. To avoid this, create dedicated validation and test splits where each sample meets or exceeds a specified minimum token length, and where samples are independent of the training set to prevent leakage.

One simple protocol is to reserve a fixed set of long documents for validation and test before any chunking or training data construction. For example, from a books corpus, set aside 100 books each for validation and test, selecting books whose tokenized length exceeds a threshold $L_{\min}$ (say $32{,}768$ tokens or whatever your target stress length is). Then, for each held‑out book, construct evaluation items that span the full book or large contiguous slices (e.g., sliding windows of length $L$ across the book). This preserves document coherence and ensures you can test models' abilities at many positions within a long text, including positions that require very distant context.

If holding out whole documents is infeasible (e.g., because documents are scarce), ensure that your validation/test examples are sampled from documents disjoint from the training corpus to avoid content overlap. Use deterministic sampling for these splits and record the RNG seed so you can reproduce and share the exact split. When evaluating, avoid mixing samples of wildly different lengths without reporting performance conditioned on length. Good practice is to bin evaluation results by context length (e.g., 2k, 8k, 32k, full document) and report metrics separately, so readers can see how performance changes as the context grows.

Also be mindful about metric leakage from tokenization or boundary effects: if you evaluate next‑token prediction across a held‑out slice that begins in the middle of a sentence, that seed may bias perplexity estimates. To reduce this bias, align evaluation starts to sentence or paragraph boundaries when possible, or at least report whether starts are aligned. Finally, consider multiple evaluation tasks per split: next‑token prediction (perplexity), question answering requiring retrieval from earlier parts of the document, and long‑range coherence metrics. This multipronged approach gives a more complete picture of long‑context competence.

# Passkey retrieval and synthetic tasks for measuring retrieval over long context

Real‑world retrieval behavior—finding a small piece of information buried deep in a long document—can be hard to measure robustly because gold‑standard retrieval annotations are scarce. Synthetic "passkey" retrieval tasks provide a controlled way to evaluate whether a model can locate and use information far from the query position. The basic idea is simple: embed a unique secret string (a passkey) into a specific document position and ask the model to retrieve it when prompted, with many distractor tokens between the passkey and the prompt. Because you control where passkeys are placed, you can vary retrieval distance, density of distractors, and the number of passkeys per document, and measure retrieval accuracy as a function of distance.

Constructing a passkey task typically involves these steps. First, choose a set of documents reserved for evaluation. Second, generate or select unique passkeys—random strings with low prior probability under the model so accidental guessing is unlikely. Third, insert a passkey at a specified token offset within each document. Fourth, design retrieval prompts that occur only after the passkey (e.g., "What is the secret code that appeared earlier in this document?"). Finally, evaluate whether the model outputs the passkey exactly when given the long context that includes the passkey. Because the passkey is unique and unpredictable, a single exact match constitutes a correct retrieval.

Passkey tasks are powerful because they isolate retrieval as a capability separate from language modeling fluency. They can be used to measure the effective "decay" of retrievable signals: for instance, if retrieval accuracy drops with distance, that indicates the model's cross‑attention or memory for early tokens diminishes. You can also use passkey density experiments—placing multiple passkeys into a document—to study whether retrieval probability depends on the number of candidates or their relative recency.

Complementary synthetic tasks include "question insertion" where factual statements (e.g., "The capital of Nulonia is Zort") are placed early and then asked about later, or synthetic reference chains that require chaining information across multiple distant anchors. Another useful synthetic setup is the "needle in a haystack" test: plant a small fact in one of many documents concatenated into a single context and test whether the model retrieves the correct document and fact. These synthetic designs let you control variables cleanly and attribute performance differences to distance, noise, or model architecture rather than dataset idiosyncrasies.

When reporting results from synthetic tasks, always disclose how passkeys were generated, whether they were deterministic (allowing replication), and whether the model or tokenizer might have seen similar substrings during pretraining. Random seeds and the held‑out document identifiers are essential for reproducibility. Synthetic tasks should not replace natural tasks but rather complement them: use passkeys to diagnose capacity and decay curves, and use real QA or summarization tasks to measure practical utility.

# Sliding window stride, overlap choice and its effect on metrics

Sliding window stride and overlap determine how many contexts cover each token during training and evaluation. They influence the effective sample size, the computational budget, and—perhaps most importantly—evaluation metrics. Understanding how stride interacts with metrics such as perplexity, exact match, and recall is essential for fair comparisons across models and papers.

Mathematically, if windows have length $L$ and stride $s$, then the number of windows covering a document segment of length $D$ is approximately $1 + \lfloor (D - L)/s \rfloor$ for $D\ge L$. Overlap fraction is $1 - s/L$. High overlap (small $s$) means each token appears in many windows, so your evaluation metric may overcount or overweight certain tokens if you average naively across windows. For example, computing mean token loss across all windows will count overlapping tokens multiple times, biasing the overall loss towards positions more likely to be in the central region of windows.

Two common evaluation aggregation strategies are "per‑token aggregation" and "per‑window aggregation." In per‑token aggregation, you compute a loss for each token based on the single window that contains that token and then average once across tokens, ensuring each token contributes equally to the metric. This approach requires tracking which window's prediction is used for each token (e.g., choose the window where the token is in the center to maximize context) or averaging logits per token across windows and then computing loss. In per‑window aggregation, you compute loss separately for each window and average across windows; this is simpler but yields metrics that overweight overlapped tokens. When comparing models, be explicit about which aggregation you used.

Overlap also affects evaluation of retrieval and exact match tasks. Consider a QA evaluation where a question and long document are broken into windows: if a correct answer spans a boundary between windows, high overlap increases the chance at least one window contains the whole answer, improving recall. Conversely, with no overlap, you may get artificially lower recall simply because of boundary misalignment. For passkey retrieval tests, overlap can make retrieval easier if windows are constructed so that a window always includes the passkey and the prompt together; this artificially inflates performance. To avoid this, ensure that test windows reflect the real operational setting (e.g., a streaming input where prompts arrive at arbitrary offsets) and use consistent alignment rules.

During training, overlap can be beneficial because it exposes tokens in varied contexts, potentially improving generalization. However, it also multiplies the effective sample count and can create data redundancy. Many training pipelines choose a moderate overlap (e.g., $25\%$–$50\%$) to gain contextual diversity without massive compute blowup. For evaluation intended to measure per‑token predictive ability, prefer per‑token aggregation and minimal or no overlap unless your evaluation specifically targets overlapping window setups.

Finally, sliding windows interact with model architectures and inference strategies. Sparse attention or retrieval‑augmented models that only index non‑overlapping chunks may behave differently from dense attention models evaluated with heavy overlap. Therefore, when comparing architectures, evaluate them under identical chunking and stride choices, or better yet report results under multiple stride/overlap regimes to demonstrate robustness.

# Reproducibility: random seeds, sample selection, and reporting

Reproducibility is a cornerstone of scientific progress. In long‑context work, many seemingly small choices—random seeds for sampling documents, which exact documents were held aside for test sets, whether boundary tokens were used—can materially affect reported results. To make your experiments reproducible and that others can fairly compare to them, follow explicit practices for seeding, deterministic preprocessing, and reporting.

First, set and record all random seeds for sampling and data shuffling. This includes the RNG for choosing held‑out documents, selecting passkey strings, and any stochastic augmentation. If your preprocessing uses multiple languages or tokenizers that may handle unknown characters non‑deterministically across versions, note tokenizer versions and exact tokenization settings. Make seeds accessible: provide a mapping from seed to sample indices or a script that deterministically reconstructs the sample set.

Second, when constructing training and evaluation splits, publish the exact identifiers (e.g., file paths or document IDs) of held‑out documents and any code used to generate chunk boundaries. If storage size prohibits sharing the full corpora, share a compact manifest that records offsets and lengths for each sample so others with access to the raw corpus can reconstruct your splits.

Third, report the full preprocessing pipeline: tokenizer type and version, special tokens used (bookends, paragraph markers), minimal length thresholds, overlap/stride choices, whether you align to sentence boundaries, and any filtering steps (e.g., removing non‑UTF8 content, normalizing whitespace). For passkey or synthetic tasks, publish the generator script, passkey list (or seed), and insertion offsets. These details are often the difference between replicable and irreproducible results.

Fourth, document evaluation aggregation choices: per‑token vs per‑window averaging, how you handle overlapping predictions, and how metrics were computed and bootstrapped. If you use segmentation or binning by length, define the bins explicitly (e.g., 0–2k, 2k–8k, 8k–32k tokens) and report the number of samples per bin.

Finally, provide either the evaluation code or a dockerized environment or a Colab notebook that demonstrates the full pipeline from raw documents to final metrics. If you cannot release data, share synthetic or distilled datasets that reproduce the statistical properties of your splits so others can still validate methodological claims.

Concluding remarks

Dealing with long contexts requires careful dataset selection, deliberate preprocessing choices, and transparent evaluation protocols. The corpora discussed—PG19, The Pile (Books3, Pile‑Books), RedPajama, and Proof‑pile—offer different genres and challenges; choose based on the phenomena you want to study. Chunking strategies, minimum length thresholds, and bookending tokens shape the signals models learn; sliding window overlap and stride choices affect both training diversity and evaluation bias. Synthetic tasks like passkey retrieval provide controlled probes of long‑range retrieval, and reproducibility hinges on recording seeds, sample selections, and exact preprocessing steps.

When designing experiments, prioritize evaluations that directly reflect the use case you target (e.g., retrieval QA, long‑form coherence), and report metrics stratified by context length. By following the practices in this chapter, you will obtain meaningful, comparable, and reproducible measurements of long‑context capabilities.
