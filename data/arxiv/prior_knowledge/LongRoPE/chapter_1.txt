Introduction

The Transformer architecture, introduced in "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017), is the foundational building block for modern large language models (LLMs). At its core is the mechanism of attention: a way for a model to compute context-dependent combinations of token representations. This chapter explains the mathematical and architectural primitives of Transformers, develops consistent notation for queries, keys, values and attention weights, and details the forward pass used throughout the literature. We cover encoder/decoder and decoder-only variants, the multi-head self-attention mechanism, the canonical attention computation, positional dependence and ways to inject position information, common architectural scaffolding such as residual connections, layer normalization and feed-forward blocks, computational and memory considerations, and typical modifications used in LLMs such as causal attention and rotary positional embeddings. The goal is to give you both the formulae and the intuition necessary to read and understand research papers about Transformers and LLMs.

# Overview of encoder/decoder and decoder-only Transformer variants

The original Transformer paper introduced an encoder-decoder architecture tailored to sequence-to-sequence tasks (for example, machine translation). The encoder maps an input sequence to a sequence of continuous representations. The decoder generates an output sequence, conditioning on the encoder outputs and previously generated tokens. In contrast, many LLMs are built using decoder-only Transformers that perform autoregressive modeling: they predict the next token given previous tokens and do not explicitly have a separate encoder component.

More concretely, let an input token sequence be represented as tokens indexed by positions $1,\dots,n$. In the encoder-decoder variant, the encoder takes a sequence of token embeddings $X = [x_1,\dots,x_n]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ and transforms it by repeated attention and feed-forward layers into a new sequence of representations $E = [e_1,\dots,e_n]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$. The decoder then autoregressively produces outputs: at decoder layer timestep $t$ it attends to encoder outputs $E$ (cross-attention) and to previously produced decoder states (self-attention). Cross-attention lets the decoder query the encoder representations so that the decoder's token at position $t$ can incorporate information from the entire source sequence.

Decoder-only models collapse the architecture: a single stack of Transformer blocks operates on the same sequence for both context and prediction. At training time the model is typically given full sequences and learns to predict the next token for each prefix (teacher forcing), while at generation time the model produces tokens one at a time, conditioning on all previously generated tokens. Because decoder-only models do not use encoder-decoder cross-attention, each self-attention layer in the stack must both integrate context and maintain autoregressive behavior (see causal attention below).

The design choice between encoder-decoder and decoder-only affects several practical matters. Encoder-decoder models are naturally suited for conditional generation problems (input-to-output mapping) and can be more sample-efficient for tasks that require conditioning on long or external inputs. Decoder-only models are simpler, conceptually unified, and have proven extremely effective as large pretrained models for a wide range of downstream tasks using prompting and fine-tuning.

# Multi-head self-attention: queries, keys, values, and heads

Self-attention is the mechanism that produces context-dependent representations by computing weighted sums of "value" vectors, where the weights express how relevant each context position is to a given query position. To express this, we introduce consistent notation.

Given an input sequence of token representations at one layer, denote the sequence as a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ where $n$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the model (hidden) dimension. For self-attention, we produce three linear projections of $X$:
- Queries $Q = XW^Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$,
- Keys $K = XW^K \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$,
- Values $V = XW^V \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}$,

where $W^Q, W^K \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$ and $W^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}$ are learned weight matrices. In many designs $d_k = d_v = d_h$ and $d$ is partitioned across multiple heads. The intuition is that for each position $i$, the query vector $q_i$ represents what that position is "looking for" from other positions; each key vector $k_j$ represents what information position $j$ "offers"; and each value vector $v_j$ is the content that will be combined according to attention weights.

Multi-head attention extends this by running several attention computations in parallel, each with its own set of projection matrices. Suppose there are $H$ heads. For head $h$ we have projection matrices $W_h^Q, W_h^K, W_h^V$ producing $Q_h, K_h, V_h$ with dimension $d_k = d/H$ typically. Each head computes attention independently, producing an output matrix of size $n\times d_k$. The outputs of all heads are concatenated along the feature dimension to form a matrix of size $n\times d$, which is then linearly projected by $W^O\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ back into the model dimension. Mathematically, if AttentionHead denotes the attention operation for a single head (described precisely in the next section), then multi-head attention is

$${\rm MultiHead}(X) = {\rm Concat}({\rm AttentionHead}_1,\dots,{\rm AttentionHead}_H)\,W^O.$$

The reason for multiple heads is both statistical and representational: different heads can learn to focus on different kinds of relationships (local syntactic relations, long-range coreference, positional patterns, etc.), and parallel heads increase the representational capacity without increasing the size of each attention computation proportionally. Intuitively, each head is a distinct subspace in which the model can learn specialized attention patterns.

# Attention computation: softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) V

The core computation of scaled dot-product attention is compact to state and rich in meaning. For a single head with query matrix $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$, key matrix $K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$, and value matrix $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}$, the attention output is

$$A(Q,K,V) = {\rm softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.$$

This expression is evaluated row-wise: for each query position $i$ we compute similarity scores with all keys $j$ by the dot product $q_i\cdot k_j$. Form the score matrix $S = QK^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ where $S_{ij} = q_i^\top k_j$. The entries $S_{ij}$ quantify how compatible or relevant position $j$ is to the needs of position $i$. The scaling factor $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ is important: without it, dot products grow with $d_k$ in magnitude, pushing the softmax into regions with extremely small gradients; scaling stabilizes gradients and learning dynamics.

Next, apply row-wise softmax to convert raw scores into attention weights:

$$\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(S_{ij}/\sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{j'=1}^n \exp(S_{ij'}/\sqrt{d_k})}.$$

These $\alpha_{ij}$ form a probability distribution over source positions $j$ for each query position $i$. Finally, the output for query position $i$ is the weighted sum of value vectors:

$$o_i = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{ij} v_j.$$

In matrix form the outputs for all positions are $O = \Alpha V$ where $\Alpha = {\rm softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})$. Intuitively, attention routes information: a query fetches value vectors from positions it deems relevant, combining them to produce a context-aware representation at that query position.

Several practical notes: the softmax uses row-wise normalization because each query attends over keys; different normalizations would yield different behaviors. The value vectors $V$ carry the information content, whereas keys and queries control how that content is mixed. During backpropagation the gradients flow not only into the value vectors but also into the parameters that produced $Q$ and $K$, allowing the model to learn what to look for and what to present.

# Positional dependence in attention and why positional information is required

The attention operation described so far is permutation equivariant: if you apply the same permutation to the rows of $X$, then $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are permuted in the same way, and the attention output is permuted accordingly. This implies that a Transformer with only attention (and token-wise feed-forward networks) has no inherent notion of token order. For language and most sequence tasks, order matters: "dog bites man" is not the same as "man bites dog". Therefore, Transformers need some way to inject positional information into the token representations so that the model can reason about sequence order and relative position.

Several approaches exist to provide position information:

1. Absolute positional encodings. In the original Transformer, fixed sinusoidal encodings were added to token embeddings. At position $p$ and embedding dimension index $i$, the encoding is
   $$\text{PE}(p,2i) = \sin\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right),\qquad \text{PE}(p,2i+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right).$$
   These continuous functions allow the model to access absolute positions and to generalize to longer sequences than seen during training to some degree, due to their extrapolative properties.

2. Learned absolute positional embeddings. Instead of fixed sinusoids, a learned lookup table of embeddings $P = [p_1,\dots,p_n]$ is added to token embeddings: $X \leftarrow X + P$. The model learns a direct mapping from position indices to vectors.

3. Relative positional encodings. Instead of encoding absolute positions, some methods encode relative distances between query and key positions into the attention computation. This often modifies the score matrix $S$ by adding functions of $(i-j)$ so that the attention weight depends explicitly on relative separation. Relative encodings can be preferable when the task depends on relative positions and when invariance to input shifts is desirable.

4. Operator-based methods such as rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) or ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases). RoPE rotates the query and key vectors in a position-dependent way so that the dot-product naturally includes positional phase differences. ALiBi adds a fixed, monotonically increasing bias to the attention logits that depends on distance, encouraging attention to prefer nearby tokens.

Why positional information is required: Without positional signals, the model cannot distinguish tokens that are identical in content but in different positions or cannot learn ordering-dependent phenomena such as syntactic structure or temporal dependencies. Position encodings break permutation symmetry, allowing the model to represent ordered sequences and to learn to use order in attention. The choice between absolute, learned, relative, or operator-based encodings changes the inductive bias of the model and can have significant empirical effects, especially for generalization to different sequence lengths and for long-range dependency modeling.

# Residual connections, layer normalization, and feed-forward blocks

Transformers are built not only from attention modules but also from architectural scaffolding that stabilizes optimization and increases representational power. Two of the most important components are residual (skip) connections and layer normalization (LayerNorm), accompanied by position-wise feed-forward networks (FFNs).

Residual connections: Each sublayer (for instance, multi-head attention or the feed-forward block) is wrapped with a residual connection: the sublayer's input is added to its output. If $x$ is the input to a sublayer with function ${\cal F}(x)$, the residual output is $x + {\cal F}(x)$. Residual connections make it easier to train deep networks by providing identity paths for gradient flow, mitigating vanishing gradients, and allowing the network to learn perturbations around the identity mapping. They also encourage the model to preserve information from earlier layers unless modifications are needed.

Layer normalization: LayerNorm normalizes activations across the feature dimension for each token individually. Given an input vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$ for a token, LayerNorm computes
$$\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma \odot \hat{x} + \beta,$$
where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the mean and variance of the components of $x$, and $\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are learned scale and shift parameters. LayerNorm stabilizes training by reducing internal covariate shift across features and allowing larger learning rates and deeper stacks. In Transformers LayerNorm is usually applied either before the sublayer (pre-norm) or after it (post-norm). Pre-norm variants (LayerNorm before attention/FFN) have been found to stabilize training of very deep Transformers and are commonly used in modern LLMs.

Feed-forward blocks: Between attention layers, each position independently passes through a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN) that applies two linear layers with a nonlinearity in between. For a token representation $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$, a common FFN form is

$${\rm FFN}(x) = W_2\,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2,$$

where $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{ff}\times d}$, $W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{ff}}$, $d_{ff}$ is typically larger than $d$ (often 2–4 times $d$), and $\phi$ is a nonlinearity such as ReLU, GELU or others. The FFN enriches per-position computation and introduces nonlinearity and cross-feature mixing that attention alone (which is linear in $V$ for fixed attention weights) cannot produce. Because FFNs are applied identically to each position (no information mixing across positions within the FFN), all cross-token interactions are mediated by attention layers; FFNs expand representational capacity inside each position’s vector.

Typical Transformer block structure with pre-norm is:

1. $x_{1} = x + {\rm MultiHead}({\rm LayerNorm}(x))$,
2. $x_{2} = x_{1} + {\rm FFN}({\rm LayerNorm}(x_{1}))$.

Alternate ordering (post-norm) places LayerNorm after the residual addition, but pre-norm has become more common in deep LLMs because it eases optimization.

# Computational complexity (O(n^2) attention) and memory considerations

A central computational characteristic of self-attention is its quadratic dependence on sequence length $n$ in both time and memory (for dense full attention). Constructing the pairwise score matrix $S = QK^\top$ costs $O(n^2 d_k)$ operations, and storing $S$ requires $O(n^2)$ memory. After applying softmax we multiply by $V$, which is another $O(n^2 d_v)$ operation. Therefore, for a model with hidden dimension $d$ and a sequence length $n$, the per-layer time complexity is roughly $O(n^2 d)$ (ignoring constants and the number of heads), and space complexity dominated by attention matrices is $O(n^2)$.

This quadratic scaling imposes practical limits on sequence length. For typical LLMs with $n$ in the thousands, the memory and compute required for full attention become the dominant cost. Several consequences and workarounds have emerged:

- Hardware implications: GPUs/TPUs have finite on-device memory. During training, intermediate activations (such as $QK^\top$ and the softmax outputs) must be stored for backpropagation unless recomputed, which multiplies memory pressure. Large-batch training and long sequences exacerbate the problem.

- Efficient implementations: Libraries implement memory-efficient kernels that fuse operations (compute $QK^\top$ and softmax and multiply by $V$ in a single fused kernel) to reduce temporary storage and memory traffic. Mixed precision (FP16 or bfloat16) is also used to reduce memory footprint.

- Approximate or sparse attention: Many methods approximate full attention to achieve sub-quadratic scaling. Sparse attention restricts the set of keys each query attends to (local windows, strided patterns, or learnable sparsity). Low-rank approximations attempt to factorize the attention matrix into smaller components. Kernel-based linear attention methods rewrite softmax attention to an algebraic form that permits associative reorderings and linear-time computation. These approaches trade expressivity or exactness for computational tractability and are active research areas.

- Memory/computation trade-offs: Sequence chunking, gradient checkpointing, reversible layers, activation recomputation, and model parallelism are practical techniques to manage memory. For generation, caching key and value matrices for previous tokens avoids recomputing $K$ and $V$ for tokens already processed; this reduces per-step cost when generating autoregressively but still requires storing $K$ and $V$ for the entire past, so memory scales as $O(n)$ per head.

Understanding the quadratic scaling is crucial when interpreting claims about model capabilities and when designing models for long-context tasks. Many recent architectural and algorithmic innovations are driven by the need to handle longer sequences in a computationally feasible manner.

# Common modifications used in LLMs (causal attention, rotary attention hooks)

Large language models incorporate several practical and theoretical modifications to the canonical attention mechanism to support autoregressive generation, longer contexts, and improved generalization. Two important modifications commonly seen in LLMs are causal (masked) attention and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). We will also touch on other typical tweaks.

Causal (masked) attention: Autoregressive generation requires that a token at position $i$ should not attend to tokens at positions $j>i$ (future tokens), otherwise the model could "cheat" by seeing targets during training or generation. Causal attention enforces this by masking out upper-triangular entries of the attention score matrix before the softmax. Concretely, let $M$ be an $n\times n$ mask where $M_{ij}=0$ if $j\le i$ (allowed) and $M_{ij}=-\infty$ if $j>i$ (disallowed). The attention computation becomes

$$\Alpha = {\rm softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top + M}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right).$$

In practice $M$ may be implemented by adding a large negative number to forbidden logits. During autoregressive decoding, incremental caching is used: at generation step $t$, the model computes the new keys and values for the newly generated token and concatenates them to the cached $K$ and $V$ so that the next step's attention can query the entire past without reprocessing it. This caching reduces compute per generated token from $O(t)$ per layer back to $O(1)$ for $QK^\top$ computation with a small cost to multiply $Q$ by the growing $V$ cache, and storage grows linearly with generated length.

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) and related "attention hooks": RoPE injects positional information directly into the dot products used for attention by rotating components of $Q$ and $K$ as a function of position. The central observation is that a rotation matrix applied to the embeddings can encode relative phase differences which are preserved in dot products. Formally, if $R_p$ is a block-diagonal rotation matrix parameterized by position $p$, then RoPE replaces $q_i \leftarrow R_i q_i$ and $k_j \leftarrow R_j k_j$ before computing $q_i^\top k_j$. Because rotations are orthogonal, the dot product becomes $q_i^\top R_i^\top R_j k_j$, which depends only on the relative position $(j-i)$ through $R_i^\top R_j$. This property gives RoPE a clean way to encode relative position while preserving certain algebraic properties desirable for extrapolation beyond training length. RoPE is implemented efficiently by splitting vectors into pairs and applying 2D rotations parameterized by sinusoidal frequencies, similar in spirit to the sinusoidal positional encoding but applied multiplicatively in the query/key space.

Other common modifications include:

- ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases): Instead of explicit embeddings, ALiBi adds a bias term to attention logits that is linear in distance, favoring nearer context positions. This is simple and has been found to help long-range extrapolation without learned positional embeddings.

- Biases or learned terms in attention: Some LLMs add small learned biases or global tokens to attention patterns to nudge certain behaviors (e.g., prompt tokens that always attend globally).

- Sparse or block-sparse attention patterns: To handle very long sequences, models use windows, strided patterns, or hierarchical attention to keep complexity manageable while preserving critical long-range connectivity.

- Normalization and gating variants: Some models experiment with RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, SwiGLU or other FFN activations, and gating mechanisms to improve optimization or parameter efficiency.

- Pretraining-specific tweaks: Large models often use pre-normalization, custom initialization, Adam variants with weight decay, and careful learning-rate schedules tuned empirically for stability and scaling.

Understanding these modifications is important because papers often describe their innovations against a baseline Transformer using these practical defaults (causal masking for autoregressive models, RoPE or ALiBi for positional information, pre-norm LayerNorm, etc.). When reading literature, recognize which of these choices are orthogonal to the central claim and which materially affect model behavior for the tasks at hand.

# Putting it all together: the Transformer forward pass

To close, we recap a single Transformer layer's forward pass in equations using the notation established earlier. Let $X^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ be the input embeddings to the layer (after token embeddings plus positional information). A standard pre-norm Transformer block computes:

1. Multi-head attention:
   For each head $h=1,\dots,H$ compute
   $$Q_h = X^{(0)} W_h^Q,\qquad K_h = X^{(0)} W_h^K,\qquad V_h = X^{(0)} W_h^V.$$
   Compute attention outputs for each head,
   $$O_h = {\rm softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_h K_h^\top + M}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V_h,$$
   where $M$ is an optional mask (e.g., causal mask). Concatenate heads:
   $$O = {\rm Concat}(O_1,\dots,O_H) W^O.$$

2. Residual and layer norm around attention:
   $$X^{(1)} = X^{(0)} + O,$$
   with LayerNorm applied either before the attention (pre-norm) or after this addition depending on the chosen variant.

3. Feed-forward:
   $$F = W_2 \phi(W_1 {\rm LayerNorm}(X^{(1)}) + b_1) + b_2.$$

4. Residual and next normalization:
   $$X^{(2)} = X^{(1)} + F.$$

The output $X^{(2)}$ is fed into the next layer or, after the final layer, into a task-specific head such as a linear projection and softmax for language modeling. During autoregressive generation, $M$ enforces causality and $K$ and $V$ from previous steps are cached to avoid recomputing them.

This sequence of attention + residual + layer norm + FFN + residual is repeated many times (tens to hundreds of layers) in modern LLMs. The multi-head attention provides the cross-token mixing needed for context understanding, FFNs provide per-token processing, residual and normalization keep the network trainable at scale, and positional encodings break permutation symmetry so that the model can reason about order.

Conclusion

Transformers and self-attention are powerful because they provide a simple, flexible mechanism to compute context-dependent representations across sequences. The core mathematical objects—queries, keys, values, attention logits, and softmax-normalized weights—explain both what the model does and why it can capture diverse dependencies. Practical LLMs build on these primitives with positional encodings, masking for autoregression, normalization, and architectural and algorithmic variants to scale to long sequences and many parameters. With the notation and intuition presented in this chapter, you should be prepared to read and critically evaluate Transformer-based research, understand implementation and scaling choices, and follow innovations that modify attention, positional encodings, or architectural scaffolding.
