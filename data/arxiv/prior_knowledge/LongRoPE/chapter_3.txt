Introduction

Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) are a simple, elegant, and highly practical way to inject absolute position information into transformer models. Unlike learned position embeddings that add a learned vector to token representations, RoPE multiplies token representations by position-dependent rotations. This multiplicative, rotational approach has several advantages: it preserves inner-product geometry in a way that makes attention scores sensitive to relative position, it is compatible with existing attention mechanisms with minimal change, and it exhibits useful extrapolation behavior to positions beyond those seen during training.

This chapter develops RoPE from first principles, shows how to parameterize the rotary angles, explains how to compute the required cosine and sine pairs efficiently, demonstrates how RoPE integrates with the query/key inner product in attention, interprets the construction in terms of frequencies, discusses efficient implementation choices including memory layout and interleaving of cosines and sines, and finally analyzes the effect of position scaling and the implications for extrapolating to new sequence lengths. Throughout, mathematical notation is used to make the construction precise while the text emphasizes intuition and practical consequences.

# Derivation of RoPE as complex (2D) rotations on sub‑vectors

The core idea of RoPE is to treat each token representation as composed of a sequence of 2-dimensional sub-vectors and to apply a rotation in each 2D plane whose angle depends linearly on the position index. To see this, start with a $d$-dimensional token embedding $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$. For simplicity assume $d$ is even; if not, one can pad by one dimension or treat the last dimension separately. Partition $x$ into $d/2$ pairs $(x_{2j-1}, x_{2j})$ for $j=1,\dots,d/2$. Each pair can be interpreted as the real and imaginary parts of a complex number:
\[
z_j = x_{2j-1} + i\,x_{2j},
\]
where $i$ denotes the imaginary unit. A rotation in the 2D plane corresponding to coordinate pair $j$ is then a multiplication by a complex phase $e^{i\phi_j}$:
\[
z_j' = e^{i\phi_j} z_j = (\cos\phi_j + i\sin\phi_j)(x_{2j-1} + i\,x_{2j}).
\]
In real terms this rotation is the $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix
\[
R(\phi_j) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi_j & -\sin\phi_j \\ \sin\phi_j & \cos\phi_j \end{pmatrix}
\]
acting on $\begin{pmatrix} x_{2j-1} \\ x_{2j} \end{pmatrix}$. Concatenating these rotations across all sub-vectors yields a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $R(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_{d/2}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that rotates each 2D plane by its corresponding angle.

Rotary embeddings choose the rotation angles $\phi_j$ to be proportional to the token position $n$; that is, $\phi_j = n\theta_j$ where $\theta_j$ is a per-dimension base angle. Applying the rotation for position $n$ to the token representation $x$ produces a position-dependent representation
\[
x^{(n)} = R(n\theta_1,\dots,n\theta_{d/2})\,x.
\]
Equivalently in complex notation,
\[
z_j^{(n)} = e^{i n \theta_j} z_j.
\]
Because rotations are orthogonal linear transforms, they preserve vector norms and inner products within each 2D subspace. This property underlies several key invariances of RoPE when used inside attention: notably, inner products between rotated vectors incorporate relative phase differences that depend on position differences, producing a simple dependence on relative position.

# Rotary angle parameterization: theta_i and base values (e.g., 10000)

The choice of base angles $\{\theta_j\}$ — sometimes denoted $\theta_{2j}$ or $\omega_j$ in different sources — determines how quickly the rotation angle grows across the $d/2$ subspaces. A common parameterization borrows the geometric progression used in the sinusoidal positional encodings of Vaswani et al. (2017): set
\[
\theta_j = \frac{1}{\text{base}^{2j/d}},
\]
or equivalently in terms of an angular increment,
\[
\theta_j = \frac{1}{\text{base}^{(2j-2)/d}} \quad\text{or}\quad \theta_j = \frac{1}{\text{base}^{(2j)/d}},
\]
depending on whether indexing begins at $0$ or $1$ and on an implementation's interleaving. The canonical choice of the hyperparameter "base" is $10000$. To convert these inverse-length values into actual angles per position, some presentations multiply by $2\pi$, many do not, and different conventions exist. The essential property is that the sequence $\{\theta_j\}$ should span a range of frequencies: some dimensions vary slowly with position (small $\theta_j$), capturing long-range position information, while others vary quickly (large $\theta_j$), capturing fine-grained position differences. Using a geometric progression of wavelengths ensures a roughly logarithmic coverage of frequency space analogous to using multiple sinusoids at different scales.

A common concise formulation used in code is to define, for $k=0,\dots,d/2-1$,
\[
\theta_k = \frac{1}{\text{base}^{2k/d}},
\]
and then set the phase for position $n$ as $n\theta_k$. The choice of base controls the density of frequencies: larger base values push frequencies towards zero (slower), and smaller base values create more rapid oscillations. In practice, the base of $10000$ works well and mirrors the success of sinusoidal encodings; it provides a mixture of high and low frequency components that together encode positions across different scales.

# Mapping from position index n to cos(n theta_i), sin(n theta_i) pairs

To apply the rotation for a position $n$, RoPE needs the cosine and sine of the phase $n\theta_k$ for each subspace $k$. Given the angle parameterization $\theta_k$, the pair
\[
\bigl(\cos(n\theta_k),\,\sin(n\theta_k)\bigr)
\]
fully specifies the $2\times 2$ rotation matrix $R(n\theta_k)$ for that subspace. Concretely, if the original sub-vector is $\begin{pmatrix} x_{2k+1} \\ x_{2k+2} \end{pmatrix}$, then after rotation we have
\[
\begin{pmatrix} x_{2k+1}^{(n)} \\ x_{2k+2}^{(n)} \end{pmatrix}
= 
\begin{pmatrix} \cos(n\theta_k) & -\sin(n\theta_k) \\ \sin(n\theta_k) & \cos(n\theta_k) \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x_{2k+1} \\ x_{2k+2} \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because the rotation angle is linear in $n$, these cosine and sine pairs can be computed efficiently for a whole range of positions using recurrence relations or vectorized trigonometric functions. A common trick uses the complex recurrence: if we maintain the complex phase $e^{i n\theta_k}$ for each $n$ and $k$, then $e^{i(n+1)\theta_k} = e^{i\theta_k} e^{i n\theta_k}$, allowing iterative multiplication by a constant complex number $e^{i\theta_k} = \cos\theta_k + i\sin\theta_k$. In floating-point code this can be done with complex arithmetic or with two real multiplications per subspace per step using the recurrence
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_{n+1,k} &= c_{n,k}\cos\theta_k - s_{n,k}\sin\theta_k,\\
s_{n+1,k} &= c_{n,k}\sin\theta_k + s_{n,k}\cos\theta_k,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $(c_{n,k},s_{n,k}) = (\cos(n\theta_k),\sin(n\theta_k))$. For batched GPU computation, however, it is often more efficient to compute $(\cos(n\theta_k),\sin(n\theta_k))$ directly with vectorized calls to library trig functions for all $n$ and $k$ at once, relying on hardware-accelerated implementations.

Because the same cosine and sine pairs are used to rotate both queries and keys (see next section), RoPE typically precomputes a table of $(\cos(n\theta_k),\sin(n\theta_k))$ for positions $n=0,\dots,N-1$ up to the training sequence length $N$ and for all $k$ in the model dimension. This table is then applied by elementwise multiplication and addition following the rotation formula.

# How RoPE integrates with Q/K (multiplicative application) and invariances

The standard scaled dot-product attention computes scores from inner products of queries and keys. If queries $q$ and keys $k$ are position-aware via rotations, let $q^{(n)} = R(n\theta) q$ and $k^{(m)} = R(m\theta) k$ be the rotated vectors for query at position $n$ and key at position $m$. Because rotations are orthogonal and block-wise across subspaces, the inner product decomposes into contributions from each 2D plane. Using complex notation, with $z_j^{(n)} = e^{i n\theta_j} z_j$ for the $j$th complex coordinate of $q$ and $w_j^{(m)} = e^{i m\theta_j} w_j$ for keys, we have
\[
\langle q^{(n)}, k^{(m)} \rangle
= \sum_j \Re\left( \overline{z_j^{(n)}}\, w_j^{(m)} \right)
= \sum_j \Re\left( e^{i(m-n)\theta_j}\, \overline{z_j}\, w_j \right).
\]
Here $\overline{z}$ denotes complex conjugation. Crucially, the dependence on positions $n$ and $m$ appears only through the phase factor $e^{i(m-n)\theta_j}$, that is, through the relative position difference $m-n$ rather than their absolute values. This produces a natural shift-invariance property: if both $q$ and $k$ are shifted by the same amount in position, their inner product remains unchanged because $m-n$ is unchanged. Consequently, RoPE induces position-dependent attention that depends primarily on relative positions.

This multiplicative integration is minimal and compatible with existing transformer pipelines: instead of adding positional embeddings to token vectors prior to projection, one multiplies queries and keys by the rotation matrix corresponding to their positions before computing attention. In code, after computing the projected queries $Q$ and keys $K$ (shaped e.g. $(\text{batch}, \text{seq}, \text{heads}, d)$), one multiplies each 2D slice by the corresponding rotation matrix based on the token position. Because the attention score uses the inner product $QK^\top$, the rotations for $Q$ and $K$ combine into a factor that depends only on differences between position indices.

Two important invariances follow from this multiplicative structure. First, absolute shift invariance: the attention weights for a pair of positions $n,m$ depend only on $m-n$ and on the unrotated dot products of corresponding components. Second, norm preservation within each 2D subspace: because rotations are orthogonal, the norms of $Q$ and $K$ are preserved by the positional transform. These properties help stabilize training by decoupling position information from magnitude changes and by providing a consistent relative-position bias.

# Frequency interpretation of RoPE dimensions (high vs low frequency dims)

The per-subspace base angles $\theta_k$ act like angular frequencies. For a fixed change in position $\Delta = m-n$, the phase increment in subspace $k$ is $\Delta\theta_k$, so the subspaces with large $\theta_k$ (high-frequency dimensions) see large phase changes even for small $\Delta$. Conversely, subspaces with small $\theta_k$ (low-frequency dimensions) vary slowly across positions and primarily encode long-range position trends.

Interpreting each complex coordinate as an oscillator with angular frequency $\theta_k$ clarifies why a geometric progression of $\theta_k$ values is useful: it provides a set of oscillators at exponentially spaced frequencies, enabling the model to represent a mixture of fine and coarse position cues. High-frequency dimensions are sensitive to small relative offsets and can discriminate close positions precisely, while low-frequency dimensions respond to longer-relative offsets and provide a stable signal for more distant relationships. When the attention score sums contributions from all dimensions, the result is a superposition of multiple phase-shifted correlations across different scales, providing a rich representation of relative position.

This frequency view also helps understand possible aliasing and periodicity effects. Because each dimension is periodic modulo its wavelength $\lambda_k = 2\pi/\theta_k$ (if $\theta_k$ is in radians), the representation of positions becomes identical modulo $\lambda_k$ for that dimension. Using multiple incommensurate frequencies reduces the probability of exact aliasing across all dimensions within a reasonable range, but if sequence lengths exceed certain scales relative to the smallest wavelengths, repeated patterns can occur. This motivates the choice of a base that yields wavelengths long enough to cover the intended extrapolation range.

# Implementation considerations (efficient routines, interleaving cos/sin)

Implementing RoPE efficiently in modern deep learning frameworks requires attention to data layout and vectorized operations. Two common strategies are used for storing and applying the rotations.

The first strategy is an explicit 2D rotation applied to interleaved pairs. If features are laid out as [..., d] with consecutive coordinates forming pairs, then one computes
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{2k+1}^{(n)} &= x_{2k+1}\cos(n\theta_k) - x_{2k+2}\sin(n\theta_k),\\
x_{2k+2}^{(n)} &= x_{2k+1}\sin(n\theta_k) + x_{2k+2}\cos(n\theta_k).
\end{aligned}
\]
Writing this in vectorized form across batch, sequence, heads, and subspace dimensions allows highly parallel execution on GPUs. Many implementations precompute the cos and sin tables shaped $(\text{seq}, d/2)$ and then broadcast them to match $Q$ and $K$ tensors. Because the operations are elementwise multiplies and adds, they can be fused or JIT-compiled for speed.

The second strategy uses a concatenated layout where even and odd dimensions are separated into two tensors $x_{\text{even}}$ and $x_{\text{odd}}$. The rotation then becomes
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{\text{even}}^{(n)} &= x_{\text{even}}\cos(n\theta) - x_{\text{odd}}\sin(n\theta),\\
x_{\text{odd}}^{(n)} &= x_{\text{even}}\sin(n\theta) + x_{\text{odd}}\cos(n\theta).
\end{aligned}
\]
This formulation can be convenient when tensor libraries provide fused operations for pairwise elementwise arithmetic.

A common micro-optimization is to interleave the cos and sin values to match the feature layout so that a single fused multiply-add kernel reads contiguous memory and writes contiguous transformed features. For example, constructing a tensor of shape $(\text{seq}, d)$ whose entries alternate between $\cos(n\theta_k)$ and $\sin(n\theta_k)$ for each $k$ allows one to perform the rotation using reshapes and elementwise combinations with minimal indexing overhead. Another optimization is to compute the cos/sin table only once and reuse it for both $Q$ and $K$, since the same angles apply to both.

Numerical stability and precision are also practical considerations. When using the recurrence relation to generate cosine and sine pairs, rounding error can accumulate over long sequences, causing drift from the true values. Recomputing trig values directly for each $n$ avoids this drift but can be slightly more expensive. In mixed-precision training, care must be taken to compute cosines and sines at sufficient precision (e.g., float32) before possibly casting to float16 for subsequent arithmetic, to prevent substantial phase errors.

Finally, memory layout for multi-head attention matters. If the per-head projection results in a tensor of shape $(\text{batch}, \text{seq}, \text{heads}, \text{head_dim})$, it is common to store the cos/sin table as $(\text{seq}, \text{head_dim})$ and then expand it into $(\text{seq}, \text{heads}, \text{head_dim})$ by broadcasting, allowing the same head dimension angles across heads. Some variants choose distinct angle sets per head, but the standard approach shares $\theta_k$ across heads and even across layers.

# Behavior of RoPE under position scaling and implications for extrapolation

One of the appealing empirical properties of RoPE is its relatively good extrapolation to sequence lengths longer than those seen during training. Understanding why requires examining how RoPE behaves under scaling of position indices and how the inner products depend on relative distances.

Because the rotation angle is proportional to position ($\phi_k(n) = n\theta_k$), scaling positions by a factor $s$ multiplies each phase by $s$. If the model is trained on positions $n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}$ and is evaluated at positions up to $sN$ where $s>1$, the phases encountered at test time are $s$ times larger than any seen in training. The consequence depends on the frequencies $\theta_k$. For low-frequency dimensions (small $\theta_k$), even a large multiplicative increase in $n$ may still produce phases within a manageable range and the relative-phase structure seen during training remains informative. For high-frequency dimensions, phases can wrap around many times (modulo $2\pi$) and the effective relative phase pattern can be very different, potentially harming performance.

However, attention uses inner products between rotated $Q$ and $K$ vectors, and these inner products depend on differences in positions through factors $e^{i(m-n)\theta_k}$. If both $m$ and $n$ are scaled by the same factor $s$, then their difference scales as $s(m'-n')$, so the relative phase scales as well. Extrapolation therefore succeeds when the model has learned to interpret the frequency spectrum so that relative phase differences at the new scales still correlate with appropriate attention patterns. The presence of low-frequency dimensions that vary slowly across positions helps because they provide a stable slowly-varying signal even when positions are scaled up.

An instructive way to think about extrapolation is via the Nyquist-like notion of coverage of position differences by the set of wavelengths $\{\lambda_k\}$. If the largest position differences encountered during inference exceed the wavelengths associated with many dimensions, those dimensions will cycle through their periodic patterns multiple times. If the model's learned parameters rely heavily on the absolute phase patterns of those high-frequency dimensions, performance may degrade. Conversely, if the model has learned to weight low-frequency components more for long-range attention, or to combine multiple frequencies robustly, then extrapolation will be better.

Empirically, RoPE often generalizes well to moderate extrapolation beyond training lengths. But extreme extrapolation can fail if base frequencies are too high or if the model has overfit to phase patterns within the training window. Some practical mitigations include choosing a larger base (so frequencies are generally lower), training with a range of sequence lengths or with masking patterns that expose the model to varied relative distances, or applying relative position scaling techniques that adjust phases during inference.

A more formal observation is that because RoPE encodes relative position linearly in phase, it is more amenable to rescaling than additive absolute embeddings: additive embeddings that were only trained up to position $N$ provide no principled way to infer embeddings beyond $N$, whereas rotational embeddings produce a deterministic continuation via the trigonometric functions. That determinism does not guarantee preserved semantics, but it gives the model a consistent signal beyond training support that often yields reasonable behavior.

Conclusion

RoPE is a compact, mathematically principled approach to positional encoding. By interpreting adjacent pairs of embedding dimensions as complex coordinates and applying position-dependent rotations, RoPE turns absolute positions into multiplicative phase factors that naturally produce relative-position sensitivity in attention scores. Practical design choices — the geometric progression of base angles, efficient computation and storage of cosine and sine pairs, and consideration of frequency coverage — determine how well RoPE performs and generalizes. Understanding RoPE in terms of rotations and frequencies gives clear intuition for its strengths and limits, and explains why it has become popular in modern transformer architectures.
