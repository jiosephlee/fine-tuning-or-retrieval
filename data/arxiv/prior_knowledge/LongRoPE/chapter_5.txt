# Introduction

Neural networks are powerful function approximators, but their behavior can be subtle: small changes in input representation can lead to very different learned functions. Positional encodings—explicit features that represent input position or time—are a commonplace mechanism for helping networks represent location-dependent structure. Yet different choices of positional encoding (for example, low-frequency versus high-frequency basis functions, fixed versus learned encodings, or different amplitude scalings) often produce markedly different training and generalization behavior. Why?

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) provides a principled, high-level lens for reasoning about many aspects of wide neural networks. In the infinite-width limit under standard parameterizations, gradient descent learning of a neural network linearizes around its random initialization and is equivalent to kernel regression with a deterministic kernel: the NTK. This kernel encapsulates how signals at different inputs are coupled during training, and, crucially for positional encodings, it tells us how different frequency components of the input representation are learned and generalized.

This chapter develops an intuitive NTK-based account of how positional signals map to learning behavior: how different frequency components are amplified or suppressed, how the kernel imposes receptive scales and spatial smoothness, why high-frequency components tend to be treated differently than low-frequency ones, and what practical heuristics one can derive for interpolation and extrapolation. We also cover the limits of this perspective: where NTK intuition is instructive, and where finite network effects, feature learning, and optimization dynamics make the story more complicated.

# Basic NTK concepts: kernel view of infinitely wide neural networks

At a high level, the NTK emerges from analyzing the training dynamics of a neural network in a particular limit: as widths of the hidden layers go to infinity while weights are initialized with certain variance scalings (for example, the "NTK parameterization"). Consider a neural network $f(x; \theta)$ where $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\theta$ are parameters. Under gradient flow (continuous-time gradient descent) minimizing squared loss and under the infinite width limit, the function evolution obeys a linear dynamics around the initial parameters:

$f_t(x) \approx f_0(x) - \int_0^t \Theta(x, X) (f_s(X) - y) ds,$

where $X$ is the training input set, $y$ the labels, and $\Theta$ is the Neural Tangent Kernel defined by

$\Theta(x, x') = \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0)^\top \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta_0)$,

evaluated at the random initialization $\theta_0$, and then averaged (the law of large numbers makes this deterministic in the infinite width limit). For finite networks, $\Theta$ is a random function that concentrates around its expectation as widths grow.

This representation means that learning $f$ is equivalent to kernel regression with kernel $\Theta$ (plus an initial function $f_0$ that typically goes to zero or a small random function). Concretely, when training with squared loss, the predictor at time $t$ can be expressed in closed form (for full-batch gradient flow) in terms of kernel matrices. In the limiting case of training to convergence, the learned predictor equals the kernel ridge/regression solution (with zero ridge, if no weight decay and noise-free), which interpolates the training data in the function space induced by $\Theta$.

Intuitively, the NTK measures similarity between inputs in terms of how their output derivatives respond to parameter changes at initialization. If two inputs have a large kernel value, then a change to parameters induced by training on one input will strongly affect predictions at the other input. Thus the NTK captures which input locations share "receptive fields" or mutually beneficial gradients; it induces an implicit prior on the family of functions the network can easily learn.

Important properties of many NTKs for common architectures (fully connected, convolutional, etc.) are that they are stationary or translation-invariant (for convolutional architectures or inputs augmented with positions in a Euclidean domain, under some conditions), often depend only on inner products or distances between inputs, and can be described by eigenfunctions and eigenvalues under suitable domains. The eigenvalue spectrum of $\Theta$ determines the "spectral bias": directions (functions) with large eigenvalues are learned quickly and with low sample complexity, while directions corresponding to small eigenvalues are learned slowly or not at all.

# How positional signals map to kernel behavior and receptive scales

Positional encodings provide additional coordinates or features that vary with input position. The NTK sees the positional encoding as part of the input $x$, and so changing the encoding changes $\Theta(x, x')$ directly. To understand this mapping, it helps to think of the kernel as a similarity measure and to analyze how positional features affect similarity between nearby or far-away inputs.

Consider a simple supervised regression problem on a one-dimensional domain $u \in \mathbb{R}$ (a spatial coordinate or time). Suppose we build input vectors $x = \phi(u)$ where $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is the positional encoding. Two common choices are (a) raw coordinate encodings, $\phi(u) = [u]$ (or normalized variants), and (b) sinusoidal Fourier features, $\phi(u) = [\sin(\omega_1 u), \cos(\omega_1 u), \ldots, \sin(\omega_m u), \cos(\omega_m u)]$ for a set of frequencies $\{\omega_k\}$. The NTK $\Theta(\phi(u), \phi(u'))$ will depend on dot products and norms of these feature vectors and thus will be modulated by how $\phi$ maps distances $|u - u'|$ into feature-space angles and magnitudes.

If $\phi$ maps nearby $u, u'$ to feature vectors that have very high inner product, then $\Theta$ will be large for nearby inputs, meaning the model will tend to share learning across small positional displacements. This gives the model a local receptive scale—training at $u$ significantly impacts predictions in a neighborhood around $u$. Conversely, if the encoding decorrelates nearby positions (for example, high-frequency sinusoids with large $\omega$ can change rapidly so that $\phi(u)$ and $\phi(u + \delta)$ become nearly orthogonal unless $\delta$ is tiny), then $\Theta$ will be small away from exact matches, creating narrow receptive fields. Thus the mapping $\phi$ directly shapes receptive scales via the geometry it induces in feature space.

A more formal way to see receptive scale is to examine the kernel as a function of displacement, $\kappa(\delta) = \Theta(\phi(u), \phi(u + \delta))$, assuming translation invariance in $u$ (common when $\phi$ uses only $u$ and not absolute position-dependent biases). The width of $\kappa(\delta)$ around $\delta=0$ acts like a correlation length or effective receptive field. If $\kappa(\delta)$ decays slowly, the kernel has a long receptive scale; if it decays quickly, the receptive scale is short.

For sinusoidal encodings, different frequency components contribute terms to $\kappa(\delta)$ that oscillate with period related to the frequency and that decay with $\delta$ depending on amplitude scalings. Low-frequency sinusoids vary slowly with $u$, so they maintain high inner product for larger $\delta$, widening the kernel and encouraging smooth, low-frequency function fits. High-frequency sinusoids introduce rapid phase changes that cause inner products to fall off quickly with $\delta$, narrowing the kernel and enabling the model to represent fine-grained, high-frequency variations—but only if the kernel has sufficient capacity (eigenvalues) in those high-frequency directions.

Therefore, the positional encoding determines a geometry in which the NTK translates to inductive biases about smoothness and receptive size. When designing positional encodings, you are effectively choosing which lengthscales and patterns should be easy or hard for the network to learn under gradient descent.

# Frequency response and NTK: why high-frequency components behave differently

The NTK perspective helps explain the empirical observation often called spectral bias: neural networks trained by gradient descent tend to learn low-frequency components of a target function faster and with fewer samples than high-frequency components. In kernel language, this is simply a statement about the eigenvalue spectrum of the kernel: low-frequency basis functions (those that vary slowly across input space) typically correspond to large eigenvalues of the NTK, whereas higher-frequency basis functions correspond to small eigenvalues.

To make this concrete, consider decomposing functions on the domain into an orthonormal basis of Fourier modes or eigenfunctions of some Laplacian. For a translation-invariant NTK $\kappa(\delta)$ on the real line or torus, the eigenfunctions are Fourier modes $e^{i \omega u}$ and the eigenvalues are the Fourier transform (power spectrum) of the kernel: $\lambda(\omega) = \int \kappa(\delta) e^{-i \omega \delta} d\delta$. The learning dynamics for each Fourier mode are decoupled and follow scalar linear ODEs with rates proportional to the corresponding eigenvalues. Hence a mode with eigenvalue $\lambda(\omega)$ is learned at an effective rate proportional to $\lambda(\omega)$; if $\lambda(\omega)$ is small for large $|\omega|$, then high-frequency modes are learned slowly.

Why do many NTKs decay at high frequency? Two intuitive reasons: smoothness of architecture and finite feature bandwidth. Many activation functions and layerwise compositions produce kernels $\kappa(\delta)$ that are smooth and decaying functions of $\delta$. By Fourier duality, smooth, localized kernels have rapidly decaying power at high frequencies. Architectures also implicitly average across many neurons; random initial feature maps (as in NTK derivations) are low-pass in expectation unless the input encoding injects strong high-frequency components with significant amplitude.

When positional encodings include explicit high-frequency features, they can counteract this low-pass tendency by placing energy at high frequencies in the input representation. But even then, whether those high-frequency directions are learned quickly depends on how the network's NTK projects onto those components. If the NTK has small eigenvalues at those high frequencies, training will still be slow or require more samples. Moreover, extremely high frequencies can lead to near-orthogonality between adjacent positions: the kernel becomes narrow and the effective sample complexity grows because the model must learn almost separate parameters for many small neighborhoods.

There is also an amplitude consideration. Suppose a sinusoidal component at frequency $\omega$ is scaled by amplitude $a_\omega$ in the input encoding. The contribution of that component to inner products scales with $a_\omega^2$, and hence the NTK entries that depend on that frequency will scale with $a_\omega^2$. Thus, even if a frequency is present, if its amplitude is tiny, it will have small eigenvalue weight and be learned slowly. This provides a lever for practitioners: frequency amplitudes can be tuned to allocate the kernel's spectral mass across frequency bands.

Another important nuance is that high-frequency components can interact with nonlinear activations to produce harmonics or higher-order contributions. For instance, applying a nonlinear activation $\sigma$ to a sum of sinusoids can produce cross-terms and new frequencies in the feature space; the NTK computed at initialization averages over the random weights and thus captures expected contributions from these nonlinear interactions. In some cases, these interactions can create nontrivial support at higher frequencies even when the base encoding lacks them, but typically the strength of such generated components still decays as frequency increases.

In summary, high-frequency components are treated differently because the NTK often places less spectral weight there: the kernel spectrum commonly decays with frequency, causing spectral bias toward smooth, low-frequency functions. Positional encodings can modify where the kernel puts its weight, but amplitude, architecture, and activation nonlinearity determine how effectively high frequencies become learnable.

# NTK‑motivated interpolation/extrapolation heuristics

The NTK perspective yields several practical heuristics for designing and tuning positional encodings to achieve desired interpolation and extrapolation behavior.

First, control receptive scale via amplitude scaling. Remember that the kernel's effective correlation length depends on how positional distances map to feature-space similarities. If you want the model to generalize smoothly over longer spatial extents (favor interpolation across wide gaps), emphasize lower-frequency components or scale down high-frequency amplitudes so the kernel decays slowly with displacement. Concretely, when using Fourier features $\sin(\omega_k u)$ and $\cos(\omega_k u)$, scaling frequencies by a factor or multiplying components by amplitude weights $a_k$ changes the kernel's effective spectrum. Smaller amplitude for high $\omega_k$ reduces the NTK spectral weight at high frequencies and widens $\kappa(\delta)$.

Second, if you want the model to represent fine details (high-frequency content) locally, increase the amplitude of high-frequency components and ensure the network architecture does not overly suppress their contribution. However, be mindful: increasing high-frequency amplitude narrows receptive fields and increases the number of effectively independent "degrees of freedom" needed to cover the domain, so you may need more data or stronger regularization to avoid overfitting or instability.

Third, use frequency annealing or multiscale encodings to provide coarse-to-fine learning. NTK dynamics imply that low-frequency modes are learned quickly; if the task requires both coarse structure and fine detail, one can initialize with stronger low-frequency encodings and gradually increase high-frequency amplitudes during training (or allow the network to learn them). This matches the intuition that the model first fits broad structure (large-eigenvalue modes) and later picks up fine details (smaller-eigenvalue modes). In practice this can be implemented by progressively raising the scale of the Fourier features or by using learnable weights on frequency channels with an annealing schedule.

Fourth, for extrapolation beyond training domain, favor encodings and kernel spectra that do not force overly localized behavior. Extrapolation is inherently hard: kernels that decay to zero rapidly imply that predictions beyond the training support will revert to the prior (often zero) quickly. If the application requires smooth extrapolation of trends, design $\phi$ so that $\kappa(\delta)$ has long tails—this means emphasizing low-frequency encodings or adding polynomial or linear positional features that produce slowly-decaying kernel components. Conversely, if you need precise, localized extrapolation (for example, reconstructing fine textures known to be locally repeatable), then localized kernels may be acceptable.

Fifth, consider learnable positional encodings but regularize them. If positional parameters are learned, the NTK at initialization only partly describes the subsequent learned kernel, because feature learning can change the kernel substantially. Still, the initial NTK can act as a prior: initializing with a diverse set of frequencies but with modest amplitudes allows the network to initially favor smooth solutions while retaining the capacity to adapt and amplify needed high-frequency channels during training. Regularization (weight decay, early stopping) helps keep learned positional filters from overamplifying noise.

Sixth, match encoding bandwidth to data bandwidth. If the target function is known to be bandlimited below some frequency $\Omega$, encoding frequencies beyond that provides little benefit and can harm learning by introducing unnecessary degrees of freedom. Conversely, if the target contains high-frequency content, failing to include corresponding encoding components or using amplitudes too small will preclude learning those components rapidly.

Seventh, for architectures with locality (convolutions) or in higher-dimensional domains, consider the anisotropy of positional encodings. NTK analysis generalizes: for multi-dimensional inputs, the kernel spectrum is a function of the multi-dimensional frequency vector. If one spatial axis requires finer resolution, allocate higher-frequency components along that axis in the encoding. The NTK will then reflect different receptive scales per axis.

These heuristics are born from the NTK’s role as a spectral prior. In practice they translate to design choices: frequency ranges, amplitude scaling, annealing schedules, whether to use fixed vs learnable features, and architecture choices that influence the kernel (depth, nonlinearity, width). Remember that NTK-guided heuristics primarily characterize early- and small-step training dynamics and the inductive bias around initialization; for long training with significant feature learning, behavior can deviate from the NTK predictions.

# Limitations and empirical interpretation of NTK in finite networks

While NTK provides a clean, mathematically tractable picture, its applicability has limits. It most accurately describes infinitely wide networks (or very wide networks with appropriate parameterization) trained with small learning rates and near the initialization regime. Finite-width networks can exhibit substantial departures because they undergo feature learning: the tangent kernel itself evolves during training. This evolution allows the network to carve out representations or features tailored to the data, enabling qualitatively different generalization than a fixed kernel would predict.

Several concrete limitations are worth keeping in mind.

First, feature learning. In finite networks, the Jacobian $\nabla_\theta f(x; \theta)$ changes appreciably during training, and the NTK $\Theta$ evolves. This allows the model to amplify certain input directions and suppress others dynamically, often overcoming spectral bias implied by the initial NTK. For many practical architectures and tasks, feature learning is crucial to achieve state-of-the-art performance. NTK captures only the linearized dynamics at initialization.

Second, finite-width noise and random fluctuations. In finite networks, the empirical NTK at initialization is random and can vary sample-to-sample. This can produce variability in which frequencies are emphasized. Averaging over random initializations (the theoretical NTK) may not reflect the behavior of a particular trained network.

Third, optimization dynamics and hyperparameters. Practical training uses finite learning rates, momentum, batch normalization, dropout, data augmentation, and various optimizer choices (Adam, SGD with momentum, etc.). These factors alter the effective training dynamics in ways that may be poorly approximated by the continuous-time gradient descent dynamics assumed in NTK derivations. For instance, adaptive optimizers rescale gradients per-parameter, effectively changing the kernel weighting across parameters.

Fourth, architecture specifics. The NTK form depends on architecture, activation, and parameterization. Depth, residual connections, normalization layers, and parameter-sharing (convolution) all affect the kernel. While NTKs can be derived for many architectures, the results are sometimes mathematically involved and may not yield simple intuition. Moreover, practical modifications like positional biases or coordinate concatenation may alter kernels in nontrivial ways.

Fifth, label noise and non-square losses. NTK theory is clearest for squared-error loss in regression problems; classification losses and stochastic mini-batch training introduce complications. Nonetheless, some qualitative insights (spectral bias, receptive scales) still translate.

Despite these caveats, NTK remains valuable as a diagnostic and design tool. Empirically, one can compute the empirical NTK for a finite network at initialization (or during training) on a representative set of inputs to examine its spectrum and how it distributes mass across frequencies. This can guide choices: if the empirical NTK has tiny eigenvalues at frequencies relevant to the task, one might increase the amplitude or density of those positional features, widen the network (to move toward NTK regime), or enable feature learning strategies.

Practical suggestions for empirical interpretation:

- Compute the empirical kernel matrix on sampled positions and examine its decay as a function of distance. This provides a heuristic receptive scale. If you see an overly narrow kernel but want smooth interpolation, reduce high-frequency amplitude.

- Diagonalize the kernel (via discrete Fourier transform on uniformly sampled domain) to inspect eigenvalue decay across frequencies. A rapidly decaying spectrum indicates strong low-frequency bias and potential difficulty learning high-frequency targets.

- Track the NTK during training. If the NTK evolves significantly, that indicates feature learning is important and initial NTK-based heuristics may only be partially predictive. If the NTK stays near-initial (concentrated), then kernel predictions are more reliable.

- Run small controlled experiments with synthetic targets (pure sinusoids at given frequencies) to validate whether your network and encoding can learn those frequencies at the sample sizes and training regimes you expect.

Finally, note that finite networks sometimes beat their corresponding infinite-width NTK predictors on real tasks because feature learning can adapt to the data manifold and concentrate representational capacity where it is needed. Thus the NTK should be considered a guiding approximation illuminating inductive tendencies rather than an exact model of realistic training in every setting.

# Closing remarks

The Neural Tangent Kernel offers an accessible, quantitative way to think about the inductive biases introduced by positional encodings. By mapping encodings into kernel geometry, NTK clarifies how frequency content, amplitude scaling, and architecture jointly determine receptive scales, spectral bias, and the rates at which different components of a target function are learned. These insights yield actionable heuristics—balancing low- and high-frequency components, annealing or learning frequency weights, and matching encoding bandwidth to task bandwidth—that practitioners can test and refine empirically.

At the same time, the NTK is an idealized limit. Finite networks learn features and have richer dynamics. Use NTK analysis as an interpretable starting point: compute empirical kernels, validate with controlled experiments, and be prepared to depart from NTK prescriptions when feature learning, optimization choices, or architectural details dominate performance. Together, NTK intuition and empirical verification form a productive cycle for designing positional encodings that achieve the desired balance between interpolation, extrapolation, and representational capacity.
