Chapter 7: Fine‑Tuning Large Language Models and Efficient Tuning Methods

Introduction

Pre‑trained large language models (LLMs) provide a strong foundation for many downstream tasks, but to achieve task‑specific performance we typically perform fine‑tuning. Fine‑tuning is the process of updating model weights with task‑relevant data after the model has been trained on broad, general corpora. As model sizes have grown into the billions and trillions of parameters, traditional full‑model fine‑tuning has become increasingly expensive in compute and memory, which has motivated the development of parameter‑efficient tuning methods. Alongside algorithmic innovations are practical strategies for stabilizing training, avoiding forgetting of pre‑trained knowledge, handling long input sequences, and fitting training into available GPU resources.

This chapter explains the core concepts of fine‑tuning LLMs, contrasts full‑model fine‑tuning with parameter‑efficient approaches such as LoRA and adapters, and covers the most important hyperparameters and practical constraints. We explain why certain choices (learning rate schedules, batch size, mixed precision) matter, how catastrophic forgetting manifests and how regularization mitigates it, and how to format datasets when fine‑tuning on long sequences. Finally, we give actionable techniques for reducing memory footprint and increasing throughput during long‑sequence fine‑tuning, including gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing.

# Full‑model fine‑tuning vs parameter‑efficient approaches (LoRA, adapters)

Full‑model fine‑tuning updates every weight in the model using gradients computed on task data. For a model with parameters vectorized as $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^P$, full‑model fine‑tuning optimizes
\[
\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x), y)
\]
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the task loss. This approach has the advantage of maximum expressivity: the optimizer can adjust all parameters to tailor representations and internal computations to the task. In practice, full‑model fine‑tuning typically achieves the highest possible performance if compute, memory, and data are sufficient.

However, full‑model tuning has several drawbacks as models grow large. First, it requires memory proportional to $P$ to store parameters and optimizer states (for Adam‑style optimizers that keep first and second moments, memory for optimizer states can be several times larger than the model itself). Second, it requires storing a separate copy of $\theta$ per task or frequent checkpointing if many task variants are needed. Third, full fine‑tuning can be unstable or prone to overfitting when available labeled data is small relative to model capacity.

Parameter‑efficient tuning methods aim to reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving much of the performance of full fine‑tuning. Two popular families of methods are low‑rank adaptations (LoRA) and adapter modules.

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) injects low‑rank trainable updates into specific weight matrices (often the attention projection matrices or MLPs) while keeping the original pre‑trained weights frozen. Suppose a layer contains a weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ used to compute $W_0 x$. LoRA parameterizes the update as a product of two smaller matrices $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{in}}$ and computes
\[
W x \approx W_0 x + \alpha \, A (B x)
\]
where $r \ll \min(d_{out}, d_{in})$ is the rank and $\alpha$ is a scaling factor. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained. Intuitively, LoRA assumes that the change needed for a downstream task lies in a low‑dimensional subspace; optimizing a few low‑rank directions is much cheaper and often sufficient. The number of trainable parameters becomes $r(d_{out}+d_{in})$, which is orders of magnitude smaller than updating all entries of $W_0$. LoRA is simple to implement and works well for many tasks, particularly when applied to projection matrices in attention or feedforward networks.

Adapter modules insert small bottleneck MLPs into each transformer layer. A typical adapter consists of a down‑projection \(W_d\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times d}\), a nonlinearity, and an up‑projection \(W_u\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times b}\), where \(b\ll d\) is the bottleneck dimension. Given a layer input \(h\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the adapter computes
\[
\text{adapter}(h) = W_u \, \sigma(W_d h)
\]
and either adds this residual to the layer output or replaces the layer's intermediate activation with it. Adapters train only the small projection matrices \(W_d\) and \(W_u\), freezing the rest of the model. Adapters have been used successfully in many NLP tasks and allow task‑specific modules to be stored compactly, enabling modular composition of many tasks.

Other parameter‑efficient approaches include prompt tuning (optimizing a set of continuous tokens prepended to inputs), prefix tuning (optimizing prefix key/value vectors), and BitFit (tuning only bias terms). Each method trades off between parameter count, ease of integration, and performance. Prompt and prefix tuning are particularly lightweight but tend to need more data or longer compute to match LoRA or adapters. In practice, LoRA and adapters often provide a strong balance between parameter efficiency and final accuracy.

Choosing between full fine‑tuning and parameter‑efficient methods depends on constraints. If you have abundant compute, enough GPU memory, and need the absolute best task performance, full fine‑tuning may be appropriate. If you must support many downstream tasks, prefer fast iteration, or are limited by memory, LoRA or adapters can allow efficient, modular fine‑tuning with dramatically smaller storage for each task.

# Learning rate schedules, batch size, and mixed precision

Learning rate, batch size, and numeric precision are among the most influential hyperparameters in fine‑tuning. Learning rate controls the step size of parameter updates. A learning rate that is too high can cause divergence or large deviations from pre‑trained representations; too low leads to slow adaptation and being trapped in local minima. Typical learning rates for full fine‑tuning of transformers range from $5\mathrm{e}{-6}$ to $5\mathrm{e}{-5}$ for large models, but the optimal scale depends on model size, optimizer, and whether parameter‑efficient methods are used (LoRA often allows higher effective learning rates on its smaller parameter set).

Learning rate schedules modify the learning rate over training. A common schedule in fine‑tuning is a short linear warmup followed by a cosine decay or constant learning rate. Warmup for the first $w$ steps prevents large gradients from immediately upsetting pre‑trained weights; linear warmup increases the learning rate from 0 to the initial value over $w$ steps. After warmup, cosine decay follows
\[
\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \cos\left(\pi \frac{t-w}{T-w}\right)\right)
\]
for $t\in[w, T]$, where $T$ is the total number of steps. Alternatively, many practitioners use an exponential or linear decay, or keep the learning rate constant after warmup when fine‑tuning on small datasets. When using LoRA, because the number of trainable parameters is smaller, you often choose a larger $\eta_0$ and shorter warmup.

Batch size interacts with the learning rate and generalization. Larger batch sizes give more stable gradient estimates and allow larger learning rates; however, they can reduce the number of optimization steps (epochs) completed per unit of compute and in some settings harm generalization. For transformers, batch sizes (measured in tokens) are often kept between $2^{12}$ and $2^{16}$ tokens per step in large training runs, but in fine‑tuning you may use much smaller token counts per GPU and rely on gradient accumulation to emulate larger batch sizes. There are practical guidelines such as scaling the learning rate linearly with batch size (the "linear scaling rule"), but in fine‑tuning this should be applied cautiously due to stability concerns.

Mixed precision training—usually using float16 (FP16) or bfloat16 for activations and weights, with a small float32 master copy for optimizer states—reduces memory usage and increases throughput on modern GPUs. In practice, frameworks implement Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) that casts operations to FP16 where safe and keeps accumulations in FP32. One important detail is loss scaling: small gradients in FP16 may underflow to zero, so dynamic loss scaling multiplies the loss by a scale factor during backward passes and divides gradients by that factor before updating the FP32 master weights. Mixed precision often yields 1.5–2× speedups and significant GPU memory savings, enabling larger batch sizes or longer sequences.

When combining mixed precision with parameter‑efficient tuning, be mindful of operations that are numerically sensitive (softmax in attention, layer normalization). Many libraries automatically handle these, but you should monitor for NaNs or instability and adjust loss scaling or learning rate if they occur.

# Catastrophic forgetting and regularization strategies

Catastrophic forgetting is the phenomenon where a model fine‑tuned on new task data loses performance on the types of tasks it previously solved, including the broad linguistic and world knowledge encoded by pre‑training. In LLMs, forgetting can manifest as degradation in fluency, incorrect facts, or reduced robustness. The deeper the fine‑tuning and the higher the learning rate, the more the model can drift from its pre‑trained basin.

Regularization strategies aim to constrain updates so that the model retains pre‑trained knowledge while learning task specifics. One straightforward strategy is weight decay, which penalizes large parameter magnitudes via an $L_2$ term in the loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(\theta) + \lambda \|\theta\|_2^2.
\]
Weight decay tends to keep weights small and can improve generalization. However, weight decay applied uniformly may not optimally preserve the most important pre‑trained directions.

A more targeted regularization is elastic weight consolidation (EWC). EWC estimates the importance of each parameter to the original task (pre‑training) using the Fisher information matrix $F$ and penalizes deviations proportional to importance:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_i F_i (\theta_i - \theta_{0,i})^2
\]
where $\theta_0$ are the pre‑trained parameters and $F_i$ approximate the sensitivity of the original loss to $\theta_i$. EWC tries to protect critical parameters while allowing others to change. Estimating $F$ for very large models is expensive, and approximations or diagonal versions are typically used.

Another practical approach is learning rate and optimizer selection: using a small learning rate and conservative optimizer state (for example, smaller Adam betas or switching to SGD in some layers) reduces the magnitude of change and thus forgetting. Freezing early layers and fine‑tuning only later transformer blocks (or using parameter‑efficient methods that only train adapters) also limits drift from pre‑trained representations.

Replay‑based and data‑aware techniques are useful when the problem is to keep model abilities across a set of tasks. These include interleaving a small sample of pre‑training or validation data during fine‑tuning (experience replay), or mixing a fraction of generic corpora with the task data to anchor the model. A common practice is to interleave a small percentage (e.g., 5–10%) of general language data during fine‑tuning to reduce catastrophic forgetting.

Contrastive or distillation‑based regularization can also preserve behavior. For instance, behavior cloning or knowledge distillation constrains fine‑tuned outputs to be close to pre‑trained model outputs on some anchor inputs, using a loss term like
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \tau^2 \, \mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\theta_0}(\cdot\mid x) \,||\, p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\big)
\]
with temperature $\tau$. This KL penalty encourages the fine‑tuned model to remain similar to the teacher (pre‑trained) distribution where appropriate.

Finally, parameter‑efficient approaches themselves inherently mitigate forgetting because they limit which parts of the model are changed. LoRA updates a low‑rank subspace and adapters introduce small residual modules; both leave the majority of pre‑trained parameters unchanged, so generic capabilities are typically preserved better than with full fine‑tuning. That said, even LoRA and adapters can cause undesired shifts in behavior if misused or trained with excessive learning rates.

# Dataset formatting for long‑sequence fine‑tuning (chunking, BOS/EOS bookending)

Fine‑tuning on long sequences—such as books, legal documents, or long dialogues—introduces both modeling and practical data handling challenges. Transformer architectures typically have an upper limit on sequence length due to quadratic attention cost and memory constraints. When fine‑tuning for long contexts, dataset formatting choices affect learning dynamics, context utilization, and downstream generation behavior.

Chunking is the canonical method to handle long documents. A long document is split into chunks of length at most $L$ tokens (where $L$ is the model's context window) and each chunk becomes a training example. There are important choices in chunking: overlapping vs non‑overlapping chunks, stride length, and how to handle labels for causal vs encoder‑decoder models. Overlapping chunks (sliding windows) provide context continuity: if you have tokens numbered sequentially in a document, using chunks that overlap by a stride $s$ means each token near a boundary appears in multiple training examples, which helps the model learn cross‑chunk dependencies. For causal language modeling, labeling the target tokens requires care to avoid leaking future tokens. Typically, each chunk will be used in a left‑to‑right prediction setup where each token predicts the next one within the chunk.

Bookending inputs with BOS (beginning of sequence) and EOS (end of sequence) tokens affects how the model learns to start and stop generations. In autoregressive fine‑tuning, including a BOS token at the start of each chunk signals the model to place special mass on initial positions; including EOS tokens in targets teaches the model when to stop. For tasks such as summarization or question answering, explicit BOS and EOS tokens for prompts and answers make the model learn delimiting behavior. When fine‑tuning on long documents where you want the model to generate continuations across chunk boundaries, avoid inserting EOS tokens at chunk ends unless you actually want the model to stop there. If you want the model to treat chunks as parts of a continuous stream, use sliding windows with overlap and avoid premature EOS insertion.

Another consideration is positional encoding: if you split a document into chunks and reset positional indices for each chunk, the model will not learn absolute positions beyond $L$. If you instead maintain global positional indices (i.e., position offsets across chunks), you must ensure the model supports positions that large and that the tokenizer and model positional embedding scheme handle offsets correctly. For Transformer models with absolute learned positional embeddings limited to $L$, a workaround is to use relative positional encodings or architectural support for larger windows (or apply fine‑tuning only with resets and hope the model stitches context with overlaps).

For supervised tasks where inputs and targets are present (e.g., instruction fine‑tuning), format each example so that the model sees the prompt and the desired output separated by a clear delimiter and optionally bookended by BOS/EOS. For mixtures of short and long examples, consider bucketing by length to reduce padding waste and maintain stable batch token counts.

When building datasets from multiple documents, avoid concatenating unrelated documents into a single sequence unless you mask boundaries. Unlabeled concatenation can cause the model to learn spurious continuations across unrelated documents. If you do concatenate, insert a special separator token to mark boundaries, or restart the context by inserting BOS tokens so the model learns independence across documents.

Finally, ensure tokenization aligns with expected evaluation: if your downstream use involves generating long continuations, practice with generation during fine‑tuning by using targets that mimic real generation behavior, including EOS tokens only when appropriate.

# Practical fine‑tuning constraints: steps, GPU memory, and throughput

In practice, fine‑tuning is constrained by compute budget, available GPU memory, and throughput requirements. These constraints determine how many training steps you can run, what batch sizes and sequence lengths you can afford, and which optimization techniques are feasible.

Number of steps: For small datasets or adaptation tasks, the number of fine‑tuning steps $T$ is often small (hundreds to thousands), whereas for larger domain adaptation you may run tens of thousands. The choice of $T$ interacts with learning rate schedule: a longer schedule typically requires a lower initial learning rate or a different decay plan. Overfitting is a real risk with small datasets; fewer epochs with early stopping monitored on a validation set are common.

GPU memory: Memory is often the hard limit. Total memory usage includes model parameters, optimizer states, activations, gradients, and various buffers. For Adam‑like optimizers, optimizer states (first and second moment estimates) double the memory per parameter compared to SGD. If the model is stored in FP16, parameter memory halves, but optimizer states are often in FP32. For a model with $P$ parameters, approximate memory for parameters and Adam states is
\[
M_{\text{params+opt}} \approx P\cdot(2\cdot 4\,\text{bytes} + 2\cdot 4\,\text{bytes}) = P\cdot 16\,\text{bytes}
\]
assuming FP16 parameters (2 bytes) with FP32 moment estimates (4 bytes each for $m$ and $v$), plus a FP32 master copy (4 bytes). This is a rough estimate; frameworks and optimizers differ. Activation memory during forward/backward depends on sequence length $L$ and batch size $B$ as well as model depth. The quadratic nature of attention ($O(L^2)$) and $O(BL)$ activations make long sequences particularly costly.

Throughput: Throughput measures tokens or samples processed per second. To improve throughput, you can reduce sequence length, decrease batch size per device, use mixed precision, or use optimized kernels and libraries (e.g., fused attention, tensor cores). Parameter‑efficient methods like LoRA reduce trainable parameter counts and therefore optimizer state memory, improving throughput when many GPUs would otherwise be limited by optimizer memory. If your workload is memory‑bound, zero‑redundancy optimizer partitions (ZeRO) or model/sharded parallelism can help, but these introduce communication overhead.

Checkpointing and storage: With many fine‑tuning experiments, storing full checkpoints for each run can be expensive. Parameter‑efficient methods reduce checkpoint size dramatically: a LoRA adapter checkpoint may be a few megabytes compared to gigabytes for full weights. When storing full checkpoints, decide whether to save optimizer state, which speeds resumption but costs space.

Budgeting a fine‑tuning run involves estimating steps that fit within compute limits. For example, with $N$ samples, a batch size of $B$ (in samples), and $E$ epochs, the total steps are approximately
\[
T \approx \frac{E\cdot N}{B}.
\]
If you control compute in FLOPs, convert steps into token processing based on average tokens per sample and estimate wall clock time given throughput.

# Techniques for long‑sequence fine‑tuning (gradient accumulation, checkpointing)

When sequence length or model size prevents large batches or single‑step updates, gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing become essential techniques to trade time for memory and enable effective fine‑tuning.

Gradient accumulation simulates a larger batch size by accumulating gradients across multiple forward/backward passes before performing an optimizer update. Suppose your GPU can handle a micro‑batch size of $b$ samples; to emulate an effective batch size of $B = k\cdot b$, run $k$ forward/backward passes and sum gradients, then apply the optimizer once. Algorithmically, you repeat forward/backward $k$ times, each time computing gradients $\nabla\mathcal{L}_i$ and accumulating them; after $k$ steps you perform the parameter update and optionally zero gradients. The effective learning rate should be adjusted to reflect the larger batch size according to your scaling rule. Gradient accumulation comes at the cost of longer wall clock iterations (because you do $k$ passes) but enables either larger effective batch sizes or fitting within memory.

Activation checkpointing (also called gradient checkpointing or rematerialization) reduces activation memory by trading compute for memory. Normally, backpropagation requires storing intermediate activations for each layer until the backward pass. With checkpointing, you store activations only for a subset of layers (the checkpoints) and recompute activations for intervening layers during the backward pass as needed. If a model has $L$ layers and you checkpoint every $s$ layers, memory for activations drops roughly by factor $s$, while the backward pass requires recomputing forward work for those $s-1$ layers, increasing compute by a factor depending on $s$. Libraries provide automated checkpointing policies; selecting a checkpoint interval balances memory savings against extra compute. For very long sequences, checkpointing is often necessary to fit activations into memory.

Combining gradient accumulation and checkpointing: Because both techniques reduce memory per device, they can be combined. Checkpointing reduces activation memory, allowing a slightly larger micro‑batch $b$, while accumulation handles the rest to reach the effective batch size $B$. When using both, be mindful of interactions with mixed precision and dynamic loss scaling.

Sequence packing and bucketing reduce wasted tokens in batches. Pack multiple shorter sequences into a single batch sequence to better utilize context window $L$, or bucket sequences by length so that examples in a batch have similar lengths, reducing padding. When packing multiple samples into one sequence, ensure you mask attention across sample boundaries so tokens do not attend to tokens from unrelated examples. Packing increases throughput but complicates loss computation and index bookkeeping.

Other model‑level techniques for long sequences include linearized or sparse attention variants that lower the attention complexity from $O(L^2)$ to $O(L\log L)$ or $O(L)$, and position compression methods, but these require architectural support and are not pure fine‑tuning techniques. If you need to fine‑tune for very long contexts regularly, choose a model that already supports efficient long attention patterns or relative positional encodings.

Finally, profiling and instrumentation are practical necessities. Use memory profilers to understand where memory is spent (parameters, optimizer, activations) and benchmark throughput with realistic sequence lengths and batch sizes. Tweak micro‑batch size, checkpointing intervals, and accumulation steps iteratively to find an optimal point for your hardware budget.

Conclusion

Fine‑tuning LLMs is both an art and a science. Full‑model fine‑tuning provides maximum adaptability but at high cost and risk of catastrophic forgetting, while parameter‑efficient methods such as LoRA and adapters offer compact, stable, and modular adaptation options. Careful tuning of learning rates, batch sizes, and mixed precision settings is essential for stability and performance. When working with long sequences, thoughtful dataset formatting—chunking strategies and BOS/EOS handling—determines how the model learns to use context. Practical constraints around GPU memory and throughput require techniques like gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing to enable effective fine‑tuning. With these tools and an experimental mindset—monitoring validation behavior, applying conservative regularization, and preferring smaller, task‑specific modules when appropriate—you can adapt large language models to a wide range of tasks efficiently and reliably.
