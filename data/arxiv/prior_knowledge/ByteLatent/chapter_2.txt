# Introduction

Language modeling is a central problem in natural language processing (NLP). At its core, a language model assigns probabilities to sequences of tokens (words, subwords, characters) so that it can judge how plausible a sentence is, complete a fragment, or generate new sentences. Modern neural language models, especially those based on recurrent neural networks and transformers, power many downstream applications: machine translation, question answering, summarization, code synthesis, and more.

This chapter develops the foundational concepts needed to understand how neural language models are constructed, trained, and evaluated. We introduce the key modeling paradigms (autoregressive vs. auto-encoding), explain the predominant training objective (maximum likelihood, implemented via cross-entropy loss), describe the common training technique of teacher forcing and its consequences, and cover practical issues around dataset splits and overfitting. We then survey decoding and generation algorithms (greedy, sampling, beam search) and end with a concise review of common benchmark tasks used to evaluate sequence models, their particular evaluation metrics, and what they measure about model abilities.

# Autoregressive vs. auto-encoding models

Neural language models can be organized by how they model dependencies in text and what inference tasks they support. Two broad categories are autoregressive models and auto-encoding models. Understanding their differences clarifies the kinds of training objectives, architectures, and downstream applications each is suited for.

Autoregressive models define a probability distribution over a sequence by factorizing the joint probability of tokens into a product of conditional probabilities in a fixed order (usually left-to-right). For a token sequence $x_{1:T} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T)$, an autoregressive model parametrized by $\theta$ computes
\[
P_\theta(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
Each conditional probability $P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1})$ is produced by a neural network (for example, a Transformer decoder or an LSTM) that consumes the prefix $x_{1:t-1}$ and outputs a distribution over the next token. Autoregressive models are natural for generation tasks because sampling from the joint distribution proceeds by sampling one token at a time from the conditional distribution and appending it to the context.

Auto-encoding models approach sequences differently. Instead of prescribing a left-to-right factorization, they typically learn latent representations of entire sequences or masked parts of sequences. A canonical example is BERT-type models that are trained with a masked language modeling objective: some subset of tokens in the input is replaced with a special mask token, and the model is trained to predict the original token at those masked positions, conditioning on the unmasked tokens in the same sequence. Formally, if $M \subset \{1,\dots,T\}$ is the set of masked indices, an auto-encoding model learns to predict $x_M$ given $x_{\setminus M}$. These models typically employ a bidirectional encoder architecture (e.g., Transformer encoder) that attends to both left and right context simultaneously.

Intuitively, autoregressive models are inherently generative in a sequential sense: they explicitly model the process of producing tokens one after another and hence are straightforward to use for generation. Auto-encoding models, by contrast, are trained to reconstruct missing parts from global context, encouraging rich contextual representations that are useful for classification, sequence labeling, or extracting features for downstream tasks. Because they see both left and right context during training, auto-encoding models are often stronger at tasks that require bidirectional context, such as classification and many question-answering tasks where the full passage is available.

There are hybrid architectures as well. Encoder–decoder models (sequence-to-sequence) use an encoder to ingest an input sequence and a decoder to generate an output sequence, combining ideas from both classes. The decoder is typically autoregressive (it generates output tokens one-by-one conditioned on encoder representations), while the encoder processes the entire input sequence in parallel. Such models are standard for machine translation and many conditional generation tasks.

# Next-token prediction and teacher forcing

Next-token prediction is the canonical supervised training task for autoregressive language models. Given training sequences $x_{1:T}$, the model is trained to predict token $x_t$ given the preceding ground-truth prefix $x_{1:t-1}$ for each position $t$. This reduces sequence modeling to a set of classification problems: at each position the model outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary, and the target is the single correct token.

Teacher forcing is the standard technique used during training that implements this idea: when predicting the token at time step $t$, the model is fed the true previous tokens $x_{1:t-1}$ (from the dataset) rather than its own previously generated tokens. Concretely, for a recurrent or autoregressive model, the input at step $t$ is the ground-truth token $x_{t-1}$ (or an embedding thereof) and the hidden state is derived from the sequence of true previous tokens. The loss is computed between the model’s predicted distribution and the true token $x_t$.

Teacher forcing greatly stabilizes and accelerates training because it avoids compounding errors from the model’s own outputs during training time. However, it introduces a mismatch between training and generation-time conditions, which is often called exposure bias. At generation time the model conditions on its own previous samples (which can be erroneous), and it may therefore encounter contexts never seen during training. This mismatch can lead to cascading errors and degeneration of output quality during long-generation tasks.

Researchers have proposed various strategies to mitigate exposure bias. Scheduled sampling interpolates between feeding the model ground-truth tokens and feeding it tokens sampled from its own predictions according to a schedule. Other approaches train models with objectives that explicitly account for generation-time behavior, such as sequence-level reinforcement learning or minimum risk training, which optimize metrics computed on full generations rather than per-token cross-entropy. Nevertheless, teacher forcing remains the dominant approach in practice because it is simple, efficient, and effective for learning good conditional distributions when combined with large datasets and architectures.

# Maximum likelihood training and cross-entropy loss

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the foundational principle for training probabilistic language models. Given a dataset of sequences $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(n)}_{1:T_n}\}_{n=1}^N$ sampled from some unknown distribution, MLE seeks parameters $\theta$ that maximize the likelihood of the observed data under the model $P_\theta$. Equivalently, one minimizes the negative log-likelihood:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLE}}(\theta) = -\sum_{n=1}^N \log P_\theta\big(x^{(n)}_{1:T_n}\big).
\]
Using the autoregressive factorization, this decomposes as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLE}}(\theta) = -\sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_n} \log P_\theta\big(x^{(n)}_t \mid x^{(n)}_{1:t-1}\big).
\]
In classification terms, at each position $t$ the model outputs a categorical distribution over the vocabulary and the target is the one-hot indicator of the true next token. Minimizing the negative log-likelihood for categorical predictions is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution (a delta on the true token) and the model distribution. For a single position, the cross-entropy loss is
\[
\ell_{\text{CE}}(\theta) = -\log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
This is the standard training loss used for autoregressive language models.

Training by MLE has several desirable properties. If the model family is expressive enough and we have infinite data, MLE recovers the true data distribution. MLE is also computationally convenient because the loss decomposes across time steps and across examples; gradients are computed via backpropagation and stochastic gradient descent (or variants such as Adam).

However, MLE with teacher forcing can emphasize predicting the most likely next token in contexts that occur in the training data, which does not always align with producing diverse or controllable text in generation. Additionally, since MLE minimizes token-level cross-entropy, it optimizes a surrogate objective rather than directly optimizing many application-specific metrics (e.g., BLEU for translation, exact match for QA or functional correctness for code generation). This discrepancy motivates research into sequence-level training objectives, fine-tuning on task-specific losses, and evaluation-time calibration.

A useful derived metric from likelihood is perplexity, which measures how well the model assigns probability mass to held-out data. For a test set with total token count $N_{\text{tokens}}$, perplexity is defined as
\[
\text{Perplexity} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{tokens}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{tokens}}} -\log P_\theta(x_i \mid \text{context}_i)\right).
\]
Lower perplexity indicates the model assigns higher probability to the test tokens; it has an intuitive interpretation as the effective branching factor of the model’s distribution.

# Training and evaluation splits, overfitting considerations

Proper evaluation of a language model requires careful separation of data into training, validation, and test splits. The training set is used to fit model parameters. The validation (or development) set is used to monitor generalization performance during model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and early stopping. The test set is held out until the final evaluation to provide an unbiased estimate of performance.

Typical practice uses a single training set and splits the held-out portion: for example, 80% train, 10% validation, 10% test, although proportions vary depending on dataset size. Crucially, the validation set must not be used to train the model, and the test set must not be used for model selection decisions. Otherwise reported test performance will be optimistically biased.

Overfitting occurs when a model learns patterns that are specific to the training set and do not generalize to new examples. Overfitting can be subtle in language modeling because very large neural models trained on relatively small datasets can memorize entire sequences or idiosyncrasies. Regularization techniques mitigate overfitting: weight decay, dropout, data augmentation, early stopping based on validation loss, and model capacity control. For large-scale pretraining with massive corpora, overfitting is less of a concern, but when fine-tuning on smaller supervised tasks, it is a primary risk.

Another practical concern is data contamination: if exact or near-duplicate examples from the test set appear in the training data (especially common when scraping large web corpora), evaluations can be artificially inflated. Researchers therefore perform dataset deduplication checks and source-aware splits where possible.

When monitoring training, two curves are important: training loss and validation loss. If training loss decreases while validation loss flattens or increases, the model is likely overfitting. Early stopping halts training when validation loss stops improving. For language models, validation perplexity is the standard quantity to watch. For downstream tasks, task-specific metrics on the validation set guide fine-tuning and hyperparameter choices.

Finally, reporting uncertainty in evaluation (e.g., confidence intervals, multiple random restarts) is good scientific practice, because model performance can vary due to random initialization, dataset sampling, and training nondeterminism.

# Generation methods: greedy, sampling, beam search

Once a language model has been trained, generating sequences from it is a central operation. Different decoding methods trade off between quality, diversity, and computational cost. We describe the main approaches: greedy decoding, stochastic sampling (including top-k and nucleus sampling), and beam search.

Greedy decoding is the simplest method: at each time step $t$, select the token with the highest conditional probability
\[
\hat{x}_t = \arg\max_{v} P_\theta(v \mid \hat{x}_{1:t-1}).
\]
Repeat this until a termination token (e.g., end-of-sequence) is generated or a length limit is reached. Greedy decoding is fast and deterministic, but it can get stuck in locally high-probability but globally suboptimal sequences, and it tends to produce repetitive or generic outputs, particularly for open-ended generation.

Sampling-based decoding injects randomness into generation by drawing tokens from the model’s conditional distribution. The simplest is ancestral sampling: at each step sample $x_t \sim P_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{1:t-1})$. Pure sampling can produce highly diverse outputs, but it may generate low-quality tokens with tiny probability causing incoherence. Two widely used practical constraints on sampling are top-k and nucleus (top-p) sampling.

Top-k sampling restricts the sampling distribution to the $k$ tokens with highest probability and renormalizes. Formally, let $V_k$ be the set of top-$k$ tokens by $P_\theta$. Sample $x_t$ from the distribution proportional to $P_\theta(v \mid \cdot)$ for $v \in V_k$. Choosing $k$ controls the diversity vs. quality tradeoff.

Nucleus sampling (top-p sampling) instead chooses the smallest set $V_p$ of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds $p$ (e.g., $p=0.9$), and samples from this adaptive set. Nucleus sampling adapts to the entropy of the predictive distribution: when the model is confident, $V_p$ is small and sampling is conservative; when the model is uncertain, $V_p$ is larger, allowing more diversity.

Temperature is another parameter used to control randomness: scaling the logits (pre-softmax scores) by $1/\tau$ before applying softmax yields a softened or sharpened distribution:
\[
P_\theta^\tau(v \mid \cdot) \propto \exp\left(\frac{\logit_\theta(v)}{\tau}\right).
\]
Temperature $\tau < 1$ makes the distribution sharper (more deterministic), while $\tau > 1$ makes it flatter (more random). Temperature can be combined with top-k or nucleus sampling.

Beam search is a deterministic search algorithm that attempts to approximate the highest-probability sequence under the model. Rather than keeping a single best prefix, beam search maintains a set (beam) of the top $B$ partial hypotheses (prefixes) at each time step according to their accumulated scores. At step $t$, each hypothesis in the beam is extended by all possible next tokens (or the top-$k$ tokens per hypothesis), and the top $B$ resulting extended hypotheses by joint score are retained. Scores are typically the sum of log-probabilities:
\[
\text{score}(x_{1:t}) = \sum_{i=1}^t \log P_\theta(x_i \mid x_{1:i-1}).
\]
Beam search with beam width $B=1$ reduces to greedy decoding. Larger $B$ tends to produce higher-scoring sequences under the model, which often improves metrics like BLEU in conditional generation tasks such as translation. However, for unconditional or open-ended generation, larger beams can lead to generic, bland outputs and increase repetitions because the beam favors safe, high-probability continuations.

A subtle but important detail is length bias in beam search: because scores sum log-probabilities, longer sequences accumulate more negative log-probability and therefore may be penalized; practitioners often apply length normalization, dividing the total log-probability by $T^\alpha$ or by $T$ or using other heuristics to favor suitable lengths. Moreover, the highest-likelihood sequence under a model is not necessarily the one humans prefer, so beam search optimizes the model's objective, which might not align with human judgments or task-specific evaluation metrics.

Selecting a decoding method depends on the application. For deterministic, high-precision needs (e.g., translating sentences in a constrained setting), beam search is common. For creative or diverse generation (dialogue, story generation), sampling with nucleus and temperature controls is usually better. In many deployed systems, sampling is followed by reranking or filtering stages to enforce constraints and improve quality.

# Common benchmark tasks (classification, QA, code generation)

Language models are evaluated along a variety of downstream tasks that probe different capabilities: understanding (classification), reasoning and retrieval (question answering), and structured generation (code synthesis). Each task has its own data characteristics and evaluation metrics, and models are typically fine-tuned or prompted to perform these tasks.

For classification tasks, such as sentiment analysis, topic labeling, or natural language inference (NLI), the model must map an input text (or pair of texts) to a discrete label. Auto-encoding models like BERT are naturally suited to classification because they produce contextualized representations for each token or for a pooled sequence vector that can be fed into a classifier head. Evaluation metrics are usually accuracy for balanced label sets or F1 score for imbalanced classes. Fine-tuning minimizes cross-entropy loss over labels rather than token-level next-token prediction, though fine-tuned autoregressive models can also perform classification via prompt-based formulations.

Question answering (QA) is a broad family of tasks. Extractive QA (e.g., SQuAD) provides a context paragraph and asks the model to extract a span containing the answer. These tasks are well-suited to models that can attend globally over the context; evaluation uses exact match (EM) and F1 at the token level between predicted and gold spans. Generative QA or open-domain QA requires producing free-form answers, often combining retrieval of supporting documents with a generation model; evaluation typically uses metrics like exact match, F1, or human judgment for correctness and fluency.

Multiple-choice QA tasks (e.g., RACE, MMLU) present a question and several answer choices; the model must pick the correct choice. Language models can be adapted by computing the likelihood of each choice given the question and selecting the highest-likelihood choice, or by fine-tuning on labeled examples.

Code generation tasks evaluate a model's ability to generate syntactically and semantically correct code given a specification or docstring. Datasets such as HumanEval measure functional correctness by executing generated code on test cases: a program passes if it returns the expected outputs for the hidden test inputs. Unlike natural language, code correctness can often be evaluated precisely by execution, giving a strong criterion (pass@k) for model output quality. Code generation stresses not only fluency but exactness, adherence to API semantics, and reasoning over program structure.

Translation and summarization are conditional generation tasks evaluated with automatic metrics like BLEU (for translation) and ROUGE (for summarization), which measure n-gram overlap with reference outputs. These metrics correlate imperfectly with human judgment, so human evaluation is often used to assess adequacy and fluency. For translation, sequence-level methods like beam search with length normalization are standard to improve BLEU scores.

Across these tasks, there are broader evaluation concerns. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts raw tokens but does not directly indicate downstream task performance; a model with better perplexity may not necessarily perform better on a specific classification or generation task after fine-tuning or prompting. Task-specific metrics (accuracy, F1, exact match, BLEU, ROUGE, pass@k) better reflect performance on the task of interest. Human evaluations remain essential for measuring qualities such as coherence, factuality, and helpfulness that automatic metrics do not capture.

Finally, benchmark suites such as GLUE, SuperGLUE, XTREME, and the various code and reasoning benchmarks provide standardized tasks that make it possible to compare models across a range of skills. When reading research papers, pay attention to the exact experimental setup: whether models were pretrained or fine-tuned, what decoding parameters were used, and whether any task-specific postprocessing or reranking was applied. These details significantly influence reported performance.

# Conclusion

Neural language modeling brings together probabilistic modeling, scalable neural networks, and practical decoding strategies to produce systems capable of understanding and generating language. Autoregressive models naturally support sequential generation and are commonly trained by maximum likelihood with teacher forcing; auto-encoding models excel at producing rich bidirectional representations useful for classification and extraction tasks. Training and evaluation practices—proper data splits, monitoring for overfitting, and guarding against data contamination—are essential for reliable research. Finally, decoding methods and task-specific benchmarks determine how model quality is measured in practice. With these foundations, you are prepared to read and critically analyze contemporary research on sequence models and their applications.
