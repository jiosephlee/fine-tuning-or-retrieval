Introduction

Understanding uncertainty and information is central to modeling sequences, evaluating model performance, and reasoning about compression. This chapter introduces the mathematical foundations, intuitions, and practical metrics used in machine learning and information theory for sequences: Shannon entropy and conditional entropy, cross-entropy and negative log-likelihood, Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and model uncertainty, perplexity, Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent metric, methods for estimating next-token or next-byte entropy, and the relationship between entropy and compressibility. Throughout, we emphasize intuition, concrete formulas, and how these quantities are used to compare models and assess compressibility of data.

# Shannon entropy and conditional entropy

Shannon entropy formalizes the notion of uncertainty or information content in a random variable. For a discrete random variable $X$ taking values in a finite set $\mathcal{X}$ with probability mass function $p(x)=\Pr(X=x)$, the Shannon entropy is defined as
\[
H(X) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} p(x) \log p(x).
\]
The logarithm base determines the units: base 2 gives bits, base $e$ gives nats. Intuitively, $H(X)$ is the expected number of bits needed to describe a draw from $X$ under an optimal prefix-free code. Low entropy means outcomes are predictable (one or a few values have high probability); high entropy means outcomes are more uniformly distributed and less predictable.

Entropy has several useful properties. It is nonnegative, and it is maximized for a given alphabet size when the distribution is uniform: $H(X)\le \log |\mathcal{X}|$. It is additive for independent variables: if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y)$. Entropy also connects to variance-like concepts but is fundamentally about information rather than spread.

Conditional entropy measures the remaining uncertainty about one variable given knowledge of another. For discrete variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint distribution $p(x,y)$, conditional entropy is
\[
H(X|Y) = -\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} p(y) \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} p(x|y) \log p(x|y).
\]
Equivalently, $H(X|Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)$. Conditional entropy captures the expected uncertainty in $X$ after observing $Y$. If $X$ is completely determined by $Y$, then $H(X|Y)=0$. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $H(X|Y)=H(X)$ because observing $Y$ provides no information about $X$.

Conditional entropy is especially important for sequences. For a stochastic process $X_1,X_2,\dots$, we often care about the uncertainty of the next element given the past. If we write the past as $X_{<t}=(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1})$, the next-step conditional entropy is $H(X_t|X_{<t})$. For a stationary process, the average conditional entropy per symbol, sometimes called the entropy rate $H_{\infty}$, is
\[
H_{\infty} = \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{1}{t} H(X_1,\dots,X_t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} H(X_t|X_{<t}).
\]
This entropy rate governs the long-run per-symbol uncertainty and is the key quantity linking probabilistic models and compression.

# Cross-entropy and negative log-likelihood

Cross-entropy measures the discrepancy between two distributions and arises naturally when we evaluate probabilistic models. Suppose there is a true data distribution $p$ (unknown, but from which samples are drawn) and a model distribution $q$ that assigns probabilities to outcomes. The cross-entropy of $p$ with respect to $q$ is
\[
H(p,q) = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log q(x).
\]
Cross-entropy can be interpreted as the expected number of bits required to encode a sample drawn from $p$ if we use a code optimized for $q$ rather than for $p$. If $q=p$, then $H(p,q)=H(p)$; otherwise $H(p,q)>H(p)$ because using the wrong distribution increases expected code length.

In machine learning, negative log-likelihood (NLL) over data is exactly a sample-based estimator of cross-entropy. Given data samples $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)}$ drawn i.i.d. from $p$, the average negative log-likelihood of model $q$ is
\[
\text{NLL} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log q(x^{(i)}).
\]
By the law of large numbers, as $N\to\infty$ this converges to $H(p,q)$. Minimizing NLL during training is equivalent to minimizing cross-entropy, which in turn minimizes the KL divergence from $p$ to $q$ (see next section).

When dealing with sequences or conditional prediction, cross-entropy generalizes to conditional cross-entropy. For sequence data with distribution $p$ over sequences and a model giving conditional probabilities $q(x_t|x_{<t})$, the average token-level cross-entropy is
\[
H(p,q) = -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log q(x_t|x_{<t})\right],
\]
or per-token version dividing by $T$. The negative log-likelihood used to train autoregressive models is precisely this conditional cross-entropy.

Cross-entropy is both a loss to optimize and an evaluation metric. It is often more informative than accuracy for probabilistic models because it penalizes overconfident wrong predictions severely and rewards well-calibrated probabilities even when the most likely token is correct.

# KL divergence and model uncertainty

Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence quantifies how one probability distribution diverges from another. For discrete distributions $p$ and $q$ on the same support, the KL divergence from $p$ to $q$ is
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = H(p,q)-H(p).
\]
KL divergence is nonnegative and zero if and only if $p=q$ almost everywhere. It is asymmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ in general, and this asymmetry reflects different operational meanings in estimation and hypothesis testing.

In modeling, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ measures how much extra expected code length is incurred by using $q$ instead of the true $p$. Minimizing the expected negative log-likelihood of $q$ on samples from $p$ implicitly minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ because
\[
\text{NLL} = H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q).
\]
Since $H(p)$ is fixed with respect to $q$, minimizing NLL reduces $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$.

KL divergence also helps formalize model uncertainty and what it means for a model to be "wrong." If $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ is large, then $q$ places mass in very different places than $p$, and samples are not well explained by $q$. In practice, $p$ is unknown and we estimate KL divergence via NLL on held-out data, which provides an empirical lower bound on $H(p)$ plus the KL gap.

KL divergence connects to Bayesian reasoning about uncertainty. If we interpret $q$ as a model distribution conditioned on parameters and $p$ as the true data-generating distribution, then Bayesian model averaging or posterior distributions aim to reduce divergence. In variational inference, one minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ in a different direction because it is computationally simpler, and the asymmetry yields different approximations (e.g., mode-seeking vs. mass-covering behavior).

Model uncertainty appears in two distinct senses: aleatoric and epistemic. Aleatoric uncertainty is the irreducible randomness in the data: it is captured by the entropy $H(p)$ or conditional entropy $H(X|Y)$. Epistemic uncertainty comes from limited data or model capacity and corresponds to uncertainty about the correct $p$; its effect is captured by $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ — if the model class cannot represent $p$ or if parameters are poorly estimated, the KL divergence remains large and the model is miscalibrated or under-confident/over-confident in regions.

# Perplexity and its interpretation

Perplexity is a commonly used evaluation metric for probabilistic sequence models, particularly in language modeling. For a distribution $p$ over strings or a model $q$, the perplexity associated with cross-entropy $H(p,q)$ (measured in nats or bits depending on log base) is defined as
\[
\text{Perplexity} = 2^{H(p,q)} \quad\text{(when entropy is in bits)}.
\]
If we use natural logarithms, the base of the exponential should be $e$, but the more common convention in language modeling is to use base 2 and report perplexity in terms of bits. Perplexity can be interpreted as the effective branching factor: on average, the model is as uncertain as if it had to choose uniformly among $\text{Perplexity}$ equally likely options at each prediction.

Concretely, for token-level cross-entropy measured in bits per token, $\text{Perplexity} = 2^{H/\text{token}}$. If a unigram uniform distribution over $V$ tokens yields entropy $\log_2 V$ bits per token, then perplexity equals $V$. Lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance: a perfect model that always assigns probability 1 to the true next token has $H=0$ and perplexity $1$; a worse model has higher perplexity.

Perplexity is multiplicative over time steps for independent predictions: if the per-token cross-entropy is constant, perplexity reflects the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities the model assigns to true tokens. This multiplicative interpretation makes perplexity sensitive to systematic errors in long predictions. When comparing models, a model with half the cross-entropy yields a perplexity equal to the square root of the original perplexity, so perplexity differences can magnify small entropy differences.

While perplexity provides an intuitive measure, it has limitations. It depends on the tokenization: different tokenizers change the vocabulary size and token boundaries and therefore the per-token entropy. Hence comparing perplexity across models that use different tokenizations is not straightforward. Moreover, perplexity is an average-case metric and may obscure distributional mismatches such as poor calibration on rare events. For these reasons, other measures like bits-per-byte (BPB) or negative log-likelihood per raw unit can be preferable when comparing across tokenizers or seeking a tokenization-agnostic view.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent metric

Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) is a metric designed to evaluate models on raw byte sequences rather than tokenized units, making it independent of any particular tokenizer. For text or binary data, consider representing sequences as sequences of bytes $b_1,b_2,\dots,b_T$ (each $b_t\in\{0,\dots,255\}$). Given a model $q$ that assigns conditional probabilities $q(b_t|b_{<t})$, the average negative log probability per byte, measured in bits, is
\[
\text{BPB} = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \log_2 q(b_t|b_{<t}).
\]
BPB directly measures how many bits the model assigns, on average, to each raw byte of data. It has a natural interpretation in terms of compression: an ideal arithmetic coder using the model $q$ would compress the sequence to about $T\cdot\text{BPB}$ bits. Because it is defined on bytes, BPB allows fair comparisons across models that use different tokenizations, byte-level models, or character models.

BPB relates to cross-entropy: it is simply the conditional cross-entropy per byte between the true byte distribution $p$ and the model $q$, measured in bits. In practice, computing BPB requires models that can provide probabilities at the byte level. For models defined over tokens (e.g., subword tokenizers), one can convert token probabilities into byte-level probabilities if the tokenizer and encoding mapping are deterministic and invertible, but this is often inconvenient. Using byte-level evaluation datasets and models that directly predict the next byte avoids these complications.

The lower bound for BPB is the entropy rate of the byte source, measured in bits per byte: no model can have BPB smaller than the true conditional entropy per byte $H_{\infty}$ of the generating process. In experimental comparisons, BPB differences are interpretable as the potential improvement in compressed size per byte. For example, a BPB reduction of 0.1 bits/byte on a 1 GB dataset corresponds to roughly $0.1\times$1 billion / 8 = 12.5 MB of saved storage after compression (since 8 bits per byte).

A practical advantage of BPB is its fine granularity: because bytes are small, differences in log-probabilities per byte can accumulate to measurable differences over large corpora. A caveat is that BPB depends on the text encoding (UTF-8 or other encodings), since the representation of characters as bytes affects the byte sequence distribution. To be tokenizer-independent but consistent, experiments should specify the exact byte encoding used.

# Estimating next-token/next-byte entropy

Estimating the entropy of the next token or next byte is crucial for evaluating models and for understanding the intrinsic unpredictability of a data source. The target quantity for a stochastic process is the conditional entropy rate $H(X_t|X_{<t})$. In practice, because the true distribution is unknown, we estimate entropy from data samples and possibly model predictions.

A straightforward estimator for entropy uses the empirical distribution of outcomes. For a collection of contexts (past sequences) observed in data, if we can group identical contexts and record the empirical frequencies of next tokens after each context, the empirical conditional entropy is
\[
\hat{H}_{\text{emp}} = -\sum_{\text{context }c} \hat{p}(c)\sum_x \hat{p}(x|c)\log \hat{p}(x|c),
\]
where $\hat{p}(x|c)=\frac{n(c,x)}{n(c)}$ and $\hat{p}(c)=\frac{n(c)}{\sum_c n(c)}$. This estimator converges to the true conditional entropy if contexts occur sufficiently often. However, in sequences with large or infinite context spaces (e.g., natural language), most specific contexts are rare and empirical frequencies are noisy, so direct empirical estimation is impractical.

Model-based estimation uses a fitted probabilistic model $q$ to estimate conditional probabilities and thus entropy. Using held-out data drawn from $p$, we compute
\[
\widehat{H}(p,q) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log q(x_t^{(i)}|x_{<t}^{(i)}),
\]
which estimates the cross-entropy $H(p,q)$. If we have a very good model approximating $p$, this will be close to the true entropy $H(p)$. The gap $\widehat{H}(p,q)-\widehat{H}(p)$ corresponds to KL divergence. Because direct estimation of $H(p)$ is infeasible without knowing $p$, model-based cross-entropy is a practical proxy.

There are also specialized estimators for entropy rate based on compression algorithms. If an asymptotically optimal compressor is applied to a long sequence, the compressed size per symbol approaches the entropy rate. So using a strong compressor (e.g., PPM or modern context-mixing compressors) and measuring compressed length gives an empirical, model-free estimate of entropy rate. This connection is exploited in practice: for binary sequences, run-length encoding or arithmetic coding based on context models produces compressed sizes that approximate the lower bound $H_{\infty}$.

Another approach uses held-out predictive loss curves as context length increases. One can estimate $H(X_t|X_{t-k}^{t-1})$ for finite $k$ and see how it approaches a limiting value as $k$ grows. For stationary ergodic processes, conditional entropies are nonincreasing in $k$ and converge to the entropy rate. Practically, one trains models that use limited context sizes and measures per-token cross-entropy on validation data for various context lengths; the plateau indicates effective memory of the process.

Estimators have trade-offs: empirical counts are unbiased but high-variance for rare contexts; model-based estimates are biased toward the model but can leverage generalization; compression-based estimates are model-free but depend on the compressor's effectiveness. Good practice is to use several methods where feasible and to report limitations, including dependence on dataset size, stationarity assumptions, and context coverage.

# Relationship between entropy and compressibility

Entropy and compressibility are intimately connected by Shannon's source coding theorem. For a discrete memoryless source with entropy $H(X)$, the theorem states that it is impossible to losslessly compress the source, on average, to fewer than $H(X)$ bits per symbol, and it is possible to approach this bound using proper coding schemes as the block size grows. More generally, for stationary ergodic sources, the entropy rate $H_{\infty}$ is the lower bound on achievable expected compressed bits per symbol in the limit of long sequences.

Operationally, if you have a model $q$ estimating $p$, you can use arithmetic coding or range coding to convert the model's per-symbol probabilities into a near-optimal prefix code. The expected length of the encoded sequence will be close to the cumulative negative log probability under $q$:
\[
\mathbb{E}[\text{code length}] \approx -\sum_{t} \log_2 q(x_t|x_{<t}).
\]
Thus cross-entropy directly predicts practical compressed size when combined with an ideal coder. The better the probabilistic model approximates the true distribution (lower KL divergence), the closer the compressed size will come to the entropy limit.

There are practical caveats. Shannon's result is asymptotic: finite-block codes incur overheads due to integer-length constraints and model description costs. Universal compression algorithms aim to compress without prior knowledge of the source distribution but typically converge to entropy rate with a redundancy term that vanishes with increasing sequence length. For many natural data sources (text, images, audio), strong context models and compressors can achieve near-entropy efficiency in practice for large files.

Compression also provides a tool for empirical entropy estimation and model comparison. If compressor A produces smaller compressed size on a dataset than compressor B, then the model implicit in A achieves smaller cross-entropy (or lower BPB) on that data, indicating a better fit to the empirical distribution. This is why researchers sometimes use standardized compression benchmarks (e.g., compression on Wikipedia or books) to compare language models in a tokenizer-independent way.

Finally, the relationship between entropy and compressibility explains why entropy rates differ across representations. For example, character-level entropy is different from byte-level or token-level entropy because the symbol alphabets and dependencies differ. A carefully chosen representation that captures higher-level structure (e.g., subword tokens that align with morphemes) may reduce per-symbol entropy and yield better compression given the same modeling power. However, because entropy rates are measured in bits per basic unit, if you compare per-byte entropy, the fundamental limit is invariant: the minimum achievable bits per raw byte is determined only by the true byte-level entropy of the source under the chosen encoding.

Conclusion

This chapter has covered the key information-theoretic concepts used to quantify uncertainty and evaluate sequence models. Shannon entropy and conditional entropy formalize unpredictability; cross-entropy and negative log-likelihood provide practical loss functions and evaluation metrics; KL divergence captures model mismatch and epistemic uncertainty. Perplexity translates entropy into an intuitive branching-factor measure but depends on tokenization; Bits-Per-Byte offers a tokenizer-independent alternative aligned with compression. Methods for estimating next-token or next-byte entropy include empirical counts, model-based cross-entropy, and compression-based approaches. Finally, Shannon's coding theorem links entropy directly to compressibility: better probabilistic models yield smaller compressed sizes, with the entropy rate as the theoretical lower bound. These ideas form the foundation for understanding and evaluating modern sequence models and for reasoning about the limits of compression and prediction.
