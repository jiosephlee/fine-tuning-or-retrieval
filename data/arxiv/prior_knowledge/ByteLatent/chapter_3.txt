Chapter 3: The Transformer: Architecture and Components

Introduction

The Transformer architecture is the core building block for modern large language models (LLMs). Its success arises from a small set of modular components that, when combined, enable efficient parallel computation, long-range dependency modeling, and scalable training. In this chapter we will develop a detailed understanding of those components: attention (both scaled dot-product and multi-head), the feed-forward network (FFN) with modern activations, residual connections and LayerNorm variants, positional encodings (absolute, relative, and rotary), causal masking used in autoregressive models, and the typical parameterization choices and layer dimensions used in practice. Our goal is to explain not only the formulas but the intuition behind them and the practical implications of design choices so that you can read and reason about contemporary research papers and implementations.

# Scaled dot-product attention and multi-head attention

Attention is the mechanism that allows a model to weigh the importance of different positions in a sequence when computing a representation for a position. At the core of the Transformer attention is scaled dot-product attention. Suppose we have an input sequence of length $n$ that has been mapped to a sequence of vectors (often referred to as tokens embedded into a "model" space) with dimensionality $d_{\text{model}}$. We form three matrices: queries $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$, keys $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_k}$, and values $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}$. In practice, $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are obtained by applying learned linear projections to the same input representations (or to different inputs in encoder-decoder attention).

Scaled dot-product attention computes an attention-weighted sum of the values, where the weights come from the similarity of queries to keys. The raw attention logits are the dot products $QK^\top$. To prevent these dot products from growing large in magnitude when $d_k$ is large, they are scaled by $1/\sqrt{d_k}$. The attention output is then
\[
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V,
\]
where $M$ is an optional mask (discussed later). The softmax is applied row-wise so each query yields a probability distribution over keys. Intuitively, the query vector asks "which keys are most relevant right now?" The keys answer by giving similarity scores; softmax turns those into mixing weights; and values carry the content that gets mixed into the output.

Multi-head attention extends this idea by allowing the model to compute multiple, parallel attention "heads" that each focus on different subspaces or patterns. We split the model dimension $d_{\text{model}}$ into $h$ heads, each with head dimension $d_k = d_{\text{model}}/h$ (commonly $d_k = d_v$). For head $i$, learned projection matrices $W_i^Q$, $W_i^K$, and $W_i^V$ map the input to $Q_i$, $K_i$, and $V_i$. Each head computes its own attention:
\[
\text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(Q_i, K_i, V_i).
\]
The heads are then concatenated and linearly projected with a matrix $W^O$ to produce the final multi-head output:
\[
\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h) W^O.
\]
The value of multiple heads is both expressive and practical: different heads can capture different relations (syntactic, semantic, positional biases) or attend to different positions simultaneously, while the concatenation mixes these diverse signals back into the model space. From an implementation viewpoint, multi-head attention is efficiently implemented by performing the projection to a combined $3d_{\text{model}}$ matrix and then reshaping to $(\text{batch}, \, n, \, h, \, d_k)$ to compute attention per head in parallel on hardware accelerators.

Several implementation details are important. First, the choice of scaling $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ is crucial for stable gradients; without it, the softmax can be pushed into regions with tiny gradients for large $d_k$. Second, numerical stability in the softmax is improved by subtracting the maximum logit per row, and masks (e.g., causal masks) are implemented by adding large negative values to forbidden logits. Third, some variants use $d_v$ different from $d_k$, but most common designs set $d_v = d_k$ and use $d_{\text{model}} = h \cdot d_k$.

# Feed-forward networks (FFN) and activation functions (GELU, SwiGLU)

Between attention sublayers, each Transformer layer contains a position-wise feed-forward network. This FFN is applied independently at each position (token), and it is the main source of per-position nonlinearity and cross-channel mixing. The standard FFN has two linear layers with a nonlinearity between them:
\[
\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2,
\]
where $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$, $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{ff}}}$, and $\phi$ is an activation function. The intermediate dimensionality $d_{\text{ff}}$ is typically larger than $d_{\text{model}}$ (commonly 2--4$\times$ or more), allowing the FFN to expand the representation and implement complex nonlinear transformations before projecting back.

The choice of activation function $\phi$ meaningfully affects training dynamics and modeling power. Two popular modern activations are GELU and SwiGLU.

The Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) is defined elementwise as
\[
\text{GELU}(x) = x \cdot \Phi(x),
\]
where $\Phi(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian. A common practical approximation is
\[
\text{GELU}(x) \approx 0.5 x \left(1 + \tanh\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,(x + 0.044715 x^3)\right)\right).
\]
GELU smoothly gates inputs: small inputs are downweighted, large positive inputs are passed through, and negative inputs are partially suppressed. Compared to ReLU, GELU is smoother and often yields better performance in Transformers.

SwiGLU is a gated linear unit variant that has proven effective in recent LLMs. The general GLU family splits the intermediate representation into two parts and uses one part to gate the other. SwiGLU combines a SiLU (also called Swish) nonlinearity with this gating structure. Concretely, suppose $W_1$ is replaced by two matrices $W_{1a}$ and $W_{1b}$ producing vectors $u = W_{1a} x$ and $v = W_{1b} x$. Then
\[
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = W_2 \left( u \odot \text{SiLU}(v) \right) + b_2,
\]
where $\text{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \sigma(z)$ and $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication. The gating in SwiGLU can increase representational capacity and often reduces parameter counts while improving performance compared to plain GELU FFNs; many modern models set $d_{\text{ff}}$ smaller when using SwiGLU but achieve equal or better performance.

Both GELU and SwiGLU support efficient parallel computation since the nonlinearity is applied elementwise. Choice between them is empirical: SwiGLU and other Gated Linear Unit variants (GLU, GEGLU) are frequently used in the most recent architectures because they improve throughput and final performance, but GELU remains standard and performs well.

# Residual connections and pre/post LayerNorm

Residual (skip) connections and Layer Normalization are critical for training deep Transformers. Residual connections were introduced to facilitate gradient flow across many layers by providing direct, short paths from inputs to outputs. In a Transformer layer, a common pattern for a single sublayer (attention or FFN) is:
\[
\text{output} = \text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x)),
\]
which is sometimes called "post-LayerNorm" because the normalization is applied after the residual addition. Here $x$ is the input to the sublayer. The residual connection $x + \text{Sublayer}(x)$ ensures that if the sublayer is close to zero, the layer can pass $x$ through unchanged; this stabilizes training and allows deeper stacks of blocks.

An alternative arrangement is "pre-LayerNorm", in which the input is first normalized, then passed to the sublayer, and finally the residual is added:
\[
\text{output} = x + \text{Sublayer}(\text{LayerNorm}(x)).
\]
Pre-LayerNorm has become popular because it tends to improve training stability, especially for very deep models. With pre-LayerNorm, gradients propagate through the LayerNorm but do not have to flow through both normalization and the sublayer nonlinearities to reach earlier layers, which reduces issues such as gradient vanishing or exploding. Practically, pre-LayerNorm often allows for larger learning rates and more stable convergence; however, some recent work shows that small differences in parameterization or initialization can make post-LayerNorm viable as well.

Why does the placement of LayerNorm matter? LayerNorm standardizes the activations across channels for each token, preventing some channels from dominating and keeping the scale of inputs to subsequent layers more consistent. When LayerNorm is applied before the sublayer (pre-LN), it constrains the input to the sublayer to a consistent scale; when applied after (post-LN), the residual addition is normalized which can affect the effective update direction for parameters. From a probabilistic and optimizer dynamics perspective, pre-LN tends to produce more stable independent updates per layer, while post-LN may yield slightly better final performance in some regimes because the normalized residual can facilitate representational coupling — but this is empirical and model-dependent.

In addition to LayerNorm placement, another common stabilization mechanism is scaling of the residual or output projections (for example, multiplying the residual branch by a small constant or using scaled initialization). Layer scaling, often implemented as a learned scalar initialized to a small value, can help mitigate early-stage instability. In summary, residuals plus LayerNorm are indispensable for deep Transformer training, and the pre/post placement is a practical hyperparameter with implications for stability and final performance.

# Positional encodings: absolute, relative, RoPE (rotary embeddings)

Attention is permutation-invariant: if you feed the same multiset of token embeddings in different orders, dot-product attention alone cannot distinguish their order. To allow the model to reason about sequence order, we provide positional information. There are several effective approaches: absolute positional encodings, relative positional encodings, and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). Each has different trade-offs.

Absolute positional encodings add or concatenate a positional vector to the token embedding that depends only on the token's index in the sequence. The original Transformer used sinusoidal absolute encodings: for position $p$ and dimension $i$, the encoding is
\[
\text{PE}(p,2i) = \sin\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right),\qquad
\text{PE}(p,2i+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right).
\]
These encodings offer a smooth, unlearned way to represent positions, and they allow extrapolation to longer sequences than seen in training to some extent. Learned absolute positional embeddings are also popular: a table of vectors indexed by position is added to token embeddings. Learned embeddings can be more expressive but typically require the maximum sequence length to be fixed (or rely on interpolation/extrapolation tricks).

Relative positional encodings instead make the attention computation aware of relative distances between query and key positions. This is often implemented by adding a bias to the attention logits that depends on the relative distance $r = p_{\text{query}} - p_{\text{key}}$. For example, one can add a learned scalar $b_r$ per relative offset or a more sophisticated low-rank parametrization. Relative encodings have several advantages: they naturally generalize to sequences of different lengths and emphasize relationships between nearby tokens regardless of absolute positions. Many transformer variants (e.g., Transformer-XL, T5) use forms of relative position bias that improve performance on tasks requiring local generalization or longer contexts.

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) implement positional information by rotating the query and key vectors in a way that depends on position, before computing the dot product. The intuition is that a rotation encodes position as a phase shift, and dot products between rotated vectors reflect relative phase differences corresponding to relative positions. Concretely, partition the $d_{\text{model}}$ dimension into pairs so that each pair $(x_{2i}, x_{2i+1})$ represents a 2D vector. For position $p$, apply a position-dependent rotation matrix $R(p)$ to the pair. If $q$ and $k$ are query and key vectors originating from positions $p_q$ and $p_k$, then the attention logit component for that pair is $(R(p_q) q_{(2i)})^\top (R(p_k) k_{(2i)})$. Using properties of rotation matrices, this equals $q_{(2i)}^\top R(p_q - p_k) k_{(2i)}$, meaning the dot product depends only on the relative position difference $p_q - p_k$. RoPE therefore gives a relative-position effect while being applied as an absolute-position operation at encoding time. RoPE has practical advantages: it is simple to implement, it works well with multi-head attention, and it often improves extrapolation to longer sequences and performance on autoregressive tasks.

Comparing methods, absolute learned embeddings are straightforward and powerful when sequences lengths are within training ranges. Relative biases and RoPE emphasize relative distances and often yield better generalization to longer contexts and better performance on tasks where local relations matter. The precise choice depends on model objectives and the importance of extrapolation; RoPE and relative biases are common in state-of-the-art autoregressive LLMs.

# Causal masking for autoregressive models

Autoregressive language modeling aims to predict the next token given past tokens. To enforce causality—preventing the model from attending to future tokens during training—we use a causal mask that blocks attention from positions to any subsequent positions. In practice, this is implemented by adding a mask matrix $M$ to the scaled dot-product logits, where $M_{ij} = -\infty$ (or a large negative number) if key position $j$ is greater than query position $i$ (i.e., if $j > i$) and $M_{ij} = 0$ otherwise. The attention computation becomes:
\[
\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right),
\]
ensuring that after the softmax, the attention weights for forbidden positions are zero.

Causal masking affects both training and inference. During training we can compute attention for all positions in parallel by applying the causal mask to the full $n\times n$ attention matrix. During autoregressive generation (inference), we commonly perform incremental decoding where we maintain cached keys and values for previous tokens and compute attention for the new query against that cache, again ensuring keys corresponding to future positions are absent. Properly implemented caching reduces computation per generated token and allows efficient long-context generation.

Causal masking interacts with positional encoding choices. For absolute learned embeddings, the model can still see absolute indices up to the maximum length; for RoPE or relative encodings, the relative position information interacts directly with the masked attention mechanics. Care must be taken with RoPE extrapolation and cache reuse: rotary embeddings use position indices, so when decoding with an incremental cache we must apply the correct rotations matching the absolute position of the query and the cached keys, or else modify the scheme to use relative indexing.

Finally, causal masking is crucial not only for correctness but for representation behavior: it produces the autoregressive inductive bias where each token's representation is formed only from earlier context. This results in left-to-right dependencies learned by the model and supports likelihood-based training objectives.

# Parameterization choices and typical layer dimensions

Designing a Transformer requires choosing a number of architectural hyperparameters. These determine model capacity, compute cost, parameter count, and memory usage. The principal parameters are the model dimension $d_{\text{model}}$, the number of attention heads $h$, the head dimension $d_k$ (usually $d_k = d_{\text{model}}/h$), the feed-forward inner dimension $d_{\text{ff}}$, and the number of layers $L$. Additionally, choices about activation functions, normalization placement, positional encodings, and parameter tying affect performance.

Typical ratios and magnitudes used in practice are:

- Model dimension $d_{\text{model}}$: Common values range from a few hundred for small models (e.g., 256, 512) to several thousand for large models (e.g., 1024, 2048, 4096). Many high-performance LLMs use values like 768, 1024, 1536, 2048, 4096, or 8192 depending on total parameter targets.

- Number of heads $h$: Often chosen so that $d_k$ is in a modest range (e.g., 64, 128). For instance, for $d_{\text{model}}=1536$, one might use $h=24$ giving $d_k=64$. For very large models, $h$ might range from 16 to 128. Keeping $d_k$ reasonably small helps with computational efficiency and stable attention scaling.

- Head dimension $d_k$ and value dimension $d_v$: Commonly $d_k=d_v=64$ or 128. Keeping $d_k$ constant across layers is typical, though some variants experiment with varying head sizes.

- Feed-forward dimension $d_{\text{ff}}$: Historically $d_{\text{ff}}=4d_{\text{model}}$ in the original Transformer. Many modern LLMs use $d_{\text{ff}}$ in the range $2d_{\text{model}}$ to $6d_{\text{model}}$ depending on compute budget and activation choice. When using gated activations (SwiGLU), it is common to set $d_{\text{ff}} = 2 \times d_{\text{model}}$ but with internal gating the effective capacity increases, so this smaller multiplier suffices.

- Number of layers $L$: Depth trades off with width and determines sequence modeling depth. For large LLMs, $L$ may range from dozens to hundreds. For example, some models have $L=24, 48, 96$, or more, balancing with $d_{\text{model}}$ to hit a parameter target.

- Parameter sharing and embeddings: Token embeddings and the final output projection (the "language model head") are often tied to share parameters, reducing parameter count and encouraging consistency. Layer-wise parameter sharing (reusing the same Transformer block multiple times) is less common in very large models but appears in memory- and parameter-efficient designs.

- Bias terms and output projections: Some architectures remove biases from linear layers or attention projections to reduce parameter count or for better numerical properties. The output projection $W^O$ in multi-head attention typically maps the concatenated heads back to $d_{\text{model}}$ and is essential unless you choose an architecture that eliminates it.

A canonical example: a "base" Transformer used in many papers has $d_{\text{model}}=512$, $h=8$ (so $d_k=64$), $d_{\text{ff}}=2048$, and $L=6$ (layers). Modern LLMs might scale these to $d_{\text{model}}=2048$, $h=32$, $d_{\text{ff}}=8192$, and $L=48$, producing billions of parameters. Practical engineering also considers per-layer parameter budgets: attention parameters per layer are dominated by the projection matrices of size $3d_{\text{model}}^2$ (for query/key/value combined) plus $d_{\text{model}}^2$ for $W^O$, and the FFN contributes approximately $2d_{\text{model}}d_{\text{ff}}$ parameters. Thus the parameter count scales roughly as $O(L d_{\text{model}} d_{\text{ff}})$ plus $O(L d_{\text{model}}^2)$ for attention projections.

When choosing dimensions, one must balance compute and memory. Larger $d_{\text{model}}$ increases per-token compute quadratically in practice for attention (with $O(n^2 d_{\text{model}})$ for sequence length $n$) and linearly for FFN. Number of heads affects parallelism and head expressivity, while $d_{\text{ff}}$ controls per-position MLP capacity. Empirical tuning and compute constraints guide final choices; the trend in modern LLM design is to scale width and depth jointly to maximize performance per compute.

# Bringing it together: common layer block and training considerations

A typical Transformer block for an autoregressive decoder stack using pre-LayerNorm and SwiGLU-style FFNs looks like this: given input $x$,

1. Compute $x_1 = \text{LayerNorm}(x)$.
2. Compute multi-head attention output $a = \text{MultiHead}(x_1, x_1, x_1)$ with causal masking, then form $x' = x + a$ (residual add).
3. Compute $x_2 = \text{LayerNorm}(x')$.
4. Compute FFN output $f = \text{FFN}(x_2)$ (e.g., SwiGLU or GELU), then form $y = x' + f$.

This arrangement yields stable training and clear causal semantics. Using RoPE for positional encoding, attention uses rotated queries and keys prior to their dot-product. During autoregressive sampling, cached keys and values must be rotated with the same positional indices used in training.

Training considerations include initialization schemes that preserve signal variance across layers, learning rate schedules with warm-up and decay, careful batching to utilize attention compute effectively, gradient clipping or adaptive optimizers (Adam variants), and regularization such as dropout applied in attention and FFN sublayers. Layer scaling or small initial residual weights can mitigate early instability in very deep models. Mixed-precision training (floating-point 16 or bfloat16) is common to reduce memory and speed up training, but attention to numerical stability (for example, in softmax and LayerNorm) is required.

Conclusion

Understanding the Transformer requires grasping its modular components and their interplay: attention mechanisms (scaled dot-product and multi-head) provide flexible context-dependent mixing; FFNs with activations like GELU or SwiGLU supply per-position nonlinear capacity; residuals and LayerNorm placement govern training dynamics; positional encodings inject sequence order information using absolute, relative, or rotary methods; causal masking enforces autoregressive constraints; and parameterization choices determine capacity and efficiency. Together these components form a compact, powerful, and highly tunable architecture that underpins most modern LLMs. With the detailed mechanics and intuitions in this chapter, you should be equipped to read architectures and experiment with design choices in research and implementation.
