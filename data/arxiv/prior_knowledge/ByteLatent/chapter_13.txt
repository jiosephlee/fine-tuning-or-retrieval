Introduction

Evaluation protocols form the backbone of empirical progress in natural language processing and related fields. They provide standardized tasks, datasets, scoring metrics, and experimental conventions that allow researchers to compare models meaningfully, to diagnose strengths and weaknesses, and to measure improvements over time. A good evaluation protocol both quantifies aggregate performance and reveals failure modes: where models fail, why they fail, and what types of improvements matter in practice.

This chapter surveys widely used benchmarks and metrics for classification, reasoning, code generation, machine translation, and character-level and orthographic tasks, and then turns to robustness testing, with an emphasis on character-level perturbations. For each area we discuss what is measured, how scores are computed, interpretability of the metrics, common pitfalls, and recommended analysis practices. Where appropriate, we present mathematical definitions (in LaTeX) and intuition, and point out how to design experiments that produce trustworthy conclusions.

# Classification and reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC)

Benchmarks for classification and reasoning evaluate models on discrete decision tasks such as multiple-choice questions, commonsense inference, or domain knowledge quizzes. A few widely used datasets illustrate the variety of properties such benchmarks can have: the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark (MMLU), HellaSwag, Physical Interaction Question Answering (PIQA), and the AI2 Reasoning Challenge (ARC). Each was designed with different goals — measuring broad factual knowledge, commonsense physical reasoning, everyday engineering intuition, and elementary-science reasoning respectively — and each has unique evaluation considerations.

MMLU was created to test broad knowledge across many subjects and difficulty levels. It contains multiple-choice questions drawn from 57 tasks spanning STEM, humanities, and professional topics. The key characteristic is breadth: MMLU probes whether a model has memorized or learned general-purpose knowledge. Evaluation is straightforward: compute accuracy, the fraction of questions with the correct multiple-choice answer. When comparing models on MMLU, it is important to control for prompt format, number of few-shot examples, and whether chain-of-thought or other reasoning guidance is allowed, because such protocol details can shift accuracy substantially. Also be mindful of dataset contamination: some model pretraining corpora may contain MMLU questions or their solutions, which inflates scores without corresponding generalization.

HellaSwag emphasizes commonsense reasoning and out-of-distribution robustness. Each example consists of a short context and several candidate endings; the model must select the most plausible ending. HellaSwag was designed to be adversarial: distractor endings are stylistically similar to the true ending, making surface-level token prediction less effective. Metrics are again accuracy. However, when evaluating on HellaSwag, measuring performance in zero-shot versus few-shot settings is informative; human performance is often high, so gap-to-human can indicate remaining challenges. Because HellaSwag tests plausibility rather than factual knowledge, ensemble or calibration-based selection strategies can behave differently than on factual multiple-choice tasks.

PIQA focuses on physical and practical commonsense knowledge: given a goal and two candidate solutions, pick the more physical-plausible method. The binary choice nature simplifies statistics: report accuracy and often per-category breakdowns (e.g., tools, kitchen, sports). PIQA highlights an important theme in evaluation design: tasks requiring procedural or embodied knowledge may require models to possess reasoning about physical constraints rather than only surface-level text matching. Misleadingly high performance on PIQA can arise if models exploit lexical cues or spurious correlations, so probing is often necessary.

ARC targets scientific reasoning for grade-school-level science questions. It exists in two flavors: the Challenge set, consisting of questions that simple retrieval-based systems fail at, and the Easy set of more straightforward questions. ARC emphasizes multi-step reasoning and connects closely with scientific concepts. Evaluation is accuracy; however, because ARC includes questions of varying difficulty and because retrieving supporting facts is sometimes sufficient, careful experiment design is necessary. Researchers often separate a model's ability to retrieve relevant knowledge from its ability to reason over it.

Across these classification and reasoning benchmarks, several methodological caveats apply. First, report not only aggregate accuracy but also confidence intervals or standard errors, especially when comparing models whose scores are close. Second, evaluate under consistent prompting strategies: zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompts should be specified and held constant when comparing models. Third, examine calibration and confidence: a model's softmax output or likelihood ranking can misrepresent actual uncertainty. Finally, consider targeted analyses: per-topic breakdowns, question difficulty stratification, and performance on adversarially filtered subsets can reveal strengths hidden by a single overall accuracy number.

# Code generation evaluations (HumanEval, MBPP) and pass@k

Evaluating code generation models introduces domain-specific goals: syntactic correctness, semantic correctness (the program behaves as intended), and style or efficiency considerations. Two common benchmark suites are HumanEval and the Mostly Basic Python Problems (MBPP). Both provide problem descriptions, function signatures, and reference solutions along with unit tests that operationalize semantic correctness. The most widely reported metric in this area is pass@k, which captures the model's ability to generate at least one correct program among k sampled outputs.

HumanEval consists of programming problems with professional-style prompts and unit tests that validate functional correctness. MBPP contains many short, student-level Python problems with carefully curated prompts and test cases. When evaluating, a model is queried multiple times per problem to produce a set of candidate solutions. The pass@k metric is defined as the probability that at least one of k sampled programs passes all unit tests for a given problem, averaged across problems.

Formally, suppose for a fixed problem the model produces n independent samples, and among them c are correct (i.e., pass the tests). If sampling without replacement or assuming Bernoulli correctness, an unbiased estimator of pass@k for k \le n is given by

pass@k = 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},

which denotes the probability that a randomly chosen subset of size k from the n samples contains at least one correct program. In practice, to estimate pass@k when the model is sampled a moderate number n times and c is observed, the estimator above is commonly used. If n is large or sampling is with replacement, one can equivalently treat each draw as independent with success probability p = c/n and compute

pass@k \approx 1 - (1-p)^k.

This approximation is useful when n is substantially larger than k or when sampling independent draws. When reporting pass@k, specify n (the number of samples used to compute c) and whether the estimator corrects for finite-sampling bias.

There are several important nuances in code evaluation. First, unit tests are an operationalization of correctness; they may be incomplete. A program can overfit the given tests (e.g., by hardcoding outputs) yet fail on unseen inputs. To mitigate this, benchmarks often include multiple hidden tests or use test generation strategies. Second, nondeterministic or time-limited programs can cause flaky test results; robust evaluation requires reproducible runtimes and controlled random seeds when possible. Third, code style and readability are not captured by pass@k; a model could produce correct but unreadable solutions. Fourth, for larger k, pass@k increases, but gains are expensive because sampling multiple candidates scales computation linearly. Thus comparing models at fixed computational budgets (e.g., fixed number of tokens generated, or fixed wall-time) yields a more realistic picture. Finally, when comparing codex-style models, ensure that the temperature and decoding strategy are specified, as temperature affects diversity and thus pass@k.

In addition to pass@k, supplementary metrics include exact match of program text, functional similarity metrics (e.g., unit-test coverage), and human evaluation for subjective qualities like style or clarity. For robust claims, report multiple metrics and analyze failure cases: do failures stem from misunderstanding the prompt, incorrect algorithmic logic, off-by-one errors, or missing edge-case handling?

# Machine translation metrics (BLEU, SentencePiece BLEU, FLORES)

Machine translation (MT) evaluation traditionally distinguishes automatic metrics that correlate with human judgment from human evaluations themselves. Automatic metrics are indispensable for large-scale benchmarking because human evaluation of every system and configuration is expensive. The BLEU score is perhaps the most ubiquitous automatic metric, with later variants and complementing datasets (e.g., SentencePiece BLEU, FLORES) developed to address BLEU's limitations and multilingual coverage.

BLEU measures n-gram overlap between a candidate translation and one or more reference translations, with a brevity penalty to punish overly short outputs. The BLEU score for up to n-gram order \(N\) is computed from modified n-gram precisions \(p_n\), where each matching n-gram in the candidate is clipped to the maximum count appearing in any reference. The geometric mean of precisions is combined with a brevity penalty \(BP\) to yield

\(BLEU = BP \cdot \exp\left( \sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n \log p_n \right),\)

where \(w_n\) are typically uniform weights (e.g., \(w_n = 1/N\)) and the brevity penalty is

\(BP = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } c > r, \\
\exp(1 - r/c) & \text{if } c \le r,
\end{cases}\)

with \(c\) the length of the candidate corpus and \(r\) the effective reference length. Intuitively, BLEU rewards matching phrases of increasing length and penalizes overly short translations.

BLEU's strengths are speed and reproducibility, but it has known weaknesses: it is insensitive to meaning-preserving paraphrases that use different wording, it treats all n-grams equally regardless of semantic importance, and corpus-level BLEU can hide per-sentence failures. To mitigate tokenization and subword mismatches, practitioners commonly use SentencePiece or BPE tokenization and report SentencePiece BLEU, which computes BLEU on subword-tokenized text. SentencePiece BLEU often yields more stable comparisons for models operating with subword vocabularies.

FLORES is a multilingual evaluation dataset designed by Facebook/Meta to provide high-quality, human-translated references for many low- and high-resource language pairs. FLORES focuses on sentence-level evaluation across dozens of languages and is paired with modern tokenization-compatible scoring recipes. Because BLEU can understate quality for morphologically rich languages or for systems producing valid paraphrases, researchers increasingly complement BLEU with newer metrics like chrF (character n-gram F-score), BERTScore (embedding-based similarity), COMET (a learned regression model trained to predict human judgments), or direct human ratings for fluency and adequacy.

When designing MT experiments, follow these practices. Use consistent tokenization and reporting scripts (e.g., SacreBLEU) so scores are reproducible and comparable across papers. Report both corpus-level and sentence-level statistics; include measures of variance if reporting multiple test sets. Where possible, provide multiple reference translations, since BLEU and related scores improve with more reference alternatives. Finally, for multilingual models, measure performance across a variety of languages and domains (in-domain and out-of-domain), and be cautious interpreting absolute BLEU values — improvements in BLEU of a fraction of a point may or may not correspond to meaningful differences in translation quality.

# Character-level benchmarks and manipulation tasks (G2P, orthography tests)

Character-level tasks probe models at a finer granularity than word- or subword-based evaluations. These tasks are important for languages with rich morphology, nonstandard orthographies, or for applications where exact character output matters (e.g., transliteration, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion). Two representative classes of character-level tasks are grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion and orthography manipulation or normalization tests.

G2P tasks map sequences of written characters (graphemes) to sequences of phonetic symbols (phonemes). They are central in text-to-speech, pronunciation modeling, and in speech recognition systems that must reconcile spelling-to-sound irregularities. Evaluation usually measures accuracy of the predicted phoneme sequence against a gold sequence. Exact-match sequence accuracy is common: a predicted phoneme sequence is correct only if it exactly equals the reference. When partial correctness is relevant, edit-distance measures (Levenshtein distance) or phone error rate (PER) are reported. The Levenshtein distance between sequences \(x\) and \(y\) is the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, and substitutions required to transform \(x\) into \(y\); phone error rate is the Levenshtein distance normalized by the length of the reference:

\(PER = \frac{\text{Levenshtein}(pred, ref)}{\text{Length}(ref)}.\)

G2P is a useful diagnostic because it requires the model to learn orthographic rules, irregular exceptions, and sometimes morphological cues.

Orthography tests cover a range of tasks: normalization of text with diacritics, handling of capitalization, punctuation, transliteration between scripts (e.g., Romanization of Cyrillic), and recognition of token boundaries. Such tests are particularly important in multilingual contexts or for low-resource languages where tokenization can dramatically affect downstream performance. Evaluation metrics include character-level accuracy, normalized edit distance, and task-specific measures such as word error rate (WER) when the task is part of a speech or OCR pipeline.

Manipulation tasks can deliberately stress models with small character edits: inserting diacritics, swapping characters that are visually similar, or altering case. These tests reveal whether models depend on brittle surface patterns or have learned robust, higher-level representations. For instance, a model may perform well on sentence-level translation in standard orthography but fail when diacritics are dropped; such failure modes are important to document. In evaluating character-level tasks, one should carefully design test sets that include both frequent regular patterns (to measure consistency) and rare, irregular items (to test generalization), and report per-item difficulty analyses.

# Robustness tests: character-level noise strategies and analysis

Robustness testing measures how small perturbations to inputs affect model outputs. Character-level robustness is especially relevant because many real-world inputs are noisy: users make spelling mistakes, OCR systems introduce errors, and formats differ across platforms. Robustness testing explores model sensitivity to these perturbations and helps identify brittle behavior.

A basic taxonomy of character-level noise strategies includes insertion, deletion, substitution, transposition, homoglyph replacement, case variation, diacritic removal, and subword boundary manipulations. Each strategy probabilistically alters input strings to produce noisy variants. For example, a simple substitution noise model might pick each character independently with probability \(p\) and replace it by a random character from the character set. Transposition swaps adjacent characters; insertion adds a random character at a random position; deletion removes a character. Homoglyph replacement replaces a character by another that looks similar (e.g., replacing Latin "o" with Greek "ο" or Cyrillic "о"). Diacritic removal strips accents, changing "résumé" to "resume"; case variation randomizes capitalization patterns.

When applying these strategies, one must select the perturbation intensity parameter \(p\) carefully. A too-large \(p\) makes the task unrealistic and may obscure useful comparisons; a too-small \(p\) leads to tiny effects that do not stress models. Common practice is to sweep \(p\) (e.g., \(p \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}\)) and plot performance degradation curves. Such curves reveal not only absolute robustness but also how gracefully models degrade.

Analysis of robustness should go beyond aggregate score drops. First, categorize errors by perturbation type: do substitutions cause more failures than deletions? Second, examine localization: do errors near syntactically important tokens (e.g., verbs or numbers) cause bigger failures than errors in function words? Third, inspect model confidence: models may become overconfident on noisy inputs. Measure calibration changes with noise, for example by comparing negative log-likelihood or expected calibration error across noise levels.

Adversarially chosen perturbations are another direction: rather than applying random noise, search for minimal character edits that change the model's prediction. These adversarial edits can be generated via gradient-based methods for differentiable models or via heuristic search. They expose worst-case vulnerabilities and are valuable for stress-testing. However, adversarial settings differ from realistic noise patterns, so both random and adversarial evaluations are informative.

Character-level robustness matters across tasks. For classification and reasoning benchmarks, a single character error can change a number or named entity and flip the correct answer. For code generation, character-level perturbations in a prompt (e.g., misspelling a variable name) can cause compilation errors. For machine translation, noisy source text with missing diacritics can reduce adequacy. Character-level perturbations also interact with tokenization: subword tokenizers may absorb small edits without changing token sequence, but when edits cross subword boundaries or alter rare tokens, they can have outsized effects. Therefore, report experiments both at raw text level and after tokenization.

Practical steps for robust evaluation include: create controlled noise test sets with different perturbation types and intensities; measure and plot performance degradation curves; analyze failure modes qualitatively and quantitatively; measure calibration and confidence; test both random and adversarial perturbations; and evaluate tokenization sensitivity by comparing raw-text perturbations and token-level perturbations. When reporting robustness, contextualize observed fragility: is the fragile behavior likely to occur in real user data, or only under pathological edits? Where applicable, propose simple mitigations (data augmentation with noisy examples, robust tokenization, spelling-correction front-ends, or specialized training objectives) and report their effectiveness.

Conclusion

Benchmarks and evaluation metrics are indispensable tools, but they are also lenses that shape research directions. Choosing the right benchmark and reporting protocol, understanding the metric's meaning and limitations, and conducting careful robustness analyses are all necessary to draw reliable conclusions about model capabilities. Classification and reasoning datasets test general knowledge and commonsense; code generation benchmarks operationalize functional correctness via pass@k; machine translation metrics like BLEU and its variants provide reproducible but imperfect proxies of translation quality; character-level tasks probe fine-grained orthographic and phonetic abilities; and robustness tests uncover brittleness to realistic and adversarial noise. Together these tools enable more complete and honest appraisals of model performance and provide guidance for building more robust and generalizable systems.
