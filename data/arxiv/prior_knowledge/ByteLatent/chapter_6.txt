Introduction

Most natural language processing systems work with discrete "tokens" such as words, subword units, or morphemes. Tokenization simplifies modeling by compressing text into sequences of relatively short length and by providing linguistic units that often align with semantics. However, tokenizers introduce design choices and limitations: they can break on rare words, create inconsistent segmentations across languages and scripts, and obscure orthographic patterns that are useful for downstream tasks. Byte- and character-level modeling takes the opposite approach: treat the raw input as a sequence of bytes (or characters) and build models that operate directly on those low-level units. This approach avoids dependence on a separate tokenizer and can improve robustness, multilingual coverage, and morphological sensitivity.

This chapter explains why and when one might prefer byte- or character-level models, what difficulties those models face, and how prior work has attempted to overcome the technical and computational challenges. We will cover core advantages such as out-of-vocabulary handling and exploiting orthographic information; the computational cost of longer sequences and its implications for model design; prominent prior architectures including CharRNNs, ByteNet, and ByT5; strategies to reduce cost such as downsampling and local modeling; and finally evaluation considerations when comparing token-based and byte-based systems.

# Advantages of byte-level models (OOV handling, orthography)

One of the clearest advantages of modeling at the byte or character level is that the input vocabulary is fixed and tiny. For bytes that is the set $\{0,\dots,255\}$ (or the subset actually used by a text encoding such as UTF-8), and for characters in a language the alphabet is typically a few dozen to a few hundred symbols. By contrast, subword or word vocabularies are often tens of thousands of entries. A small, fixed vocabulary immediately solves the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem: any sequence of bytes is representable without the need for special "unknown" tokens or fallback heuristics. This is particularly important for names, typos, product codes, programming identifiers, or languages with productive morphology or compounding (e.g., Turkish, Finnish, or German).

Beyond coverage, modeling at the character/byte level preserves orthographic and morphological cues that tokenizers sometimes hide. Consider the English words "run", "running", "rerun", and "runs". A subword tokenizer might split these in various ways depending on frequency and training corpus, producing tokens that obfuscate shared morphemes. A character-level model, however, sees the shared sequence of letters and can learn representations that capture morphological alternations and orthographic patterns. This is particularly valuable in low-resource settings or for rare inflectional forms, where learning that "-ing" signals a gerund can transfer across many stems.

Byte-level modeling also helps with multilingual models and scripts. When building a multilingual system, having a single fixed byte-level input avoids the need to construct and manage separate token vocabularies for each language or to balance subword allocations. While character sets themselves differ (Latin, Cyrillic, Devanagari, Han, etc.), operating over bytes with a consistent encoding like UTF-8 provides a universal input layer. This simplifies deployment and can make transfer across languages easier because the model does not need to learn a different tokenization scheme per language.

Another advantage is robustness to noisy text: social media, OCR outputs, or ASR transcriptions often contain misspellings, nonstandard punctuation, or ad-hoc tokens (URLs, emoticons). Byte/character models can often handle these naturally since they can represent arbitrary byte sequences. This improves graceful degradation when inputs deviate from canonical text.

Finally, certain tasks inherently require fine-grained control over characters: language identification for short snippets, spelling correction, diacritic restoration, text normalization, or program synthesis where identifiers matter at the character level. For these tasks, tokenization may erase the necessary information, while byte-level models can directly attend to the relevant features.

# Sequence length inflation and computational challenges

Working at the byte or character level has a clear cost: sequences become longer. Roughly speaking, converting a tokenized sequence into bytes or characters increases length by a factor that depends on the average token length. For English text, a typical word might be 4–6 characters; with spaces and punctuation included, a sentence that is 20 tokens might become a sequence of 80–120 characters. If the model uses UTF-8 bytes, non-ASCII scripts may further expand length per character. In all cases, the number of modeling steps $n$ grows, and many neural architectures have computation or memory costs that scale superlinearly with $n$.

The most commonly discussed complexity arises in attention-based transformers. The standard self-attention mechanism computes pairwise interactions among tokens, so the time and memory complexity per layer are approximately $O(n^2 d)$ and $O(n^2)$ respectively (where $n$ is sequence length and $d$ is hidden dimensionality). Doubling $n$ quadruples the attention computation and memory footprint. This quadratic scaling makes it expensive to process long byte-level sequences, especially for deep models with many layers.

Recurrent models have linear per-step complexity, but their sequential nature limits parallelism and increases wall-clock training time. For example, a recurrent model processes $n$ time steps, leading to $O(n d^2)$ operations for some implementations, and dependencies prevent easy parallelization across time steps as with transformers.

Longer sequences also change optimization dynamics and the effective receptive field. To capture dependencies that span many characters, models must propagate information across more steps, which can increase training difficulty (vanishing/exploding gradients in recurrent nets, or the need for many attention layers in transformers). The effective context window becomes more demanding: a token-level model with a context window of 512 tokens might cover many more characters than a byte-level model with the same positional cutoff. This can harm performance for tasks that require long-range semantics unless the model and training regime explicitly account for it.

Memory bandwidth and I/O must also be considered. Training on longer sequences increases data transfer between memory and compute units, which can become a bottleneck on GPUs/TPUs. Larger batches exacerbate the problem, so practitioners often reduce batch size when switching to bytes, which can affect optimization stability and convergence speed.

Finally, longer sequences affect downstream inference latency and throughput. Even if a byte-level model is trained successfully, generating text token-by-token (or byte-by-byte) may be slower, and beam search or sampling procedures are more expensive because they operate on more positions. These practical trade-offs often determine engineering choices: sometimes the theoretical benefits of byte-level modeling are balanced against the increased computational cost.

# Prior byte/character models (CharRNNs, ByteNet, ByT5)

The research literature contains a range of architectures that model text at the character or byte level. These differ in their core building blocks (RNNs, convolutions, transformers), their treatment of long-range dependencies, and the trade-offs they make between performance and efficiency.

CharRNNs and related recurrent models were among the earliest widely-used character-level approaches. A CharRNN uses an RNN (vanilla, LSTM, or GRU) to process one character at a time and predict the next character (in language modeling) or produce representations for downstream tasks. Early work showed that CharRNNs could generate realistic-looking text, capture morphological patterns, and handle OOV words gracefully. However, CharRNNs suffer from limitations: they are sequential in nature, which limits parallelism during training, and they often struggle with very long-range dependencies. Techniques such as gated recurrent units, LSTMs, and gradient clipping helped, and attention mechanisms were later added to alleviate the long-range problem, but the fundamental sequential cost remains.

ByteNet, introduced by Kalchbrenner et al., proposed a convolutional architecture for byte-level sequence modeling. ByteNet uses dilated 1D convolutions to enlarge the receptive field exponentially with depth while preserving linear computational complexity in $n$ (sequence length) for each layer. Dilations and residual connections allow the model to efficiently capture long-range dependencies without the sequential bottleneck of RNNs. ByteNet was designed for sequence transduction tasks and stacked convolutional modules in encoder-decoder forms, enabling parallel training. The key insight was that convolutional layers with increasing dilation rates can achieve a receptive field comparable to deep recurrent or transformer layers while remaining more computation-friendly for long sequences.

ByT5 is a more recent and influential work that adapts the T5 text-to-text transformer architecture to operate directly on bytes. ByT5 processes raw UTF-8 bytes as the input tokenization, leveraging a transformer encoder-decoder stack similar to T5. ByT5 demonstrated that, despite the increased sequence length, a sufficiently large transformer can perform well across many tasks and in some cases outperform subword-based baselines, particularly for tasks sensitive to orthography or requiring robustness to noisy/misspelled inputs. ByT5 also showed that training on bytes can be practical when model size and compute budget are scaled appropriately.

Other notable architectures include hybrid models that combine character-level front-ends with token-level back-ends. For example, models that use convolutional or recurrent "character encoders" to produce token-level embeddings, and then apply a token-level transformer, attempt to merge the best of both worlds: they retain byte-level robustness within a short local window but avoid a global sequence-length explosion. There are also language models built over code points or Unicode characters rather than raw bytes; the engineering differences are subtle but relevant for multilingual handling.

Across these approaches, empirical results reveal consistent patterns. Character/byte models often show improved robustness and better handling of rare forms, but they typically require more parameters or compute to match token-level models on high-level semantic tasks. The trade-off between robustness and efficiency is central: the benefits of operating on raw units are often most visible in niche settings (noise, rare words, certain languages), while generic tasks with large-scale training data sometimes favor subword tokenization for efficiency.

# Strategies for reducing cost (downsampling, local models)

Given the computational burden of long byte sequences, several strategies have been proposed to reduce cost while preserving the advantages of raw input modeling. These strategies can be grouped into methods that reduce sequence length (downsampling, pooling), architectures that impose locality (local attention, CNNs), and hybrid schemes that combine low-level character processing with higher-level abstractions.

Downsampling (pooling and merging). One simple idea is to compress sequences early in the model by combining groups of characters or bytes into a single representation. For instance, apply a strided convolution with stride $k$ to map non-overlapping windows of size $k$ into one vector, reducing sequence length by a factor of $k$. Alternatively, use pooling operations (mean, max) over $k$-sized blocks. More sophisticated learned pooling mechanisms can decide which characters within a window are most informative and produce a compact summary. The goal is to preserve local orthographic detail while reducing the number of positions that later expensive layers (e.g., global attention) must process. When downsampling, a common design choice is to downsample only within the encoder or to retain different granularities at different layers, e.g. a hierarchical structure where lower layers operate at character resolution and higher layers at a coarse token-like resolution.

Strided and dilated convolutions. As in ByteNet or WaveNet, strided convolutions and dilations can expand receptive fields while gradually reducing sequence length. A stack of convolutions with alternating strides can reduce $n$ geometrically, making later layers cheaper. Dilated convolutions maintain sequence length but increase receptive field; combining dilation with occasional strided layers gives both broad context and length reduction.

Local attention and blockwise processing. Rather than computing global attention over all $n$ positions, restrict attention to local windows of size $w$. The complexity reduces from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n w)$ per layer. This is the idea behind local or sliding-window attention in models such as Longformer. For many linguistic phenomena, most dependencies are local, so local attention suffices for a large fraction of information. To capture longer dependencies, one can mix local attention with sparse global attention at selected tokens (e.g. special markers or punctuation positions), or use chunked (hierarchical) attention where chunk-level summaries attend globally.

Hierarchical and two-stage models. Hybrid models first encode small character sequences into intermediate "super-tokens" and then process these with a standard token-level model. For example, a character encoder (a small CNN or RNN) aggregates characters within word boundaries or fixed-size chunks into vectors; these vectors become the input to a transformer that operates at a compressed granularity. This approach retains character-level sensitivity in local contexts but benefits from reduced length when modeling long-range semantics. Training can be end-to-end so the character encoder learns to produce representations useful for downstream transformer layers.

Adaptive computation and pooling. Dynamic methods decide, per input or per position, how much computation or compression to apply. Mechanisms such as adaptive span, dynamic pooling, or routing networks can identify parts of the sequence that require fine-grained processing and compress or skip others. This per-instance adaptivity can yield efficiency gains if much of the text is easy to summarize (e.g. repeated patterns or long stretches of whitespace).

Sparse attention and low-rank approximations. Methods that approximate full attention (Nyström, Performer, linearized attention) aim to reduce the quadratic dependence on $n$. These techniques can be applied directly to byte-level sequences to make global attention feasible for longer inputs. While these approximations can reduce cost, they often come with accuracy trade-offs and additional implementation complexity.

Caching and incremental processing. For generation tasks, caching key/value pairs from previous steps avoids recomputing attention over the entire prefix at each step. This is a standard technique for transformers in autoregressive generation and is equally helpful in byte-level decoding. For encoder-decoder models used in seq2seq tasks, encoder-side downsampling reduces the decoder's cross-attention cost because the decoder attends to fewer encoder positions.

In practice, the most effective strategies often combine multiple ideas: use character-level processing to capture orthographic features, downsample via learned convolution/pooling into a compact sequence of "tokens", and apply efficient attention variants or local attention to reduce cost. The engineering choices depend on the target tasks, hardware constraints, and whether training is done from scratch or by adapting pretrained token-level models.

# Evaluation nuances when comparing tokens vs bytes

Comparing token-based and byte-based models requires careful experimental design because the two approaches differ along multiple axes beyond model architecture. Specifically, differences in input length, vocabulary size, and output units can confound direct comparisons. To draw meaningful conclusions, evaluations must control for these factors or at least report them transparently.

Unit mismatch. The most basic issue is that evaluation metrics may be computed on different units. Perplexity is commonly reported for language models, but perplexity is sensitive to tokenization: a model that predicts characters will have a very different numerical perplexity than a model predicting words or subwords. To make perplexities comparable, convert both systems to the same unit (for example, compute bits per byte or bits per character), or report cross-entropy in nats/bits normalized by sequence length in bytes. Bits-per-byte (or bits-per-character) is a tokenizer-agnostic measure and is recommended when comparing models with different granularities.

Downstream task evaluation. For classification, tagging, or generation tasks, ensure that the evaluation metric reflects the same task-level objective. For example, in machine translation or summarization, BLEU or ROUGE are computed on generated text after decoding and detokenization. Byte-level models may decode into byte sequences that need to be converted to Unicode text before computing BLEU. Care must be taken to apply identical detokenization and normalization pipelines so that differences are due to the model and not downstream processing. For token-level metrics (e.g., word error rate), alignments between model outputs and reference text must account for different tokenizations.

Computational cost reporting. Because byte-level models often require more compute to reach the same level of performance, it is essential to report training compute, number of parameters, sequence lengths used, batch sizes, and wall-clock training time. A token-level model that achieves comparable performance with less compute might be preferable in many practical settings. Conversely, a byte-level model that uses significantly more compute but yields robustness benefits may still be attractive for particular applications. Without cost information, claims about superiority are incomplete.

Ablations and hybrid baselines. To isolate the effect of operating on bytes rather than tokens, evaluations should include ablations such as: (1) same architecture trained with tokenization and with bytes, (2) hybrid models that combine character front-ends with token-level back-ends, and (3) varying degrees of downsampling. These comparisons reveal whether improvements arise from the input granularity itself or from other differences (e.g., model capacity, data augmentation, or preprocessing).

Robustness and rare-case evaluation. One of the main motivations for byte-level models is robustness to OOVs, noise, and rare morphological forms. Therefore evaluations should include stress tests: datasets with misspellings, named entities, noisy social media inputs, cross-lingual or script-mixed examples, and low-resource languages. Reporting average performance on a clean benchmark alone can hide significant differences that are crucial in practice.

Interpretability of errors. When a byte-level model makes a mistake, the error analysis needs to reflect fine-grained units. For example, a model might produce a misspelled word that still has acceptable semantics. Token-level metrics might count this as an error while character-level metrics might show smaller deviation. Complementary analyses — measuring semantic similarity, human judgments, and edit distance — can give a fuller picture.

Statistical significance and dataset size. Because some improvements from byte-level models are modest, especially on well-resourced tasks, proper statistical testing and multiple dataset splits are important. Moreover, the benefits of byte models often show up in low-data regimes; therefore, experiments should vary training data size to understand where bytes help most.

In summary, fair evaluation demands unit-normalized metrics, explicit reporting of compute and model capacity, targeted robustness tests, and careful ablations. When these elements are present, comparisons between token-level and byte-level modeling become informative and actionable.

Conclusion and practical guidance

Byte- and character-level modeling offers compelling advantages: fixed and small vocabularies that remove OOV failure modes, preservation of orthographic and morphological information, better multilingual flexibility, and robustness to noise. However, these benefits come with substantial computational and modeling challenges due to longer sequences and different optimization dynamics.

Historically, researchers have tackled these challenges using various architectures: CharRNNs provided early proof-of-concept but were limited by sequential computation; ByteNet and convolutional models exploited dilations and striding to achieve large receptive fields with better parallelism; modern transformer-based approaches like ByT5 have demonstrated that byte-level training is feasible at scale, especially when combined with careful engineering and efficient attention variants.

Practical systems often adopt hybrid solutions: character encoders that produce compact representations, selective downsampling to reduce sequence length before expensive layers, and local or sparse attention mechanisms to limit quadratic costs. Evaluation should be carefully normalized and include robustness tests and cost reporting.

If you plan to experiment with byte-level models, start small: test character encoders with a token-level transformer to validate whether orthographic signals improve your downstream task. Measure bits-per-byte or normalized cross-entropy to compare units fairly. Explore strided convolutions or pooling to reduce sequence length, and consider local attention or Nyström-style approximations if you need large context windows. Finally, prioritize tasks where byte-level strengths matter — noisy text, multilingual transfer, rare morphological forms, or program/identifier-sensitive applications — since these are where the added cost is most likely to pay off.
