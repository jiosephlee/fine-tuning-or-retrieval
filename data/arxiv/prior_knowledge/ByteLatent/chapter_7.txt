Introduction

Modern machine learning systems often need to process data that exhibits structure at multiple spatial, temporal, or semantic scales. Images contain both fine-grained texture and global scene layout; audio and language contain local sequential dependencies and long-range coherence; sensor arrays and scientific data have local correlations and global latent factors. Hierarchical and latent-array architectures are a family of model designs that explicitly separate processing into different scales or representational substrates: densely observed "local" arrays (pixels, tokens, waveform samples) and compact "global" latents that summarize or coordinate those locals. This chapter develops the motivations for this separation, describes common latent-array representations such as Perceiver-style latents, explains how to move information between input and latent spaces via pooling and readout operations, clarifies how encoder-decoder splits allocate responsibilities between local and global modules, and examines causality patterns like block-causal and hierarchical attention masks. The presentation emphasizes intuition and practical considerations so you can read and design models that combine local and global computation.

# Motivation for hierarchical processing (local vs global context)

The central motivation for hierarchical processing is that real-world data exhibits both local dependencies and global structure, and treating all interactions at a single scale is inefficient or ineffective. Consider natural language: the relationships between adjacent words are often crucial for syntax and local meaning, whereas co-reference, topic, and discourse structure require aggregating information across sentences or paragraphs. If a model tries to represent both types of dependency at the same resolution — for example, by applying full self-attention across millions of tokens — the computational cost rises quadratically with sequence length, and the model must store all information at a uniform level of granularity, which is often wasteful.

Hierarchical architectures address this by allocating computation and capacity differently at different scales. Lower-level modules focus on dense, local computation: convolutions, short-range attention, or recurrent updates that exploit locality and translation invariance. Higher-level modules operate on compressed representations or "latents" that carry abstracted, summary information needed for global coordination. This separation allows the model to apply expensive global operations on a small set of latents while using lighter-weight, local operations on the raw inputs.

From an information-theoretic perspective, many domains have a smaller intrinsic dimension for global factors than for local variation. For example, a scene's overall lighting, viewpoint, or object identity can often be described with a low-dimensional latent, while the exact pixel values contain high-dimensional noise and texture. A hierarchical model can map the high-dimensional inputs into a lower-dimensional latent manifold that captures the essential global factors, greatly reducing the cost of global reasoning.

Finally, hierarchical processing supports modularity and inductive bias. Local modules can be designed to be translation-equivariant, causal, or efficient on hardware (e.g., convolutions for images, streaming attention for audio). Global modules can focus on relational reasoning, memory, or planning. This separation often improves sample efficiency and generalization because each module can exploit different priors about the data.

# Latent arrays and Perceiver-style latent representations

Latent arrays are compact, learnable sets of vectors that serve as a fixed-size conduit between raw inputs and higher-level processing. A canonical instantiation is the Perceiver family of models, where a small number $L$ of latent vectors $\{z_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^L$, each of dimension $d_z$, are learned and repeatedly cross-attended with a potentially very large set of input vectors $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^N$. The key idea is to avoid quadratic interactions among inputs by mediating through the latent array: inputs attend to latents and latents attend back to inputs, or latents self-attend, but full input-input attention is never computed directly.

Formally, the simplest Perceiver block alternates two sublayers: a cross-attention from latents to inputs that updates latents, and a self-attention among latents. If we denote the latent matrix as $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_z}$ and the input matrix as $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_x}$, a cross-attention update for latents can be written as
\[
\tilde{Z} = \mathrm{LayerNorm}(Z + \mathrm{CrossAttn}(Z, X, X)),
\]
where $\mathrm{CrossAttn}(Q,K,V)$ computes attention using queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$. Cross-attention compresses information from the $N$ inputs into the $L$ latents, and because $L \ll N$, the computation scales as $O(NL)$ rather than $O(N^2)$. After cross-attention, latents can refine their representation with self-attention:
\[
Z' = \mathrm{LayerNorm}(\tilde{Z} + \mathrm{SelfAttn}(\tilde{Z}, \tilde{Z}, \tilde{Z})).
\]

Perceiver-style latents are flexible: they can be learned tokens with no direct positional anchoring to inputs, or they can be seeded with positional priors or conditional information. Because the latents interact with inputs via learned attention patterns, the model can learn to focus different latents on different modalities, spatial regions, or functional roles. This modularity makes the approach attractive for multi-modal inputs: the same set of latents can attend to visual patches, audio frames, and text tokens, integrating them into a shared semantic space.

The Perceiver architecture also addresses the asymmetry between inputs and latents: whereas inputs are typically fixed in number or extremely large, latents are compact and designed to be reused across many attention layers. Repeated cross-attention allows latents to iteratively refine their summaries, analogous to an encoder iterating until convergence. In practice, a Perceiver stack may alternate cross-attention with several rounds of latent self-attention, enabling increasingly abstract representations without touching the full input-input attention matrix.

# Pooling and readout operations from input to latents

Pooling and readout operations are the mechanisms that transfer information from the high-dimensional input array into the compact latent array and, conversely, project latent information back to the original data space when needed. There are several design choices and trade-offs to consider: deterministic pooling versus learned readout, hard assignment versus soft attention, and whether pooling is symmetrical across inputs.

A simple deterministic pooling operation is global average pooling, which computes a summary vector
\[
s = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i.
\]
This is cheap and permutation-invariant, but it throws away detailed structure and cannot focus on particular inputs. Max-pooling provides a different inductive bias by selecting the most active dimension-wise responses, but it too is information-losing.

Learned readout via attention offers more flexibility. Using cross-attention, each latent $z_\ell$ can compute a soft, differentiable weighted average of input values:
\[
z_\ell \leftarrow z_\ell + \sum_{i=1}^N a_{\ell i} v(x_i),
\qquad
a_{\ell i} = \frac{\exp(q(z_\ell)^\top k(x_i) / \sqrt{d})}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(q(z_\ell)^\top k(x_j) / \sqrt{d})},
\]
where $q$, $k$, and $v$ are learned linear projections. Soft attention allows latents to specialize: some latents may attend to local texture, others to large-scale layout. This procedure is differentiable and learns to select relevant inputs for different latents.

Another approach is routing or mixture-of-experts style pooling, where each input is assigned (hard or soft) to a subset of latents. Routing reduces computation because an input interacts with only a few latents, and it can induce sparsity useful for scaling. For example, a learned gating function $g(x_i)$ can produce selection indices, and only the selected latents retrieve values from that input. Hard routing requires special care in backpropagation (e.g., Gumbel-softmax or straight-through estimators) or using sparse attention primitives.

When latents must be projected back to input space — for reconstruction, classification per token, or dense prediction — readout operations invert pooling. For per-token supervision, one can use attention from inputs to latents: each input retrieves relevant latent information and combines it with its local representation. If $u_i$ is the updated input feature, a readout via attention looks like
\[
u_i \leftarrow u_i + \sum_{\ell=1}^L b_{i\ell} w(z_\ell),
\qquad
b_{i\ell} = \frac{\exp(q(u_i)^\top k(z_\ell) / \sqrt{d})}{\sum_{m=1}^L \exp(q(u_i)^\top k(z_m) / \sqrt{d})}.
\]
This enables per-position predictions that are informed by global latents. In many architectures this readout is parameter-efficient because $L$ is small.

Practical designs often combine several pooling/readout mechanisms. For example, a model may first use strided convolutions to reduce spatial resolution (deterministic pooling) and then apply learned cross-attention to extract task-specific latents. The key design decision is matching the pooling capacity to the complexity of the global information: too small a latent set will bottleneck the model, while too large defeats the computational gains.

# Encoder-decoder splits and roles of local/global modules

Splitting a model into encoder and decoder components clarifies responsibilities and supports scalable processing. In hierarchical architectures, the encoder typically maps raw inputs to a compact latent representation; the decoder maps latents back to task outputs, sometimes conditioned on additional inputs. The encoder is the place to perform heavy compression, exploit locality, and extract features. The decoder is where global reasoning, generation, or per-position refinements occur.

In many designs, local modules dominate the encoder. For images, early encoder stages employ convolutions or local attention to produce feature maps that capture texture and small-scale structure. These local modules exploit spatial inductive biases like translation equivariance and can be implemented in a streaming or tiled fashion to limit memory. After several local layers, a pooling or cross-attention step aggregates information into latents. The latent encoder then performs global self-attention among latents to build a holistic representation of the input.

The decoder's role depends on the task. In classification or retrieval, the decoder might simply apply an MLP to a pooled latent summary. For dense prediction (segmentation, per-patch classification) or autoregressive generation, the decoder needs to combine global latents with local contexts. In encoder-decoder transformer variants, the decoder attends to latents (from the encoder) while using local self-attention among its own tokens. This design keeps the decoder scalable: local token-token interactions are limited to a sliding window or causal neighborhood, while global context comes in via attention to the small latent set.

An important benefit of this split is modularity for multi-task or multi-modal settings. The same encoder latents can be shared across different decoders (e.g., one decoder for segmentation, another for captioning), or multiple encoders (for different modalities) can write into a shared latent space that a single decoder reads. The encoder-decoder boundary is therefore a natural interface for transferring abstracted information.

Another practical consideration is how iterative refinement happens. In some systems, the encoder produces a one-shot latent summary. In others, latents are updated iteratively by alternating cross-attention with the encoder inputs and self-attention among latents, yielding progressive abstraction. The decoder may also iteratively read and refine outputs via cross-attention to the same latents. The exact partition of local versus global computation and the timing of read/write steps significantly affect both performance and computational cost.

# Block-causal and hierarchical attention masks

Attention masks control which positions may attend to which other positions, enabling causal behavior and multi-scale dependency patterns. Block-causal and hierarchical masks are two strategies that structure attention to respect temporal causality, limit computational burden, and encode hierarchical dependency patterns.

Block-causal attention partitions a sequence into contiguous blocks and allows full attention within a block but restricts cross-block attention to a causal pattern. Consider a sequence divided into blocks of length $B$. Within a block, tokens can freely attend to each other (and possibly to some previous blocks), but attention from a token to tokens in future blocks is forbidden to preserve causality. A simple block-causal mask lets token at position $i$ attend to tokens in block indices $\leq \lfloor i/B \rfloor$ and optionally to a limited look-back across block boundaries. Block-causal designs are attractive for streaming or long-sequence autoregression because they reduce the size of the attention context per query and allow efficient caching of past block summaries.

Hierarchical attention masks extend this idea by introducing multiple levels of granularity with different attention radii. At the lowest level, small neighborhoods are allowed detailed attention (e.g., convolutional or windowed self-attention). At higher levels, aggregated nodes (latents or pooled tokens) allow long-range interactions. A hierarchical mask may allow tokens to attend locally at every layer but permit occasional global attention through connections to higher-level nodes. This pattern enforces that long-range effects must propagate through the hierarchy: local changes influence their aggregated node, which then influences other parts of the sequence via higher-level attention.

Formally, a hierarchical attention scheme can be represented by a directed acyclic graph of nodes where edges indicate allowed attention flows. Let the nodes be partitioned into levels $\ell = 0,\dots,L$ with level $0$ being raw inputs and level $L$ the global latents. The attention mask $M$ is a block matrix where $M_{pq}=1$ if node $p$ at level $\ell_p$ may attend to node $q$ at level $\ell_q$, and $0$ otherwise. Typical patterns include allowing attention from level $\ell$ to $\ell$ (intra-level), from $\ell$ to $\ell-1$ (reading lower-level summaries), and from higher levels to lower ones (broadcasting global context). Causality constraints can be layered into this graph: a node may be allowed to read only from earlier positions within a level, or it may read from aggregated tokens that themselves obey causality.

An important trade-off is latency versus expressivity. Block-causal masks with small blocks enable low-latency streaming because updates only depend on current and previous blocks; however, immediate cross-block coordination is limited. Hierarchical masks that let tokens consult global latents quickly reduce the number of attention hops needed for long-range effects, but they may introduce additional latency or require careful synchronization to maintain causality in generation.

In practice, implementing block-causal and hierarchical masking requires attention to computational details: sparse attention libraries, streaming caches for previous block keys/values, and efficient implementations of cross-level attention. Theoretical analysis of these masks often examines the number of attention "hops" needed to propagate information across distant tokens: a well-designed hierarchy reduces the hop-count from $O(N)$ to $O(\log N)$ or constant time via global latents.

Conclusion

Hierarchical and latent-array architectures provide principled and practical ways to reconcile the competing demands of local detail and global reasoning. By using latent arrays such as Perceiver-style tokens, learned pooling/readout mechanisms, and carefully partitioned encoder-decoder roles, models can scale to large inputs while retaining the ability to perform fine-grained tasks. Attention masks that enforce block-causality and hierarchical connectivity let designers control latency, computational cost, and the flow of information across scales. Understanding these building blocks and their trade-offs is essential for reading contemporary research and for designing models tailored to complex, structured data.
