Chapter 12: FLOPs, Compute Accounting, and Scaling Laws

Introduction

Modern deep learning research is constrained by compute. Whether you are designing experiments, comparing models, or reading papers that report results for large language models, you need to be able to estimate computational cost, reason about tradeoffs between model size and dataset size, and apply scaling-law intuitions to choose a compute-optimal design. This chapter explains how to count floating point operations (FLOPs) for Transformer layers, describes which subcomponents dominate cost, contrasts inference vs. training costs, explains the Chinchilla-style parameter/data trade-off, and gives practical guidance on how to control and account for compute in experiments.

We will use concrete, reproducible formulas and clear intuitions. First we state a common FLOP convention and notation so that counts are unambiguous.

Conventions and notation

Throughout this chapter we count FLOPs in the multiply-add convention where one multiply followed by one add is counted as 2 FLOPs. This is the convention most commonly used in the model-scaling literature. We denote:
- $B$ = batch size (number of sequences processed together).
- $S$ = sequence length (tokens per sequence).
- $D$ = model hidden dimension (sometimes called $d_{model}$).
- $H$ = number of attention heads.
- $d_h = D / H$ = dimensionality per head.
- $P$ = total number of model parameters.
- $N$ = total number of training tokens seen (dataset size in tokens).
- $C$ = compute budget, typically measured in FLOPs (or PFLOP-days, etc.).

We will express per-layer and per-token FLOP counts for a Transformer block, and then aggregate to whole-model cost. We always assume dense matrix operations dominate; cheaper elementwise operations such as layernorms, bias adds, or activation functions exist but usually contribute a much smaller fraction of total FLOPs.

# Counting forward and backward FLOPs for Transformer layers

To reason about compute you must be able to count FLOPs for the main Transformer building blocks: linear (dense) projections, attention score matmuls, attention-weighted sum, and the feedforward network (FFN). We will derive per-layer forward FLOPs, and then explain how backward/gradient computation increases the cost.

A standard Transformer block for one forward pass on a batch of $B$ sequences of length $S$ includes:

1. Input linear projections to compute queries, keys, and values (Q, K, V).
2. Scaled dot-product attention: compute attention scores $Q K^\top$, apply softmax along the key dimension, and multiply by $V$.
3. Output projection after concatenating heads.
4. Feedforward network: a gated or two-layer MLP typically with an inner dimension $D_{ff} = r D$ (e.g., $r=4$).
5. Residual connections and layer normalizations (cheap relative to matmuls).

Linear projections (Q, K, V and output)
- The combined QKV projection is usually implemented as a single linear operation mapping $(B, S, D)$ to $(B, S, 3D)$. The cost of a dense matrix multiply of an $(B S) \times D$ input by a $D \times 3D$ weight is approximately
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{QKV}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D \cdot (3D) = 6 B S D^2.
  $$
  The output projection (from concatenated heads back to $D$) is a multiplication of $(B, S, D)$ by a $D \times D$ weight, costing
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{out-proj}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D \cdot D = 2 B S D^2.
  $$

Attention score computation, softmax, and weighted sum
- For each attention head, $Q$ and $K$ have shapes $(B, S, d_h)$. Computing attention scores $Q K^\top$ for one head is a multiply-accumulate over two matrices of size $(B, S, d_h)$ and $(B, d_h, S)$, costing
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{QK^\top\text{ per head}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot S \cdot d_h.
  $$
  Summed over $H$ heads, because $H d_h = D$, this becomes
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{QK^\top} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S^2 \cdot D.
  $$
- Applying the softmax along the $S$ keys for each of the $B \cdot H \cdot S$ queries involves exponentials and sums. Roughly, the cost is on the order of $O(B H S^2)$ scalar operations; compared to the $O(B S^2 D)$ matmuls, the softmax cost is typically smaller when $D \gg 1$ but not always negligible if $D$ is small or $S$ is large.
- Multiplying attention weights by $V$ (to compute the weighted sum) is another matmul per head with the same cost as the score computation:
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn-weighted sum}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S^2 \cdot D.
  $$

Feedforward network (FFN)
- A typical two-layer FFN applies a linear expansion $D \to D_{ff} = rD$, a nonlinear activation (e.g., GELU) elementwise, then a projection $D_{ff} \to D$. The two dense matmuls cost
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{FFN}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D \cdot (rD) + 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot (rD) \cdot D = 4 r \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D^2.
  $$
  For the common choice $r=4$, this becomes $16 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D^2$.

Putting it together (forward pass for one Transformer block)
- Summing the dominant matmul costs yields a per-block forward FLOP count approximately
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{block, forward}} &\approx \underbrace{6 B S D^2}_{\text{QKV proj}} + \underbrace{2 B S^2 D}_{QK^\top} + \underbrace{2 B S^2 D}_{\text{attn-weighted sum}} + \underbrace{2 B S D^2}_{\text{out-proj}} + \underbrace{4 r \, B S D^2}_{\text{FFN}} \\
  &= B S \, D^2 \left( 8 + 4r \right) + 4 B S^2 D,
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  where we grouped the $D^2$ and $S^2 D$ terms. For the common $r=4$, this simplifies to
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{block, forward}} \approx B S D^2 \cdot 24 + 4 B S^2 D.
  $$
- Note that when $S \ll D$ (short sequences, large model width) the $D^2$ terms dominate; when $S$ is large relative to $D$ (long contexts), the $S^2 D$ attention terms can dominate.

Counting for the whole model over sequence length and number of layers
- If the model has $L$ identical Transformer blocks, the forward cost per training step (one forward pass over $B$ sequences of length $S$) is approximately
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward step}} \approx L \cdot \text{FLOPs}_{\text{block, forward}}.
  $$
  To convert per-step cost into per-token cost, divide by $B \cdot S$. Often we care about forward FLOPs per token:
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{forward per token}} \approx L \left[ D^2 (8 + 4r) + 4 S D \right].
  $$

# Contributions to FLOPs: attention, FFN, projections, output vocab

Which parts dominate compute depends on architecture choices and hyperparameters. Here we give intuition for each contributor and what changes it affects.

Attention (QK^T and attention-weighted sums)
- The attention mechanism scales roughly as $O(B S^2 D)$ because you compute a score for every query-key pair within the sequence. For short sequences (small $S$) and large $D$, attention can be relatively cheap compared to FFN matmuls which scale like $O(B S D^2)$. But for large $S$ (long contexts, e.g., retrieval, long-document modeling), the $S^2$ factor quickly makes attention dominant. This is why for very long contexts people invent sparse or linearized attention variants—avoiding the $S^2$ scaling is essential when $S$ becomes large.
- The attention heads parameterization (number of heads $H$ and per-head dimension $d_h$) does not change the total asymptotic cost if $H d_h = D$ is fixed: $QK^\top$ across $H$ heads sums to $2 B S^2 D$ FLOPs. However, implementation constants and memory layout can change practical throughput.

Feedforward network (FFN)
- The FFN cost scales as $O(B S D^2)$ times the expansion factor $r$. When $D$ is large relative to $S$, FFN becomes the dominant cost. Increasing the expansion ratio $r$ (e.g., moving from 4 to 8) increases FFN cost linearly in $r$. Changes to $D$ (model width) scale FFN cost quadratically, so increasing width is expensive.

Projections: QKV and output
- The three-way projection to Q, K, V and the output projection together contribute significantly to the $D^2$ terms. The combined QKV + output projections are about $8 B S D^2$ in the block formula above (6 for QKV + 2 for output). These are dense matmuls identical in asymptotic scaling to FFN but with smaller constants.

Output vocabulary and final softmax
- For language modeling, the final linear projection from hidden state to logits over a vocabulary of size $V$ costs per token
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{lm-head}} \approx 2 \cdot B \cdot S \cdot D \cdot V.
  $$
  If $V$ is large (hundreds of thousands), this projection can be substantial, but in many setups the LM head is either tied to the input embeddings (reusing their weights) or vocabulary size is not dominant compared to the model matmuls.
- The softmax and cross-entropy cost per token is $O(V)$ elementwise operations; if you use techniques like sampled softmax or adaptive softmax for very large $V$, you can reduce FLOPs.

Negligible and secondary costs
- Layernorms, bias adds, GELU evaluations, and residual adds are all elementwise and scale as $O(B S D)$; these are usually negligible when $D^2$ or $S^2 D$ terms dominate. However, when evaluating extremely small models or very long sequences with optimized matmul kernels, these can become nontrivial, so always sanity-check profiling numbers.

# Inference vs training FLOP differences

Training and inference differ substantially in FLOP requirements and in the kinds of operations performed.

Relative FLOPs: forward and backward
- A full parameter update (one training step) includes a forward pass, a backward pass, and an optimizer update. The backward pass computes gradients with respect to activations and weights; computing these gradients generally costs more FLOPs than a single forward pass. A common rule-of-thumb is that the backward pass costs about 2–3 times the forward pass, so a full training step (forward + backward + optimizer overhead) is roughly 3–6 times the forward-only FLOPs. The exact factor depends on architecture, implementation details (e.g., checkpointing), optimizer choice (Adam has extra operations compared to SGD), and whether you count weight-update FLOPs.
- Many scaling-law papers adopt a simplified accounting rule to turn tokens and parameters into total training FLOPs. A commonly used practical approximation is:
  $$
  \text{FLOPs}_{\text{training}} \approx k \cdot P \cdot N,
  $$
  where $P$ is the number of parameters, $N$ is the number of training tokens, and $k$ is a constant that aggregates how many FLOPs per token per parameter are needed (including forward/backward/optimizer). A widely-cited rule-of-thumb is $k \approx 6$ under the multiply-add convention, but $k$ can range based on model and training details. We will discuss the origin of such factors and their limitations below.

Why backward is heavier: intuition
- The backward pass reuses the forward pass intermediate values but must compute gradients for every weight and activation. For dense layers the gradient computations can be implemented using matmuls similar in size to forward matmuls, so the backward cost is roughly comparable to forward per-layer. Additionally, you compute weight-gradient accumulation and parameter updates. Activation gradients often require recomputing activation function derivatives (cheap elementwise), but if you use activation recomputation (checkpointing) to save memory, you may deliberately re-run parts of the forward computation during backward, increasing FLOPs further in exchange for lower memory use.
- Optimizer overhead: adaptive optimizers like Adam maintain moment estimates and apply per-parameter operations each step, adding extra FLOPs, though compared to large matmuls these are often smaller.

Inference differences beyond FLOPs
- Inference typically uses just a forward pass but might also use autoregressive decoding with caching: for auto-regressive generation, the decoder can cache past keys and values to avoid recomputing attention over all past tokens, making per-token inference FLOPs scale as $O(S D)$ rather than $O(S^2 D)$ for each new token. In contrast, training computes attention over full contexts repeatedly and cannot benefit from autoregressive caching in the same way.
- Latency-bound inference and batch size: inference is often run with small batch sizes, which results in less efficient utilization of GPU/TPU matmul throughput (lower FLOPS/s). Training is often done with large batch sizes (or gradient accumulation) to maximize hardware throughput.

Counting for experiments: an example
- Suppose you have $P=100$M parameters and you train on $N=1$B tokens, and you accept the practical rule $k=6$ FLOPs per parameter per token. Then estimated training FLOPs are
  $$
  C \approx 6 \cdot P \cdot N = 6 \cdot 10^8 \cdot 10^9 = 6 \times 10^{17} \text{ FLOPs}.
  $$
  Convert to practical units as needed (for instance, PFLOP-days: divide by $10^{15}$ and by 86400 to get days at petaflops).

Caveats
- These aggregate approximations are useful for high-level planning, but precise accounting requires summing the per-layer costs we derived earlier and adjusting for batch size, sequence length, number of optimization steps, mixed-precision speedups, and kernel-specific efficiencies. Always profile on your hardware for exact timings.

# Chinchilla-style compute-optimal parameter/data trade-offs

One of the most important results for planning large-scale experiments is that, for a fixed compute budget, there is an optimal allocation between model size (number of parameters $P$) and the number of training tokens $N$. The Chinchilla paper (2022) provided empirical evidence and a practical rule-of-thumb for parameter/data trade-offs that corrected previous misconceptions (e.g., the claim that larger models should be trained on proportionally more compute-limited tokens). Here we explain the general derivation and the practical takeaway.

Basic model of training loss vs. parameters and data
- A convenient approximation is to model the generalization loss $L$ after training as a sum of two terms: one that decreases with model size and one that decreases with training data:
  $$
  L(P, N) \approx a P^{-\alpha} + b N^{-\beta},
  $$
  where $a, b, \alpha, \beta$ are empirical constants describing how model and data improvements behave. The first term captures approximation error due to finite parameters; the second term captures estimation error due to finite data. This is a simplified, separable model but it captures the intuition that both more parameters and more data reduce loss, with diminishing returns governed by exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

Compute constraint
- Training compute $C$ is approximately proportional to the product of parameters and tokens:
  $$
  C \approx k \cdot P \cdot N,
  $$
  where $k$ is the constant from the previous section. Given a fixed compute budget $C$, you can express $N$ as $N = C / (k P)$ and substitute into the loss model:
  $$
  L(P) = a P^{-\alpha} + b \left( \frac{C}{k P} \right)^{-\beta} = a P^{-\alpha} + b k^\beta C^{-\beta} P^{\beta}.
  $$
  Minimizing $L(P)$ with respect to $P$ gives the compute-optimal $P$ as a power-law function of $C$. Taking derivative and setting to zero leads to
  $$
  -\alpha a P^{-(\alpha+1)} + \beta b k^\beta C^{-\beta} P^{\beta-1} = 0.
  $$
  Solving for $P$ yields
  $$
  P^\star \propto C^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}.
  $$
  Correspondingly,
  $$
  N^\star = \frac{C}{k P^\star} \propto C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.
  $$

Interpretation
- The exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$ quantify how much benefit you get from scaling parameters versus data. If $\alpha$ is small relative to $\beta$, performance improves more quickly with data than with parameters, and thus the compute-optimal solution puts relatively more resources into data (larger $N$) and fewer into parameters (smaller $P$), and vice versa.
- Chinchilla estimated these exponents empirically for language modeling and found the compute-optimal regime required significantly more data per parameter than some earlier recommendations suggested. Practically, Chinchilla reported a simple and memorable rule: train on roughly 20 tokens per parameter for compute-optimal setups. That is,
  $$
  N^\star \approx 20 \cdot P.
  $$
  This came from fitting empirical loss surfaces and solving the compute-constrained optimization. It implies that, e.g., a 70B-parameter model should be trained on about $70 \times 10^9 \times 20 = 1.4 \times 10^{12}$ tokens (1.4 trillion tokens) to be compute-optimal under the assumptions and training setup used.

Implications of the rule
- If you have a fixed compute budget and you previously planned to train a huge model on a relatively small number of tokens (or vice versa), the Chinchilla-style result suggests you may be operating off the Pareto frontier. For example, training an oversized model on insufficient data wastes parameter capacity; conversely, a small model trained on massive data can be under-parameterized for the data and also be suboptimal.
- The exact numeric coefficient (20 tokens per parameter) depends on many choices: model architecture, optimizer, pretraining objectives, tokenization, regularization, and how you count compute. The qualitative takeaway—there is an optimal tokens-per-parameter ratio and for many modern language-modeling setups that ratio is on the order of tens of tokens per parameter—is robust.

Practical derivation with sample constants
- If you fit empirical curves for language modeling and find exponents consistent with Chinchilla, you recover a numeric exponent giving $P^\star \propto C^{0.73}$ (this is one reported exponent in that literature), but rather than memorizing the exponent it is often easier to adopt the tokens-per-parameter rule and use it as a starting point for experiment design. Always allow for tuning around the predicted optimum.

# Interpreting and controlling compute in experiments

Knowing how to count FLOPs and where they come from lets you make better experimental choices. This section gives practical guidance for estimating, measuring, and controlling compute in real experiments.

Estimating compute before running
- Use the per-layer FLOP formulas from above to compute the FLOPs for a single forward pass and for a full training step (including backward and optimizer overhead). Multiply by number of steps and by batch size to get total training FLOPs. Convert to wall-clock using your hardware's peak FLOPs/s only as a rough ceiling; real throughput will be lower because of memory, communication, and kernel efficiency.
- For planning experiments at scale, the simplified estimate
  $$
  C \approx k \cdot P \cdot N
  $$
  is useful. Choose $k$ consistent with your framework and precision. Many practitioners use $k \in [3, 9]$ depending on whether they count forward-only, backward, optimizer, and whether they count multiply-add pairs as 1 or 2 FLOPs. Document your convention.

Profiling and measurement
- Always profile on real hardware. FLOP estimates tell you the theoretical cost, but real runtime depends on memory bandwidth, kernel implementations, and parallelism strategy. Profile the actual runtime of representative training steps to determine effective FLOPs/s.
- Use tools like NVIDIA Nsight, PyTorch profiler, TensorFlow profiler, or vendor-provided profiling for TPUs to identify bottlenecks: are you memory-bound, compute-bound, or communication-bound (in distributed setups)?

Control knobs to trade compute, memory, and time
- Batch size and gradient accumulation: increasing batch size increases throughput on modern accelerators but may affect generalization. Gradient accumulation lets you simulate large batches without needing more device memory, at the cost of more steps between synchronization.
- Sequence length: reducing $S$ reduces both forward and backward FLOPs (and memory) linearly in $S$ for FFN-dominated regimes and quadratically in $S$ for attention-dominated regimes. For long-context tasks try sparse attention to reduce $S^2$ costs.
- Model width vs depth: increasing $D$ increases cost roughly as $D^2$ in the FFN and projections, while increasing $L$ (layers) scales cost linearly in $L$. This gives different tradeoffs for compute and representational capacity; the scaling-law derivation helps choose an optimal $P$ given compute $C$ but other constraints (memory or latency) may push you to prefer depth or width.
- Precision: switching to mixed precision (e.g., FP16/ bfloat16) reduces memory and often increases throughput, but does not change FLOP counts. However, it reduces wall-clock training time for the same theoretical FLOPs.
- Checkpointing / recomputation: trading extra forward recomputation during backward for reduced activation memory saves memory but increases FLOPs. Use it when memory is the limiting resource but you can afford extra computation.

Reporting compute in papers and reproducibility
- When you report compute, be explicit: report the FLOP convention (multiply-add pairs counted as 1 or 2), whether you count forward-only or include backward and optimizer overhead, your estimate of $k$ in $C \approx k P N$, and how many effective tokens $N$ you used (careful with deduplication and repeated passes). Also report batch size, sequence length, number of optimization steps, and wall-clock training time on the hardware used.
- For reproducibility and fair comparisons, it is very helpful to provide both theoretical FLOP counts and measured wall-clock times on specific hardware. The former lets others reason about compute budgets across hardware; the latter shows practical costs on specific systems.

Interpreting experimental results with compute in mind
- When comparing model variants, normalize by compute when possible. A larger model that wins but used many more FLOPs may not be strictly better for a given compute budget.
- Use scaling-law extrapolations cautiously. Scaling laws are empirical fits; they provide strong guidance but are not strict physical laws. Architecture changes, new optimizers, or different data distributions can shift the fitted exponents and constants.
- If results deviate from the predicted compute-optimal frontier, investigate possible causes: insufficient training steps (early stopping), data quality or diversity issues, implementation inefficiencies, regularization, or mismatch between pretraining and evaluation tasks.

Practical checklist for compute-aware experiments
- Before running: estimate FLOPs using per-layer formulas; compute predicted optimal $P$ and $N$ for your compute budget if you want to follow Chinchilla-style tradeoffs.
- During implementation: profile one training step to measure effective throughput; tune batch size, precision, and parallelism to improve utilization.
- While training: monitor wall-clock FLOPs/s, parameter updates per second, and memory usage. Use gradient-checkpointing if memory-limited.
- After training: when reporting, state exact FLOP counting conventions and provide per-step and total FLOP estimates alongside wall-clock time and hardware specs.

Conclusion

Understanding FLOPs, where they come from, and how they interact with model and data scale is essential for planning experiments and interpreting results. Use the per-layer accounting we provided to make concrete estimates, be aware of the main contributors (FFN versus attention versus projections), and remember the important distinction that training FLOPs are substantially larger than inference FLOPs. Apply the compute-optimal trade-off framework to decide how to split your budget between parameters and data, and always measure and report compute carefully for reproducibility and fair comparison. With these tools you will be able to read scaling-law papers critically, design experiments that use compute efficiently, and justify design choices to peers and reviewers.
