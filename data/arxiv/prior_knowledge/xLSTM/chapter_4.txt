# Introduction

Numerical stability is a central concern when training and using deep neural networks. Modern models involve many layers, repeated nonlinearities, and operations like exponentials and divisions that interact with the finite precision arithmetic of hardware. Small numerical issues — overflow, underflow, catastrophic cancellation, or poorly scaled gradients — can derail training, produce NaNs, or lead to models that are brittle at inference time. This chapter explains the principal sources of numerical instability that arise in deep learning, and describes the practical normalization and stabilization techniques researchers and engineers use to mitigate them. We will build intuition by examining floating-point behavior, show concrete algebraic tricks (like the log-sum-exp transformation) that prevent overflow in exponentials, explain the leading normalization families (BatchNorm, LayerNorm, GroupNorm), analyze residual block ordering choices (Pre-LayerNorm vs Post-LayerNorm) and why they matter, and cover bias/scale initialization and pragmatic stabilizers (clipping, scaling, loss scaling for mixed precision). The goal is to equip you with both theoretical understanding and hands-on practices so you can read papers and apply methods safely in experiments.

# Floating-point representation and sources of numerical instability

All computations on GPUs or CPUs are performed with finite-precision floating-point numbers, typically following the IEEE 754 standard. The most common formats in deep learning are single precision (FP32) and half precision (FP16, or the related bfloat16). A floating-point value is represented as
$(-1)^{s}\times m \times 2^{e}$,
where $s$ is the sign bit, $m$ is the significand (also called mantissa) and $e$ is the exponent. This representation gives a wide dynamic range via the exponent, but only a limited precision in representing real values (determined by the number of significand bits). Important consequences follow.

First, overflow and underflow. Overflow occurs when a result's magnitude exceeds the largest representable value and typically produces an infinity (Inf). Underflow occurs when a nonzero result is smaller in magnitude than the smallest normalized representable number, leading either to a denormal/subnormal number with reduced precision or to zero. Operations that involve exponentials, very large sums, or repeatedly multiplying values can easily push values into overflow/underflow regimes.

Second, precision loss and catastrophic cancellation. When subtracting two nearly equal numbers, many leading bits cancel; the result may retain only a few correct bits and hence have large relative error. For example, computing differences between large and nearly-equal floating values or computing probabilities by subtracting comparable log-likelihoods can be unstable.

Third, rounding and accumulation error. Each floating operation rounds to the nearest representable value. When summing many numbers, the rounding errors accumulate. The order of summation matters: summing small numbers into a large accumulator can lose the contribution of the small numbers entirely.

Fourth, denormals (subnormals) and performance. Values smaller than the smallest normalized floating point are represented with less precision (fewer significant bits). Many processors handle denormals slowly or have hardware flags to flush them to zero; therefore, tiny values can both reduce numerical fidelity and dramatically slow execution.

Finally, reduced-precision formats increase these hazards. FP16 and bfloat16 have narrower significands and smaller exponent ranges (especially FP16), so overflow/underflow and precision loss are more likely. Mixed-precision training uses FP16 for speed but must manage these numerical risks (loss scaling, keeping some accumulators in FP32).

Understanding these failure modes underpins the design of the algebraic and algorithmic tricks we introduce next.

# Log-sum-exp trick and stabilized exponentials

Exponentials and softmax-like operations are ubiquitous in machine learning — softmax for classification, log-likelihoods for probabilistic models, or normalized attention weights. Direct evaluation of expressions like $\log\sum_{i} \exp(x_i)$ can overflow when some $x_i$ are large, or underflow when all are very negative. The log-sum-exp trick is an algebraic rearrangement that preserves exactness in infinite precision and greatly improves numerical stability in finite precision.

Consider the quantity
$\operatorname{LSE}(x) = \log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \exp(x_i)\right)$.
If the maximum entry $\max_i x_i$ is large, then $\exp(x_i)$ may overflow. Subtracting the maximum inside the exponential avoids computing huge exponentials:

Let $m = \max_i x_i$. Then
\[
\operatorname{LSE}(x) = \log\left(\sum_{i} \exp(x_i)\right)
= \log\left(\exp(m)\sum_{i} \exp(x_i - m)\right)
= m + \log\left(\sum_{i} \exp(x_i - m)\right).
\]
Now each exponent $\exp(x_i - m)$ is at most $1$ (when $x_i = m$), so you avoid overflow, and if $x_i - m$ are very negative they will underflow to zero — but this is usually acceptable because extremely small contributions are negligible. This form is numerically stable and is essentially always used when implementing softmax or log-sum-exp.

Similarly, to compute softmax probabilities $p_i = \frac{\exp(x_i)}{\sum_j \exp(x_j)}$, use:
\[
p_i = \frac{\exp(x_i - m)}{\sum_j \exp(x_j - m)}.
\]
Subtracting $m$ prevents overflow and preserves relative ratios exactly (up to rounding).

When computing $\log p_i$ for a numerically stable cross-entropy, combine softmax and cross-entropy algebraically rather than computing probabilities then log. For labels represented as one-hot, the standard stable formulation for cross-entropy of label class $k$ is:
\[
-\log\left(\frac{\exp(x_k)}{\sum_j \exp(x_j)}\right)
= -x_k + \operatorname{LSE}(x).
\]
Compute $\operatorname{LSE}(x)$ with the stabilized form shown above.

Stabilized exponentials also appear in attention mechanisms. In scaled dot-product attention the weights are computed as $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d})$. The division by $\sqrt{d}$ reduces variance of the dot product to keep values in a numerically safe range, and applying subtraction of the rowwise maximum before the softmax is a best practice to avoid overflow.

A related trick is log-domain arithmetic. When many quantities are very small, it is preferable to represent values in log-space, convert multiplications to additions, and use log-sum-exp to add probabilities. For example, multiplying many small probabilities often underflows if done in the probability domain; summing logs avoids this.

# Batch Normalization, Layer Normalization, and Group Normalization

Normalization techniques transform intermediate activations to have controlled mean and variance. They reduce internal covariate shift, stabilize and accelerate training, and often allow higher learning rates. Several popular methods differ in how they compute statistics and where they are applied.

Batch Normalization (BatchNorm) normalizes activations using statistics computed across the examples in a minibatch and spatial locations (for convnets). Given a minibatch of activations $\{x_{i}\}$ along a channel, BatchNorm computes the batch mean $\mu_B$ and variance $\sigma_B^2$:
\[
\mu_B = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_i, \qquad
\sigma_B^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - \mu_B)^2.
\]
Then it computes normalized activations
\[
\hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}},
\]
and finally an affine rescaling
\[
y_i = \gamma \hat{x}_i + \beta,
\]
where $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learnable per-channel scale and shift parameters, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant to prevent division by zero. BatchNorm reduces covariate shift across training and has effects on the loss landscape that allow faster optimization. However, BatchNorm relies on reasonably sized minibatches to estimate stable statistics; with tiny batch sizes its estimates are noisy and performance degrades. During inference, running averages of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ collected during training are used.

Layer Normalization normalizes across features within each example, not across the batch. For a vector activation $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ of a single token or neuron collection, LayerNorm computes
\[
\mu = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} x_j, \qquad
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} (x_j - \mu)^2,
\]
and normalizes
\[
\hat{x}_j = \frac{x_j - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad
y_j = \gamma_j \hat{x}_j + \beta_j,
\]
with learnable $\gamma,\beta$ per feature dimension. LayerNorm is independent of batch statistics and works well for recurrent networks and transformers where batch-wise normalization is inappropriate or inconsistent across variable-length sequences. It stabilizes gradients by ensuring each token's representation has controlled scale.

Group Normalization is an intermediate approach: it partitions the feature channels into groups and normalizes within each group for each example. For convolutional feature maps with $C$ channels, grouping into $G$ groups yields group size $C/G$, and normalization statistics are computed per group across the spatial positions. GroupNorm is robust to varying batch sizes and can match BatchNorm performance in many vision architectures when batch sizes are small.

Each normalization method includes learnable scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$ so the layer can recover identity transformations if needed. The choice among BatchNorm, LayerNorm, and GroupNorm depends on model architecture (CNN vs Transformer), batch size constraints, and whether one needs deterministic behavior at inference.

From a stability perspective, normalization mitigates exploding or vanishing activations and gradients, reduces sensitivity to weight initialization and learning rate, and makes the optimization landscape smoother. However, normalization also interacts with other techniques (dropout, residual connections) and can introduce its own pitfalls: BatchNorm makes per-example loss interdependent on the batch, and LayerNorm can be sensitive to the position where it is applied relative to nonlinearities and residual connections, as we discuss next.

# Pre-LayerNorm vs Post-LayerNorm residual blocks

Residual connections are fundamental to deep models: they allow information to bypass transformations and prevent degradation in very deep networks. Consider a residual block with input $x$ and a transformation $F(x)$; a canonical residual update is
\[
y = x + F(x).
\]
When normalization is used, it can be placed before the transformation ("Pre-Norm") or after the addition ("Post-Norm"). In transformers, "Pre-LayerNorm" and "Post-LayerNorm" refer to whether the LayerNorm is applied before each sublayer or after adding the residual. These choices have important stability consequences.

Post-LayerNorm residual blocks (original in early Transformer papers) compute
\[
y = \operatorname{LayerNorm}(x + F(x)).
\]
Pre-LayerNorm places layer normalization before the sublayer:
\[
y = x + F(\operatorname{LayerNorm}(x)).
\]
Why does this matter? The core issue is gradient flow and the presence of normalization after the residual sum. In Post-LN, the gradient that flows back through many residual additions must traverse many LayerNorms applied after sums. LayerNorm's normalization depends on activations and hence complicates the backward path: small changes in earlier layers can be scaled or shifted nonlinearly, and in deep stacks this can cause instability during training, especially when using large learning rates or when sequence lengths and batch sizes vary. Practically, Post-LN transformer training can suffer from exploding or vanishing gradients when networks become very deep, unless careful learning rate schedules and initialization are used.

Pre-LN addresses this by normalizing the input to each sublayer. Because each transformation $F$ sees normalized inputs, its outputs tend to have controlled scale, and the residual addition $x + F(\cdot)$ lets gradients pass directly to earlier layers without being immediately squashed or expanded by a post-addition normalization. This yields more stable gradient norms and allows training of very deep transformer variants without special tricks. Empirically, Pre-LayerNorm transformers often converge faster and are easier to train with larger learning rates or deeper stacks.

However, Pre-LN changes the representational properties and may affect the final model's behavior. Post-LN ensures each block's outputs have normalized statistics, which can ease optimization for some architectures. Pre-LN may reduce the implicit regularization effect of Post-LN. To reconcile stability with performance, many modern transformer architectures use Pre-LayerNorm during training and add an additional final LayerNorm at the output, or apply small-scale learnable residual scaling (see next section) to control the magnitude of $F(\cdot)$.

In summary, Pre-LayerNorm improves gradient flow and training stability for deep stacks, whereas Post-LayerNorm enforces normalized outputs at each block. The right choice depends on depth, optimizer, learning rate schedule, and empirical validation.

# Scaling and bias initialization strategies to control gate behavior

Many neural network components use gating nonlinearities: sigmoid gates in LSTMs, the gating in highway networks, attention weight computations, and multiplicative gates in transformers (such as the output of a sigmoid used to scale something). The initialization of biases and scaling of weights determine whether these gates start in a saturated regime (close to 0 or 1) or in a sensitive mid-range (around 0.5 for sigmoids). Properly selected initializations avoid saturating nonlinearities at the start, improving gradient flow.

For sigmoidal gates $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-z)}$, if $z$ is initialized with large magnitude (positive or negative), the gate saturates at 0 or 1 and gradients vanish through the gate. To keep gates "open" and trainable initially, biases are often initialized to small negative values or zero so the initial gate outputs are near 0.5 for symmetric zero-mean $z$ distributions. For example, in LSTM networks it is common to initialize the forget-gate bias $b_f$ to a positive value (e.g., $b_f = 1$ or $2$) so that the forget gate $\sigma(z + b_f)$ starts biased towards retaining memory (closer to 1), which empirically stabilizes training. Conversely, for gates where we prefer them initially closed, a negative bias may be used.

Scaling strategies also help. Weight initializations like Xavier/Glorot or Kaiming/He aim to preserve the variance of activations across layers. Under Xavier for a layer with fan-in $n_{\text{in}}$ and fan-out $n_{\text{out}}$, weights are drawn from a distribution with variance roughly $2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})$ to keep forward and backward signal variances stable. Kaiming initialization is tailored for ReLU-like activations and sets variance as $2/n_{\text{in}}$. In transformers, the scaled dot-product attention divides the dot product by $\sqrt{d}$ to control magnitude; similarly, some architectures multiply residual branches by small scalars to prevent residual outputs from dominating the identity path initially. For very deep networks, it is common to apply per-layer learnable scaling initialized near zero (e.g., a multiplicative scalar $\alpha$ with $\alpha \approx 10^{-2}$) so that the network initially behaves like the identity mapping and gradually learns nontrivial transforms. This practice reduces initial instability and helps optimization.

Bias initialization can control early numerical dynamic range. Consider a softmax-based classifier whose logits are initialized to large random values; the softmax would be peaked and gradients small for many classes. Zeroing the output layer biases or initializing weights with smaller variance is standard to produce well-behaved initial gradients.

A special case is LayerNorm's scale parameter $\gamma$. In many setups $\gamma$ is initialized to $1$, recovering identity scaling at the start, but sometimes it is initialized to a small value (e.g., $0.1$) to dampen updates early in training. Similarly, attention output projections or feed-forward layer outputs can be scaled down at initialization so the residual connection preserves the input initially.

Proper initialization choices are therefore a combination of variance-preserving weight schemes and bias settings tailored to gate types, along with optional small multiplicative scales that keep layers near identity at the start. These choices reduce the risk of saturation, exploding activations, and poor gradient flow.

# Clipping, scaling and other pragmatic stabilizers used in practice

Beyond the algebraic and architectural fixes discussed above, practitioners use many pragmatic techniques to guard against numerical and optimization instabilities. These methods are especially important when training large models with mixed-precision or on massive datasets.

Gradient clipping is widely used to prevent exploding gradients. Two common variants are gradient norm clipping and value clipping. Norm clipping rescales the whole gradient vector if its Euclidean norm exceeds a threshold $T$:
\[
g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\left(1,\frac{T}{\|g\|_2}\right).
\]
This prevents any single update step from being excessively large and destabilizing parameters. Value clipping clamps individual gradient components to lie within $[-c,c]$, but norm clipping is generally preferred because it preserves the direction of the update.

Loss scaling is essential for mixed-precision training with FP16. Because FP16 has limited dynamic range in the mantissa, gradients may underflow to zero. To prevent this, multiply the loss by a large factor $S$ during the forward pass, compute gradients in FP16, then divide gradients by $S$ before applying updates. Modern frameworks provide automatic mixed precision (AMP) that handles dynamic loss scaling: it increases $S$ until overflow is detected, then reduces it. This keeps gradients in the representable range while avoiding overflow.

Clipping logits and probabilities can also avoid numerical trouble in evaluation. For example, computing $\log p$ where $p$ may be zero due to underflow is hazardous; instead, clamp probabilities to a minimum positive value $\epsilon$ and compute $\log(\max(p,\epsilon))$. While mathematically crude, in practice clamping with a tiny $\epsilon$ (e.g., $10^{-12}$) prevents NaNs.

Epsilon additions in denominators are ubiquitous. Whenever variance appears in a denominator, add a small constant $\epsilon$ to avoid division by zero or tiny numbers. Choose $\epsilon$ consistent with the numerical precision of your platform: $10^{-5}$ or $10^{-8}$ for FP32, and larger if underflow risk is high.

Adaptive gradient methods like Adam include a small $\epsilon$ in the denominator of the update to avoid division by zero in the second moment estimate:
\[
\Delta \theta = -\eta \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon}.
\]
Careful selection of Adam's $\epsilon$ can materially affect training stability; values of $10^{-8}$ are common for FP32, but for mixed precision some practitioners use larger $\epsilon$ to stabilize updates.

Clip or rescale activations that might grow with depth. For example, when stacking many residual blocks, some implementations multiply the residual branch by a small constant $c$ (learnable or fixed), so the update is $y = x + cF(x)$. Setting $c$ small initially prevents early layers from being overwhelmed and can be annealed or learned during training.

Use numerically stable reductions and accumulate in higher precision. When summing many numbers (e.g., computing a loss across many elements), use pairwise or Kahan summation to reduce rounding error, or accumulate sums in FP32 even if inputs are FP16. Many libraries do reductions in FP32 for this reason. In multi-GPU distributed training, use careful moment accumulation and consistent reduction ordering or compensated summation to avoid non-deterministic drift.

Finally, detect and handle NaNs and Infs proactively. Many training scripts check for NaN/Inf in gradients or parameters and abort or roll back to the last checkpoint, lower the learning rate, reduce batch size, or reinitialize problematic layers. Logging gradient norms and activation statistics can alert you to instability early.

Putting it all together: stabilize exponentials via log-sum-exp, normalize activations with Batch/Layer/GroupNorm depending on architecture and batch size, prefer Pre-LayerNorm in deep transformer stacks, initialize scales and biases to avoid gate saturation and keep early layers near identity, and use pragmatic measures like gradient clipping, loss-scaling for mixed precision, epsilons in denominators, and cautious reduction/accumulation strategies. These practices form a toolbox that lets you translate mathematical models into robust, trainable implementations.

# Summary and practical checklist

Numerical stability is not a single fix but a set of interlocking design decisions. Keep in mind these practical rules:

- Understand floating-point limits: watch for overflow, underflow, and cancellation errors, especially with exponentials and long chains of operations.
- Use algebraic rearrangements like log-sum-exp and max-subtraction in softmax to avoid overflow and preserve precision in log-domain computations.
- Choose normalization appropriate to your architecture: BatchNorm for CNNs with large batches, LayerNorm for transformers and per-token normalization, GroupNorm for small-batch vision settings.
- Prefer Pre-LayerNorm for deep transformer stacks to improve gradient flow, while being mindful that Post-LayerNorm enforces normalized outputs per block.
- Initialize biases and scales to avoid gate saturation and consider small initial residual scaling so layers start close to identity.
- Employ pragmatic stabilizers: gradient clipping, loss scaling for mixed precision, epsilons in denominators, safe reductions, and NaN/Inf monitoring.

Combining theoretical understanding with these practical techniques will let you implement and train complex deep models reliably and interpret numerical issues when they arise.
