Introduction

Linear algebra is the language in which most of modern machine learning is expressed. Models, data, and operations are represented using vectors and matrices; optimization, regularization, and many algorithmic insights rely on properties of these objects. In this chapter we assemble the linear algebra and matrix-calculus fundamentals you need to read model definitions, follow derivations, and reason about algorithmic costs. We begin with the basic objects (vectors and matrices) and standard operations, then move on to key matrix concepts (outer product, rank, low-rank approximations), spectral decompositions (eigenvalues and singular value decomposition), and matrix derivatives (how to differentiate scalar and vector functions with respect to vectors and matrices). We conclude with Jacobians and gradients for common neural network layers and a practical discussion of computational complexity (flops and memory) for matrix operations. At each step we emphasize intuition and give formulas you will repeatedly use in machine learning research.

# Vectors, matrices, and basic matrix operations

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. In this chapter we treat vectors as column vectors unless otherwise specified. A column vector in $\\mathbb{R}^n$ is written as $x \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$, with components $x = [x_1, x_2, \\dots, x_n]^\\top$. A row vector is the transpose of a column vector: $x^\\top \\in \\mathbb{R}^{1\\times n}$. A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ with $m$ rows and $n$ columns; entry $(i,j)$ is written $A_{ij}$.

Basic operations include addition, scalar multiplication, and multiplication by vectors and matrices. Two matrices $A$ and $B$ can be added only if they have the same shape, and addition is elementwise: $(A+B)_{ij} = A_{ij} + B_{ij}$. Multiplication of a matrix by a scalar $\alpha \\in \\mathbb{R}$ scales every entry: $(\\alpha A)_{ij} = \\alpha A_{ij}$.

Matrix-vector multiplication produces a vector: for $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ and $x \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$, the product $y = Ax \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$ has components
$y_i = \\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij} x_j$.
This operation is a linear combination of the columns of $A$ where the coefficients are given by $x$. Equivalently, $Ax$ can be seen as taking inner products of the rows of $A$ with $x$, since $y_i = A_{i:} x$ where $A_{i:}$ denotes the $i$-th row as a row vector.

Matrix-matrix multiplication is defined when shapes align: for $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times p}$ and $B \\in \\mathbb{R}^{p\\times n}$, the product $C = AB \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ with entries
$C_{ij} = \\sum_{k=1}^p A_{ik} B_{kj}$.
Matrix multiplication composes linear maps: if $A$ maps $\\mathbb{R}^p \\to \\mathbb{R}^m$ and $B$ maps $\\mathbb{R}^n \\to \\mathbb{R}^p$, then $AB$ maps $\\mathbb{R}^n \\to \\mathbb{R}^m$. Note that matrix multiplication is associative ($A(BC) = (AB)C$) but not generally commutative ($AB \\neq BA$ in general).

Two other basic operations are important: the Hadamard (elementwise) product $A \\circ B$ defined by $(A \\circ B)_{ij} = A_{ij} B_{ij}$, and the Kronecker product $A \\otimes B$, which creates a block matrix useful in representing structured linear operations. The Hadamard product preserves the shape and is distributive over addition. For elementwise nonlinearities (applied componentwise to vectors or matrices) gradients are often expressed using Hadamard products.

# Matrix multiplication, transpose, trace, and Frobenius norm

Matrix transpose: for $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ the transpose $A^\\top \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times m}$ is defined by $(A^\\top)_{ji} = A_{ij}$. Transpose flips rows and columns and satisfies $(AB)^\\top = B^\\top A^\\top$, $(A^\\top)^\\top = A$.

The trace of a square matrix $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times n}$ is the sum of diagonal entries:
$\\operatorname{tr}(A) = \\sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}$.
Trace has several useful properties: it is linear, $\\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\\operatorname{tr}(A)+\\operatorname{tr}(B)$ and $\\operatorname{tr}(\\alpha A)=\\alpha \\operatorname{tr}(A)$. It is invariant under cyclic permutations: $\\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \\operatorname{tr}(BCA) = \\operatorname{tr}(CAB)$ when the products are defined. This cyclic property is very useful in matrix calculus and in deriving gradients.

The Frobenius norm of a matrix $A$ measures the root-sum-of-squares of its elements:
$\\|A\\|_F = \\sqrt{\\sum_{i=1}^m \\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}^2 }$.
It is equivalent to treating the matrix as a vector of length $mn$ and using the Euclidean norm. There is a useful link between Frobenius norm and trace:
$\\|A\\|_F^2 = \\operatorname{tr}(A^\\top A) = \\operatorname{tr}(A A^\\top)$.
This identity follows from expanding the traces and is often used to simplify expressions in optimization and regularization (e.g., weight decay is typically $\\lambda \\|W\\|_F^2$).

Another identity relates inner products of matrices to trace: the Frobenius inner product of $A$ and $B$ is $\\langle A, B \\rangle_F = \\sum_{i,j} A_{ij} B_{ij} = \\operatorname{tr}(A^\\top B)$. This is a convenient way to express sums of elementwise products using trace, which then interacts naturally with cyclic properties in derivatives.

Matrix multiplication can be seen in terms of these inner products: column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $B$, and each entry $(AB)_{ij}$ equals the inner product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$.

# Outer product, rank, and low-rank approximations

The outer product between a column vector $u \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$ and a row vector $v^\\top \\in \\mathbb{R}^{1\\times n}$ produces a matrix $uv^\\top \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ with entries $(uv^\\top)_{ij} = u_i v_j$. This matrix is of rank at most 1 (assuming neither $u$ nor $v$ is the zero vector, the rank is exactly 1). The outer product is the natural way to construct matrices from pairs of vectors and frequently appears in algorithms that update matrices incrementally: for example, a rank-1 update has the form $A \\leftarrow A + \\alpha u v^\\top$.

Rank of a matrix $A$ is the dimension of its column space (equivalently, its row space). Rank quantifies the number of linearly independent columns or rows. A full-rank matrix $A\\in\\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ has rank $\\min(m,n)$. Low-rank matrices are those with rank much smaller than $\\min(m,n)$. Many machine learning problems exploit low-rank structure: collaborative filtering, dimensionality reduction, and model compression all rely on low-rank approximations.

Low-rank approximation: Given $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$, a rank-$k$ approximation seeks a matrix $\\tilde A$ of rank $k$ that is close to $A$ in some norm (often Frobenius norm or operator norm). The Eckart–Young theorem states that the best rank-$k$ approximation in Frobenius norm (or spectral norm) is obtained by truncating the singular value decomposition (SVD) of $A$ to its top $k$ singular values and corresponding singular vectors. Concretely, if $A = U \\Sigma V^\\top$ with singular values $\\sigma_1 \\ge \\sigma_2 \\ge \\dots \\ge 0$, then the best rank-$k$ approximation is
$A_k = \\sum_{i=1}^k \\sigma_i u_i v_i^\\top$,
and it minimizes $\\|A - B\\|_F$ over all matrices $B$ of rank at most $k$. Intuitively, the outer-product decomposition $\\sigma_i u_i v_i^\\top$ expresses $A$ as a weighted sum of rank-1 factors sorted by importance $\\sigma_i$. Truncation keeps the most important components.

Low-rank factorization is also a parameterization: instead of storing $A$ explicitly, we can store $U \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times k}$ and $V \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times k}$ so that $A \\approx UV^\\top$. This reduces memory from $mn$ to $k(m+n)$ and speeds some operations when $k \\ll \\min(m,n)$. Many model compression techniques use this idea, and many optimization algorithms produce low-rank updates (outer products) during training.

# Eigenvalues, singular value decomposition (SVD)

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are defined for square matrices. For $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times n}$, a nonzero vector $v$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\\lambda$ if
$Av = \\lambda v$.
This means the linear map $A$ acts on $v$ by scaling. Not every matrix is diagonalizable, but if $A$ is symmetric ($A = A^\\top$), then it has orthonormal eigenvectors and real eigenvalues. A symmetric matrix can be decomposed as
$A = Q \\Lambda Q^\\top$,
where $Q$ is orthogonal ($Q^\\top Q = I$) with columns equal to eigenvectors, and $\\Lambda$ is diagonal with eigenvalues. This spectral decomposition is fundamental: for symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices all eigenvalues are nonnegative and $A$ can be written as a sum of rank-1 outer products,
$A = \\sum_{i=1}^n \\lambda_i q_i q_i^\\top$,
with $\\lambda_i \\ge 0$.

The singular value decomposition (SVD) generalizes spectral decomposition to arbitrary rectangular matrices. Any $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ admits an SVD
$A = U \\Sigma V^\\top$,
where $U \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times m}$ and $V \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times n}$ are orthogonal, and $\\Sigma \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ is diagonal (usually written with singular values $\\sigma_1 \\ge \\sigma_2 \\ge \\dots \\ge 0$ down the diagonal, with zeros padding if necessary). The nonzero singular values of $A$ are the square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^\\top A$; the right singular vectors $v_i$ are eigenvectors of $A^\\top A$, and the left singular vectors $u_i$ are eigenvectors of $A A^\\top$.

SVD gives several important consequences. First, it yields the best low-rank approximations (Eckart–Young theorem described earlier). Second, the operator (spectral) norm is $\\|A\\|_2 = \\sigma_1$, the largest singular value, and the Frobenius norm satisfies $\\|A\\|_F^2 = \\sum_i \\sigma_i^2$. Third, SVD provides a basis for understanding conditioning and stability: matrices with rapidly decaying singular values are close to low-rank and may be easier to approximate or compress.

Intuitively, SVD expresses $A$ as a sum of orthogonal rank-1 components:
$A = \\sum_{i=1}^{r} \\sigma_i u_i v_i^\\top$,
where $r = \\operatorname{rank}(A)$. Each term contributes a mode with strength $\\sigma_i$ and directional patterns $u_i$, $v_i$. In machine learning models like PCA, these singular vectors capture principal directions of data spread.

# Differentiation with respect to vectors and matrices (matrix derivatives)

Optimizing machine learning models requires computing derivatives of scalar and vector functions with respect to vectors and matrices. Understanding conventions and useful identities makes these calculations straightforward.

Gradient with respect to a vector: For a scalar function $f: \\mathbb{R}^n \\to \\mathbb{R}$, the gradient $\\nabla_x f$ is the column vector of partial derivatives:
$(\\nabla_x f)_i = \\frac{\\partial f}{\\partial x_i}$.
This column-vector convention is common in machine learning. Under this convention, for a linear function $f(x) = a^\\top x$ with $a \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$, we have $\\nabla_x f = a$.

For vector-valued functions $y(x) \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$ we use the Jacobian. The Jacobian matrix $J \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ has entries $J_{ij} = \\frac{\\partial y_i}{\\partial x_j}$. Note the row indexing by output coordinate and column by input coordinate is common; others prefer the transpose convention, so always check the author’s convention in papers.

Matrix derivatives: For functions that map matrices to scalars or matrices, similar conventions apply by stacking matrices into vectors using the vec operator: $\\operatorname{vec}(A)$ stacks columns of $A$ to form a long vector. Many derivative formulas use identities with $\\operatorname{vec}$ and Kronecker products to convert between matrix derivatives and standard vector calculus. For example, for $f(A) = \\operatorname{tr}(B^\\top A)$, the derivative with respect to $A$ is $\\frac{\\partial f}{\\partial A} = B$. We treat gradients with respect to matrices as matrices of the same shape as the variable, whose entries are the corresponding partial derivatives.

Useful differentiation identities (assuming dimensions match):

- Scalar-by-matrix trace identity:
If $f(X) = \\operatorname{tr}(A X)$ then $\\frac{\\partial f}{\\partial X} = A^\\top$ when treating gradient as matrix of partials aligned with $X$. If $A$ is symmetric this becomes $A$.

- Quadratic form in vector:
If $f(x) = x^\\top A x$ with $A$ symmetric, then $\\nabla_x f = (A + A^\\top) x = 2A x$ when $A$ is symmetric. More generally, $\\nabla_x (x^\\top A x) = (A + A^\\top) x$.

- Trace and matrix product:
$\\frac{\\partial}{\\partial X} \\operatorname{tr}(A X B X^\\top) = A^\\top X (B + B^\\top)$ under appropriate dimensions. These kinds of identities are common; deriving them uses the cyclic property of the trace and elementwise differentiation.

- Matrix inverse:
If $f(X) = X^{-1}$, then the differential is $d(X^{-1}) = -X^{-1} (dX) X^{-1}$. Therefore if $g(X) = \\operatorname{tr}(A X^{-1})$, then $\\frac{\\partial g}{\\partial X} = -X^{-\\top} A^\\top X^{-\\top}$.

Differentials vs. partial derivatives: A powerful way to derive matrix derivatives is via differentials. For a function $f(X)$, compute the differential $df$ in terms of $dX$ and then identify the gradient such that $df = \\langle \\nabla_X f, dX \\rangle_F = \\operatorname{tr}((\\nabla_X f)^\\top dX)$. This approach leverages trace identities and avoids working elementwise.

Be careful with shapes and transposes; always ensure the inner product under trace gives a scalar and that the gradient has the same shape as the variable. Many standard references (e.g., matrix calculus "cookbook") list commonly used derivatives; memorize a handful (linear, quadratic, trace, inverse, determinant, log-determinant) and use the differential method for the rest.

# Jacobians and gradients for common neural network layers

Neural networks are compositions of linear maps and elementwise nonlinearities. Building Jacobians and gradients for these components allows backpropagation derivations.

Linear (affine) layer: Consider $y = W x + b$ where $W \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$, $x \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$, $b \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$. The Jacobian of $y$ with respect to $x$ is simply $\\frac{\\partial y}{\\partial x} = W$, because $y_i = \\sum_j W_{ij} x_j + b_i$. For gradients in backpropagation, if the loss scalar is $L$ and we have $\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial y} = \\delta \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$, then the gradients are:
$\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial W} = \\delta \\, x^\\top$ (an outer product),
$\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial x} = W^\\top \\delta$,
$\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial b} = \\delta$.
Note that the gradient with respect to $W$ is an outer product of the upstream gradient and the input, which is why many optimization updates for fully connected layers are rank-1 updates scaled by the learning rate.

Elementwise nonlinearities: Let $z = f(x)$ be applied componentwise: $z_i = f(x_i)$ for some scalar nonlinear function $f$. The Jacobian is diagonal:
$\\frac{\\partial z}{\\partial x} = \\operatorname{diag}(f'(x_1), \\dots, f'(x_n))$.
In backprop, the upstream gradient $\\delta$ is multiplied elementwise: $\\delta_{\\text{in}} = f'(x) \\circ \\delta_{\\text{out}}$, where $\\circ$ denotes Hadamard product.

Softmax: Softmax maps $x \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$ to probabilities $y \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$ with $y_i = \\frac{e^{x_i}}{\\sum_j e^{x_j}}$. The Jacobian is
$\\frac{\\partial y_i}{\\partial x_j} = y_i (\\delta_{ij} - y_j)$,
or in matrix form $J = \\operatorname{diag}(y) - y y^\\top$. When combining softmax with cross-entropy loss for one-hot labels, the gradient simplifies to $\\nabla_x L = y - t$ where $t$ is the one-hot label vector; this cancellation explains why softmax+cross-entropy is numerically and analytically convenient.

Convolutional layers and structured linear maps: Convolutions are linear operations with weight sharing and local connectivity. The Jacobian of a convolution is a large sparse/block-sparse matrix determined by the convolutional kernel and stride; in practice we implement convolutions via fast algorithms (im2col + matrix multiply, FFT) and compute gradients using efficient backprop kernels. Conceptually, gradients with respect to kernels are sums of outer products across spatial positions: each position contributes a rank-1 update formed from the input patch and the upstream gradient at the output location.

Batch operations: When processing a batch of inputs, shapes grow an extra leading dimension. Gradients across the batch are sums (or averages) of per-example gradients because the loss is additive across examples. For example, if processing batch inputs $X \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times B}$ through $Y = W X + b 1_B^\\top$, the gradient with respect to $W$ is $\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial W} = \\Delta X^\\top$ where $\\Delta \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times B}$ collects upstream gradients for each example.

Chain rule and backprop: The chain rule in matrix/vector form uses Jacobians: if $y = g(x)$ and $z = h(y)$, then the Jacobian of $z$ with respect to $x$ is $\\frac{\\partial z}{\\partial x} = \\frac{\\partial z}{\\partial y} \\frac{\\partial y}{\\partial x}$. In backprop, we propagate gradients efficiently without forming full Jacobian matrices by relying on vector–Jacobian or Jacobian–vector products implemented using matrix multiplies (e.g., $\\nabla_x L = J^\\top \\delta$ without forming $J$ explicitly).

# Complexity of matrix operations (flops, memory)

Understanding computational cost and memory usage of matrix operations is essential for designing scalable models and algorithms. We measure compute in floating-point operations (flops); one flop is typically one floating-point add or multiply. For memory, we count numbers stored (and their byte sizes in practice, e.g., 4 bytes for float32).

Matrix multiplication: Multiplying $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times p}$ by $B \\in \\mathbb{R}^{p\\times n}$ to compute $C = AB$ requires, using the standard algorithm, $m n p$ scalar multiplications and $m n (p - 1)$ scalar additions, so approximately $2 m n p$ flops. For square matrices with $m = n = p = N$, complexity is $O(N^3)$ flops. In practice, highly optimized BLAS libraries use blocked algorithms and take advantage of cache to get much better performance than naive implementations. There exist asymptotically faster algorithms (Strassen, Coppersmith–Winograd), but they are rarely used in machine learning because constant factors and numerical stability make classical algorithms preferable.

Matrix-vector multiplication: For $A \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ and $x \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$, computing $Ax$ costs about $2 m n$ flops (multiplications plus additions) or more precisely $m (2n - 1)$ operations. Memory cost involves storing $A$ ($mn$ numbers) and $x$ ($n$ numbers) and producing $y$ ($m$ numbers).

Outer product: Computing the outer product $uv^\\top$ for $u \\in \\mathbb{R}^m$ and $v \\in \\mathbb{R}^n$ requires $mn$ multiplications (no additions) — about $mn$ flops. An outer product update $A \\leftarrow A + \\alpha u v^\\top$ is often cheaper than a full matrix multiply if we only need to add one rank-1 component.

SVD and eigen decomposition: Computing full SVD of an $m\\times n$ matrix typically costs $O(m n \\min(m,n))$ flops (for square $n\\times n$ matrices, $O(n^3)$). For large matrices where only a few singular values/vectors are needed, iterative methods (power method, Lanczos, randomized SVD) can compute the top-$k$ singular values in $O(m n k)$ flops or better, often much cheaper in practice. These approximate methods are important for low-rank approximations and PCA on large datasets.

Memory considerations: Storing a dense $m\\times n$ matrix requires $mn$ numbers, so memory scales quadratically in dimension for square problems. This can be a limiting factor. Low-rank factorizations store $U \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times k}$ and $V \\in \\mathbb{R}^{n\\times k}$ instead, reducing memory to $k(m+n)$. Sparse matrix representations store only nonzero entries and indices, so memory depends on the sparsity pattern.

Practical implications for neural networks: Fully connected layers with weight matrix $W \\in \\mathbb{R}^{m\\times n}$ store $mn$ parameters. If $m$ and $n$ are large, both compute for forward/backward passes and memory for parameter storage and gradients can be large. Convolutional layers have fewer parameters because of weight sharing; complexity depends on kernel size, input channels, and output channels. Batch processing increases temporary memory usage; storing activations for backprop requires memory proportional to the number of neurons times batch size unless checkpointing or recomputation is used.

Flops vs. memory bandwidth: On modern hardware, performance is often limited by memory bandwidth rather than raw flops. Highly compute-bound operations like large matrix multiplications can achieve high arithmetic intensity and be limited by compute, while operations with low arithmetic intensity (e.g., elementwise operations, small matrix multiplies) are often memory-bound. Understanding the trade-off helps in algorithm design (favoring blocked computations, reducing memory transfers, using fused kernels).

Summary and final remarks

This chapter covered the linear algebra and matrix calculus foundations used across machine learning. Vectors and matrices are the primary data structures; understanding operations such as multiplication, transpose, trace, and norms allows you to manipulate and simplify expressions. Outer products, rank, and low-rank approximations show how structure in data or models can be exploited for compression and efficiency. Eigenvalues and SVD give spectral perspectives that underlie PCA, conditioning, and approximation theory. Matrix derivatives and Jacobians are essential for deriving backpropagation and optimization updates; practical rules (trace identities, differentials) make many derivatives easy to obtain. Finally, complexity analysis in terms of flops and memory shapes design choices for scalable models and algorithms.

Keep a short list of commonly used identities and gradient formulas handy: trace identities, gradients of linear and quadratic forms, outer-product form of gradients for affine layers, Jacobian of softmax, and how to use differentials. These will accelerate your reading of research papers and your own derivations when designing or analyzing machine-learning models.
