Introduction

Scaling laws describe regular, quantitative relationships between model performance and resources invested in training: model size (number of parameters), amount of training data (tokens), and compute (measured in FLOPs or similar). Beginning with empirical observations and power-law fits published over the last several years, the field has moved from qualitative judgements ("bigger is better") to more actionable guidance: given a budget, how should you allocate compute between model size and dataset size to get the best performance? This chapter walks through the empirical scaling-law discoveries, the trade-offs they imply, the important revision introduced by Chinchilla (re-evaluating the model/data trade), practical consequences for training and dataset curation, how we should evaluate scaled models, and the limitations and caveats that must temper any simple recipe.

# Empirical scaling laws (Kaplan et al.) and power-law fits

The starting empirical observation is simple: holding other factors fixed, test loss (or other performance metrics) tends to decrease smoothly as you increase model size or dataset size. Kaplan et al. (2020) formalized this by fitting power laws to held-out cross-entropy loss as a function of the number of parameters $N$ and the number of training tokens $D$. A compact way to write a two-variable scaling law is
\[
L(N, D) \approx L_\infty + a_N N^{-\alpha_N} + a_D D^{-\alpha_D},
\]
where $L$ is the loss, $L_\infty$ is an irreducible loss floor (model- and data-independent residual), $a_N,a_D$ are prefactors and $\alpha_N,\alpha_D$ are positive exponents. This functional form says that gains from increasing $N$ or $D$ fall off as power laws rather than, say, exponentially or in abrupt steps. The fitted exponents in Kaplan et al.'s experiments were small positive numbers, indicating slow but steady improvement with scale. The key features to understand are qualitative:

- Power-law decrease: improvements scale as a power of $N$ or $D$. Doubling parameters gives a fixed fractional reduction in loss determined by the exponent $\alpha_N$; likewise for data.
- Separability: to first approximation the contributions of model size and dataset size can be expressed as separate power-law terms, enabling simple reasoning about trade-offs.
- Smoothness: performance changes smoothly with scale, which allows confident extrapolation (within reason) from measured regimes to somewhat-larger regimes.

The practical consequence of a power law is predictability: you can fit exponents and prefactors on smaller-scale experiments and use these to forecast marginal gains for larger investments. That property is what made scaling laws immediately useful for planning research and engineering: once you know the exponents, you can answer questions like "If I double my compute, should I double parameters or double training tokens?" The empirical separable fit above also suggests there exist iso-performance contours in $(N,D)$ space: combinations of $N$ and $D$ that yield the same loss.

It is important to note that Kaplan et al.'s fits were empirical and limited to ranges of $N$ and $D$ they studied. The exponents and prefactors depend on model architecture, training recipe, and the particular loss being predicted (cross-entropy vs. reward-model loss vs. other objectives). Therefore the laws are best interpreted as descriptive within the studied regime, rather than universal physical laws.

# Trade-offs between model size, dataset size, and compute budget

When you train models you face a three-way allocation problem: how to distribute compute budget between making the model larger (increasing parameters) and training it on more data (increasing the number of tokens seen). Compute used during training is roughly proportional to the product of model size and the number of tokens processed, though details (e.g., optimizer steps, sequence length, model architecture) change the constant factors. A simplifying model for many discussions is
\[
C \propto N \cdot D,
\]
where $C$ denotes total training compute in some unit. With that relationship, and a scaling law for loss $L(N,D)$, we can ask: given fixed compute $C$, what pair $(N,D)$ minimizes $L$? The answer emerges from minimizing $L(N,D)$ subject to $N\cdot D = C$; graphically, this is finding the point where an iso-compute hyperbola touches a loss contour.

Because the marginal improvement from additional parameters and additional tokens follows power laws with different exponents, there is a nontrivial optimum. If the exponent for data $\alpha_D$ is larger than that for parameters $\alpha_N$, investing in more data yields greater loss reduction per unit compute than enlarging the model, and vice versa. Hence compute-optimal allocation depends on the relative magnitudes of the exponents and the prefactors in the scaling law.

Two often-discussed regimes are parameter-limited and data-limited training. In a parameter-limited regime you have many tokens relative to model size: further gains mostly come from enlarging the model. In a data-limited regime you have a large model that has not seen enough data: gains come from providing more tokens. Historically, many large models were trained in a parameter-limited regime (large models but relatively modest datasets), which biases how we interpret scaling results if we do not account for allocation.

Another practical viewpoint is iso-loss lines. Suppose you want to reach a particular loss target. The set of $(N,D)$ pairs that reach that target typically form a power-law curve. Following along such curves you can trade off parameters for tokens: for example, if you halve parameters you may need to increase tokens by a factor determined by the ratio of exponents to stay on the same loss contour. Understanding this trade-off lets managers and researchers choose allocations that match hardware and data-availability constraints.

# Chinchilla scaling and data/model compute-optimal regimes

Hoffmann et al. (2022), the Chinchilla paper, re-examined the compute allocation question by training many models where both model size and the number of tokens were varied carefully at fixed compute budgets. Their central empirical correction relative to earlier work was that many contemporary large models were undertrained in data: they were too large for the amount of data they saw given the compute spent. Chinchilla argued and demonstrated that for optimal use of a compute budget you should make models smaller and train them on more tokens than was common practice.

The simplified message from Chinchilla can be expressed as: there exists an approximately linear relationship between training tokens $D$ and model parameters $N$ in the compute-optimal regime,
\[
D_{\mathrm{opt}} \approx k \cdot N,
\]
for some constant $k$ (tokens per parameter). Chinchilla reported a value of $k$ on the order of tens (commonly quoted as roughly 20 tokens per parameter), meaning that optimal training often requires many more tokens than were used in some earlier large-scale training runs. Put another way, for a fixed total compute $C$, the compute-optimal choice is to reduce $N$ and increase $D$ relative to the strategy that had been common: make the model somewhat smaller and train it longer, which yields better generalization per unit compute.

A useful way to see why a linear relationship emerges is to combine the power-law loss model with the compute constraint. If the dominant loss contributions from parameters and data are of similar magnitude at optimality, minimizing loss naturally leads to a scaling where $D$ and $N$ are proportional. The constant of proportionality depends on the fitted exponents and prefactors. Chinchilla’s empirical sweeps gave numbers that made $D$ much larger relative to $N$ than earlier practice.

Chinchilla’s finding has several consequences. For example, under the Chinchilla prescription, smaller models trained on more data can outperform larger models trained on less data for the same compute. This flips some earlier intuitions about the direction of scale. It also emphasized data acquisition and curation: if optimal models need considerably more tokens, then dataset availability and quality become first-order constraints on improving models.

It is worth emphasizing the conditional nature of this result: Chinchilla’s conclusions are tied to the architectures, optimization recipes, and data types used in their experiments. Other architectures or objective functions might have different proportionality constants or different optima. Nevertheless, the conceptual point—compute-optimality requires jointly choosing model size and data quantity—holds broadly.

# Practical implications for training regimes and dataset curation

The empirical scaling laws and the Chinchilla correction change how engineers and researchers should plan training. First, when given a compute budget, one should not assume that increasing model size alone is the best use of compute. Instead, perform small sweeps or rely on validated scaling fits to determine the optimal $N$–$D$ trade for your architecture and task.

From a training-regime point of view, the data-optimal recommendation often translates into training models for more steps (or over more examples) and using smaller models that are trained thoroughly. This has numerous operational consequences: smaller models have lower memory and communication costs per parameter, enabling larger effective batch sizes or more sophisticated optimizers; training for more tokens increases I/O pressure and amplifies the importance of dataset management (shuffling, deduplication, dedup-aware evaluation splits, etc.). Practitioners should also be mindful of the optimizer schedule: longer training runs can run into issues like overfitting to repeated data, optimizer hyperparameter drift, or the need for learning-rate re-warmup strategies.

Dataset curation becomes markedly more important when the optimal regime favors more tokens. The naive route of endlessly concatenating web-crawled text can lead to severe duplication, contamination of test sets, and domain imbalance. Deduplication and dedup-aware evaluation are crucial because repeated examples can make the model memorize or leak test data, creating misleadingly optimistic evaluations. Quality filtering (e.g., removing low-quality scrape artifacts, non-natural language noise, or irrelevant code) improves sample efficiency: high-quality tokens are worth more than low-quality ones, and scaling laws implicitly assume tokens convey signal.

Diversity of data also matters. If your dataset is excessively concentrated in a narrow domain, more tokens may yield diminishing returns due to limited representational diversity. Conversely, many downstream tasks benefit from broad, diverse pretraining corpora. Therefore curators should assemble balanced mixtures and consider stratified sampling to ensure adequate representation of under-covered domains if those domains are important for downstream behavior.

Finally, practical training allocations must account for hardware constraints. Disk throughput, network bandwidth (for distributed training), and checkpointing strategy interact with the compute-optimal choices. A theoretically optimal $N$ and $D$ might be impractical if the training pipeline cannot deliver the required steady stream of tokens at the needed throughput. In such cases, approximate optimal choices that respect system boundaries are the sensible engineering compromise.

# Evaluation regimes for scaled models (downstream tasks, zero/few-shot)

Scaling laws show predictable improvements on held-out loss, but the ultimate goal of many applications is downstream task performance: classification, question-answering, code generation, reasoning, and so on. Empirical studies have shown that many downstream capabilities correlate with (and often improve smoothly with) pretraining loss: reductions in loss translate into better few-shot and zero-shot performance on many benchmarks. However, the transfer curve is not uniform across tasks.

Zero-shot and few-shot evaluation became central with the emergence of in-context learning: rather than fine-tuning, you present a few labeled examples in the context and prompt the model. Scaling tends to improve few-shot performance more rapidly than fixed-architecture fine-tuned performance for many language tasks; large models can do in-context learning that smaller models cannot. Nevertheless, for some tasks—especially those requiring fine-grained, long-chained reasoning or domain-specific knowledge—simply increasing scale does not produce a perfect substitute for fine-tuning or for encoding explicit reasoning modules.

When evaluating scaled models, it is important to go beyond aggregate accuracy numbers. Larger models can exhibit different failure modes: they may be better calibrated or worse; more likely to confidently generate plausible-sounding but incorrect statements (hallucinations); more or less biased depending on pretraining data; and different in terms of robustness to distribution shift. Therefore evaluation regimes should include (a) held-out test sets drawn from realistic downstream distributions, (b) zero- and few-shot probes across diverse task types, (c) stress tests for adversarial or out-of-distribution inputs, (d) calibration and uncertainty assessment, and (e) ethical and safety evaluations (toxicity, privacy leakage, etc.).

A further subtlety is data contamination. As models are trained on ever-larger web-scale corpora, there is increasing likelihood that test-set examples for downstream tasks (e.g., popular benchmark questions) appear in pretraining data. This contamination can lead to overestimates of model capability if not properly mitigated. Good evaluation practice therefore includes careful deduplication and validation that the model did not see evaluation examples during pretraining.

Finally, scaling changes how we interpret benchmarking suites. Some benchmarks saturate as models grow: once a simple approach reaches very high accuracy, further scale may show little improvement even though other capabilities continue to grow. Benchmark design should therefore include challenging, compositional, and diverse tasks to remain informative across wide ranges of model scale.

# Limitations and caveats of scaling laws

While scaling laws are powerful tools, they come with many caveats that must be remembered when applying them.

First, empirical laws are extrapolations. Power-law fits are valid within the range of data used to fit them; extrapolating far beyond that range can fail because new phenomena (phase transitions, saturation, different failure modes) can appear. For example, architectural changes, changes in tokenization, or multi-modal extensions can alter the exponents and prefactors.

Second, scaling laws describe average or aggregate behavior (e.g., cross-entropy loss) but do not fully capture metrics that are sensitive to rare events or specific capabilities. Some abilities may emerge rapidly at certain scales (sometimes called "phase changes") and are not predicted by smooth power laws. Conversely, some problems may require qualitatively different architectures or training objectives rather than mere scale.

Third, dataset quality and distribution matter. Tokens are not homogenous units of signal. Low-quality data, spam, repeated passages, or language that is irrelevant to target tasks can blunt the efficacy of additional tokens. Scaling laws measured on cleaned, diverse corpora may not hold if you flood training with low-signal tokens. That’s part of why Chinchilla’s prescription emphasizes tokens per parameter but does not dispense with careful curation.

Fourth, compute accounting is messy. Different ways of counting compute (including or excluding evaluation, hyperparameter search, data pre-processing, optimizer cost differences, and architecture-specific operation counts) change the numbers used in "optimal" formulas. Distributed training overheads, communication costs, and memory constraints also influence the best practical choices. Thus a compute-optimal solution in theory may be suboptimal in practice.

Fifth, architecture and algorithmic advances change the landscape. Optimizers, mixed-precision arithmetic, efficient attention mechanisms, mixture-of-experts, retrieval-augmented approaches, and better pretraining objectives can change the relationship between compute, parameters, and required tokens. For instance, retrieval or memory mechanisms can reduce the need for memorization within the model parameters, changing the effective tokens-per-parameter trade-off.

Sixth, social and ethical considerations are not captured by simple scaling metrics. Large datasets scraped from the web may contain private data, copyrighted material, or harmful content. Expanding data to follow a tokens-per-parameter prescription must be balanced against legal, ethical, and social constraints.

Seventh, the metrics used to fit scaling laws (commonly cross-entropy loss) are only proxies for downstream utility. Optimizing for a proxy without testing the actual end-task objectives can yield mismatches.

In short, scaling laws are actionable but not prescriptive: they provide valuable guidance but must be applied with domain knowledge and careful engineering judgment.

Conclusion

Empirical scaling laws and the Chinchilla re-evaluation have shifted how we think about allocating compute between model size and data. The big takeaways are that performance improvements follow predictable power-law relationships within studied regimes, that compute-optimal allocations often require training smaller models on more tokens than previously common, and that data quality, evaluation rigor, and practical system constraints are crucial for realizing the predicted gains. At the same time, these laws are empirical, contingent, and incomplete: architecture changes, dataset issues, and emergent capabilities mean that continual measurement and careful extrapolation remain essential. For students entering the field, mastering these ideas equips you to design experiments, budget resources, and critically read contemporary research on large language models.
