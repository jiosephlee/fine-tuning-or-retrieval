Introduction

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are a fundamental class of models designed to process sequential data. Unlike feedforward networks, which assume input examples are independent, RNNs maintain a hidden state that evolves over time and can carry information across sequence positions. This makes them suitable for tasks such as language modeling, speech recognition, time series prediction, and any problem where past context matters for current predictions. In this chapter we develop the standard "vanilla" RNN formulation, explain how to view the model as an unrolled computational graph over time, and describe how learning is performed by Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT). We analyze the important training challenges—vanishing and exploding gradients—their causes and consequences, and practical techniques to mitigate them. Finally, we consider the expressive power and limitations of simple RNNs and discuss computational and memory trade-offs that arise when training RNNs on long sequences.

# Vanilla RNN formulation and unrolling across time

The simplest recurrent neural network, often called the "vanilla RNN," maintains a hidden state vector that is updated at each time step by combining the current input with the previous hidden state. Let the sequence inputs be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$ where each $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$. Let $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$ denote the hidden state at time $t$ and $y_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_y}$ the output at time $t$ (if outputs are produced at each step). A common formulation is:
$$
h_t = \phi\big(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t + b_h\big),
$$
$$
o_t = W_{ho} h_t + b_o,
$$
$$
y_t = \psi(o_t),
$$
where $W_{hh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$ is the recurrent weight matrix, $W_{xh} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_x}$ maps inputs into hidden space, $W_{ho} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_y \times d_h}$ maps hidden states to outputs, $b_h$ and $b_o$ are bias vectors, $\phi$ is an elementwise nonlinearity for the hidden state (commonly $\tanh$ or ReLU), and $\psi$ is an output nonlinearity (softmax for classification, identity for regression, etc.). The initial hidden state $h_0$ can be set to zeros, learned, or initialized in other ways.

To build intuition, think of $h_t$ as a memory that is updated incrementally. The term $W_{hh} h_{t-1}$ propagates past information forward; the new input $x_t$ modifies the memory via $W_{xh} x_t$. The nonlinearity $\phi$ enables the model to perform nonlinear transformations and to gate or reshape information.

Training and reasoning about the RNN are easiest when we view the model as an unrolled computational graph over time. Unrolling means drawing a copy of the network cell for every time step and connecting them through the hidden states. In the unrolled graph, the parameters $W_{hh}$, $W_{xh}$, and $W_{ho}$ are shared across time steps. The unrolled representation explicitly shows the dependencies of each output and loss on earlier hidden states and inputs, and it is the foundation for deriving gradients in BPTT.

For a sequence of length $T$, the unrolled graph has $T$ nodes (cells) arranged sequentially. The output at time $t$, and any loss contributed at that time, depends on the path through previous states back to $h_0$. Mathematically, if we define some loss terms $\ell_t(y_t, \hat{y}_t)$ at each time step, the total loss for the sequence is $L = \sum_{t=1}^T \ell_t$. The gradient of $L$ with respect to parameters must account for how changes at early times propagate forward and influence later losses—a key reason why training RNNs is delicate.

# Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) and truncated BPTT

Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) is the algorithm to compute gradients in RNNs. Conceptually, BPTT applies standard backpropagation to the unrolled network. Because the parameters are shared across time, the gradient with respect to a parameter is the sum (over time) of gradients computed at each time step where that parameter was used, and each of those single-step gradients accounts for the influence of the parameter through future time steps.

To see the mechanics, consider the gradient of the total loss $L$ with respect to the recurrent weights $W_{hh}$. Using the chain rule on the unrolled graph,
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_t} \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial W_{hh}}.
$$
But $\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_t}$ itself aggregates gradients from losses at time $t$ and from later times because $h_t$ affects $h_{t+1}, h_{t+2}, \dots$. One way of writing the recursive relationship is
$$
\delta_t \equiv \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_t} = \frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial h_t} + \left(\frac{\partial h_{t+1}}{\partial h_t}\right)^\top \delta_{t+1},
$$
with base case $\delta_{T+1} = 0$. Here $\frac{\partial h_{t+1}}{\partial h_t}$ contains the Jacobian of the recurrent update, for the vanilla RNN typically $J_{t+1} = \operatorname{diag}(\phi'(a_{t+1})) W_{hh}$ where $a_{t+1} = W_{hh} h_t + W_{xh} x_{t+1} + b_h$. Thus gradients are propagated backward in time via repeated multiplication by such Jacobian matrices. Implementationally, this is handled by iterating backward from $t = T$ to $1$ and accumulating gradients for parameters.

A practical problem is that sequences in real tasks can be very long (hundreds or thousands of steps), and full BPTT over all time steps can be computationally expensive and memory intensive: you must store all intermediate activations to compute gradients, and the computational cost grows linearly with sequence length. Moreover, because of vanishing/exploding gradient issues (discussed next), contributions from far past to the gradient can be negligible, making full BPTT inefficient.

To mitigate these concerns, practitioners often use truncated BPTT. In truncated BPTT, instead of backpropagating through the entire history, gradients are backpropagated through a fixed window of $k$ time steps. There are two common patterns. In "fixed-window" truncated BPTT you divide a long sequence into segments of length $k$ and perform forward and backward passes inside each segment, carrying the final hidden state of one segment as the initial hidden state of the next during the forward pass but not connecting gradient flows across segment boundaries. In other implementations, one runs the forward pass across many steps but accumulates gradients and then truncates the backward pass every $k$ steps. Truncated BPTT reduces memory usage and computation per parameter update and can serve as a regularizer by ignoring extremely long-range dependencies that the model cannot learn reliably.

However, truncated BPTT introduces a bias: the gradients no longer account for dependencies longer than the truncation window. If the task requires learning long-term dependencies beyond $k$ steps, training may fail. Choosing $k$ is therefore a trade-off between computational cost and the ability to learn long-term relationships.

# Vanishing and exploding gradients: causes and implications

When we propagate gradients backward through time in an RNN, we repeatedly multiply by Jacobian matrices that represent how the hidden state at one time affects the hidden state at the next time. Consider the product of Jacobians from time $t$ to $t+\tau$:
$$
\frac{\partial h_{t+\tau}}{\partial h_t} = \prod_{i=1}^{\tau} \left.\frac{\partial h_{t+i}}{\partial h_{t+i-1}}\right. .
$$
If the norms of these Jacobian matrices are, on average, less than 1 in the sense of their dominant singular values, then the product tends to shrink exponentially with $\tau$, causing gradients propagated from distant future losses back to $h_t$ to become exponentially small. This is the vanishing gradient problem. Conversely, if the dominant singular values are greater than 1, the product can grow exponentially, causing exploding gradients. The core cause is the multiplicative nature of backpropagation through long chains of dependencies.

For a more concrete intuition, consider a linearized version of the RNN around typical activations: $h_t \approx W_{hh} h_{t-1}$. Then
$$
\frac{\partial h_{t+\tau}}{\partial h_t} \approx W_{hh}^\tau.
$$
If the spectral radius $\rho(W_{hh})$ (the largest absolute eigenvalue) is less than 1, then $W_{hh}^\tau \to 0$ as $\tau$ grows, producing vanishing gradients. If $\rho(W_{hh}) > 1$, then $W_{hh}^\tau$ will grow and can lead to exploding gradients. Nonlinearities complicate the picture but do not eliminate this multiplicative effect; elementwise derivatives such as $\phi'(a)$ also contribute factors (for $\tanh$ these derivatives are at most 1 and often less than 1), which tends to push dynamics towards vanishing gradients unless special structure prevents it.

Implications: Vanishing gradients make it difficult for standard RNNs to learn long-range dependencies—information from many steps in the past cannot influence parameter updates for losses far in the future because the gradient signal decays to near zero. This manifests in practice as an inability to learn tasks requiring remembering information across long delays. Exploding gradients produce extremely large parameter updates that destabilize learning, leading to overflow, loss divergence, or model collapse unless mitigated.

From an optimization perspective, vanishing gradients cause early layers (or early time steps) to receive negligible gradient magnitudes, so they either learn extremely slowly or effectively stop learning. Exploding gradients, on the other hand, can cause overshooting and break the assumptions of gradient-based optimization methods. Both phenomena are pervasive when backpropagating through many time steps and are central to understanding RNN training dynamics.

# Techniques to mitigate gradient issues (clipping, initialization)

Several techniques have been developed to mitigate vanishing and exploding gradients. Some address exploding gradients directly, others help alleviate vanishing gradients by modifying architecture or initialization. We cover the most widely used practical methods.

Gradient clipping is the most common technique to control exploding gradients. The idea is simple: if the norm of the gradient (for a parameter vector or for the gradient contribution accumulated over a time window) exceeds a threshold, rescale it to have that threshold norm. Formally, for gradient vector $g$, clipped gradient $g_{\text{clip}}$ is
$$
g_{\text{clip}} = \begin{cases}
g & \text{if } \|g\|_2 \leq \tau,\\
\tau \cdot \dfrac{g}{\|g\|_2} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
$$
with threshold $\tau > 0$. Clipping stabilizes updates by preventing extremely large changes and works well in practice with stochastic gradient optimizers. An alternative is elementwise clipping (clipping each component to lie in some interval), but norm clipping is often preferred because it preserves gradient direction.

Proper weight initialization helps with both vanishing and exploding gradients. The goal is to start with parameter values that keep the Jacobians near scale 1 so that gradient magnitudes neither vanish nor explode rapidly. For feedforward networks there are well-known initializations (Xavier/Glorot, He). For RNNs, one common heuristic is to initialize $W_{hh}$ to be orthogonal (or approximately orthogonal). Orthogonal matrices have spectral radius 1 and preserve vector norms (up to the effect of nonlinearities), which can help prevent vanishing or exploding dynamics at initialization. More precisely, if $W_{hh}$ is orthogonal and $\phi$ is linear, then $W_{hh}^\tau$ preserves norms; with nonlinear $\phi$, preservation is approximate but can still be beneficial.

Scaling $W_{xh}$ and other non-recurrent weights carefully can also help. Using small initial biases (or special biases, e.g., large forget bias for gated architectures) is another practice that influences dynamics. For activation functions, while $\tanh$ produces bounded activations and derivatives in $(0,1]$, ReLU activations introduce piecewise linear behavior that can help gradient flow because derivatives are 0 or 1 instead of being small numbers like 0.01 in sigmoid saturations. But ReLU RNNs must be handled carefully because ReLU unbounded growth can cause instability.

Architectural solutions are the most effective for vanishing gradients over long time scales. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) cells introduce gating mechanisms and paths with nearly identity Jacobians that can better propagate gradients across long time steps. For example, the LSTM has a cell state with additive updates gated by input and forget gates, enabling near-linear gradient flow when gates allow. These architectures were explicitly designed to address the vanishing gradient problem and have become standard for many sequence tasks.

Other techniques include using layer normalization or batch normalization (adapted for RNNs) to keep activations in ranges where gradients are stable, and using skip connections across time (residual connections in time) which create shorter gradient paths. Using second-order optimization methods (e.g., natural gradient, K-FAC approximations) can also help by adapting steps to curvature; these methods are more complex and computationally heavier but can mitigate sensitivity to gradient scaling.

Finally, combining truncated BPTT with larger truncation windows, curriculum training, or auxiliary losses that encourage carrying useful information across time can help the model learn long-term dependencies indirectly.

# Expressive power and limitations of simple RNNs

Simple or "vanilla" RNNs are, in principle, Turing-complete under certain conditions: with arbitrary precision and unbounded state, recurrent networks can implement arbitrary computable functions over sequences. More concretely, for rational weights and useful nonlinearities, RNNs can emulate finite-state machines and, with more complex configurations, pushdown automata or even Turing machines under idealized assumptions. However, these theoretical expressivity results are of limited practical importance because they often rely on unrealistic conditions: arbitrarily precise weights, infinite precision arithmetic, and arbitrarily long hidden states.

In practice, vanilla RNNs have several limitations that affect their ability to learn complex sequential patterns. First, as discussed above, learning long-range dependencies is hard due to vanishing gradients; even though the model could represent a long-term dependency in principle, training via gradient descent may fail to find the necessary weights. Second, vanilla RNNs with simple activation functions (e.g., $\tanh$ or sigmoid) have difficulty preserving information over long time scales because the activations saturate and the state can be overwritten easily. ReLU-based RNNs can preserve some information but introduce other issues like dead units or exploding activations.

Another limitation is difficulty in representing hierarchical or compositional structure that appears in language or program-like sequences. While RNNs can approximate many functions, they often need many hidden units and complex training regimes to learn structured algorithms like precise counting or nested dependencies. Architectural modifications such as gating (LSTM, GRU) or external memory (Neural Turing Machines, memory networks) are more effective at learning such behaviors.

From a statistical perspective, RNNs are powerful sequence models but can overfit on small datasets because parameter sharing across time reduces effective sample size but the model can still have many parameters. Regularization techniques (dropout adapted to RNNs, weight decay, early stopping) are necessary in practice.

Finally, the hidden state size $d_h$ limits expressivity: small $d_h$ constrains the capacity to store multiple independent pieces of information. Increasing $d_h$ increases capacity but also computational cost and risk of overfitting. Therefore, practical RNN design must balance model size with the nature of the sequential dependencies one expects to capture.

# Computational/time complexity and memory trade-offs for RNN training

Training RNNs involves trade-offs between computation, memory, and the quality of gradient estimates. Consider a sequence of length $T$ and batch size $B$. For a vanilla RNN with hidden size $d_h$ and input dimension $d_x$, the forward pass at each time step computes $W_{hh} h_{t-1}$ costing $O(d_h^2)$ per time step (if $W_{hh}$ is dense) and $W_{xh} x_t$ costing $O(d_h d_x)$. Over $T$ steps, forward computation is $O(T d_h^2 + T d_h d_x)$. The backward pass through time requires similar complexity; backpropagating gradients also needs $O(T d_h^2)$ operations. Thus per parameter update, computation scales linearly with sequence length $T$—long sequences increase compute time proportionally.

Memory is another constraint. Full BPTT requires storing the activations at every time step that are needed for gradient computation: the hidden states $h_t$, preactivations $a_t$ (for computing $\phi'(a_t)$), and possibly intermediate results of linear transforms. The memory cost is $O(T d_h)$ per sequence (and multiplied by batch size $B$), which can become the dominant resource for long sequences and large hidden sizes. This is why truncated BPTT is often used: by truncating backpropagation to windows of length $k$, one only needs to store activations for $k$ steps at a time, reducing memory to $O(k d_h)$ per sequence.

There are trade-offs between computation and gradient quality. Using smaller truncation windows reduces memory and computation per update but ignores dependencies longer than $k$ steps, possibly biasing learning. Using full BPTT gives unbiased gradients but may be infeasible on long sequences. A compromise is to use truncated BPTT with overlapping windows or to compute gradients with different truncation lengths across training (e.g., occasionally doing longer BPTT passes).

Batching sequences is another practical design choice. For variable-length sequences, padding to a common length allows vectorized computation but increases wasted work; bucketing sequences of similar length reduces waste. Stateful RNN training, where hidden states are carried across mini-batches, can amortize computation when training on very long continuous streams, but it complicates gradient bookkeeping and may require special care with resetting states for independent sequences.

Parallelism is challenging for RNNs because the time steps are sequentially dependent: you cannot compute $h_{t+1}$ before $h_t$ is known. This limits the degree to which training can exploit intra-sequence parallelism. However, parallelism across different sequences in a batch is straightforward and commonly used. Architectural modifications that loosen strict sequential dependence—e.g., transformers using attention—are more parallelizable, which explains their adoption for many sequence tasks despite different inductive biases.

Modeling choices affect complexity: sparse or low-rank parameterizations of $W_{hh}$ can reduce $O(d_h^2)$ costs; shared linear layers or factorized decompositions lower parameter counts. Using more complex cells like LSTM increases per-step computation and memory (additional gates and cell states), but often yields better sample efficiency because they learn long-term dependencies more effectively, potentially reducing the total number of training steps required.

Finally, optimization choices such as gradient clipping add small extra cost (computing norms), while second-order methods add significant overhead but can sometimes reduce the number of required iterations. Practitioners must balance these factors—sequence length, hidden dimensionality, truncation length, batching strategy, and hardware constraints—when designing RNN training pipelines.

Concluding remarks

Vanilla RNNs provide a clear, compact formalism for modeling sequential data, and the unrolled view of computation makes the dynamics and gradient flows explicit. Training with BPTT is a direct application of backpropagation across time, but it brings practical challenges—vanishing and exploding gradients, memory and compute scaling, and difficulties learning long-range dependencies. The field has developed a rich set of mitigation strategies, from simple techniques like gradient clipping and careful initialization to architectural innovations like LSTMs and GRUs, which have become standard tools. Understanding these foundational aspects is essential for reading research papers in sequence modeling, diagnosing training issues, and choosing appropriate models and training regimes for real-world sequence tasks.
