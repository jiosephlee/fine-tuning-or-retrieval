Introduction

This chapter introduces the probabilistic and information-theoretic foundations used to formulate, train, and evaluate sequence models, with a particular focus on language modeling. Language models assign probabilities to sequences of discrete tokens (characters, subwords, words). Understanding how these probabilities are defined, estimated, and compared is essential for both building models and interpreting research results. We begin with the basic language of random variables and probability distributions, then move to estimation (likelihood and maximum likelihood), and then cover information-theoretic quantities that serve both as objectives and evaluation metrics: cross-entropy, Kullback–Leibler divergence, entropy, and information content. We then connect these concepts to practical metrics like perplexity, discuss evaluation protocols specific to autoregressive models (including issues such as tokenization and dataset splits), and close with standard tools for assessing whether observed differences between models are statistically meaningful.

# Random variables and joint/conditional distributions

A random variable is a formal object that represents the outcome of a stochastic process. For language modeling, a random variable might represent the next token in a sequence, denoted by $X$, which takes values in a discrete set (vocabulary) $\mathcal{V}$. A sequence of tokens of length $T$ can be treated as a vector of random variables $(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_T)$, where each $X_t$ takes values in $\mathcal{V}$.

The joint distribution of several random variables describes the probability assigned to each possible combination of their values. For a sequence $(X_1, \dots, X_T)$, the joint distribution is a function $P(X_1=x_1, \dots, X_T=x_T)$ that maps each length-$T$ token sequence $(x_1,\dots,x_T)$ to a probability in $[0,1]$, subject to the normalization constraint that the sum over all sequences equals 1:
\[
\sum_{(x_1,\dots,x_T)\in\mathcal{V}^T} P(X_1=x_1,\dots,X_T=x_T) = 1.
\]
In practice the number of possible sequences grows exponentially with $T$, which motivates structured models such as autoregressive factorizations.

Conditional distributions express the probability of one subset of variables given fixed values for another subset. For two variables $X$ and $Y$, the conditional distribution $P(X=x \mid Y=y)$ quantifies the uncertainty about $X$ assuming we know $Y=y$. The fundamental relation linking joint and conditional distributions is
\[
P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x \mid Y=y)\,P(Y=y).
\]
For sequences, the chain rule of probability provides a factorization of the joint distribution into a product of conditional probabilities:
\[
P(X_1,\dots,X_T) = P(X_1)\,P(X_2\mid X_1)\,P(X_3\mid X_1,X_2)\,\cdots\,P(X_T\mid X_1,\dots,X_{T-1}).
\]
Autoregressive models use precisely this factorization: a model defines $P_\theta(X_t\mid X_{<t})$, where $X_{<t}$ denotes the history $(X_1,\dots,X_{t-1})$, and the joint probability of a full sequence under the model is the product of these conditionals. This factorized view makes both modeling and training tractable since we can parameterize and optimize these per-step conditional distributions.

When discussing these distributions it is important to distinguish between the true data-generating distribution (sometimes denoted by $P^*$ or simply $P$) and a model distribution parameterized by parameters $\theta$, denoted $P_\theta$. The true distribution is unknown; the goal of modeling is to find $P_\theta$ that approximates the true distribution well with respect to some criterion.

# Likelihood, log-likelihood, and maximum likelihood estimation

Given observed data, the likelihood function measures how well a candidate model explains that data. Suppose we observe $N$ independent sequences $S^{(1)},\dots,S^{(N)}$, where $S^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_1,\dots,x^{(i)}_{T_i})$. Under an autoregressive model $P_\theta$, the likelihood of the entire dataset is
\[
L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N P_\theta\big(S^{(i)}\big) = \prod_{i=1}^N \prod_{t=1}^{T_i} P_\theta\big(x^{(i)}_t \mid x^{(i)}_{<t}\big).
\]
Because products of many probabilities can become extremely small, and because optimization is easier with sums than products, we typically work with the log-likelihood:
\[
\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log P_\theta\big(x^{(i)}_t \mid x^{(i)}_{<t}\big).
\]
Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) chooses parameters $\hat\theta$ that maximize the log-likelihood:
\[
\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta).
\]
Intuitively, MLE picks the model that assigns as much probability mass as possible to the observed data. Under regularity conditions (identifiability, sufficiently rich parameterization, appropriate priors for Bayesian variants), MLE has desirable properties: consistency (the estimator converges to the true parameter value as $N\to\infty$), asymptotic normality, and efficiency (achieving minimal possible variance among unbiased estimators asymptotically).

When training neural language models, we usually perform stochastic optimization (e.g., stochastic gradient descent) on the negative log-likelihood per token, often called the cross-entropy loss (we will formalize that term below). Minimizing the average negative log-probability per token over the dataset is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood.

It is important to remember that maximizing likelihood directly optimizes a probabilistic objective, not necessarily downstream task performance. For example, two models with similar likelihoods may produce different qualities of generated text depending on how they allocate probability mass across sequences. Still, likelihood is a principled and widely used quantity for fitting and comparing probabilistic sequence models.

# Cross-entropy and KL divergence

Cross-entropy and Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence quantify differences between probability distributions and play central roles both as training objectives and evaluation metrics.

Let $P$ denote the true (unknown) data distribution over some discrete set $\mathcal{X}$, and let $Q$ be a model distribution. The entropy of $P$ is defined as
\[
H(P) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} P(x)\,\log P(x).
\]
Entropy measures the intrinsic uncertainty (average information content) in a random variable drawn from $P$, and will be discussed in the next section.

The cross-entropy from $P$ to $Q$ is
\[
H(P,Q) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} P(x)\,\log Q(x).
\]
Cross-entropy measures the expected negative log-probability assigned by $Q$ to a sample drawn from $P$. If we had access to the true distribution $P$, minimizing cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ would be the right way to make $Q$ assign high probability to typical outcomes of $P$. In practice we often approximate $P$ with a finite dataset and replace the expectation with an empirical average, yielding the negative log-likelihood per sample.

The KL divergence from $P$ to $Q$ is defined as
\[
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) = \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}.
\]
KL divergence measures the extra number of nats (if using natural log) required to code samples from $P$ using a code optimized for $Q$ rather than the optimal code for $P$. KL divergence can be decomposed as
\[
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) = H(P,Q) - H(P),
\]
so cross-entropy equals the entropy plus the KL divergence. Since $H(P)$ depends only on the true distribution, minimizing cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ is equivalent to minimizing $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q)$.

Several important properties of KL divergence: it is non-negative and zero iff $P=Q$ almost everywhere; it is not symmetric (i.e., $\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q)\neq \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\parallel P)$ in general); and it is not a metric because it does not satisfy the triangle inequality. In the context of maximum likelihood, the MLE tries to find $Q_\theta$ that minimizes empirical cross-entropy (hence empirical KL divergence to the data distribution).

For sequences, cross-entropy and KL divergence are typically computed per token by expanding the joint distribution with the chain rule. If we denote the true sequence distribution by $P$ and a model by $Q_\theta$, the expected negative log-probability per token is
\[
\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}_{(X_1,\dots,X_T)\sim P}\left[-\log Q_\theta(X_1,\dots,X_T)\right] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{X_{<t}\sim P}\left[ H\big(P(X_t\mid X_{<t}), Q_\theta(X_t\mid X_{<t})\big) \right].
\]
Thus training often focuses on minimizing the conditional cross-entropy at each time step averaged over histories.

# Entropy and information content

Entropy quantifies the average amount of uncertainty or information in a random variable. For a discrete variable $X$ with distribution $P$, we repeat the definition:
\[
H(X) = -\sum_{x} P(x)\,\log P(x).
\]
Interpretation: entropy gives the expected number of nats (if using natural logs) required to encode a random draw from $P$ using an optimal code. Using base-2 logarithms instead yields bits as the unit. If $X$ is deterministic (all probability mass on one outcome), then $H(X)=0$. If $X$ is uniform over $M$ outcomes, then $H(X)=\log M$.

Information content (also called "surprisal") of a single outcome $x$ is defined by
\[
I(x) = -\log P(x).
\]
This is the contribution of the outcome $x$ to the entropy: the expectation of $I(X)$ under $P$ equals $H(X)$. Intuitively, unlikely events have high information content: being surprised indicates that the outcome carried a lot of information relative to prior expectations. In language, predicting a rare token yields a high surprisal.

Conditional entropy quantifies uncertainty of one variable given another:
\[
H(X\mid Y) = -\sum_{y} P(y) \sum_{x} P(x\mid y)\,\log P(x\mid y).
\]
This measures the expected remaining uncertainty about $X$ after observing $Y$. For sequences this is key: $H(X_t\mid X_{<t})$ measures how much uncertainty remains about the next token given its history. Language typically has lower conditional entropy than marginal entropy because context reduces uncertainty.

Mutual information between two random variables $X$ and $Y$ measures how much knowing one reduces uncertainty about the other:
\[
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = \sum_{x,y} P(x,y)\log\frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)}.
\]
Mutual information is symmetric, non-negative, and zero iff $X$ and $Y$ are independent. In the sequence modeling context, mutual information between distant tokens quantifies long-range dependencies.

These information-theoretic quantities have operational meanings in coding and compression. For example, the entropy sets a lower bound on the average number of bits needed to losslessly encode samples from a distribution. Cross-entropy and KL divergence appear when using a suboptimal code derived from a model distribution.

# Perplexity and its relation to cross-entropy

Perplexity is a widely used metric in language modeling that converts average negative log-probability into an interpretable scale. For a discrete distribution $P$ on a single random variable $X$, the perplexity is defined as
\[
\mathrm{ppx}(P) = \exp\big(H(P)\big) = \exp\Big(-\sum_x P(x)\log P(x)\Big).
\]
Perplexity can be seen as the effective branching factor: the number of equally likely choices that would yield the same entropy. For example, if $X$ is uniformly distributed over $M$ outcomes, then $H(X)=\log M$ and $\mathrm{ppx}(P)=M$.

For evaluating a model $Q$ against data drawn from $P$, we typically use the exponentiated cross-entropy (or exponentiated average negative log-likelihood). The model perplexity with respect to data is
\[
\mathrm{ppx}(P,Q) = \exp\big(H(P,Q)\big) = \exp\Big(-\sum_x P(x)\log Q(x)\Big).
\]
Because $H(P,Q)=H(P)+\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q)$, model perplexity equals $\exp(H(P))\exp(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q))$. Thus model perplexity is always at least $\exp(H(P))$, with equality only when $Q=P$.

In practice, perplexity is computed per token on a held-out dataset: given a dataset of tokens $(x_1,\dots,x_M)$ believed to be drawn i.i.d. from (or sampled according to) the true sequential process, the empirical (per-token) cross-entropy is
\[
\widehat{H}(Q) = -\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \log Q(x_i\mid \text{context}_i),
\]
and the empirical perplexity is $\exp(\widehat{H}(Q))$. Lower perplexity indicates that the model assigns higher probability to observed tokens and is therefore better at predicting the data. Because perplexity is exponential in cross-entropy, a small absolute improvement in cross-entropy can translate into a multiplicative improvement in perplexity.

One should be cautious interpreting perplexity: it is sensitive to tokenization (vocabulary size and segmentation), to whether the model is normalized (some models output unnormalized scores), and to how the dataset is preprocessed (e.g., whether rare tokens are mapped to <UNK> or how numbers are handled). Nevertheless, perplexity remains the standard scalar metric for language models because it directly reflects the model's probabilistic predictions across the dataset.

# Evaluation protocols for autoregressive models (validation/test splits, tokenization effects)

Evaluating autoregressive models requires careful experimental design to ensure results are meaningful and comparable. Several practical elements matter: dataset splits, tokenization, context handling, and the exact log-probabilities being reported.

Splits. Typical practice separates data into training, validation (development), and test sets. The training set is used to fit model parameters. The validation set is used to tune hyperparameters (learning rate, architecture, early stopping) and to select the best checkpoint. The test set is used only once (or sparingly) to report final, unbiased performance. To avoid selection bias it is important that the test set remain untouched during model development. For language modeling at scale, datasets may be time-split (e.g., training on older data and testing on later data) to mimic realistic deployment scenarios and prevent leakage.

Context and evaluation mode. For autoregressive models, evaluation computes the model's probability of each next token given its preceding context. Two subtle issues arise. First, the context length: models typically use a fixed maximum context window; when evaluating on sequences longer than the context window, we must decide whether to slide the window and compute conditional probabilities accordingly. Second, statefulness: for efficiency, some implementations carry model hidden states across tokens in streaming evaluation, which can affect numeric stability if not handled correctly. Ensure that evaluation replicates the intended deployment scenario: if the model will be used in a streaming setting, measure performance in that mode.

Tokenization effects. Tokenization—the mapping from raw text to discrete tokens—has a first-order effect on perplexity and other probabilistic metrics. Consider two tokenization schemes: character-level (larger number of tokens but smaller vocabulary) and subword-level (Byte-Pair Encoding, SentencePiece) or word-level tokenization. Tokenization changes the unit for which probabilities are assigned: per-token cross-entropy depends on the tokenization granularity. To compare models across tokenizations, convert to a common unit (e.g., bits per character or bits per byte) or compute per-byte cross-entropy. For example, if model A uses subword tokens and reports perplexity over subwords while model B uses characters, comparing their raw perplexities is misleading. Another issue is vocabulary size: larger vocabularies reduce sequence length and can reduce per-token cross-entropy but may increase the computational cost per step.

Normalizations and handling of unknown tokens. Some datasets contain rare or out-of-vocabulary tokens (proper names, numbers). Handling strategies (mapping to <UNK>, byte-level fallback, or dynamic vocabularies) change the effective distribution being modeled and thus affect evaluations. When comparing models, report how unknown tokens were treated and, if possible, use tokenizers that are publicly available and fixed.

Reporting protocol. Always report the exact metric definition (per-token cross-entropy, bits per byte, or perplexity), the tokenization and vocabulary used, the way sequences were segmented for evaluation, and whether the log is natural or base-2 (nats vs bits). If reporting perplexity, specify whether it is per-token perplexity or normalized by characters/bytes. Include validation performance and test performance, and be explicit about which split was used for model selection.

Calibration, temperature, and decoding. Note that evaluation of the model as a probability estimator (perplexity) is distinct from evaluation of the model as a generator (sample quality). Generation usually involves decoding strategies (greedy, beam search, nucleus sampling) and sometimes renormalization or temperature scaling of logits. These operations can improve perceived sample quality without improving perplexity, and in some cases may even degrade likelihood. Make clear whether reported metrics reflect the raw model probabilities or modified decoding outputs.

Reproducibility. For researchers comparing models, publish tokenizers, evaluation scripts, and dataset splits. Even small differences (whitespace handling, Unicode normalization) can shift perplexity values, making comparisons difficult. When comparing to prior work, align tokenization and evaluation procedures or provide conversion factors where possible.

# Statistical significance and confidence intervals for model comparison

When comparing language models, observed differences in evaluation metrics (for example, a reduction in cross-entropy or perplexity) might arise purely from sampling variability in the test set rather than from a true difference in the underlying model quality. Statistical significance testing and confidence intervals help distinguish true improvements from noise.

Confidence intervals. A confidence interval for the difference in expected per-token negative log-likelihood between two models quantifies uncertainty due to the finite test set. Suppose we have $M$ tokens in the test set and for each token $i$ we compute the difference in negative log-probabilities assigned by the two models:
\[
d_i = -\log Q_1(x_i\mid\text{context}_i) + \log Q_2(x_i\mid\text{context}_i).
\]
The sample mean $\bar d = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M d_i$ estimates the average per-token difference in cross-entropy (positive values indicate $Q_2$ assigns higher log-probability). Under mild conditions, by the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), $\bar d$ is approximately normally distributed with variance $\sigma^2/M$, where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of $d_i$. Thus a (approximate) 95% confidence interval for the true mean difference is
\[
\bar d \pm 1.96\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{M}},
\]
where $\hat\sigma$ is the sample standard deviation of the $d_i$. This approach assumes the test tokens are independent; for sequential data this assumption can be violated because tokens within the same sequence are correlated. To address correlation, one can compute per-sequence averages and use the number of sequences as the effective sample size, or use block bootstrap methods that respect sequence structure.

Hypothesis testing. A standard null hypothesis is that two models have equal expected negative log-likelihood on the data distribution. A paired t-test is often appropriate because the per-token log-probability differences are paired: for each token we can compute both models' log-probabilities, and thus the paired test accounts for token-level correlation. The paired t-statistic is
\[
t = \frac{\bar d}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt{M}},
\]
with $M-1$ degrees of freedom. Under the null, large-magnitude $t$-values indicate statistically significant differences. However, the t-test assumes approximate normality of the sample mean; when $M$ is large this assumption is usually reasonable due to the CLT.

Bootstrap methods. Nonparametric bootstrap provides a flexible way to obtain confidence intervals without strong distributional assumptions. For sequence data, perform block bootstrap: resample whole sequences with replacement to form bootstrap test sets, compute the desired metric (e.g., perplexity or cross-entropy difference) on each resampled set, and use the empirical distribution of bootstrap replicates to form confidence intervals. Bootstrap handles dependencies within sequences and accommodates complex metrics (like perplexity) whose sampling distribution may be difficult to derive analytically.

Multiple comparisons and reporting. When comparing many models or many variants, adjust for multiple hypothesis testing to control false discovery rates (e.g., Bonferroni correction, Benjamini–Hochberg). Report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values. Small p-values with tiny effect sizes (that are practically irrelevant) are less interesting than moderate p-values with large, practically meaningful differences.

Power analysis. Before running expensive experiments, a power analysis can estimate the test set size needed to detect a target effect size (difference in cross-entropy) with desired statistical power. Since per-token variance typically decreases as the dataset grows, large evaluation corpora are helpful for detecting small but meaningful improvements.

Practical caveats. Remember that statistical significance on a given test set does not imply the model is better in deployment scenarios if the test set is not representative. Also, differences in preprocessing, tokenization, or training tricks that leak information from validation or test data can invalidate statistical conclusions. Always ensure that reported significance reflects genuine model improvements under the same evaluation protocol.

Conclusion

This chapter provided a careful review of the probabilistic foundations and information-theoretic metrics central to sequence modeling and language modeling. Random variables and joint/conditional distributions define the object we model; likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation provide the training framework; cross-entropy and KL divergence are both training objectives and measures of distributional mismatch; entropy and surprisal quantify the intrinsic uncertainty and information content of language; perplexity gives an interpretable scalar summary of predictive quality; evaluation protocols and tokenization choices critically shape reported results; and statistical tools (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, bootstrap) are essential to draw reliable conclusions when comparing models. Mastering these concepts will enable you to read research papers with a clear sense of what reported metrics mean, how to reproduce results, and how to reason about improvements in language modeling.
