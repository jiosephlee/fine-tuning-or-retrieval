# Introduction

Deep neural networks have transformed machine learning by enabling models with large representational capacity. However, training very deep architectures presents both optimization and stability challenges. Residual connections and carefully designed block patterns are among the most important advances that made it practical to train networks with hundreds or even thousands of layers. This chapter explains the core ideas behind residual learning and identity skip connections, contrasts two major normalization placement choices (Pre-LayerNorm vs Post-LayerNorm), and surveys common block design patterns that appear in modern architectures such as transformers and state-space models (SSMs). We also examine projection and bottleneck layers used to change channel dimensionality, discuss how depth affects both optimization and expressivity, and collect practical techniques for achieving stable training: initialization schemes, scaling factors, and other implementation details.

Throughout, the goal is to provide intuition and precise mathematical descriptions so you can read recent research papers and understand design trade-offs when building deep networks.

# Residual learning and identity skip connections

Residual learning was introduced to overcome the degradation problem: as networks become deeper, adding more layers sometimes increases training error rather than decreasing it. The key insight behind residual networks (ResNets) is to learn residual functions with respect to the identity mapping instead of directly learning unreferenced mappings.

Formally, consider a desired underlying mapping from input \(x\) to output \(y\) of a layer-block. If we try to learn a mapping \(H(x)\) directly, a residual block instead parameterizes \(H(x)\) as
\[
H(x) = x + F(x),
\]
where \(F(x)\) is the residual function implemented by a small network (for example, two convolutional layers with ReLU activations). The identity skip connection \(x \mapsto x\) is added so the layer block outputs \(x + F(x)\). The intuition is that if the optimal mapping is close to an identity mapping, it is easier for the optimizer to drive \(F(x)\) toward zero than to fit an identity through a stack of many nonlinear layers. In practice, this greatly mitigates vanishing gradients and accelerates convergence.

From an optimization perspective, residual connections change the geometry of the parameter space and the backward flow of gradients. Consider a network composed of \(L\) residual blocks with outputs \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_L\) where
\[
x_{k+1} = x_k + F_k(x_k).
\]
The gradient of a loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to an early activation \(x_k\) can be written by repeated application of the chain rule:
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_k}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_L} \prod_{j=k}^{L-1} \left(I + \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial x_j}\right).
\]
Because the identity matrix \(I\) is present in each multiplicative term, the product is less likely to vanish compared to chains of arbitrary Jacobians without identity terms. For small residual Jacobians \(\|\partial F_j / \partial x_j\| \ll 1\), the gradients can propagate nearly unchanged. This leads to improved flow of gradient signal to early layers and reduces the problem of vanishing gradients. At the same time, residual connections allow the forward composition to remain expressive: the network can represent complicated functions by stacking many \(F_j\) terms.

An important variant is the identity skip connection when the input and output dimensions match. When dimensions differ (for example, spatial downsampling or channel width changes), a projection or skip with parameterized linear mapping is used (see the Projection section later). The identity case is particularly valuable because it preserves the direct path for information and gradient flow with zero parameters and no dependence on initialization.

# Pre-LayerNorm vs Post-LayerNorm residual blocks

Normalization layers interact strongly with residual connections. Two common patterns place Layer Normalization either before the residual function (Pre-LayerNorm) or after the addition (Post-LayerNorm). Both appear frequently in transformer-like architectures, and the choice affects stability, convergence rate, and empirical performance.

Consider a residual block with input \(x\), residual function \(F(\cdot)\) parameterized by weights, and LayerNorm denoted by \(\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)\). In Post-LayerNorm (the original transformer design), the block computes
\[
y = \mathrm{LN}(x + F(x)).
\]
In Pre-LayerNorm, the normalization is applied before the residual function and the block computes
\[
y = x + F(\mathrm{LN}(x)).
\]
More generally, Pre-LN often uses separate LayerNorms before each sublayer (attention or MLP) while Post-LN uses one LayerNorm after the residual addition.

Each choice has trade-offs. Post-LayerNorm enforces that the output of the block is normalized, which can be helpful for downstream layers and for having bounded activations. However, Post-LayerNorm can suffer from instability when training very deep transformer stacks, especially with large learning rates, because the backward signal can diminish or explode in ways that LayerNorm after the addition does not correct for. In particular, in Post-LN the gradient must pass through the LayerNorm at the end of the block in addition to the residual path; deeper stacks can cause gradient magnitudes to shrink, harming training.

Pre-LayerNorm alleviates some of these issues by normalizing the input to each residual function. This produces more stable gradients and typically allows for more robust training of deeper networks. The backward gradient benefits from the identity path \(x \mapsto x\) being untouched by LayerNorm, so the gradient can flow unimpeded through many blocks. Pre-LN transformer variants have become popular in large-scale models for this reason.

Yet Pre-LN is not a universal win: since the output of each block is not normalized, downstream layers may see activations whose scale drifts during training. To offset this, practitioners sometimes combine Pre-LN with small initialization of residual branches or scale factors on residual outputs (e.g., multiplying \(F(\cdot)\) by a small constant or using learned gating). Another observation is that Post-LN models sometimes generalize slightly better for smaller depths and datasets where stability is less of a concern. The design choice is therefore empirical: Pre-LN favors stability and scalability to depth, whereas Post-LN can be effective for moderate depth with careful hyperparameter tuning.

Mathematically, consider the backward gradient for a single block. For Post-LN,
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathrm{LN}(x + F(x))}{\partial (x + F(x))} \cdot \left(I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\right).
\]
The LayerNorm derivative multiplies the entire residual path. For Pre-LN,
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial y} \cdot \left(I + \frac{\partial F(\mathrm{LN}(x))}{\partial x}\right),
\]
and the identity path \(I\) is free of normalization effects. This difference explains much of the empirical divergence in stability between the two placements.

# Design patterns for transformer-like and SSM-like blocks

Modern deep architectures are assembled from repeated blocks: transformer-like blocks for attention-based sequence models, and SSM-like (state-space model) blocks that implement long-range sequence dynamics more efficiently in some regimes. Despite functional differences, these blocks share common design motifs: residual connections, normalization, multi-stage sublayers (e.g., attention and feed-forward), and optional projection or gating. Understanding these patterns helps in designing and comparing architectures.

Transformer-like blocks typically alternate between a multi-head self-attention sublayer and a feed-forward (MLP) sublayer. Each sublayer is wrapped by a residual connection and a normalization. A canonical Pre-LN transformer block can be written as
\[
\begin{aligned}
z_1 &= x + \mathrm{Dropout}\big(\mathrm{MultiHeadAttn}(\mathrm{LN}(x))\big),\\
y &= z_1 + \mathrm{Dropout}\big(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(z_1))\big).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(\mathrm{LN}\) denotes LayerNorm, and \(\mathrm{Dropout}\) is optional regularization. Multi-head attention allows the block to compute context-dependent weighted averages of tokens, while the MLP provides per-position nonlinear transformations and cross-channel mixing. The residual structure lets both sublayers modify the input additively.

Transformer blocks emphasize channel-mixing with attention providing context and MLPs expanding the per-token embedding dimension (commonly by a factor of 2 to 4) before projecting back. The MLP expansion is a common "bottleneck" pattern: the hidden layer dimension \(d_{\text{ff}}\) is larger than the model dimension \(d\), enabling richer per-position transformations while keeping the final representation at dimension \(d\).

SSM-like blocks use state-space layers to model long-range temporal dependencies via structured linear dynamics. A simple SSM cell evolves a latent state \(s_t \in \mathbb{R}^n\) by
\[
s_{t+1} = A s_t + B u_t,
\quad y_t = C s_t + D u_t,
\]
where \(u_t\) is the input and \(y_t\) the output. In many neural SSMs, the matrices \(A, B, C, D\) are parameterized and possibly diagonalized to allow fast inference through convolution-like operations. When incorporated into deep stacks, SSM layers are often wrapped with normalization and residual connections to stabilize training. A modern pattern is to combine SSM layers with simple per-position feed-forward blocks and residual paths, for example:
\[
z_1 = x + \mathrm{Dropout}\big(\mathrm{SSM}(\mathrm{LN}(x))\big), \qquad
y = z_1 + \mathrm{Dropout}\big(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(z_1))\big).
\]
This mirrors the transformer layout but replaces attention with an SSM that provides efficient long-range mixing.

Key design considerations when choosing block elements include computational cost, desired receptive field, and hardware efficiency. Transformer attention scales quadratically with sequence length but naturally supports content-based interaction. SSMs can be more efficient for long sequences (linear or subquadratic) because they implement global mixing via structured linear dynamics and fast transforms, but they may require careful parameterization to match attention's flexibility.

Another recurring pattern is the gated residual block. Gating introduces multiplicative control for residual contributions, for example using a sigmoid gate \(g(x) \in (0,1)\) to scale \(F(x)\):
\[
y = x + g(x) \odot F(x).
\]
Gates can stabilize training by preventing large residual updates early in training and by giving the network a learnable mechanism to modulate information flow. Examples include Gated Linear Units (GLU) and variants used in convolutional and transformer-based networks.

Finally, block designs often include dropout, stochastic depth, or layer-wise scaling to regularize deep stacks. Stochastic depth randomly drops entire residual branches during training, which acts like an ensemble of networks with different depths and helps generalization in very deep models.

# Projection (up/down) and bottleneck layers

When stacking residual blocks, it is necessary at times to change the dimensionality of the activations: spatial resolution in vision models, sequence length in temporal downsampling, or channel width. Projection layers (also called shortcut projections) and bottleneck layers are architectural elements used to achieve these changes efficiently.

A projection in the residual context replaces the identity skip by a linear mapping \(P\) so the residual block becomes
\[
y = P(x) + F(x).
\]
A simple example is a \(1 \times 1\) convolution in ResNets used to match channel dimensions when the residual function alters the number of channels or spatial resolution. Projections ensure the shapes are compatible and preserve the skip path but now the skip is parameterized. Using parameterized projections is necessary when downsampling (e.g., stride \(>1\)) or when increasing channel width.

Bottleneck layers reduce computational cost and parameter count while permitting deep networks to learn complex features. A canonical bottleneck residual block in convolutional ResNets uses a three-layer structure: a \(1 \times 1\) convolution to reduce channels from \(d\) to \(d_b\) (the bottleneck dimension), a \(3 \times 3\) convolution that operates at the reduced dimension, and a \(1 \times 1\) convolution to expand back to the original dimension \(d\). If the block also changes the output dimension to \(d'\), the final \(1 \times 1\) expansion produces \(d'\) channels and a projection is used on the skip if \(d' \neq d\). Mathematically,
\[
F(x) = W_3 \sigma(W_2 \sigma(W_1 x)),
\]
where \(W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b \times d}\), \(W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b \times d_b}\), and \(W_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{d' \times d_b}\), and \(\sigma\) denotes a nonlinearity. The bottleneck \(d_b\) is typically smaller than \(d\), reducing the cost of the middle convolution.

In transformer MLPs, the same idea appears as an expansion followed by a contraction: given model dimension \(d\), the MLP maps \(x\) to a higher intermediate dimension \(d_{\text{ff}}\) (commonly \(d_{\text{ff}} = 4d\)), applies a nonlinearity, then projects back to \(d\). The MLP acts as a channel-wise bottleneck with richer intermediate computations. When computational budget is a concern, designers can choose smaller expansion factors or use grouped and factorized linear layers.

Down-projection and up-projection are used in encoder-decoder designs: encoders often reduce spatial or temporal resolution while increasing channels, while decoders reverse this. Residual blocks that change resolution often combine stride convolutions, pooling, or interpolations on the skip and residual branches. Care must be taken to preserve the benefits of residual identity paths: for example, using average pooling on the skip with a subsequent projection reduces abrupt changes and keeps a stable shortcut.

An important nuance is how projections interact with normalization and initialization. When the skip is parameterized, it introduces additional learned parameters and can change the distribution of activations. Common practice is to apply normalization after addition or to use zero-initialization of the last layer in the residual branch to make the block initially behave like the identity, which stabilizes training in deep stacks. We will return to the zero-init trick in the Practical Considerations section.

# Effects of depth on optimization and expressivity

Depth in neural networks influences both optimization dynamics and the representational power of the model. Historically, increasing network depth without structural modifications often made optimization harder; residual connections and other techniques have changed that landscape, but depth still imposes practical trade-offs.

From an expressivity standpoint, depth enables compositions of nonlinearities, which increases the set of functions that can be represented for a fixed number of parameters. Certain functions are exponentially more efficient to represent with deep networks than with shallow ones. For example, a deep feed-forward network can represent highly oscillatory functions or hierarchical features using fewer parameters than a shallow network that tries to emulate the same mapping.

However, optimization complexity does not scale monotonically with expressivity. Deeper networks create highly nonconvex loss surfaces with many saddle points and pathological curvature. Without architectural aids like residual connections, gradients attenuate or explode through many nonlinear compositions, making training slow or impossible. Residual identity connections mitigate gradient attenuation by preserving direct paths for gradient flow. The earlier product form
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_k}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_L} \prod_{j=k}^{L-1} \left(I + \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial x_j}\right)
\]
shows that when each \(\partial F_j / \partial x_j\) is small, the product remains closer to identity and gradient norms are preserved.

Depth also affects optimization landscape properties. Some theoretical analyses show that residual networks can be interpreted as discrete approximations to differential equations (continuous-depth models), and training deep ResNets is analogous to solving inverse problems in dynamical systems. This perspective leads to intuition that each residual block makes a small incremental change; if blocks are initialized to small updates then the overall mapping is a composition of near-identity transforms, which is easier to optimize.

Yet increasing depth has diminishing returns and practical costs. Very deep models require more memory for activations and may suffer from overfitting if not regularized. They can also increase latency in inference. Moreover, while depth improves expressivity in principle, architectural patterns such as wider layers, multi-scale features, and attention mechanisms often yield bigger practical gains than simply stacking more layers. There is therefore a balance: depth should be used along with modular design choices (residuals, normalization, bottlenecks) to reap benefits without causing optimization breakdown.

Another subtle effect is implicit regularization. Deeper networks trained with stochastic gradient descent exhibit different implicit biases compared to shallow ones, which can impact generalization. Residual architectures with near-identity initialization often start in function space close to identity and gradually move toward more complex mappings during training; this path-dependent trajectory can influence the final solution found by optimization.

# Practical considerations: stability, initialization and scaling

When building very deep architectures, practical details of initialization, normalization, scaling, and numerical stability become crucial. Small choices can determine whether training is stable and whether the model converges to a good solution. Below are several widely used techniques and the intuition behind them.

Zero-initialize the last layer of residual branches. A common trick is to initialize the final linear layer in a residual branch to zero (or to scale it by a small factor close to zero). Concretely, if a residual block computes \(y = x + W_2 \sigma(W_1 x)\), initializing \(W_2 = 0\) makes the initial block output equal to \(x\), and the network starts as an identity mapping. This stabilizes early training because the optimizer first learns useful deviations from identity rather than large arbitrary transformations. Many large-scale models use zero (or near-zero) initialization in the last dense of MLP sublayers or the output projection of attention heads.

Scale residual branches. Multiplicative scaling factors applied to residual outputs can prevent early layers from overwhelming the identity path. For example, some implementations multiply the residual by \(\alpha < 1\) at initialization, so \(y = x + \alpha F(x)\). Another variant is to include learnable scalar gates initialized to zero or small values, letting the network learn how much to rely on each residual. These techniques improve stability especially for Pre-LN architectures and very deep models.

Carefully choose weight initializations. Standard initializations such as Xavier/Glorot or Kaiming/He are still useful, but their interaction with residual additions matters. For networks with ReLU activations, Kaiming initialization keeps variance stable through layers in unresidualized stacks; in residual stacks, one must account for the additive identity path when reasoning about variances. When using LayerNorm, inputs are normalized so variance propagation is less of a concern, but for Post-LN configurations the initial scales of weights influence the normalized outputs.

Use normalization appropriately. LayerNorm and BatchNorm have different properties. BatchNorm normalizes across batch and spatial dimensions and can be problematic for small batch sizes or variable-length sequences; LayerNorm normalizes per sample and is well-suited to transformer-like architectures. The placement of normalization (Pre vs Post) affects stability as discussed earlier. In convolutional residual networks, BatchNorm was historically used in Post-LN positions, but Pre-activation ResNets (where BatchNorm + ReLU precede the convolution) have strong empirical and theoretical properties and are analogous to Pre-LN.

Control step sizes via learning rate and optimizers. Very deep models can be sensitive to large learning rates; using warmup schedules that start with small learning rates and gradually increase them can alleviate instability. Adaptive optimizers like Adam scale updates by estimated second moments, which helps but is not a panacea; large models often combine Adam with decoupled weight decay (AdamW) and learning rate warmup. Gradient clipping can prevent occasional large updates from destabilizing training.

Stochastic depth and dropout. Stochastic depth randomly drops entire residual branches during training with some probability, effectively training an ensemble of shallower networks. This acts as strong regularization for very deep models and can improve generalization. Dropout complements normalization and is sometimes less effective when strong normalization is present, but still useful in certain regimes.

Numerical issues and precision. Deep residual stacks can amplify numerical rounding errors if not managed carefully. Using 32-bit floating point is standard, but for memory reasons many practitioners use mixed precision training (float16 activations with float32 master weights). Mixed precision requires careful use of loss scaling to avoid underflow in gradients. In systems with extremely deep models, monitoring activation statistics can reveal anomalies that point to unstable layers.

Architectural monitoring and diagnostics. When training deep networks, track gradient norms, activation norms, learning curves, and layer-wise parameter updates. If a particular layer exhibits exploding gradients or saturating activations, consider reducing its initial scale, applying stronger normalization, or inserting gating/zero-init at that layer. Ablation experiments that toggle Pre vs Post normalization, residual scaling, or zero-init help identify problematic interactions.

Finally, consider compute and memory trade-offs. Deeper networks consume more activation memory, so strategies like activation checkpointing (recomputing activations on backward pass) can enable training deeper models with limited memory at the cost of extra compute.

# Conclusion

Residual connections and careful block design are foundational to modern deep learning. Identity skip connections stabilize gradient flow and make very deep networks trainable. The placement of normalization (Pre-LayerNorm vs Post-LayerNorm) strongly influences training behavior and scalability. Transformer-like and SSM-like blocks share the same high-level motifs—residuals, normalization, and sublayer composition—while differing in the mechanism of sequence mixing. Projection and bottleneck layers change channel dimensions efficiently and preserve residual benefits when used with appropriate skip mappings. Depth increases expressivity but imposes optimization challenges that are mitigated by architectural choices and practical techniques such as zero-initialization of residual outputs, scaling, careful initialization, and learning-rate scheduling. Understanding these principles will equip you to read and design deep architectures and to interpret the many variants that appear in recent research.
