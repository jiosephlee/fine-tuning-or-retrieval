Chapter 9: Linear and Efficient Attention Approaches

Introduction

Self-attention is the central operation in modern sequence models: it computes pairwise interactions between tokens and yields strong representational power. The canonical attention mechanism used in Transformers computes attention weights from queries $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and keys $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ via a scaled dot-product, producing an $n \times n$ dense matrix of affinities and then weighting values $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v}$. Concretely, the output for one head is
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V,
\]
which requires $O(n^2 d)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory to form and normalize the $n \times n$ matrix. For long sequences this quadratic cost in sequence length $n$ becomes a bottleneck in computation and memory. This chapter surveys methods that aim to reduce that cost to linear or near-linear, by approximating the attention operation while preserving useful representational behavior. We focus on two broad families: kernelized and factorized/low-rank approaches that change the algebraic structure of attention to permit linear-time implementations, and sparse/locality-biased approaches that limit the set of token-token interactions. For each method we discuss intuition, mathematics, implementation details, and practical trade-offs: how much approximation error is introduced, and what are the consequences for downstream performance.

# Kernelized attention and random feature approximations (Performer)

The key observation behind kernelized attention is that the softmax attention can be viewed as a kernel-based weighted average. The attention weights can be written elementwise as
\[
A_{ij} = \frac{\exp(q_i^\top k_j)}{\sum_{t=1}^n \exp(q_i^\top k_t)},
\]
where $q_i$ and $k_j$ are the $i$-th query and $j$-th key respectively (we omit scaling by $\sqrt{d}$ for clarity). This is a normalized kernel of the form
\[
A_{ij} = \frac{\kappa(q_i, k_j)}{\sum_{t=1}^n \kappa(q_i, k_t)},
\]
with $\kappa(x,y)=\exp(x^\top y)$. If we can express $\kappa(x,y)$ as an inner product of feature maps $\phi(x)^\top \phi(y)$, then
\[
\sum_{t=1}^n \kappa(q_i,k_t) v_t = \phi(q_i)^\top \sum_{t=1}^n \phi(k_t) v_t,
\]
so the attention output can be written without forming the $n \times n$ matrix:
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)_i = \frac{\phi(q_i)^\top \left(\sum_{t=1}^n \phi(k_t) v_t^\top\right)}{\phi(q_i)^\top \left(\sum_{t=1}^n \phi(k_t)\right)} .
\]
Because the sums over $t$ are shared across all queries, they can be precomputed in $O(n d_\phi d_v)$ time and then each query is processed in $O(d_\phi d_v)$, yielding overall linear dependency in $n$ when $d_\phi$ and $d_v$ are independent of $n$.

However, the softmax kernel $\kappa(x,y)=\exp(x^\top y)$ does not admit a finite-dimensional positive feature map $\phi$ of small size. Performer (Choromanski et al., 2021) uses random feature approximations to approximate the softmax kernel with an inner product of randomized finite-dimensional feature maps. The core idea is to find a mapping $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{r}_+$ such that
\[
\kappa(x,y) \approx \phi(x)^\top \phi(y),
\]
with $r$ reasonably small (e.g., hundreds or thousands) and $\phi(x) \ge 0$ elementwise to preserve positivity needed for normalization.

One way to obtain such approximations is via Random Fourier Features (for shift-invariant kernels) or via special constructions for non-shift kernels. The Performer uses a construction called FAVOR+ (Fast Attention Via positive Orthogonal Random features), which approximates $\exp(q^\top k)$ using random projections and elementwise nonlinearities. A simple sketch: draw random Gaussian matrices $W \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ and define
\[
\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \exp\left( \frac{\|x\|^2}{2}\right) \exp(W x),
\]
where the exponential is applied elementwise; expectations over $W$ yield $\mathbb{E}[\phi(x)^\top \phi(y)] = \exp(x^\top y)$. In practice, numerically stable variants, orthogonalized random matrices, and normalization tricks are used to reduce variance and avoid overflow. The Performer implementation yields an attention approximation called FAVOR+ attention that replaces the softmax attention with
\[
\widetilde{\mathrm{Attn}}(Q,K,V) = D(Q)^{-1} \big( \phi(Q) (\phi(K)^\top V) \big),
\]
where $\phi(Q)$ denotes applying $\phi$ rowwise to queries, and $D(Q)$ is a diagonal matrix performing per-query normalization: $D_{ii}(Q) = \phi(q_i)^\top \sum_{t}\phi(k_t)$.

There are several important practical points. First, the approximation is unbiased in expectation over the random features, and variance decreases with $r$, the number of random features. Second, the nonnegativity of $\phi$ is important to keep the normalization denominator positive and to mimic softmax behavior. Third, Performer can handle causal/auto-regressive masking by performing the sum over keys/values incrementally in a streaming fashion: the precomputed prefix sums can be used to compute causal attention outputs in $O(n)$ memory and time per layer. Fourth, Performer trades approximation error for reduced memory/computation: choosing $r$ too small increases variance and can degrade accuracy, while large $r$ approaches standard attention cost.

Despite these advantages, kernelized random-feature methods face limitations. The required number of features $r$ to reach a small approximation error may scale with sequence length or with properties of the queries/keys (e.g., their norms and angular spread). Numerical stability of exponential features must be addressed; training dynamics can differ from exact attention, and hyperparameters such as $r$, random-seed choices, and normalization require tuning.

# Linear Transformer and factorized attention methods

A different route shifts the focus from approximating the kernel to algebraically reordering the computation so attention looks like a sequence of matrix products that can be computed incrementally without $O(n^2)$ storage. Linear Transformer (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) introduced a class of “linear” attention mechanisms that replace the softmax nonlinearity with a feature map that is linear in its inputs or otherwise allows factoring.

Start from the attention formula in kernel form:
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = D(Q)^{-1} \left(\Phi(Q) \left(\Phi(K)^\top V\right)\right),
\]
where $\Phi$ applies some elementwise map to each row (the kernel feature map). If $\Phi$ is chosen such that $\Phi(x) = \psi(x)$ with relatively small dimension and simple computations, the product $\Phi(K)^\top V$ can be computed in $O(n d_\phi d_v)$, and each query can be handled in $O(d_\phi d_v)$.

Katharopoulos et al. proposed using a simple positive map like $\psi(x) = \mathrm{elu}(x) + 1$ (where $\mathrm{elu}$ is the exponential linear unit), which is positive for all inputs and empirically behaved similarly to softmax for many tasks. The resulting attention is called Linear Transformer. The key computational advantage is that attention can be computed as a sequence of matrix products that avoid forming the $n \times n$ matrix, and causal attention can be computed in a streaming left-to-right manner by maintaining appropriate running sums:
\[
S_t = \sum_{u=1}^t \psi(k_u) v_u^\top, \qquad Z_t = \sum_{u=1}^t \psi(k_u),
\]
and then the output for position $t$ is
\[
o_t = \frac{\psi(q_t)^\top S_t}{\psi(q_t)^\top Z_t}.
\]
This enables $O(n)$ memory and $O(n)$ per layer time (ignoring constants) and supports autoregressive generation with constant incremental cost per token.

Factorized attention methods are more general: they search for factorizations of the $n \times n$ attention matrix into lower-dimensional factors or products of sparse matrices. One line of work approximates attention by decomposing it as a product $A \approx L R^\top$ with $L, R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ and $r \ll n$, or as a sequence of structured multiplications that share computations. Another approach is to apply separable convolutions or Toeplitz-like structures to model local interactions efficiently. The linear transformer family emphasizes reengineering the nonlinearity to permit such algebraic factoring with small feature dimension $d_\phi$.

Intuitively, factorized attention methods succeed when the effective kernel space required to represent query-key interactions is low-dimensional or when the nonlinearity can be approximated by low-dimensional features. The simplicity of maps like $\mathrm{elu}+1$ also helps numerical stability compared to exponential random feature formulations, though there is no exact equivalence to softmax attention: the change in nonlinearity can alter the model’s inductive biases and optimization landscape. Empirically, linear transformers have shown competitive performance in tasks emphasizing streaming or very long sequences, but for many tasks with medium-length sequences, exact softmax attention still yields better performance unless careful tuning or task-specific augmentation is applied.

# Linformer and low-rank attention approximations

Linformer (Wang et al., 2020) takes a different perspective: it hypothesizes that the $n \times n$ attention matrix often has low intrinsic rank, so attention can be approximated by projecting keys and values into a lower-dimensional sequence length dimension before computing pairwise interactions. Concretely, instead of computing attention weights between all $n$ queries and $n$ keys, Linformer applies learnable linear projections $E_K, E_V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$ (or equivalent row-wise projections) to reduce the sequence dimension:
\[
\widehat{K} = E_K^\top K, \qquad \widehat{V} = E_V^\top V,
\]
where $\widehat{K}, \widehat{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ with $k \ll n$. Attention is then computed as
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,\widehat{K},\widehat{V}) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q \widehat{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\widehat{V}.
\]
Because $\widehat{K}$ and $\widehat{V}$ have only $k$ rows, the affinity matrix becomes $n \times k$ rather than $n \times n$. The time complexity becomes $O(n k d)$ and memory $O(n k)$, so if $k$ can stay constant or grow slowly with $n$, this is nearly linear.

The Linformer argument is supported by empirical observations and theoretical heuristics: in many natural signals, attention maps have low rank because each query attends only to a low-dimensional subspace of the key space, or the attention patterns can be well-approximated by a basis of size $k$. Linformer parameterizes the projection matrices and learns them end-to-end, allowing the model to discover the subspace most useful for attention. A practical implementation uses shared projection matrices across heads or layers to limit parameters.

There are important caveats. First, low-rank approximations cannot represent attention patterns that are inherently high-rank, such as near-orthogonal attention weights or patterns that require fine-grained per-position distinctions. Second, Linformer’s projection matrices are typically full-row projections across sequence positions, which limits flexibility to model arbitrary position-relative interactions; some versions add positional-specific parameters or make projections position-dependent. Third, choosing $k$ trades off between accuracy and efficiency: too small a $k$ causes representational bottlenecks; too large erodes the computational savings.

Mathematically, Linformer approximates the original matrix $A = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)$ by $A \approx B C$ where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$ and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$. If the singular values of $A$ decay rapidly, a small $k$ suffices. In practice, Linformer often performs well on tasks where attention patterns are smooth or local, but it can struggle on tasks requiring precise long-range interactions unless $k$ is large.

# Sparse attention and locality-biased mechanisms

Sparse attention restricts the set of allowed pairwise interactions so the attention matrix is sparse rather than dense. The restriction can be deterministic (fixed pattern) or learned/adaptive. Sparse patterns reduce memory and computation when each row of the attention matrix has only $s \ll n$ nonzeros; then costs are $O(n s d)$ and memory $O(n s)$. Sparse attention strategies frequently exploit locality: tokens are most likely to attend to nearby tokens, so only a local window is retained. This idea underlies architectures like the Transformer-XL local attention, Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020), and its sliding-window attention, where each position attends to a fixed window of neighbors and some positions have global attention.

Longformer combines three attention types: local sliding windows, global (learned) tokens that attend to all positions, and dilated or strided attention patterns. BigBird (Zaheer et al., 2020) builds on similar ideas by combining sparse random attention, local sliding windows, and global tokens, and proves that when combined the scheme is a universal approximator of dense attention under certain connectivity conditions. These hybrid sparse patterns aim to capture both locality and occasional global interactions with sparse structures that require only linear memory and time.

Another class of sparse approaches learns sparse attention patterns, either by parameterizing a distribution over sparsity patterns or by using top-k selection on affinity scores (sparsifying by selecting the highest k keys per query). Top-k sparsification maintains the most relevant interactions at each query but introduces non-differentiability or requires approximate differentiable top-k methods. Learned sparsity can adapt better to task structure but may demand careful optimization and regularization to avoid pathological collapse (e.g., attending to very few constant positions).

Locality-biased mechanisms exploit the observation that many sequence tasks have strong local dependencies. Convolutional or local window attention trades expressivity for efficiency but often performs very well on tasks like speech recognition or language modeling where nearby tokens are highly relevant. Multi-scale sparse patterns and hierarchical attention combine local dense attention within blocks and sparse cross-block attention, giving a compromise between full global modeling and tractable computation.

Sparse methods introduce several implementation considerations: indexing sparsity efficiently on hardware, batching with variable sparsity patterns, and ensuring efficient memory access patterns. Some sparse patterns map well to GPUs (fixed regular patterns like sliding windows), while irregular learned sparsity may be slower due to scatter/gather overheads.

# Practical trade-offs: approximation error vs efficiency

All linear or sparse attention approximations trade accuracy for computational savings. Understanding and managing approximation error is therefore crucial in practical deployments. There are multiple dimensions to this trade-off: the asymptotic cost (theoretical scaling with sequence length), the constant factors (which often dominate on realistic hardware), the introduced bias or variance of the approximation, training dynamics, and downstream task sensitivity.

Kernelized random-feature methods (Performer) introduce stochastic approximation error: approximate kernels yield unbiased estimates (in expectation) but with finite variance that depends on the number of features $r$ and the distribution of queries and keys. Increasing $r$ reduces variance but raises computation and memory. Moreover, the error can be data dependent: if queries and keys have large norms or are concentrated in a small angular sector, the variance of the random-feature approximation can be worse. In practice, one must choose $r$ large enough to make the approximation noise negligible, or otherwise incorporate variance reduction methods (orthogonal features, careful normalization). On the plus side, Performers preserve model behavior more closely to softmax attention than some other approximations when $r$ is sufficiently large.

Linear Transformers change the nonlinearity itself (e.g., replacing softmax with $\mathrm{elu}+1$), which introduces a deterministic approximation error: the attention mechanism is different and may encode different inductive biases. This can be robust in streaming/autoregressive settings and yields exact linear-time algorithms for causal decoding, but it sometimes underperforms exact softmax in tasks where precise normalization and probability-like attention weights matter. The benefit is predictable, deterministic speed and memory behavior.

Low-rank methods like Linformer reduce representation by a learned projection; their approximation error depends on how well the attention maps lie in a low-dimensional subspace. In tasks with intrinsic low-rank attention maps, Linformer provides large savings at little cost. But for tasks requiring high-rank attention (e.g., complex alignment tasks, tasks with diverse long-range dependencies), low-rank bottlenecks can markedly degrade performance. Choosing the projection dimension $k$ balances this trade-off.

Sparse methods that restrict attention patterns can be extremely efficient and, for many tasks, introduce minimal error because local and structured sparsity matches the task's dependency patterns. However, fixed sparsity patterns risk missing important global interactions and require careful design (e.g., occasional global tokens, dilated patterns) or task-specific tuning.

Beyond raw approximation error, other practical trade-offs include:

- Numerical stability: kernel approximations involving exponentials or very small denominators can cause instability; normalization schemes and positive feature maps help.
- Hardware efficiency: theoretical asymptotic savings may not translate to speedups if constant factors or memory access patterns are unfavorable. Dense matrix multiplies are heavily optimized on GPUs, so methods replacing dense $n \times n$ operations with many small or irregular operations can be slower in wall-clock time.
- Training dynamics: approximations can change gradient magnitudes and loss landscapes, sometimes requiring learning-rate adjustments or other optimizer changes.
- Flexibility: some approximations allow causal streaming and incremental decoding more naturally (linearized kernels, prefix-sum strategies), which is important for autoregressive generation.

In practice, one picks a method based on the task demands (sequence length, need for streaming, sensitivity to exact attention), available hardware, and acceptable accuracy drop. It is common to evaluate a small set of candidates (e.g., Performer with multiple $r$ values, a Linear Transformer variant, and a sparse pattern) and choose the best trade-off empirically.

# Comparisons of asymptotic cost and empirical performance

Asymptotic cost comparisons are a helpful starting point but not the whole story. We summarize the typical complexities and then comment on empirical behaviors.

- Exact softmax attention: Time $O(n^2 d)$, Memory $O(n^2)$ for the affinity matrix. This remains the gold standard for accuracy when $n$ is modest.
- Kernelized random-feature attention (Performer): Time $O(n r d + n d d_v)$ often simplified to $O(n r d)$ for feature computations; Memory $O(n r)$ for storing features. If $r$ is constant w.r.t. $n$, scaling is linear in $n$. However, $r$ may need to grow with desired precision.
- Linear Transformer (factorized kernel): Time $O(n d d_\phi)$ with $d_\phi$ usually equal to attention dimension $d$ or a small multiple; Memory $O(n d_\phi)$. Asymptotically linear in $n$.
- Linformer (low-rank): Time $O(n k d)$ and Memory $O(n k)$. If $k$ is independent of $n$, cost is linear.
- Sparse attention (sliding windows, BigBird, Longformer): Time $O(n s d)$, Memory $O(n s)$ where $s$ is the sparsity per row (window size plus global tokens). For constant $s$, linear scaling.
- Hybrid or hierarchical schemes: Complexity depends on block size and number of cross-block connections; typically near-linear with moderate constants.

Empirically, the picture is nuanced. On many long-context language modeling tasks, Performer and sparse methods (Longformer/BigBird) have shown strong performance with significant memory savings. Performers often approach the accuracy of full attention when $r$ is sufficiently large, but on some benchmarks small $r$ leads to noticeable accuracy gaps. Linear Transformers perform very well in streaming autoregressive generation tasks because of their exact causal streaming implementation, and they are very efficient for inference. Linformer can match performance on tasks where attention is effectively low-rank but can degrade when fine-grained position-dependent attention is needed.

Sparse models like Longformer and BigBird are particularly effective when locality matters or when a few global tokens suffice to relay global context. BigBird’s theoretical guarantees that certain sparse patterns can approximate dense attention under conditions suggest robust empirical performance across tasks. However, sparse models must be carefully engineered to map efficiently onto current hardware: sliding window attention can be implemented using efficient block-sparse matrix multiplication custom kernels or hand-optimized indexing, but irregular sparsity slows things down.

Benchmarks also reveal that constant factors dominate: a method with asymptotic $O(n)$ complexity but a large per-token constant (large $r$ in Performer, or large $d_\phi$ in Linear Transformer) may be slower than optimized dense attention for moderate $n$. In many practical regimes (sequence lengths of a few thousand), optimized dense attention using mixed precision and memory-efficient attention kernels can outperform poorly implemented approximations. The crossover point where linear methods become advantageous depends on hardware, batch size, and implementation.

Finally, training stability and convergence matter. Some approximations require careful initialization or optimizer settings. For instance, training with approximate attention may require gradient clipping or schedule tuning to avoid divergence. Transfer learning scenarios where pretrained dense-attention models are fine-tuned to long contexts may benefit from warm-starting or mixing approximations with full attention in early epochs.

Conclusion and practical recommendations

Linear and efficient attention approaches furnish a toolkit for scaling attention to long sequences, streaming workloads, and memory-constrained regimes. Kernelized random-feature methods (Performer) provide a principled approximation of softmax attention with tunable stochastic error and support causal streaming. Linear Transformer and related factorized models give deterministic linear-time attention by changing the feature map, often enabling efficient autoregressive decoding. Linformer and other low-rank methods exploit low intrinsic rank in attention maps to achieve large reductions when that assumption holds. Sparse and locality-biased mechanisms exploit structure in the data—locality and occasional global tokens—to obtain near-linear costs in practice.

When choosing among these methods, consider the following guidelines. If reproducing softmax behavior with bounded approximation error is important and you can afford a few hundred to a few thousand random features, Performer is a strong choice. If you need streaming autoregressive decoding with the absolute lowest incremental cost, linearized feature maps like those in Linear Transformer are attractive. If you believe attention maps are low-rank for your task or can be learned, Linformer can be very efficient. If your domain exhibits strong locality (text, audio) and only occasional global context is needed, sparse sliding-window approaches (Longformer, BigBird) are often the best practical tradeoff. In all cases, measure wall-clock performance, memory consumption, and task metrics on representative hardware and tune approximation hyperparameters ($r$, $k$, window size, sparsity pattern) carefully.

Understanding the algebraic structure behind these approximations—kernel factorization, low-rank projection, and sparsity patterns—helps reason about when they will work and how to combine them. Hybrid designs that mix sparse and low-rank components or combine random-feature kernels with block-sparse patterns often capture strengths of multiple approaches and are an active area of research. As hardware and kernels continue to evolve, these families of methods will remain important tools in scaling attention models to new applications.
