Chapter 10: State-Space Models for Sequence Modeling

Introduction

State-space models (SSMs) provide a powerful framework for modeling time series and sequential data. They originated in control theory and signal processing as a way to represent dynamical systems with latent states that evolve over time and produce observations. More recently, linear SSMs with modern neural parameterizations have emerged as competitive architectures for long-range sequence modeling in machine learning, offering an alternative to recurrent neural networks and Transformers. This chapter introduces discrete-time linear SSMs, explains how they can be interpreted as sequence filters or convolutions, surveys key parameterizations used in recent models (S4, DSS, S5 and relatives), and describes connections to convolutional and filtering views. We analyze advantages and limitations, including computational complexity and expressivity, and conclude with practical training and implementation strategies such as diagonalization and FFT-based approaches.

# Discrete-time linear state-space equations

At the core of linear state-space modeling are two linear equations: one that governs the evolution of a hidden state and one that maps the hidden state to outputs. In discrete time, for time step $n = 0,1,2,\dots$, a standard linear time-invariant state-space model is written as
\[
x_{n+1} = A x_n + B u_n,
\]
\[
y_n = C x_n + D u_n.
\]
Here $x_n \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the latent state vector at time $n$, $u_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input (or control) at time $n$, $y_n \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the output, and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$, $C \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times N}$, $D \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ are constant coefficient matrices. This is the discrete-time analogue of the continuous-time linear system given by differential equations, and it suffices for digital sequence modeling.

The first equation, called the state update or transition equation, specifies how the latent state evolves under the effect of the previous state and the current input. The second equation, often called the observation or readout equation, produces an observable output from the latent state and possibly the input. If $D\neq 0$ the model can create an instantaneous mapping from input to output; often one considers $D=0$ for simplicity and interpretability.

One important property is linear time-invariance (LTI): the matrices $A,B,C,D$ do not depend on the time index $n$. LTI systems are fully characterized by their impulse response, which describes the output sequence produced in response to an input impulse. The impulse response is central to understanding the filtering interpretation of SSMs; we will derive it explicitly below.

To make the model causal and well-posed, we also require an initial state $x_0$ (commonly set to zero). Unrolling the state update yields an explicit expression for $x_n$ in terms of past inputs:
\[
x_n = A^n x_0 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} A^{n-1-k} B u_k.
\]
Assuming $x_0 = 0$, the output becomes
\[
y_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} C A^{n-1-k} B u_k + D u_n.
\]
This shows the output at time $n$ is a linear combination of past inputs weighted by the matrices $C A^{n-1-k} B$, plus the instantaneous term $D u_n$.

The matrix powers $A^t$ thus determine how past inputs influence current outputs. Understanding and manipulating $A$ is therefore crucial: its eigenvalues control memory and stability, and its structure affects computational complexity.

# Interpretation of SSMs as sequence filters or convolutions

The unrolled expression for the output demonstrates that linear SSMs act as causal linear filters on the input sequence. For scalar inputs and outputs ($d=m=1$) and $D=0$, the output simplifies to
\[
y_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} h_{n-1-k} u_k,
\]
where the impulse response sequence is defined by
\[
h_t = C A^t B, \qquad t = 0,1,2,\dots.
\]
This is exactly a causal convolution of the input sequence $\{u_k\}$ with the kernel $\{h_t\}$. More generally, for vector-valued inputs and outputs the model defines a matrix-valued impulse response, but the convolutional interpretation remains: the SSM computes $y = h * u$ where $*$ denotes causal convolution.

Viewing SSMs as filters is valuable for intuition. The latent state $x_n$ provides a compact internal summary of all prior inputs; the matrices $A$ and $B$ determine how inputs are folded into this summary, while $C$ reads out a weighted sum of state coordinates to produce the output. The kernel $h_t$ captures exactly how an input $u_{n-t}$ affects $y_n$. Long-range dependencies correspond to kernels with significant mass for large $t$.

This filter viewpoint connects SSMs to classical signal-processing concepts such as impulse responses, frequency responses, and z-transforms. The discrete-time transfer function of the LTI system is
\[
H(z) = C (zI - A)^{-1} B + D,
\]
where $z$ is a complex variable. Expanding $H(z)$ as a power series in $z^{-1}$ yields the impulse response coefficients $h_t$. Frequency-domain analysis studies how different temporal frequencies in the input are attenuated or amplified by the system, guiding design choices for memory and smoothing properties.

The convolutional perspective is also useful computationally: if one can compute the kernel $h$ efficiently, then sequence outputs can be obtained via convolution. For very long sequences, convolution can be accelerated in the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), leading to sub-quadratic or even near-linear computational complexity in sequence length.

# Parameterizations used in S4, DSS, S5 and related models

Recent neural architectures for long-range sequence modeling parameterize linear SSMs in specific ways to balance efficiency, stability, and expressivity. We'll cover the ideas behind S4, DSS, and S5, highlighting common themes: structured $A$, continuous-time-to-discrete-time mapping, and parameter sharing with neural gating or projection layers.

S4 (Structured State Space Sequence model) introduced a parameterization designed to produce long, expressive convolution kernels while allowing fast parallel computation. The approach begins with a continuous-time SSM with matrix $\tilde{A}$ in companion form derived from a linear differential equation. More concretely, S4 uses the HiPPO (High-order Polynomial Projection Operators) framework to set $\tilde{A}$ so that the state maintains a compressed summary of the input function in a basis of orthogonal polynomials. These continuous-time matrices are then discretized (via bilinear transform or other discretization methods) to obtain discrete-time $A$ and $B$. To keep computations efficient, S4 leverages a particular normal plus low-rank (NPLR) decomposition: $A$ is represented as a diagonal matrix plus a low-rank perturbation. This structure permits fast computation of terms like $C A^t B$ and allows diagonalization or efficient resolvent evaluations.

S4 parameterizes the readout $C$ and input projection $B$ with learned vectors (or small matrices) and may include gating and elementwise nonlinearities around the linear SSM core. Practically, S4 transforms inputs through input and output projection layers and processes a window of inputs by convolving with the kernel derived from the SSM. The end effect is a layer that behaves like a convolution with a long learned kernel but computed efficiently via the SSM structure.

DSS (Diagonal-State Space) models emphasize parameterizing $A$ to be diagonal or nearly diagonal in some basis. A diagonal $A$ means each state coordinate evolves independently, which simplifies powers $A^t$ to elementwise exponentiation and makes computing the impulse response trivial: $h_t = \sum_{i} C_i (A_{ii})^t B_i$. Diagonal models can be very scalable and stable, and they can be augmented with learned complex-valued diagonal entries to capture oscillatory behavior. However, purely diagonal $A$ may lack interaction between state coordinates, limiting expressivity; DSS remedies this by allowing low-rank coupling or mixing layers that project inputs into the diagonal dynamics and project states back to outputs.

S5 (Simplified S4 and Smooth State Space) can be viewed as a practical simplification that retains the benefits of S4 while streamlining training and implementation. S5 uses a parameterization that starts from continuous-time models and applies an efficient discretization scheme. It typically uses diagonal-plus-low-rank $A$ matrices and explicitly constructs kernels in the frequency domain for convolution. The S5 family focuses on numerical stability, closed-form expressions for the convolution kernel (or its frequency response), and controllable memory via eigenvalue placement.

Common elements across these models include: working in continuous time for principled long-range memory, discretizing to obtain practical discrete-time matrices, imposing structured parameterizations (diagonal, diagonal-plus-low-rank, or companion forms) to permit efficient computation, and combining the linear SSM layer with elementwise nonlinearities or gating to provide greater expressivity in neural contexts. They also often use complex-valued parameters to naturally represent decaying oscillations and then implement real-valued operations via pairing complex conjugates.

# Connections between SSMs and convolutional/filtering views

The equivalence between LTI state-space models and convolutional filters makes SSM layers an instance of convolutional sequence processing with a particular parametrically defined kernel. This connection helps to build bridges between SSM-based architectures and other modern sequence models, and it suggests several algorithmic strategies.

First, any stable discrete-time LTI SSM defines an impulse response kernel $h_t = C A^t B$, and its action on an input sequence is convolution: $y = h * u$. Thus SSM layers can be implemented either by unrolling the state recurrence (sequentially or with scan-like parallel prefixes) or by computing the kernel $h$ and performing convolution. When the kernel can be computed for the entire desired length efficiently, convolution via FFT or overlap-add methods yields fast parallel evaluation, making SSM layers viable for very long sequences.

Second, SSMs can be interpreted as parameterized convolutions where the kernel is not a free tensor but is constrained by the low-dimensional matrices $A,B,C,D$. This constraint reduces memory and computation (because $A$ often has structure) and imposes certain inductive biases: smoothness, decay, or oscillation depending on eigenvalues and discretization. In contrast, pure convolutional networks typically parameterize kernels directly; SSM parameterizations aim to get long effective receptive fields without quadratic cost in sequence length or kernel size.

Third, frequency-domain reasoning is natural. The transfer function $H(e^{i\omega})$ describes the SSM filter in frequency space. Multiplying in frequency space corresponds to convolution in time, so FFT-based convolution computes $y$ by transforming input and kernel, pointwise multiplying, and inverse transforming. For models with parameterized $H(z)$, one can directly parameterize frequency responses or compute the frequency-domain kernel from $A,B,C$ formulas using rational functions. Some SSM approaches exploit this to compute convolution outputs directly in frequency domain, sometimes leading to better numerical stability or easier regularization.

Finally, there are links to dilated and causal convolutions used in deep learning, such as WaveNet-style architectures. An SSM can emulate many of those behaviors depending on the eigenstructure of $A$. Conversely, convolutional architectures with exponentially increasing dilation can approximate certain SSM impulse responses. The difference is that SSMs provide a compact global parameterization that can represent very long-range dependencies with fewer parameters, whereas dilated convolutions typically require stacking many layers or large kernels.

# Advantages and limitations (linear complexity, expressivity)

Advantages:

One major advantage of structured SSMs is the potential for linear or near-linear complexity in sequence length. If the kernel $h$ is precomputed or can be computed efficiently, then convolution via FFT gives complexity $O(L \log L)$ for sequence length $L$, which is much better than naive $O(L^2)$ attention. When $A$ is diagonal or diagonal-plus-low-rank, computing $h_t$ for all $t$ up to $L$ can be done in $O(NL)$ or even $O((N+k)\log L)$ per sequence depending on implementation, where $N$ is state dimension and $k$ is rank. This makes SSM layers attractive for long-context tasks.

SSMs also provide strong inductive biases for sequence modeling. The eigenvalues of $A$ control memory decay and oscillations, so one can design or regularize them to capture slowly decaying dependencies or periodicities. Continuous-time origin of many SSM parameterizations (HiPPO, etc.) gives mathematical guarantees about the ability to compress function spaces into finite-dimensional states, which underlies strong empirical performance on long-range tasks.

Another practical advantage is parameter efficiency: a small set of matrices $A,B,C$ can induce a very long effective kernel, often outperforming feedforward or convolutional layers with comparable parameter counts on long-context benchmarks.

Limitations:

Expressivity: Linear SSMs, by themselves, are linear operators. Modeling nonlinear phenomena requires stacking SSM layers with nonlinearities or combining SSMs with gating mechanisms. While stacking can recover powerful representations (like in S4 where SSM is embedded inside a residual block with nonlinearities), the core linear kernel is constrained by the spectral properties of $A$. Diagonal or low-rank structures further restrict the set of possible kernels. There exist kernels or temporal dependencies that require richer (e.g., fully dense) state transitions to represent compactly.

Stability and numerical issues: Ensuring stability (i.e., eigenvalues of $A$ lie within the unit circle after discretization) and managing numerical precision for long sequences can be challenging. Discretization from continuous time must be handled carefully to avoid exploding or vanishing dynamics. Complex eigenvalues lead to oscillatory kernels that must be paired with their conjugates to obtain real-valued outputs; implementations must ensure these pairings are respected numerically.

Computational trade-offs: While convolution via FFT yields $O(L \log L)$ time for a single convolution, precomputing kernels or computing transfer functions can be costly for large state dimension $N$ if done naively. Some SSM parameterizations (e.g., companion matrices) can require $O(N^3)$ work to diagonalize if generic; the use of structured matrices is essential to avoid cubic costs. Also, the constant factors in FFT-based methods and the overhead of moving between time and frequency domains can be significant in practice; for shorter sequences, direct methods or parallel scans might be faster.

Learning dynamics: Training deep stacks of SSM layers can encounter optimization difficulties unless appropriate parameterizations and initialization schemes are used. Some parameterizations incorporate explicit stability constraints or reparameterizations that tame gradients; others rely on careful initializations inspired by HiPPO matrices to bias dynamics toward useful regimes. Regularization and normalization can be important.

# Training and implementation details (diagonalization, FFT-based approaches)

Efficient and stable implementation of SSM-based layers is an engineering and algorithmic challenge. Two broad strategies are commonly used for evaluating an SSM layer on a sequence: time-domain recurrence (scan) and kernel-based convolution (with FFT or overlap-add). Each has trade-offs for parallelism, memory, and speed.

Time-domain recurrence and parallel scan: One can compute the state sequence {x_n} and outputs {y_n} by iterating the recurrence. This is inherently sequential if done naively, costing $O(N^2 L)$ for dense $A$. However, one can exploit matrix exponentials or parallel-scan algorithms. A useful trick for LTI systems is to compute powers of $A$ using binary exponentiation or exponentiation by squaring in a divide-and-conquer parallel scheme; another is to use linear recurrence exploitation such as the "linear recurrence as convolution" viewpoint which reduces the problem to convolution with precomputed coefficients. For diagonal or diagonal-plus-low-rank $A$ the recurrence becomes elementwise or nearly elementwise and can be parallelized heavily on GPUs.

Diagonalization: The central algebraic tool is diagonalizing (or more generally, bringing to Jordan or Schur form) the matrix $A$. If $A$ is diagonalizable as $A = V \Lambda V^{-1}$ with $\Lambda$ diagonal, then powers are easy: $A^t = V \Lambda^t V^{-1}$. Computing $h_t = C A^t B$ becomes $h_t = (C V) \Lambda^t (V^{-1} B)$, which reduces to weighted sums of scalar exponentials, and computing $h$ for all $t$ is cheap. The problem is that diagonalization for a dense arbitrary $A$ costs $O(N^3)$ and can be numerically unstable. To avoid this, modern SSM parameterizations choose $A$ with special structure that admits cheap diagonalization or even direct diagonal forms: diagonal matrices, Cauchy-like matrices, or diagonal-plus-low-rank forms where the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula enables efficient resolvent computations.

FFT-based convolution: Once the kernel $h$ for length $L$ is available (or can be computed in $O(NL)$ or better), convolution of $h$ with the input $u$ can be accelerated using FFTs. Zero-padding the sequences and using FFT-based multiplication yields complexity $O(L \log L)$ per channel for large $L$, with typical practical efficiencies when $L$ exceeds a few thousand. When one must process multiple sequences or batch items, batched FFTs are efficient. Overlap-add or overlap-save techniques enable streaming convolution of very long sequences in blocks.

Computing kernels via transfer functions: Instead of computing $h_t$ by repeatedly multiplying $A$, one can compute the transfer function $H(z) = C (zI - A)^{-1} B + D$ and then obtain the time-domain kernel by applying an inverse z-transform, which is effectively an inverse discrete-time Fourier transform if evaluated on the unit circle. For structured $A$, evaluating $(e^{i\omega}I - A)^{-1} B$ for many frequencies $\omega$ can be done efficiently using the Sherman-Morrison formula if $A$ is diagonal-plus-low-rank. This yields an $O(N + L \log L)$ overall strategy: evaluate $H(e^{i\omega_k})$ at FFT frequency bins $k$, multiply by the FFT of $u$, and inverse FFT back to time domain. This approach is used in S4/S5 implementations: parameterize $A$ as diagonal plus low rank, evaluate resolvent on frequency grid cheaply, get frequency-domain kernel, and perform convolution via FFT.

Handling complex-valued dynamics: Many SSMs are naturally represented with complex eigenvalues to produce oscillations. Implementations typically store complex diagonal entries in conjugate pairs and use real arithmetic by stacking real and imaginary parts or by implementing complex FFTs. Careful pairing ensures real outputs and reduces redundancy.

Stability and discretization: Continuous-time SSMs are often discretized using the bilinear transform (Tustin), zero-order hold, or exact matrix exponential to obtain discrete-time $A$ from continuous-time $\tilde{A}$. The bilinear transform maps the left-half complex plane to the unit disk, preserving stability in many cases. Exact discretization via matrix exponential is possible: given continuous-time $dx/dt = \tilde{A} x + \tilde{B} u(t)$ sampled with zero-order-hold at step $\Delta$, the discrete matrices are
\[
A = \exp(\tilde{A} \Delta),
\qquad
B = \left(\int_{0}^{\Delta} \exp(\tilde{A} s) ds\right) \tilde{B}.
\]
Computing matrix exponentials can be expensive for dense matrices, but for structured $\tilde{A}$ or diagonal $\tilde{A}$ this is cheap. Many SSM parameterizations simplify continuous-time matrices to allow efficient exact or approximate discretization.

Implementation tips and optimizations: For practical training, batching, GPU memory, and numerical stability are central concerns. Precomputing kernels for all layers when sequence length is known can amortize overhead. Mixed-precision training with care around stability-sensitive operations (e.g., using float32 for diagonalization/FFT kernels) helps. Regularization techniques such as spectral norm penalties on $A$ eigenvalues or explicit clipping can prevent exploding dynamics. Also, using residual connections and normalization (layer norm) around SSM layers improves gradient flow in deep stacks.

Finally, modern libraries implement many of these techniques: efficient low-level routines for diagonal-plus-low-rank resolvents, batched FFTs, and stable discretization. Understanding the algebraic structure of the chosen parameterization is key to implementing these models efficiently rather than relying on naive dense linear algebra.

Conclusion

Linear state-space models bridge classical signal processing and modern deep learning for sequence modeling. By interpreting SSMs as parameterized convolutional filters, one can leverage frequency-domain algorithms and structured linear algebra to achieve long-range receptive fields with favorable computational scaling. Models such as S4, DSS, and S5 illustrate how careful parameterization—diagonalization-friendly matrices, diagonal-plus-low-rank structure, and continuous-to-discrete design—yields practical, expressive layers suitable for long-context tasks. The trade-offs involve balancing expressivity and structure, ensuring numerical stability, and selecting efficient implementations (recurrence vs. convolution via FFT). With these tools and insights, SSM-based architectures are a valuable part of the sequence-modeling toolkit and a fruitful area for ongoing research.
