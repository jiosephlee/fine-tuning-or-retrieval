Introduction

High-dimensional nonlinear representations are central to modern machine learning. Many successful methods—kernel machines, deep neural networks, random feature models—work by mapping raw inputs into a high-dimensional space where patterns become easier to separate and simpler geometric rules can extract desired behavior. This chapter develops the theoretical principles that motivate this approach. We begin with the intuition and formal statement of Cover's theorem, which explains why increasing dimensionality (especially via nonlinear maps) makes random patterns more likely to be linearly separable. We then connect that intuition to the more operational view of nonlinear feature maps and kernels, showing how implicit or explicit high-dimensional embeddings support learning. Next we discuss how embedding dimensionality relates to expressivity and how overparameterization changes the landscape of approximation and optimization. We examine the trade-offs between capacity and generalization that arise in high dimensions and how classical notions (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity) must be reconciled with modern phenomena like benign overfitting and double descent. Finally, we describe practical architectural patterns—up-projections, down-projections, and bottlenecks—used in block designs to gain the benefits of high-dimensional embeddings while controlling computation, representation reuse, and generalization.

# Cover's theorem on separability in high dimensions

Cover's theorem provides a fundamental explanation for why mapping data into a higher-dimensional space can make otherwise complex decision boundaries simple (often linear). Informally, Cover's theorem states that a complex pattern-classification problem cast in a high-dimensional space is more likely to be linearly separable than in a low-dimensional space, provided the mapping is nonlinear and the new features are in general position. The key intuition is geometric: a finite number of points in general position in a high-dimensional space can be separated by many different hyperplanes, while in low dimensions many point configurations force intersections among class manifolds.

The original quantitative statement by Thomas Cover (1965) considers $N$ points placed randomly and independently in general position in $\mathbb{R}^d$. If we label these $N$ points arbitrarily into two classes, the probability that there exists a linear separator is equivalent to the fraction of dichotomies realizable by hyperplanes. For points in general position, the number of linearly separable dichotomies equals
\[
2 \sum_{k=0}^{d-1} \binom{N-1}{k},
\]
so the probability that a random labeling is separable is this quantity divided by $2^N$. This expression shows a sharp transition: when $d \ge N$, all $2^N$ labelings are separable (because $N$ points in $\mathbb{R}^N$ can be shattered by hyperplanes). When $d$ is much smaller than $N$, only a tiny fraction of labelings are separable.

Beyond the combinatorial counting, the deeper message is that nonlinear mappings to higher dimensions increase the chance of linear separability. Consider a nonlinear feature map $\phi: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with $d$ large. If $\phi$ is chosen so that the images $\{\phi(x_i)\}$ are in general position, Cover's theorem suggests that it becomes easier to find a linear functional $w^\top \phi(x)$ separating positive and negative examples. This does not mean that any high-dimensional map will solve every learning problem—choice of mapping, geometry of classes, and noise all matter—but it underpins why many methods seek richer nonlinear embeddings before applying simple linear decoders.

An important caveat is that Cover's theorem applies to separability for finite sets of points and does not directly address issues of generalization to new points or robustness to noise. Moreover, the combinatorial favorable scaling can be misleading if the mapping introduces degeneracies, or if the data lie on low-dimensional manifolds embedded in the high-dimensional space. Nonetheless, Cover’s theorem remains a powerful heuristic: nonlinearity plus increased dimensionality increases capacity to represent complex class boundaries.

# Nonlinear feature maps and kernel perspectives

Feature maps turn raw inputs into vectors whose coordinates capture useful nonlinear interactions. Given input $x\in\mathbb{R}^p$, a feature map $\phi:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}^d$ produces $\phi(x)$; learning then proceeds with linear methods on these features, e.g., choosing weights $w\in\mathbb{R}^d$ to predict $y = w^\top\phi(x)$. Two complementary perspectives are useful: the explicit feature map view and the kernel (implicit) view.

From the explicit perspective, designing $\phi$ aims to make linearly separable the relevant structure. Examples include polynomial features, where $\phi(x)$ contains all monomials up to some degree, and random feature maps, which approximate kernels by mapping $x$ into a randomized high-dimensional space. The explicit map is concrete and amenable to sparsity or structured representations, and it clarifies how dimensionality and feature interactions influence expressivity. For polynomial features of degree $k$, the feature dimension grows combinatorially with $p$ and $k$, which yields enormous capacity for modeling interactions but also raises computational and overfitting concerns.

The kernel perspective abstracts away $\phi$ by focusing on inner products: a symmetric positive semi-definite kernel $K(x,x')$ equals $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$ for some (possibly infinite-dimensional) $\phi$. Kernel methods like support vector machines and kernel ridge regression operate directly via $K$, using the representer theorem to express solutions as linear combinations of kernels centered at training points:
\[
f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i K(x, x_i).
\]
This avoids explicit construction of $\phi$ when the feature dimension is large or infinite. Kernels encode prior assumptions about smoothness and similarity; for example, the Gaussian (RBF) kernel
\[
K(x,x') = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x-x'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\]
corresponds to an infinite-dimensional $\phi$ and favors functions that are locally smooth.

Kernel methods link directly to Cover's intuition: using a kernel implicitly maps inputs into a high- or infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, increasing the representational capacity and making linear separation possible in that space. However, the kernel's structure constrains which labelings are realizable; the geometry induced by $K$ matters. Different kernels correspond to different inductive biases—polynomial kernels favor low-degree interactions, RBF kernels favor locality—so the choice of kernel controls which aspects of data structure become linearly separable.

Random features bridge explicit mappings and kernel methods. For shift-invariant kernels $K(x,x') = \kappa(x-x')$, Bochner's theorem gives a representation as an expectation over random features:
\[
K(x,x') = \mathbb{E}_\omega[\psi_\omega(x)\psi_\omega(x')],
\]
and one can approximate $K$ by drawing $d$ random samples $\{\omega_j\}$ and using the finite-dimensional map $\phi(x) = d^{-1/2}(\psi_{\omega_1}(x),\dots,\psi_{\omega_d}(x))$. As $d$ increases, the finite random feature model closely approximates kernel methods, and Cover's theorem again gives intuition: larger $d$ increases capacity for linear separators.

Finally, nonlinear feature maps implemented by deep neural networks learn $\phi$ from data jointly with the linear readout. This sidesteps explicit kernel selection by adapting the geometry of the embedding to the task, but many analyses connect deep networks to kernel limits (neural tangent kernel) or infinite-width features. Whether one chooses explicit $\phi$, kernel methods, or learned deep embeddings depends on computational constraints, data size, and desired inductive biases.

# Embedding dimensionality, overparameterization and expressivity

Embedding dimensionality—the number of coordinates in $\phi(x)$—is a primary driver of expressivity. Intuitively, more dimensions provide more degrees of freedom to place training examples in general position and to represent complex functions linearly. Formally, with $N$ training points, if the embedding dimension $d \ge N$ and the embedded points are in general position, any labeling can be fit by a linear model on $\phi(x)$, by linear algebra: one can always solve $W \Phi = Y$ for appropriate weights $W$ when $\Phi$ is a full-rank $d\times N$ matrix. This underlies the idea of overparameterization—models with more parameters than training examples can interpolate the dataset.

Overparameterization changes both approximation and optimization. On the approximation side, sufficiently large $d$ allows exact interpolation of finite datasets and can realize more complex decision boundaries. On the optimization side, enlarging model capacity often makes empirical risk minimization easier: with more parameters, gradient-based algorithms find zero-training-loss solutions more readily because the optimization landscape typically contains many global minima and fewer strict local minima. In deep learning, taking width to be large (many neurons per layer) often simplifies optimization, an effect formalized in mean-field and neural tangent kernel regimes.

However, expressivity is not simply a monotone function of $d$. The structure of features and the class of functions representable as linear forms on them matters. For an explicit polynomial embedding, increasing degree or adding monomials is different from adding random features: one is structured and tailored to interactions, the other is more generic. Moreover, infinite-dimensional kernels (e.g., RBF) provide enormous expressivity but are still controlled by kernel hyperparameters (length scale) that define effective dimensionality in practice. In many cases, although the nominal feature dimension may be infinite, the effective number of degrees of freedom used by the learned function can be limited by regularization and data geometry.

A more nuanced view distinguishes between parameter count and effective capacity. For linear models on features $\phi$, the relevant algebraic object is the Gram matrix $G$ with entries $G_{ij} = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle$. The rank of $G$ bounds how many independent directions the model can fit. Even if $d$ is huge, if the embedded points lie in a low-dimensional subspace then the effective rank is small and expressivity is limited. Conversely, when $G$ has many significant eigenvalues, the model can fit many independent directions and approximate complex functions.

Overparameterization has been observed to improve generalization in modern practice, contrary to classical bias-variance intuition. The mechanisms include implicit regularization by optimization algorithms (e.g., gradient descent favors low-norm solutions), alignment of learned features with task-relevant directions, and interpolation regimes where noise is small or structured. These effects will be discussed more when we cover capacity and generalization trade-offs.

# Trade-offs between capacity and generalization in high dimensions

Classical statistical learning theory suggests a trade-off: more capacity allows fitting more complex functions but risks overfitting noise, harming generalization. Measures like VC dimension and Rademacher complexity formalize this: larger capacities lead to larger uniform deviations between empirical and expected risks. In the context of high-dimensional embeddings, naive application of these bounds implies that increasing $d$ always worsens generalization. Yet empirical evidence from overparameterized models shows this conclusion is incomplete; models with enormous capacity can generalize well.

To reconcile this, consider multiple contributing factors. First, the capacity relevant to generalization is not simply raw parameter count or feature dimension but effective capacity after accounting for regularization (explicit or implicit), data distribution, and algorithmic biases. For linear predictors on features, ridge regularization or early-stopped gradient descent constrains the norm of weights $w$, and generalization bounds depend on $\|w\|$ and the spectrum of the feature covariance, not directly on $d$. If the data lie near a low-dimensional manifold, then even high-dimensional embeddings may compress the relevant variance into a few directions, reducing effective complexity.

Second, interpolation (zero training error) can be compatible with good generalization if the fitted function aligns with the underlying signal and avoids fitting high-frequency noise. The double descent phenomenon describes how test risk as a function of model complexity can decrease, then increase near the interpolation threshold, and then decrease again in highly overparameterized regimes. In the overparameterized tail, many interpolating solutions exist; optimization and implicit biases (e.g., minimum-norm solution for linear least squares when using gradient descent initialized at zero) pick solutions with favorable generalization. Thus, increasing $d$ can move a model into a regime where implicit regularization yields simpler, smoother predictors despite high nominal capacity.

Third, the geometry of the feature map interacts with noise. High-dimensional embeddings can amplify small perturbations if the mapping stretches certain directions. If noise primarily aligns with small-eigenvalue directions of the data covariance, regularization must suppress those directions to avoid overfitting. Consequently, practitioners balance dimensionality with explicit regularizers (weight decay, dropout), normalization, and careful architecture choices to control sensitivity.

Finally, statistical learning bounds that depend on norms and spectra lead to concrete prescriptions: regularize the weight norm, whiten or normalize features to avoid tiny eigenvalues, or design embeddings that concentrate signal in a moderate number of principal directions. In practice, one often designs embeddings and training protocols so that increased dimensionality improves expressivity and facilitates optimization while regularization and data geometry preserve or improve generalization.

# Practical use of up-/down-projections in block designs

In practical network architectures, designers frequently combine high-dimensional embeddings with computational and statistical efficiency by using blocks that project up to a wide representation, apply nonlinear transformations, and then project back down. These up-/down-projection patterns occur in residual blocks, transformer feed-forward layers, and bottleneck blocks in convolutional networks. They are motivated by the theoretical principles covered earlier: temporarily mapping to a higher-dimensional space to make features more linearly separable (empowering nonlinear interactions), then compressing to control parameter count and enforce parsimony.

A canonical pattern is a block that first applies a linear expansion $E:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d>m$, then a nonlinear elementwise activation (and perhaps additional mixing), and finally a projection $P:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}^m$ back to the original width. Writing input $x\in\mathbb{R}^m$, expanded features are $z = \phi(E x)$, and the block outputs $y = P z$ (often with addition of the input via residual connection). Intuitively, $E$ provides many directions to represent different nonlinear interactions, $\phi$ creates nonlinearity needed for separability, and $P$ selects and mixes the resulting features to produce a compact representation.

This design achieves several goals. First, it leverages Cover-like intuition: the expansion increases the chance that subsequent linear operations (including the final projection) can perform complex separations. Second, it controls overall parameter and computation costs: by keeping $P$ and $E$ structured (e.g., using low-rank factorizations, grouped convolutions, or sparse connections), the total number of parameters can remain manageable even with a large intermediate $d$. Third, projecting back down encourages the model to compress information and discard spurious directions, acting as an inductive bias that favors parsimonious representations and helps generalization.

There are several practical variants and design choices. Bottleneck blocks in ResNet use $1\times1$ convolutions to expand channels, followed by $3\times3$ convolutions and then $1\times1$ projection back. The expansion allows the $3\times3$ stage to operate in a higher-dimensional space that supports richer cross-channel interactions. Transformer feed-forward layers use a similar pattern: the token representation of dimension $m$ is expanded to a larger dimension $d$ via a linear layer, a nonlinearity (e.g., GELU) is applied elementwise, and the representation is projected back to $m$. This expansion factor (commonly 4) is critical to transformer performance and embodies the same up-/down-projection principle.

When implementing such blocks, attention to initialization and normalization is important. Expanding to a very high $d$ can amplify gradients and activations; techniques like layer normalization, batch normalization, and scaled initialization keep numerical behavior stable. From a statistical perspective, adding a projection back down implicitly regularizes by forcing the network to compress features; using dropout or weight decay in the expanded space further controls overfitting.

There are also algorithmic strategies to get the benefits of large $d$ without high cost. Random projection techniques can be used for expansion: instead of learning a full dense $E$, sample a random matrix (possibly sparse) and fix it, reducing parameters while still providing many random directions in which nonlinearities can act. Structured expansions—Kronecker, circulant, or block-sparse matrices—reduce computational cost while preserving expressivity. Low-rank factorization of $P$ and $E$ can control parameter counts and enable fast multiplication. Finally, adaptive compression methods learn $P$ to keep only task-relevant expanded features, sometimes implemented as attention-like gating.

In summary, block designs that expand to high-dimensional nonlinear spaces and compress back combine the best of both worlds: they exploit high-dimensional separability and nonlinear interactions to increase expressivity while keeping models tractable and encouraging generalization through enforced compression and regularization. Understanding the theoretical motivations (Cover’s theorem, kernel views, capacity considerations) helps in choosing expansion factors, regularization strength, and architectural constraints to achieve the desired empirical trade-offs.

Concluding remarks

High-dimensional nonlinear embeddings are a unifying theme across many learning paradigms. Cover's theorem explains why nonlinear expansion facilitates separability, while kernel methods and explicit feature maps provide practical ways to implement that idea. Embedding dimensionality and overparameterization interact with optimization and generalization in subtle ways: more dimensions can both make fitting easier and, when managed properly, preserve or improve generalization. Finally, practical architectures operationalize these principles via up-/down-projections and bottlenecks that balance expressivity, computation, and statistical efficiency. Keeping the geometric and spectral intuitions from this chapter in mind will help you read and understand the theoretical and empirical literature on representation learning and the design of modern models.
