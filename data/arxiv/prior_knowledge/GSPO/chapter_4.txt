# Introduction

Reinforcement learning often frames decision-making as optimizing a policy to maximize expected reward. In many applications — from robotics to language generation — the policy is a parameterized probabilistic model that maps states (or inputs) to actions (or outputs). Policy gradient methods provide a direct way to optimize such policy parameters by estimating gradients of the expected reward with respect to the parameters and performing gradient ascent. A central identity used in these methods is the score-function (or likelihood-ratio) gradient identity, which rewrites the gradient of an expectation so that it becomes an expectation of a product involving the gradient of the log-probability. This identity underlies the REINFORCE algorithm and more advanced actor-critic methods, and it makes Monte Carlo sampling a practical tool for estimating gradients even when the reward function is non-differentiable with respect to the policy parameters.

This chapter develops the policy gradient framework step by step. We begin with the objective: what exactly we are maximizing when we say "expected reward under a policy." We derive the score-function gradient identity and build toward the classic REINFORCE estimator, explaining how Monte Carlo sampling produces usable gradient estimates. Because naive estimators can have very high variance, we then introduce baselines and value-function-based variance reduction techniques. Next we distinguish between per-trajectory (whole-sequence) and per-step (per-token or per-time-step) formulations of gradients and explain when and how each is used. Finally, we show how to compute the gradient of the log-probability, $ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$, for autoregressive models (such as sequence models), which is essential for implementing policy gradient updates in practice.

# Objective: maximize expected reward under policy

The core objective in policy-based reinforcement learning is to find policy parameters $\theta$ that maximize expected reward. Let $\pi_\theta$ denote a parameterized stochastic policy. Depending on the setting, the policy maps a state $s$ (or conditioning input $x$) to a distribution over actions $a$, or, in sequence generation, maps a prefix of tokens to a distribution over the next token. We consider a general form where the policy defines a distribution over trajectories (or outputs) $y$ given an input $x$. The expected return (objective) is the expectation of a scalar reward function $R(y, x)$ under this policy:
\[
J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\big[\,R(y, x)\,\big].
\]
If $x$ itself is drawn from some distribution (dataset or environment), we incorporate that by taking expectation over $x$ as well:
\[
J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\big[\,R(y, x)\,\big].
\]
Throughout the chapter we will often suppress explicit dependence on $x$ for notational brevity and focus on a single conditioning context, understanding that practical implementations average over training examples.

Why optimize $J(\theta)$? If $\pi_\theta$ represents the agent's action-selection mechanism (or a generator model's distribution over outputs), then maximizing expected reward encourages the policy to prefer higher-reward trajectories. The difficulty arises because $R(y, x)$ may be non-differentiable with respect to $\theta$ (it may depend purely on discrete outcomes), or the distribution $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ may be high-dimensional and intractable to integrate exactly. Policy gradient methods address this by moving the gradient inside the expectation in a way that yields an estimator computable by sampling.

# Score-function gradient identity

At the heart of policy gradient methods is the score-function identity (also called the likelihood-ratio trick). This identity rewrites the gradient of an expectation in a form that separates the dependence on the parameterized distribution and the function inside the expectation.

Start from the objective for a fixed input $x$:
\[
J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(y)] \;=\; \sum_y \pi_\theta(y) R(y)
\]
in the discrete case (continuous case replaces sums with integrals). Now differentiate $J(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y) R(y) \;=\; \sum_y \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y) \, R(y).
\]
We would like to turn $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y)$ into something involving $\pi_\theta(y)$ itself, since $\pi_\theta(y)$ is a probability and we can sample from it. Use the identity
\[
\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y) \;=\; \pi_\theta(y) \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y),
\]
which follows from the chain rule applied to the log. Substitute to get the score-function form:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \sum_y \pi_\theta(y) \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \, R(y) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\,R(y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\,\big].
\]
This is the fundamental identity. It expresses the gradient of an expectation as an expectation of a product between the scalar reward and the score function $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$.

Intuitively, $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$ points in parameter space toward increasing the log-likelihood of the sampled trajectory $y$. Multiplying by $R(y)$ scales that direction by how good $y$ is. Thus the expectation steers parameters to increase probability of high-reward outcomes and to decrease probability of low-reward outcomes (since for negative rewards the update points down the likelihood gradient).

A few important properties follow directly. First, the identity does not require $R(y)$ to be differentiable with respect to $\theta$; $R$ can be any scalar-valued function of the sampled trajectory. Second, the identity works whether $y$ is discrete or continuous; the derivation only needs interchanging gradient and summation/integration, which is justified under regularity conditions. Third, the score-function estimator can have high variance because it multiplies the possibly high-variance scalar $R(y)$ with the score. Reducing that variance is a major practical consideration.

# REINFORCE algorithm and Monte Carlo gradient estimates

The score-function identity gives an expectation form for the gradient, but in practice we cannot evaluate that expectation exactly except in small problems. Instead, we estimate the expectation via Monte Carlo sampling. The simplest estimator is the REINFORCE estimator, introduced by Williams (1992).

Using the identity
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\,R(y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\,\big],
\]
draw $N$ independent samples $y^{(1)}, \dots, y^{(N)}$ from $\pi_\theta$ (for a given input $x$). The Monte Carlo estimate of the gradient is
\[
\hat{g}_N \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N R\!\big(y^{(i)}\big) \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\!\big(y^{(i)}\big).
\]
Under mild conditions, $\hat{g}_N$ is an unbiased estimator of $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ and converges to the true gradient as $N \to \infty$. We can then perform stochastic gradient ascent: update parameters by $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \, \hat{g}_N$ with learning rate $\alpha$.

In many applications, $N=1$ per parameter update is used (single-sample REINFORCE), because drawing many full trajectories is expensive. Even with a single sample per update, the estimator is unbiased. However, single-sample updates often require small learning rates due to high variance.

To implement REINFORCE in code, one must be able to (1) sample trajectories $y \sim \pi_\theta$, (2) compute the reward $R(y)$, and (3) compute the score $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$. Step (3) typically uses automatic differentiation because $\log \pi_\theta(y)$ is a differentiable function of $\theta$ when the policy is parameterized by a neural network producing probabilities. The structure of $\log \pi_\theta(y)$ depends on how the policy factorizes (e.g., autoregressively), which we address later.

A simple example clarifies the workflow. Suppose the policy chooses a single discrete action $a$ with probability vector given by a softmax over logits $f_\theta(x)$ and receives reward $R(a,x)$. Then sample $a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, compute reward $R(a,x)$, and compute the gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid x) = \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x)^\top(\mathbf{e}_a - \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x))$ (the typical softmax gradient). Multiply by $R(a,x)$ to form the update. This is the simplest instantiation of REINFORCE.

While REINFORCE is unbiased and conceptually straightforward, it often suffers from high variance, which makes learning slow and unstable. The next section presents common variance-reduction techniques built around subtracting baselines and using learned value functions.

# Use of baselines and value functions to reduce variance

A major practical issue with the REINFORCE estimator is high variance. The gradient estimate multiplies the reward $R(y)$ by the score function; if $R(y)$ varies greatly across samples, the estimator fluctuates significantly. A standard and powerful variance-reduction technique is to subtract a baseline from the reward before multiplying by the score. Crucially, if the baseline does not depend on the action (or trajectory) except through terms independent of $\theta$ (more precisely, any function $b(x)$ that is independent of the sampled action $y$ given the conditioning context), the estimator remains unbiased.

Formally, consider any baseline function $b(x)$ that depends on the conditioning input $x$ but not on the sampled $y$. Then
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\,\big(R(y) - b(x)\big) \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \,\big],
\]
because
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\,b(x) \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y) \,\big] \;=\; b(x) \, \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[1] \;=\; b(x) \, \nabla_\theta 1 \;=\; 0.
\]
That expectation vanishes because the expectation of the score function alone is zero: $\mathbb{E}_{y}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)] = \nabla_\theta \int \pi_\theta(y) dy = 0$. Subtracting $b(x)$ does not introduce bias but can reduce variance when $b(x)$ is chosen well. Intuitively, $b(x)$ serves as a baseline or reference for how good a sampled trajectory is, so only the advantage relative to that baseline influences the update.

A common choice for the baseline is the expected reward under the current policy, conditioned on $x$:
\[
b(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(y) \mid x] = V^{\pi_\theta}(x),
\]
which equals the objective value $J(\theta)$ for that $x$. Using this baseline makes the scalar inside the expectation the advantage $A(y,x) = R(y,x) - V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$. In practice $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is often unknown and estimated, e.g., via a parameterized value function $v_\phi(x)$ learned by regression. This leads to actor-critic methods, where an actor (the policy $\pi_\theta$) is trained using a critic (the value estimator $v_\phi$) to provide low-variance advantages.

More sophisticated baselines use per-state or per-prefix values when the decision problem unfolds over multiple timesteps. For episodic tasks, one can compute the return-to-go from a timestep $t$ onward and subtract a baseline that depends only on the current state. Alternatively, more advanced control variates and generalized advantage estimators (GAE) further trade bias and variance by blending multi-step returns.

Why does subtracting a baseline reduce variance? The variance of the estimator is the variance of $(R(y) - b) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)$. If $b$ approximates $R$ well (in the sense of reducing the variance of $R(y)$ across sampled $y$), then the product will have lower variance. The optimal (minimum-variance) scalar baseline for a given component of the gradient is proportional to the covariance between $R(y)$ and the corresponding score component, but computing that exactly is impractical; using an estimate of the value function is an effective, practical choice.

When a learned value function $v_\phi(x)$ is used as the baseline, one typically trains $\phi$ by minimizing mean-squared error between predicted values and empirical returns: sample trajectories, compute returns $R(y)$, and update $\phi$ to reduce $\big(R(y) - v_\phi(x)\big)^2$. Simultaneously, update $\theta$ with the advantage-weighted score gradient. This simultaneous training yields actor-critic algorithms: the actor updates aim to increase probability of trajectories that outperform the critic, while the critic improves its predictions to better estimate expected returns and reduce variance.

# Per-step (per-token) versus per-trajectory gradient formulations

So far, we have considered trajectories $y$ as whole objects. In many sequential decision problems, such as episodic RL or sequence generation, a trajectory decomposes into a sequence of actions or tokens $y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)$ taken at timesteps $t = 1, \dots, T$. The reward might be a single scalar assigned at the end of the sequence (episodic reward), or it can be decomposed into per-timestep rewards $r_t$. The policy typically factorizes autoregressively:
\[
\pi_\theta(y) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x\big).
\]
This factorization suggests two ways to write the policy gradient: per-trajectory and per-step (per-timestep). Both are algebraically equivalent if the reward is the total return, but their estimators offer different variance properties and enable different variance-reduction strategies.

Per-trajectory (whole-sequence) formulation: using the score-function identity on the joint trajectory $y$ yields
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\,R(y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y)\,\big].
\]
Since $\log \pi_\theta(y) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x)$, this becomes
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\,R(y) \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x) \Big].
\]
A Monte Carlo estimator samples full sequences and applies the total reward to each timestep’s score components. This approach is simple and correct but can be wasteful when only later tokens affect the terminal reward.

Per-step (per-timestep) formulation: we can instead associate returns with each timestep. Let $G_t$ denote the return (cumulative future reward) starting at timestep $t$; in the terminal-reward-only case $G_t = R(y)$ for all $t$. The gradient can be written as
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\,\sum_{t=1}^T G_t \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x) \Big].
\]
More generally, when rewards are given per-step, $G_t = \sum_{t'=t}^T r_{t'}$ is the return-to-go, and the gradient uses $G_t$ at each time. This per-step decomposition allows us to subtract time-dependent baselines $b_t$ that depend on the state or prefix at time $t$, giving
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\,\sum_{t=1}^T \big(G_t - b_t\big) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x)\Big].
\]
Per-step baselines reduce variance more than a single global baseline because they can capture changing expected returns as the sequence evolves.

A key concept derived from per-step formulation is the advantage function. For timestep $t$, define the action-value function
\[
Q^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\,G_t \mid s_t, a_t\,\big],
\]
and the state-value function
\[
V^{\pi}(s_t) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[\,G_t \mid s_t\,\big].
\]
Then the advantage is $A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) = Q^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) - V^{\pi}(s_t)$, and the gradient can be written using the advantage:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\,\sum_{t=1}^T A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\Big].
\]
Using advantage estimates instead of raw returns reduces variance and provides a clearer signal of whether a particular action was better than expected given the current state.

When the reward is only at the end of the sequence (as in many sequence-modeling tasks measured by BLEU, ROUGE, or human preference), $G_t = R(y)$ and the per-step and per-trajectory formulations are algebraically equivalent. However, implementing the per-step estimator allows more refined baselines (prefix-dependent) and opens the door to bootstrapped estimates of $G_t$ using a value function rather than full rollouts, which is the basis of actor-critic algorithms.

Which formulation is preferable depends on context. Per-trajectory updates are simpler conceptually and require only computing the total reward. Per-step updates with per-step baselines or learned critics usually achieve lower variance and faster learning, especially when sequences are long and rewards are distributed over time.

# Practical computation of ∇θ log π(y|x) for autoregressive models

Implementing policy gradient requires computing the gradient of the log-probability of sampled trajectories with respect to parameters. For autoregressive models used in sequence generation (language models, translation, etc.), the policy factorizes:
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x\big).
\]
Taking logs:
\[
\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x\big).
\]
Therefore, the gradient of the log-probability is the sum of per-timestep gradients:
\[
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x\big).
\]
This identity is both simple and crucial: evaluating the score for a sampled sequence reduces to summing the per-token score contributions, which are readily computed by backpropagating through the model that produced the probabilities.

In practice, modern frameworks compute $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ during the forward pass by evaluating the model on the sampled sequence and summing the log-probabilities output at each timestep. Automatic differentiation then yields $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ in the usual way. There are a few practical considerations to be aware of:

- Sampling vs. teacher forcing: During supervised training, sequence models are typically trained with teacher forcing: at timestep $t$ the model conditions on the true prefix $y_{1:t-1}$ to predict $y_t$. For policy gradient, the model must sample actions from its own predictive distribution to obtain trajectories $y$ that reflect its current policy. When computing $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$, the forward pass should use the sampled prefix at each step, since the policy’s distribution for the next token depends on previously sampled tokens. In practice, this means running the autoregressive model in sampling mode: generate a sequence token-by-token, sample each token according to the softmax distribution given the previously sampled tokens, and record the log-probabilities.

- Efficient computation: If the model exposes an API that, given a complete sampled sequence, returns log-probabilities for every position conditioned on the previous tokens, then computing $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ for the entire sequence is straightforward. Some implementations avoid repeated forward passes by caching hidden states during sampling, which is important for long sequences. For batched sampling, parallelizing sequence generation and log-prob computation is critical for performance.

- Masking and padding: In batched processing of variable-length sequences, ensure that padding tokens do not contribute to the log-probability or gradient sums. Masked sums over valid positions maintain correctness.

- Handling continuous action spaces: For continuous outputs (e.g., Gaussian policies for control), $\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ has an analytic form. To sample trajectories, draw continuous actions from the parameterized distribution and compute log densities accordingly. The same factorization principle applies when actions are vector-valued.

- Numerical stability: Softmax log-probabilities can be small; compute logs in numerically stable ways. Automatic-differentiation frameworks typically handle this, but implementing custom sampling or log-density computations must use stable log-sum-exp tricks.

To make the connection concrete, consider an autoregressive model parameterized by neural network logits $z_t = f_\theta(y_{1:t-1}, x)$ and a softmax output $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x) = \mathrm{softmax}(z_t)$. For a sampled token $y_t$, the per-timestep log-probability is
\[
\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x) = z_{t,y_t} - \log \sum_{a} \exp(z_{t,a}).
\]
Automatic differentiation backpropagates through the softmax and $f_\theta$ to produce the gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x)$. Summing over $t$ yields the sequence-level score gradient. When implementing REINFORCE, multiply this gradient (or the scalar log-probabilities during the forward pass) by the reward or advantage and accumulate across timesteps.

One subtlety arises if you try to backpropagate through the sampling operation itself. Sampling is nondifferentiable, but the score-function estimator avoids this by computing gradients of the log-likelihood of the sampled action, not of the sampling decision. In automatic-diff libraries, you typically prevent gradients from flowing into the sampling operation by treating the sampled token as a discrete value used only to select logits for computing the log-prob; gradients flow through the logits and their dependence on $\theta$, which is the intended behavior.

In summary, computing $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ for autoregressive models is straightforward: evaluate the model on the sampled sequence to obtain per-timestep log-probabilities, sum them, and differentiate. Automatic-differentiation handles the needed computations, and careful implementation of sampling and caching makes this efficient.

# Putting it together: algorithms and practical tips

Combining the pieces yields the practical REINFORCE workflow for sequence models:

1. For a minibatch of inputs $x$, run the policy in sampling mode to generate sampled outputs $y^{(i)}$ and record per-timestep log-probabilities $\log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)}_t \mid y^{(i)}_{1:t-1}, x^{(i)})$. Aggregate to form $\log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)})$.

2. Compute rewards $R\big(y^{(i)}, x^{(i)}\big)$ for each sampled sequence, and optionally form per-timestep returns $G_t^{(i)}$.

3. Compute baselines $b(x^{(i)})$ or per-timestep value estimates $v_\phi(s_t^{(i)})$ (the critic). If using a learned critic, update its parameters by regressing $v_\phi$ toward observed returns with mean-squared error.

4. Form advantage estimates $\hat{A}^{(i)}$ as $R\big(y^{(i)}\big) - b(x^{(i)})$ or per-step $\hat{A}^{(i)}_t = G_t^{(i)} - v_\phi(s_t^{(i)})$.

5. Compute policy gradient estimates by multiplying advantages with the corresponding score terms (the sum of per-step log-prob gradients or equivalently by backpropagating the scalar advantage times sum of log-probabilities). Average across the minibatch to form the gradient estimator.

6. Apply gradient ascent (or minimize negative objective via gradient descent) to update $\theta$.

A few practical tips:

- Reward normalization: When reward scales vary widely, normalizing advantages (e.g., subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation within a batch) can stabilize training and help choose learning rates.

- Entropy regularization: Adding an entropy bonus to the objective encourages exploration by preventing premature collapse to deterministic policies. The entropy term is differentiable and its gradient is computable from the policy distribution.

- Control variance with critics and bootstrapping: Instead of using full Monte Carlo returns, actor-critic methods fit a critic $v_\phi$ and use bootstrapped temporal-difference returns or GAE to trade bias for variance and accelerate learning.

- Use baseline functions that depend only on state/prefix: any baseline must not depend on the action sampled at the same decision step to preserve unbiasedness.

- Batch size and sample efficiency: Policy gradient methods are often sample-inefficient compared to value-based or off-policy methods. Increasing batch size (number of sampled trajectories per update) reduces variance in gradient estimates and allows larger learning rates.

# Summary

Policy gradient methods transform the problem of maximizing expected reward under a parameterized policy into an optimization problem solvable by stochastic gradient methods. The score-function identity, $\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[R] = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[R \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta]$, is the mathematical foundation that allows unbiased gradient estimation through Monte Carlo sampling. REINFORCE is the simplest estimator that applies this identity directly, using sampled trajectories and their rewards to form gradient estimates.

Because REINFORCE can have high variance, subtracting baselines (particularly learned value functions) yields substantial variance reduction while preserving unbiasedness. Per-step formulations of the gradient and advantage functions enable fine-grained baselines and give rise to actor-critic architectures. For autoregressive sequence models, $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ decomposes as a sum of per-token score gradients, allowing straightforward computation with automatic differentiation after sampling. Combining these tools and practical considerations (reward normalization, entropy regularization, efficient sampling) provides a robust framework for training stochastic policies to maximize expected reward.
