Introduction

In this chapter we frame language generation as a reinforcement learning (RL) problem. This viewpoint is central to modern approaches that adapt large pretrained language models (LMs) to satisfy task-specific objectives that may be hard to express as maximum-likelihood objectives, for example human preferences, safety constraints, or long-term coherence. Casting generation as RL brings a set of concepts and tools: Markov decision processes (MDPs) in an episodic setting, policies that are conditional language models, token-level actions that form trajectories, scalar reward assignment for entire sequences or at each step, return and discounting definitions, and algorithmic choices used in practice such as RL from Human Feedback (RLHF). We will present each piece formally and intuitively, with equations to make the connections precise so you can read and understand research that uses RL for sequence generation.

# MDP / episodic setup for generation tasks

Reinforcement learning problems are typically formalized as Markov decision processes (MDPs). In generation tasks we usually adopt an episodic MDP, meaning interactions happen in episodes that start from some initial state and end after a finite sequence of actions. For language generation, an episode corresponds to producing a response (or a continued sequence) conditioned on a given context or prompt.

Formally, an episodic MDP is a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma, \rho_0)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space, $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$ are transition dynamics, $r(s_t,a_t)$ is a reward function, $\gamma\in[0,1]$ is a discount factor, and $\rho_0$ is a distribution over initial states. For generation we typically fix the initial state to encode the given query or context $x$, so $\rho_0$ is concentrated on states corresponding to that context. The transition dynamics are deterministic or simple: applying an action (emitting a token) appends that token to the sequence and moves to the next state with the updated prefix. The episode terminates when a special end-of-sequence token is produced or a maximum length is reached.

Because episodes correspond to generating an entire sequence, rewards are often defined episodically: a scalar reward assigned after the episode ends that measures quality (e.g., human preference, task success, or safety). However, token-level rewards or intermediate shaping can also be used to help learning. The episodic view matches practical workflows where a model receives a prompt and must return a response; each such interaction is an episode over which we evaluate and optimize expected reward.

# Policy as a conditional language model

In the RL formulation for generation, the policy is a conditional language model. A policy $\pi_\theta$ parameterized by $\theta$ defines a distribution over the next token given the current prefix (and the fixed prompt). If we represent tokens as elements of a discrete vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, the action space is $\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{V}$, and the policy is
$$
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) = P_\theta(y_t = a_t \mid y_{<t}, x),
$$
where $x$ is the context or query, $y_{<t}$ is the sequence of tokens generated so far, and $s_t$ is a state that encodes $(x,y_{<t})$. This conditional probability is exactly what an autoregressive LM provides: a softmax over vocabulary logits produced by a neural network given the prefix and context.

Treating the LM as a policy enables the use of policy-gradient and other RL algorithms to directly optimize sequence-level objectives that may differ from the maximum likelihood training objective used during pretraining or supervised fine-tuning. It also makes clear how sampling strategies and temperature correspond to exploration strategies in RL: sampling with higher temperature increases stochasticity and thus exploration, whereas greedy decoding is nearly deterministic and reduces exploration.

Because LMs are trained with teacher forcing, where the training data provides the next token distribution, a common practical pipeline is to use a pretrained or supervised fine-tuned LM as a warm start policy and then adapt it via RL to maximize expected reward. Warm starts stabilize learning and encode desired linguistic priors.

# Trajectories: queries (contexts) and generated responses

A trajectory or episode in this setup consists of the fixed context/query and the sequence of actions (tokens) produced by the policy. Denote a prompt or context by $x$ and a generated token sequence by $y_{1:T}$. We can represent a trajectory as $\tau=(x,y_{1:T})$. Because $x$ does not change during the episode, it is often convenient to condition everything on $x$ and consider trajectories $\tau_x = y_{1:T}$. The probability of a generated sequence under policy $\pi_\theta$ is
$$
\pi_\theta(y_{1:T}\mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}).
$$
This factorization matches autoregressive generation.

The state at time $t$ can be explicitly represented as $s_t=(x,y_{<t})$ and the action as $a_t=y_t$. After the model samples $a_t$, the next state is $s_{t+1}=(x,y_{\le t})$. The episode ends when $y_T$ equals a special terminal token or when $T$ reaches a maximum allowable length. Trajectories are the basic objects that RL algorithms optimize over: we collect many trajectories (rollouts) from the current policy, evaluate their rewards, and use that data to update $\theta$ to increase expected reward.

It is important to note that in many applied setups the prompt distribution matters: prompts may come from user queries or a dataset of tasks. We therefore care about an expected reward averaged over a distribution of prompts $\mathcal{D}_x$:
$$
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R(x,y)\big].
$$
Optimization aims to increase this expected return across the population of prompts of interest.

# Reward design for sequences and scalar episodic rewards

Designing rewards for sequences is one of the most critical and delicate aspects of applying RL to language generation. Rewards can be scalar values assigned per episode, per token, or a combination. The simplest and common approach is to assign a scalar episodic reward $R(x,y_{1:T})$ when the episode terminates. This scalar might represent human preference, task success metrics (e.g., BLEU or ROUGE for translation or summarization), safety scores, or costs. Episodic rewards are attractive because they directly reflect end-to-end quality, but they also make credit assignment harder since the signal is sparse.

Per-token rewards are another option: define $r_t(s_t,a_t)$ for each generation step. Per-token rewards can encode aspects that are locally checkable (for example, whether a token is disallowed, matches style constraints, or repeats excessively), or they may be derived from a running evaluation of the partial sequence. Per-token rewards can speed learning by providing denser feedback, but care must be taken to avoid introducing biases that change the desired optima. For instance, shaping rewards to prefer shorter sequences could cause the model to truncate responses unnecessarily.

Reward shaping is the practice of modifying the reward function to make learning easier while preserving the optimal policy, but in practice shaping can change the optimization landscape and produce different behaviors. A common compromise is to use an episodic scalar reward for final evaluation while adding auxiliary cheap-to-compute per-token penalties for obvious pathologies (e.g., explicit penalties for using disallowed tokens or for violating hard constraints). When combining rewards, practitioners often normalize or scale them to keep learning stable.

In RLHF-style pipelines, the scalar episodic reward typically comes from a learned reward model trained to predict human preferences. This reward model approximates human judgment and provides scalar scores for complete sequences so the policy can be optimized with RL algorithms.

# Return, discounting (if used), and terminal rewards

The return of a trajectory is the cumulative discounted sum of rewards encountered in an episode. For a trajectory $\tau = (s_1,a_1,s_2,a_2,\dots,s_T,a_T)$ with rewards $r_t$, the return from time step $t$ is
$$
G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} r_k,
$$
and the episodic return (starting at $t=1$) is
$$
G_1 = \sum_{t=1}^T \gamma^{t-1} r_t,
$$
where $\gamma\in[0,1]$ is the discount factor. In many language generation applications $\gamma$ is set to $1$, meaning that rewards are not discounted and the objective is the undiscounted sum over the episode. This makes sense when episodes are relatively short and every part of the generated sequence matters equally for the final quality. Moreover, with terminal-only rewards it is equivalent to assigning the scalar reward $R$ only at the final time step and zero elsewhere; then $G_1=R$ when $\gamma=1$.

Discounting is more common in infinite-horizon or continuing tasks where present rewards should be preferred, or when one wants to bias earlier steps more strongly. In generation, discounting could be used to prefer shorter responses or to prioritize earlier tokens, but it is less common because we usually want to evaluate the whole sequence.

Terminal rewards are a natural formulation when the system receives a score only after completion (e.g., human preference of the entire response). In that case, $r_t=0$ for $t<T$ and $r_T=R(x,y_{1:T})$. The return is then simply $G_1=\gamma^{T-1}R(x,y_{1:T})$; with $\gamma=1$ we have $G_1=R(x,y_{1:T})$.

When training with policy-gradient methods, the return provides the learning signal used to weight log-probability gradients. Because sequence-level returns can be high variance, variance reduction techniques such as baselines, advantage estimation, and reward normalization are commonly employed to stabilize learning.

# RLHF overview: preference models, human feedback, and verifier scorers

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a practical framework that uses human judgments to train a reward model, which then guides RL-based policy optimization. The RLHF pipeline typically has three stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward model training from comparisons, and policy optimization (often with a KL penalty to avoid deviating too far from the SFT policy).

In the reward model stage humans are shown pairs (or triplets) of responses to the same prompt and asked which response they prefer. Each comparison yields a human label indicating preference. A reward model parameterized by $\phi$ is trained to predict these binary or ordinal preferences. A common training objective uses a Bradley–Terry or pairwise logistic likelihood: given responses $y^{(1)}$ and $y^{(2)}$ with human preference indicating $y^{(1)}$ preferred to $y^{(2)}$, the reward model produces scalar scores $r_\phi(x,y^{(i)})$. The likelihood is
$$
P_\phi(y^{(1)} \succ y^{(2)} \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r_\phi(x,y^{(1)}))}{\exp(r_\phi(x,y^{(1)})) + \exp(r_\phi(x,y^{(2)}))},
$$
and $\phi$ is trained to maximize the likelihood of human choices across the dataset. After training, $r_\phi$ serves as a proxy for human judgment and is used as the reward function in RL.

Human feedback can take other forms: scalar ratings, free-form critiques, or binary safety labels. Preference comparisons are popular because they are relatively easy for humans and reduce calibration issues present in scalar ratings.

Verifier scorers are automated models that assess specific properties of outputs, such as factuality, toxicity, or adherence to safety policies. These can complement human feedback and be combined into composite rewards. In some pipelines verifiers produce auxiliary signals during data collection to filter or prioritize examples; in others they provide reward shaping or constraints during RL. Care is needed because automated scorers can be gamed or misaligned with human preferences; hence mixing human feedback and automated verifiers is common.

# Batch rollouts and dataset of query–response–reward tuples

Policy optimization via gradient-based RL typically requires many rollout trajectories to estimate gradients. In practice, training proceeds by repeatedly sampling a batch of prompts from the prompt distribution, generating responses from the current policy (rollouts), evaluating rewards for these responses (via human labeling, reward model inference, or automatic metrics), and using the collected data to update the policy.

A batch rollout yields a dataset of tuples $\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}, R^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x^{(i)}$ is the prompt, $y^{(i)}$ is the generated response, and $R^{(i)}$ is the reward (scalar) assigned to that response. For token-level learning algorithms or for advantage estimation, we may also store per-token action probabilities $\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and intermediate values such as log-probabilities or value function estimates. The dataset is then used in a policy gradient update or a proximal policy optimization (PPO) step to increase expected reward.

Because human labeling is expensive, RLHF pipelines often use a mix of human-labeled comparisons and reward-model-labeled large-scale rollouts. Typically a smaller dataset of human comparisons is collected and used to train the reward model; then that reward model is used to score many rollouts cheaply and provide dense training data for the RL stage. Periodically, additional human labels may be collected to improve or recalibrate the reward model.

Batch rollouts also enable off-policy learning and replay buffers, but off-policy correction is nontrivial for autoregressive policies with high-dimensional discrete action spaces. Many practical systems therefore employ on-policy or near on-policy algorithms such as PPO, often combined with trust-region or KL penalties that constrain the new policy to remain close to the reference policy from which human labels were collected. This reduces distributional shift and helps the reward model remain predictive.

When storing a dataset of query–response–reward tuples, practitioners track provenance (which policy produced the response and what version of the reward model scored it), because mixing data from different policies or reward models without care can bias training. For evaluation and debugging, it is useful to keep a held-out validation set of prompts and human labels to measure true policy improvements over time.

Conclusion

Viewing sequence generation as an episodic RL problem provides a clear, formal framework tying together conditional language models, rollout trajectories, reward specification, and policy optimization. Key practical considerations are how to design or learn reward functions that reflect human judgment, how to provide dense enough signal for learning without altering desired behavior, and how to manage data collection and batch rollouts to enable stable optimization. RLHF operationalizes these concepts by using human preference data to learn reward models and then optimizing policies with RL algorithms constrained to remain close to a base language model. Understanding these elements—MDP structure, policies as LMs, trajectories, reward construction, returns, and the RLHF pipeline—gives you the tools to read and critically evaluate contemporary research that adapts language models via reinforcement learning.
