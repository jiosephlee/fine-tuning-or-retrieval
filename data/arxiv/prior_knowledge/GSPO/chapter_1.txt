Introduction

Autoregressive language models form the foundation of most modern generative natural language processing systems. These models express the probability of an output sequence given some input context by predicting one token at a time, conditioning each prediction on the input plus all previously generated tokens. This factorization is both conceptually simple and practically powerful: it turns the complex task of modeling a whole sentence or paragraph into a sequence of simpler next-token prediction problems. In this chapter we develop the probabilistic formulation of autoregressive models, explain how tokenization and vocabulary choices affect modeling, examine how models produce probabilities via logits and the softmax function, and show how one computes sequence log-likelihoods from per-token conditionals. We then study how sequence length influences likelihoods and why practitioners apply length normalization, review common sampling and decoding strategies (greedy decoding, temperature scaling, top-\(k\), and nucleus/top-\(p\)), and discuss evaluation metrics such as perplexity. Finally, we clarify practical differences between training and inference engines, including numerical precision issues that matter in deployed systems.

# Autoregressive factorization: \(p(y \mid x) = \prod_t p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\)

The central probabilistic idea behind autoregressive language models is a chain rule factorization of the conditional probability of a sequence. Suppose we have an input context \(x\) (this might be a prompt, previous conversation history, or any other conditioning data) and an output token sequence \(y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)\). By the chain rule of probability, the conditional probability of the whole sequence can be written as a product of conditional probabilities for each token given the input and the tokens that came before it:
\[
p(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^T p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]
where \(y_{<t}\) denotes the subsequence \((y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})\).

This factorization is exact and does not constitute an approximation; it simply rewrites the joint probability via the chain rule. What makes autoregressive models special is how they parameterize each factor \(p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\). A model (for example a Transformer) maps the context \(x\) and the prefix \(y_{<t}\) to a distribution over the next token. In training, the model learns parameters that (ideally) make these conditional distributions match the empirical next-token distributions in the training data. In generation, we sample or otherwise select tokens sequentially from these learned conditional distributions to construct a complete output sequence.

This sequential factorization has several important implications. First, the model is causal: at step \(t\) the distribution depends only on past tokens, not on future ones. This causality aligns with how generation proceeds token by token. Second, the factorization allows efficient computation: when generating many tokens, the model reuses computation from previous steps (caching activations) to avoid recomputing representations for the prefix from scratch. Third, errors can propagate: an unlikely sampled token at an early step can lead the model into contexts it rarely saw during training, making later predictions poorer. Understanding this sequential dependence is essential for designing decoding strategies that mitigate such error propagation.

# Tokenization and vocabulary considerations

Before a model can predict tokens, raw text must be converted into a discrete sequence of symbols drawn from a finite vocabulary. Tokenization is the procedure that maps text to tokens. Different tokenization schemes yield different granularities: characters, bytes, words, subwords, or byte-pair encoded (BPE) units are common choices. Modern large language models usually use subword tokenization (like BPE or unigram subword models) applied to UTF-8 byte sequences. Subword tokens strike a balance between vocabulary size and the ability to represent arbitrary strings: they allow an open vocabulary (any word can be decomposed into tokens) while keeping the number of distinct token types manageable.

The vocabulary \(V\) is the set of discrete token types the model outputs. The model outputs a probability distribution over \(V\) at each step. Vocabulary size matters: a larger vocabulary can capture more whole words or common phrases as single tokens, reducing sequence length and sometimes improving fluency. But larger vocabularies increase the output softmax dimensionality, which raises computational cost and memory requirements. Conversely, very small vocabularies (e.g., byte-level models) guarantee that any text can be encoded but increase sequence lengths and may require the model to learn longer-range dependencies.

Tokenization design choices also affect modeling biases. If tokens align poorly with linguistic units (for instance, splitting frequent words into many subword parts), the model's conditional distributions will reflect those artifacts. Tokenization also interacts with special tokens: tokens representing end-of-sequence, padding, or structured delimiters must be in the vocabulary and handled consistently. For conditional generation (when \(x\) and \(y\) are concatenated in one sequence for the model), one often inserts special separator tokens so the model can distinguish prompt from response.

In training and evaluation, consistent tokenization is crucial. Evaluating likelihoods or perplexity requires using the exact same tokenizer and vocabulary as the model. When comparing systems, note that tokenization choices change the token counts and therefore affect per-token statistics; fair comparison often demands computing metrics normalized appropriately for tokenization differences.

# Softmax output, logits, and log-probabilities

At each time step \(t\), the model produces a real-valued vector of scores called logits. If the vocabulary has size \(|V|\), the model computes logits \(z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}\). Each coordinate \(z_{t,i}\) is an unnormalized score for token \(i \in V\). To convert logits into a probability distribution over tokens, we apply the softmax function:
\[
p(y_t = i \mid x, y_{<t}) = \frac{\exp(z_{t,i})}{\sum_{j \in V} \exp(z_{t,j})}.
\]
The softmax ensures the probabilities are nonnegative and sum to one. Numerically, implementations often subtract the maximum logit before exponentiating to avoid overflow, but mathematically the formula above is the same.

Working in log-probability space is often preferable for numerical stability and for optimization. The log-probability of token \(i\) is
\[
\log p(y_t = i \mid x, y_{<t}) = z_{t,i} - \log \sum_{j \in V} \exp(z_{t,j}).
\]
The term \(\log \sum_j \exp(z_{t,j})\) is known as the log-partition function or log-sum-exp; it normalizes logits into log-probabilities.

Intuitively, logits represent the model's raw confidence about different tokens before enforcing normalization. Differences in logits matter more than absolute values: adding the same constant to every coordinate of \(z_t\) does not change the resulting probabilities. In training with cross-entropy loss, the gradient depends on the difference between the one-hot target indicator and the softmax probabilities, which encourages increasing the logit of the correct token and decreasing others.

The softmax outputs are used directly in two roles. During training, they are used to compute the cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood) loss against the next-token target. During generation, they provide the categorical distribution from which one samples or from which one selects the argmax token for greedy decoding. Understanding the relationship between logits, softmax, and log-probabilities is central to interpreting model outputs and to implementing decoding strategies that modify the logits or probabilities.

# Sequence log-likelihood and per-token log-probabilities

Given the autoregressive factorization, the log-probability of a full sequence \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)\) conditional on \(x\) is the sum of token-wise log-probabilities:
\[
\log p(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
Each term \(\log p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\) is computed from logits via the log-softmax formula described earlier. This additive form is important because it allows efficient computation (we can accumulate log-probabilities as we step through tokens) and interpretable per-token contributions to the total score.

In practice, when computing the likelihood of a reference sequence during evaluation or training, one feeds the full sequence into the model (possibly with appropriate shifting) and obtains all conditional distributions in parallel, then sums the log-probabilities of the actual next tokens. This is how the training objective of maximum likelihood (or minimizing cross-entropy) is implemented: the model parameters are adjusted to maximize \(\log p(y \mid x)\) across training examples.

Per-token log-probabilities also provide diagnostic insights. If certain tokens have low log-probabilities given their contexts, that indicates areas where the model is uncertain or underfitting. Aggregating per-token log-probabilities across a dataset yields metrics like average negative log-likelihood and perplexity, which we discuss in a later section.

When reporting sequence-level likelihoods, it is common to express them either as total log-likelihood \(\log p(y \mid x)\) or normalized per token by dividing by \(T\):
\[
\frac{1}{T} \log p(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \log p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
The per-token average makes comparisons fairer across sequences of different lengths, but it introduces its own considerations (see the length effects section). Because probabilities are typically very small for long sequences, working with log-probabilities is essential to avoid underflow.

# Length effects and length normalization of likelihoods

Sequence log-likelihoods naturally scale with sequence length because they are sums of per-token log-probabilities. Longer sequences tend to have more negative log-likelihoods simply because there are more terms in the sum. This scaling makes raw sequence likelihoods unsuitable for comparing candidates of different lengths: a longer sequence might be preferred by a model if its average per-token log-probability is higher even though its total log-likelihood is lower, or vice versa.

Two common approaches address this issue. The first is per-token normalization: comparing sequences by average log-probability per token,
\[
\frac{1}{T} \log p(y \mid x).
\]
Average log-probability penalizes sequences for having low probability per token and provides a length-normalized score that is more comparable across different lengths.

The second approach is length normalization with a tunable exponent or additive correction. When searching for the highest-likelihood sequence via beam search, practitioners often use a length normalization factor such as
\[
\text{score}(y) = \frac{\log p(y \mid x)}{T^\alpha}
\]
or
\[
\text{score}(y) = \frac{\log p(y \mid x)}{(5 + T)^\alpha}
\]
for some \(\alpha \in [0,1]\). These heuristics were introduced because absolute per-token normalization can sometimes favor short, high-probability trivial outputs (like repeating a common token), while no normalization can favor overly long outputs. The addition of a constant prevents overly severe penalty for short sequences. The exponent \(\alpha\) is tuned based on downstream performance or human preference.

Length biases can also arise implicitly from the decoding method. Greedy argmax decoding and beam search with no normalization tend to favor shorter sequences because they accumulate fewer negative log-probabilities. Conversely, sampling strategies can generate longer sequences because stochasticity can avoid premature termination. Because of these interactions, decoding hyperparameters and normalization choices need to be selected with the target application and evaluation metric in mind.

Finally, note that when evaluating models with perplexity (see below), the standard definition uses per-token average negative log-likelihood, which naturally handles length differences across examples by averaging over tokens.

# Sampling/decoding methods: greedy, temperature, top-\(k\), nucleus (top-\(p\))

Once the model defines the distribution \(p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\) at each step, we must choose how to convert that distribution into a concrete token for generation. Different decoding strategies trade off between determinism, diversity, and quality.

Greedy decoding always selects the token with the highest probability:
\[
y_t = \arg\max_i p(y_t = i \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
Greedy decoding is simple and deterministic, but it can lead to outputs that are bland or stuck in loops because it ignores alternative tokens that could produce better global sequences. Greedy decoding often underperforms probabilistic sampling on creativity and sometimes on quality because maximizing locally at each step does not guarantee globally optimal sequences.

Temperature scaling is a simple modification of the categorical distribution prior to sampling. If the model produces logits \(z_{t,i}\), temperature \(T > 0\) rescales logits to \(z_{t,i}' = z_{t,i} / T\) before applying softmax. When \(T = 1\) nothing changes. When \(T < 1\), the distribution becomes sharper (more peaked), making high-probability tokens even more likely; when \(T > 1\), the distribution becomes flatter, increasing diversity. Temperature therefore controls randomness in sampling in a smooth, interpretable way.

Top-\(k\) sampling restricts the distribution to the \(k\) tokens with highest probability and renormalizes them. Concretely, we find the set \(S_k\) of tokens corresponding to the top \(k\) probabilities and sample from the renormalized distribution:
\[
p'(i) = \begin{cases}
\frac{p(i)}{\sum_{j \in S_k} p(j)} & \text{if } i \in S_k,\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Top-\(k\) prevents the sampler from selecting any token outside the top few, which reduces the chance of sampling very unlikely (and often nonsensical) tokens. Small \(k\) yields deterministic-like behavior; large \(k\) approaches full sampling.

Nucleus (top-\(p\)) sampling instead selects the smallest set \(S_p\) of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds \(p\) (e.g., \(p=0.9\)), and samples from the renormalized distribution over \(S_p\). Nucleus sampling adapts the size of the candidate set to the shape of the distribution: for peaky distributions the nucleus will be small; for flat distributions it will be larger. This adaptivity often yields better-quality and more diverse outputs than fixed top-\(k\).

Beam search is another widely used method when one seeks higher-probability sequences deterministically. Beam search keeps \(B\) partial hypotheses (the beam) at each step, expands each by all possible next tokens (or a truncated set), and retains the top \(B\) resulting prefixes according to a scoring function (often log-likelihood possibly with length normalization). Beam search approximates finding a high-probability sequence under the autoregressive model but can still fail to find the global optimum because it prunes the search space. Beam search is commonly used in machine translation and structured prediction tasks.

Each decoding method implies different tradeoffs. Sampling with temperature and nucleus/top-\(p\) tends to produce diverse and fluent text, favored for open-ended generation. Greedy or beam search often produce higher-probability and more focused outputs, useful for tasks that require accuracy and determinism. In practice, practitioners tune sampling parameters (temperature, \(k\), \(p\), beam width, length normalization) for the application, sometimes using human evaluation to select the best setting.

# Perplexity and likelihood-based evaluation

Perplexity is a standard evaluation metric for probabilistic language models that summarizes how well a model assigns probability mass to a dataset of reference text. For a dataset composed of sequences \(y^{(n)}\) with lengths \(T_n\), the average cross-entropy per token is
\[
\text{CE} = -\frac{1}{\sum_n T_n} \sum_n \sum_{t=1}^{T_n} \log p(y^{(n)}_t \mid x^{(n)}, y^{(n)}_{<t}).
\]
Perplexity (PPL) is the exponentiation of the cross-entropy:
\[
\text{PPL} = \exp(\text{CE}) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sum_n T_n} \sum_n \sum_{t=1}^{T_n} \log p(y^{(n)}_t \mid x^{(n)}, y^{(n)}_{<t})\right).
\]
Intuitively, perplexity can be interpreted as the effective branching factor of the model: a perplexity of \(k\) means that, on average, the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among \(k\) options at each token. Lower perplexity indicates a better fit to the data.

Perplexity has appealing properties: it is directly derived from the model likelihood and is related to the cross-entropy loss minimized during training. It provides a unified scalar metric that is comparable across models when computed on the same tokenization and dataset. However, perplexity has limitations. It evaluates only how well the model predicts the held-out reference text under the same tokenization and cannot directly quantify aspects like factuality, coherence over long spans, or human preference. Moreover, perplexity improvements do not always translate to better downstream task performance or improved perceived quality in generation.

When computing perplexity in conditional settings (e.g., language modeling with prompts or encoder-decoder models), one conditions on the input \(x\) and measures the cross-entropy over the output tokens only. It is crucial to ensure that training and evaluation use the same tokenization, special tokens, and handling of sequence boundaries. Because perplexity is exponential in cross-entropy, small differences in average negative log-likelihood can correspond to substantial changes in perplexity.

Likelihood-based metrics other than perplexity include average negative log-likelihood, bits-per-byte (for byte-level models), and calibration measures comparing predicted probabilities to empirical frequencies. For some tasks, ranking metrics derived from log-probabilities (e.g., using log-likelihood to score candidate answers) are more informative than perplexity.

# Inference vs training engines and precision differences

Training and inference often run on different software stacks and use different numerical precisions. During training, models are commonly executed in mixed precision: forward passes might use float32 or float16, while gradients and certain accumulators may be kept in higher precision to stabilize learning. Training frameworks typically prioritize numerical stability and reproducibility of gradient-based updates. They also perform backpropagation, requiring storage of intermediate activations, gradient computation, and potentially dynamic computation graphs.

Inference engines, especially those deployed for low-latency or large-scale serving, often emphasize throughput and memory efficiency. To reduce memory and accelerate matrix operations, inference implementations commonly use reduced-precision formats such as float16, bfloat16, or even quantized integer formats (e.g., int8). Quantization maps floating-point weights and activations into lower-precision representations using scaling factors and possibly per-channel calibrations. While carefully designed quantization preserves model quality, it can introduce small shifts in logits and probabilities. These shifts can affect exact log-probability values, ranking of next-token probabilities, and the outcomes of sampling procedures, particularly when the distribution is sharp or when tokens have very close logits.

Another practical difference is that training frequently uses batching and parallelization patterns optimized for throughput over many examples, while inference engines may focus on single-sequence latency and incremental caching of transformer key/value states. During generation, inference implementations cache internal states to avoid recomputing expensive attention over the entire prefix. This caching behavior requires careful engineering to ensure the same probabilistic behavior as batched full-sequence evaluation.

Precision differences matter for likelihood computations and for reproducibility of generation. If you compute a log-likelihood for a sequence using a high-precision training engine, and then generate tokens step-by-step using a quantized inference engine, you may observe small differences in per-token log-probabilities. For many applications these discrepancies are negligible, but in settings that rely on exact likelihood comparisons (e.g., reranking among candidate explanations or scoring for calibration), you should be aware of potential mismatches.

When extreme efficiency is required, some systems use approximate kernels or lower-precision arithmetic optimized for GPUs, TPUs, or custom accelerators. These can further change behavior slightly. Best practices include validating that quantized or reduced-precision inference preserves metrics of interest on a representative validation set, and, when needed, performing critical scoring operations on the higher-precision engine.

Conclusion

Autoregressive language models provide a principled probabilistic framework for sequential text generation. The chain rule factorization reduces sequence modeling to next-token prediction, and tokenization choices determine the discrete building blocks of generation. Logits and the softmax map model outputs to probabilities, from which sequence log-likelihoods are composed by summation of per-token log-probabilities. Because log-likelihoods scale with length, length normalization or per-token averaging is commonly used for fair comparison. Decoding methods—greedy, temperature-controlled sampling, top-\(k\), nucleus/top-\(p\), and beam search—offer different tradeoffs between determinism and diversity and are chosen based on application needs. Perplexity summarizes likelihood-based fit but should be used alongside other task-specific measures. Finally, practical differences between training and inference engines, especially numerical precision and quantization, can affect probabilities and generation behavior; careful validation ensures deployed systems behave as intended. Together, these concepts equip you to read and understand empirical papers that report likelihoods, perplexities, and generation outcomes, and to implement and evaluate autoregressive models in practice.
