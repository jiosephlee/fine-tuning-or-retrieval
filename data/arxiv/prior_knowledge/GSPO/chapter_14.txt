Introduction

This chapter collects and explains mathematical tools and identities that are repeatedly used when analyzing reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms and likelihood-based objectives. Much of the formal analysis in RL—bias and variance calculations, concentration bounds, sensitivity of policies to parameter changes, and approximations used to build intuition—relies on a handful of algebraic manipulations, inequalities, small-perturbation expansions, and probabilistic identities. The goal of this chapter is not to be a complete probability textbook, but to give a compact, practical set of tools that you will repeatedly use to read and understand research papers in RL and likelihood-based inference. Each section explains the intuition, states the key formulas, and shows worked examples that are directly relevant to policy gradients, importance sampling, and variance approximation.

# Logarithms and exponentials: properties and manipulations

Logarithms and exponentials appear constantly in likelihoods, softmax policies, and log-likelihood gradients. The most useful properties are those that convert products into sums, ratios into differences, and exponentiations into scalings. If $a,b>0$, then
- $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$,
- $\log(a/b)=\log a-\log b$,
- $\log(a^c)=c\log a$ for real $c$,
- and $\exp(\log a)=a$.

These identities let us manipulate likelihood ratios and policy ratios. For example, for two policies $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta'}$, the log ratio
\[
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta'}(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)} = \log\pi_{\theta'}(a\mid s)-\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)
\]
is a central object in importance sampling, trust-region methods, and KL-regularized objectives.

A very common and useful approximation uses a first-order expansion of the log around a parameter $\theta$:
\[
\log\pi_{\theta+\Delta}(a\mid s) \approx \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) + \Delta^\top \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s),
\]
valid when $\Delta$ is small. Exponentiating that gives the familiar linearized ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta+\Delta}(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)} \approx \exp\!\big(\Delta^\top \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big).
\]
For small $\Delta$, $\exp(z)\approx 1+z+\tfrac{1}{2}z^2+\cdots$, so another useful format is
\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta+\Delta}(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)} \approx 1 + \Delta^\top \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\Delta^\top \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big)^2 + \cdots.
\]
This is used to get first- and second-order approximations to expectation changes under small parameter updates.

Another extremely useful identity is the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability and analytic manipulation. For numbers $x_i$,
\[
\log\sum_i \exp(x_i) = m + \log\sum_i \exp(x_i-m),
\]
where $m=\max_i x_i$. In probabilistic derivations this often appears when computing normalizing constants or partition functions, and it is algebraically useful because differentiating a log-sum-exp gives a softmax:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \log\sum_i \exp(x_i) = \frac{\exp(x_j)}{\sum_i \exp(x_i)}.
\]

As a quick practical example, consider a softmax policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\frac{\exp(f_\theta(s,a))}{\sum_{a'}\exp(f_\theta(s,a'))}$. Then $\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=f_\theta(s,a)-\log\sum_{a'}\exp(f_\theta(s,a'))$, and
\[
\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) = \nabla_\theta f_\theta(s,a) - \sum_{a'} \frac{\exp(f_\theta(s,a'))}{\sum_{a''}\exp(f_\theta(s,a''))}\nabla_\theta f_\theta(s,a') = \nabla_\theta f_\theta(s,a) - \mathbb{E}_{a'\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}[\nabla_\theta f_\theta(s,a')],
\]
which is the standard score-function gradient form.

# Taylor expansions for small perturbations

Taylor expansions are the formal tool for approximating a function around a point when the perturbation is small. For a scalar-valued smooth function $g(\theta)$ and a small vector perturbation $\Delta$, the multivariate Taylor expansion up to second order around $\theta$ is
\[
g(\theta+\Delta) = g(\theta) + \nabla g(\theta)^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_g(\theta)\Delta + R_3(\Delta),
\]
where $H_g(\theta)$ is the Hessian and $R_3(\Delta)$ denotes the remainder of order $\|\Delta\|^3$ typically bounded by constants involving third derivatives. For vector-valued functions the expansion applies componentwise.

In RL and likelihood analysis we typically use first- and second-order truncations. The first-order (linear) approximation,
\[
g(\theta+\Delta) \approx g(\theta) + \nabla g(\theta)^\top \Delta,
\]
is used to understand directional sensitivity and to obtain approximations for small updates. The second-order term provides curvature information and is used to justify natural gradient and trust-region methods:
\[
g(\theta+\Delta) \approx g(\theta) + \nabla g(\theta)^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_g(\theta)\Delta.
\]

A particularly useful special case is the Taylor expansion of the log-likelihood (or log-density) and its exponentiation. If $\ell(\theta)=\log p_\theta(x)$ then
\[
\ell(\theta+\Delta) \approx \ell(\theta) + \Delta^\top \nabla \ell(\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_\ell(\theta)\Delta.
\]
Exponentiating and keeping terms up to second order in $\Delta$ gives
\[
\frac{p_{\theta+\Delta}(x)}{p_\theta(x)} \approx \exp\!\big(\Delta^\top \nabla \ell(\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_\ell(\theta)\Delta\big) \approx 1 + \Delta^\top \nabla \ell(\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\Delta^\top \nabla \ell(\theta)\big)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_\ell(\theta)\Delta.
\]
These expansions are the basis for many variance calculations (via the delta method) and for deriving second-order optimization steps.

It is crucial to remember the domain of validity: Taylor approximations are local approximations; higher-order remainder terms can be significant when perturbations are not small. When using Taylor expansions in probabilistic bounds, one typically couples the expansion with concentration bounds or bounds on derivatives to control the remainder.

# Jensen's and other inequalities used in expectations

Inequalities let us bound expectations, variances, and tail probabilities. Jensen's inequality is central: for a convex function $\phi$ and a random variable $X$ with finite expectation,
\[
\phi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)].
\]
For concave $\phi$ the inequality direction reverses. Jensen's inequality is used, for example, to show that the log of an expectation lower-bounds the expectation of a log:
\[
\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X],
\]
when $X>0$ and $\log$ is concave. That inequality underlies variational bounds such as evidence lower bounds.

Cauchy–Schwarz is indispensable when dealing with expectations of products:
\[
\mathbb{E}[XY] \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}.
\]
This is the main tool to convert an expectation of a product into a product of norms. It is frequently used to control covariances and to bound bias or error terms that factor into two parts.

Markov's and Chebyshev's inequalities give simple tail bounds. Markov's inequality: for nonnegative $X$ and $a>0$,
\[
\mathbb{P}(X\ge a) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.
\]
Chebyshev refines this using variance: for any $\epsilon>0$,
\[
\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\epsilon^2}.
\]
For sharper exponential tails under boundedness or sub-Gaussian assumptions, Hoeffding's and Bernstein's inequalities are used. For independent bounded zero-mean variables $X_i\in[a_i,b_i]$, Hoeffding gives
\[
\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \ge \epsilon\Big) \le \exp\!\Big(-\frac{2n^2\epsilon^2}{\sum_i (b_i-a_i)^2}\Big).
\]
Bernstein includes variance in the bound and is tighter when variance is small.

Pinsker's inequality relates total variation and KL divergence. For distributions $P$ and $Q$,
\[
\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \le \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)}.
\]
This is often used to convert KL closeness (which appears in policy constraints) into action probability closeness.

An example combining Jensen and Cauchy–Schwarz: to bound the expected absolute change of a log-likelihood under a small parameter perturbation, we might write
\[
\mathbb{E}\big|\log\frac{p_{\theta+\Delta}(X)}{p_\theta(X)}\big| \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big(\log\frac{p_{\theta+\Delta}(X)}{p_\theta(X)}\big)^2}.
\]
Then use a Taylor expansion for the log ratio and bound the second moment via Cauchy–Schwarz and bounds on gradients/Hessians.

# Transformations between sums and expectations

Many derivations convert finite sums (empirical averages) into expectations and back again. For i.i.d. samples $x_1,\dots,x_n$ drawn from distribution $P$, the empirical average
\[
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_n[f] := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)
\]
is an unbiased estimate of the population expectation $\mathbb{E}_P[f]$. When data is generated by a trajectory distribution under a policy $\pi$, sums along trajectories can be written as expectations under the trajectory distribution. For example, for an episodic MDP with trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots)$ distributed according to $p_\pi(\tau)$, the expected return is
\[
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\Big].
\]
In practice we estimate $J(\pi)$ using sample trajectories and convert estimators written as sums into expectation formulas to analyze bias and variance.

Importance sampling is the key transformation when you have samples from one distribution but need expectations under another. For any integrable function $f$ and distributions $P$ and $Q$ with $P\ll Q$,
\[
\mathbb{E}_P[f(X)] = \mathbb{E}_Q\Big[f(X)\frac{dP}{dQ}(X)\Big].
\]
In discrete-action policies this becomes
\[
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'}[f(a)] = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\Big[f(a)\frac{\pi'(a)}{\pi(a)}\Big].
\]
This identity allows off-policy evaluation by weighting returns by likelihood ratios. The variance of importance sampling estimators depends on the variability of the likelihood ratios; bounding or controlling these ratios is a critical step in analysis.

An especially convenient notation is to switch between sums over data points and expectations by writing sums as sample expectations. For example, if we have trajectories $\tau_i$ for $i=1,\dots,n$, then
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(\tau_i) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_n[g(\tau)].
\]
Expectations can be split using linearity: $\mathbb{E}[X+Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\mathbb{E}[cX]=c\mathbb{E}[X]$. This linearity is frequently used to move sums inside expectations or vice versa, an essential manipulation for decomposing losses and variances.

# Back-of-the-envelope variance approximations

Full variance computations can be messy; often we need quick approximations to gauge scaling with sample size, dimension, or importance weight variability. A few rules of thumb and identities make approximate variance calculations easy and informative.

First, the variance of a sum of independent (or uncorrelated) zero-mean terms adds:
\[
\mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
\]
If $X_i$ are independent, the covariances vanish and $\mathrm{Var}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$ when variances are identical. Thus sample averages converge at rate $1/n$ in variance, and $1/\sqrt{n}$ in standard deviation.

For importance sampling estimators, consider estimating $\mu=\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X)]$ with samples from $Q$, using weights $w(x)=\frac{dP}{dQ}(x)$. The (unbiased) importance sampling estimator is $\hat\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)w(x_i)$ with $x_i\sim Q$. Then
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu) = \frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}_Q(f(X)w(X)) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_Q[f^2w^2]-\mu^2\right).
\]
A back-of-the-envelope approximation is to assume $f$ and $w$ are independent or that $\mathbb{E}_Q[f^2w^2]\approx \mathbb{E}_Q[f^2]\mathbb{E}_Q[w^2]$. This gives
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)\approx \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_Q[f^2]\mathbb{E}_Q[w^2].
\]
The key takeaway is that $\mathbb{E}_Q[w^2]$ (the second moment of weights) controls variance inflation. When $\mathbb{E}_Q[w^2]$ is large (e.g., due to heavy tails of the likelihood ratio), the estimator variance can be enormous.

The delta method gives approximate variances for smooth transformations. If $\hat\theta$ is an estimator with asymptotic variance $\Sigma/n$ and $g$ is differentiable at the true $\theta$, then
\[
\mathrm{Var}(g(\hat\theta)) \approx \frac{1}{n} \nabla g(\theta)^\top \Sigma \nabla g(\theta).
\]
This is often used to get the approximate variance of the log-likelihood ratio or of a transformed parameter.

When terms are correlated, a useful heuristic is to treat effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ instead of $n$. For instance, correlated MCMC samples or temporally correlated trajectory rewards often mean $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)\approx \sigma^2/n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ where $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is reduced by the autocorrelation structure.

Finally, dimensional scaling: for gradients in $d$ dimensions, if each component has variance $\sigma^2$ and components are roughly independent, the squared norm has expected value about $d\sigma^2$. Thus gradient magnitudes often grow with $\sqrt{d}$ or $d$ depending on whether you look at norm or squared norm. This influences step size choices and preconditioning.

# Interpreting gradients and sensitivity via linearization

Gradients quantify the first-order sensitivity of an objective to parameter changes. Using linearization, we can interpret how an infinitesimal parameter change affects an expectation or a loss. For an objective $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]$, the gradient can often be written using the score function identity:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[f(x)\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\big].
\]
This expresses the sensitivity of the expectation as an expectation of the function weighted by how the log-density changes. Intuitively, outcomes $x$ for which increasing $\theta$ increases $p_\theta(x)$ and have large $f(x)$ contribute positively to the gradient.

Linearization of $J(\theta)$ around $\theta$ gives
\[
J(\theta+\Delta)\approx J(\theta) + \nabla_\theta J(\theta)^\top \Delta.
\]
So the inner product $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)^\top \Delta$ is the directional derivative that tells how much the objective will change for small steps in direction $\Delta$. This perspective is central in optimization: choose $\Delta$ to maximize the linearized gain subject to constraints (e.g., trust-region or norm constraints). If we add second-order information, $\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H_J(\theta)\Delta$ quantifies curvature and indicates whether the first-order ascent direction is reliable for finite steps.

The Fisher information matrix connects to gradient sensitivity for log-likelihood objectives. For likelihood $\ell(\theta)=\log p_\theta(x)$, the Fisher information is
\[
I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta)\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta)^\top\big],
\]
which is the covariance of the score. For small parameter changes, the Kullback–Leibler divergence between $p_{\theta}$ and $p_{\theta+\Delta}$ satisfies
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta+\Delta}\|p_\theta) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top I(\theta)\Delta.
\]
Thus $I(\theta)$ measures (to second order) how quickly the distribution shifts in parameter space and provides a local metric. Natural gradient methods premultiply the ordinary gradient by $I(\theta)^{-1}$ to scale updates in a way that respects this local sensitivity.

Another useful linearization interprets the variance of the score: under $p_\theta$,
\[
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta)]=0,\quad \mathrm{Var}(\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta)) = I(\theta).
\]
So the score is a zero-mean random vector; its covariance determines the stochasticity in gradient estimates. Using the delta method and linearization, one can approximate the variance of any function of $\theta$ that arises from sampling noise in gradients.

A practical example: suppose an update uses a stochastic gradient estimator $g(\theta)=\nabla_\theta J(\theta) + \xi$ with mean-zero noise $\xi$ and covariance $\Sigma_g/n$ (where $n$ mini-batch size). Then after a small step $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha g(\theta)$, the expected change in the objective (to first order) is $\alpha \|\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\|^2$, and the variance of the change is approximately $\alpha^2 \nabla_\theta J(\theta)^\top \Sigma_g \nabla_\theta J(\theta)/n$. This linearized sensitivity lets you balance step size versus noise: choose $\alpha$ so that the stochastic variance does not overwhelm the expected improvement.

Conclusion

The algebraic manipulations, expansions, inequalities, and heuristics in this chapter are the workhorse tools for formal analysis in RL and likelihood-based problems. Log and exponential identities simplify ratios and softmaxes. Taylor expansions let you linearize and capture curvature. Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz, Markov, Chebyshev, Hoeffding, and Pinsker provide means to bound expectations and probabilities. Writing sums as expectations and using importance sampling identities connect empirical estimators to population quantities. Back-of-the-envelope variance estimates, the delta method, and effective sample size heuristics quickly reveal scaling behavior. Finally, interpreting gradients via linearization and Fisher information clarifies sensitivity and motivates preconditioning and natural-gradient methods. Keep these tools at hand; they will make both reading and producing theoretical RL work much more manageable.
