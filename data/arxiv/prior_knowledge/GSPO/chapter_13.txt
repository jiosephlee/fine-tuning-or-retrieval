Introduction

Evaluation is central to research progress in reinforcement learning (RL) for language models because it turns opaque learning dynamics and model capacity into measurable quantities. Without careful evaluation, claims about improvement are unreliable; with careful evaluation, comparisons become reproducible and informative. This chapter examines the concrete metrics, benchmark suites, pairwise evaluation methods, and experimental protocols that researchers commonly use to assess RL-trained language models in domains such as code generation, mathematical problem solving, and general reasoning. Along the way we emphasize statistical rigor: how to quantify uncertainty, avoid common pitfalls, and report results that let others interpret and replicate your findings. We close with guidance on designing ablation studies and hyperparameter sweeps and on how to present training curves so that they communicate both performance and resource cost.

# Pass@k metrics and sampling-based evaluation for coding/math tasks

When language models are evaluated on tasks that admit many plausible outputs (for example, program synthesis, mathematical solutions, or multi-step reasoning), deterministic single-shot accuracy is often not the right metric. Instead, sampling-based evaluation captures a model's ability to produce a correct output when allowed multiple independent attempts. Pass@k is a commonly used metric in code generation and math-solution benchmarks that formalizes this idea.

Suppose we have a test set of $N$ problem instances. For a particular instance $i$, let the model produce $n_i$ sampled outputs (for many benchmarks $n_i$ is the same across instances and we denote it $n$). We determine whether each sampled output is correct by an automated checker (for code, this typically means running the program on hidden unit tests; for math, this might mean exact symbolic equality or a canonicalized answer). Let $c_i$ denote the number of correct samples among the $n_i$ draws for instance $i$. The observed empirical pass@k (for threshold $k$ with $1 \le k \le n$) is the fraction of instances where at least one of the top $k$ unique sampled outputs is correct. If we sample $n$ outputs and report pass@k, naively the per-instance indicator is $\mathbb{I}[c_i \ge 1 \text{ among the first }k]$, but because sampling is unordered and we usually treat all $n$ samples symmetrically, the appropriate unbiased estimator for the underlying probability of getting at least one correct output in $k$ draws can be derived combinatorially.

If the model generates $n$ independent samples and $c$ of them are correct, then the exact probability that a random subset of size $k$ contains at least one correct sample is
\[
\text{pass@}k = 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},
\]
provided $n-c \ge k$. When reporting across a dataset, we average this per-instance probability:
\[
\text{pass@}k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left(1 - \frac{\binom{n-c_i}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right).
\]
When $k=1$, pass@1 reduces to the empirical frequency of a correct sample among single-shot draws (after accounting for duplicates if desired). When $k=n$, pass@n equals the fraction of instances for which at least one of the $n$ samples is correct, which is simply the empirical success rate over $n$ trials. This combinatorial correction is especially important when researchers report pass@k but generate larger $n$; using the formula above yields an unbiased estimate of the success probability for hypothetical $k$ draws without having to actually re-sample in different orders.

From an experimental perspective, there are a few practical points. First, independence of samples is assumed: when using nucleus or temperature sampling, ensure sample diversity and independence so combinatorial formulas apply. Second, deduplication of identical outputs typically matters: if many samples are identical, the observed $c$ and the combinatorics should be computed on unique outputs or the sampling protocol should be reported. Third, pass@k depends on $k$ and $n$ in predictable ways; therefore paper comparisons should report the full set of $(n,k)$ values used or the unbiased pass@k as computed above. Fourth, when correctness checking relies on running generated code, sandboxing and deterministic test suites are essential to avoid nondeterministic pass/fail outcomes that inflate variance.

Beyond pass@k, sampling-based evaluation also includes metrics like expected best-of-$k$ score (for tasks where outputs are assigned scalar scores by a judge) and distributional calibration metrics that describe output diversity. For example, if $s(\cdot)$ gives a scalar score for an output, the expected best-of-$k$ score is the expectation over $k$ independent samples of the maximum $s$ among them. This expectation can be estimated empirically or computed analytically when the score distribution is known. In coding and math tasks where correctness is binary and objective, pass@k remains the standard.

# Elo ratings and pairwise evaluation methods

Certain language model evaluations are most naturally framed as pairwise comparisons rather than absolute scoring. Examples include conversational quality, dialogue turn preference, or fine-grained downstream behaviors judged by humans. Elo and related rating systems provide a compact, transitive, and interpretable way to aggregate many pairwise comparisons into scalar skill estimates for models or agents.

Elo was developed for chess ranking and models the probability that player A defeats player B as a logistic function of the difference in their ratings $R_A$ and $R_B$:
\[
P(A \text{ beats } B) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-(R_A - R_B)/400}}.
\]
After observing a match outcome, Elo updates are proportional to the difference between the observed outcome and the expected probability. If $S_A$ is the observed score (1 for win, 0 for loss, 0.5 for draw), the update for player $A$ is
\[
R_A' = R_A + K\,(S_A - P(A \text{ beats } B)),
\]
with $K$ governing how quickly ratings change in response to new information. The symmetric update applies to $R_B$. For model evaluation, each pairwise human or automated judgment ("which of these two responses is better?") is treated like a match outcome.

Elo provides an easy-to-understand scale: a difference of 400 points implies roughly a 10x odds ratio. It also allows for online updates as new comparisons arrive. However, Elo makes implicit assumptions: transitivity and a fixed logistic functional form relating differences to win probabilities. Real-world preferences, especially from human raters, sometimes violate these assumptions (e.g., intransitive cycles). To relax these assumptions, researchers use generalized models such as the Bradley–Terry model or TrueSkill. The Bradley–Terry model posits that each item $i$ has a positive skill parameter $\theta_i$, and
\[
P(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{\theta_i}{\theta_i + \theta_j}.
\]
Taking logs gives a logistic in log-skill space and permits maximum likelihood estimation of the $\theta$ values from observed pairwise outcomes. TrueSkill generalizes Elo by maintaining uncertainty (a mean and variance per player) and properly handling multiplayer comparisons and draws.

When designing pairwise evaluations, balance and coverage are crucial. A fully connected tournament (all pairs compared) yields the most information but is often impractical as the number of models grows. A common design is a round-robin with randomized sampling of pairs to manage annotation cost while ensuring each model participates in a roughly equal number of judgments. If crowdsourced human raters are used, ensure each rater sees a balanced mix of models and provide calibration examples so raters apply consistent criteria. Also track intra-rater and inter-rater reliability to detect noisy annotators.

Reporting Elo-style results should include the number of comparisons used to estimate each rating and standard errors or credible intervals around ratings, since finite-sample noise can be substantial. Where Elo assumptions are dubious, complement rating estimates with raw pairwise win rates, confusion matrices, or latent-variable models that can account for contextual factors (e.g., prompt type, length, or topic).

# Benchmarks for reasoning and coding (e.g., AIME, CodeForces)

Benchmarks provide standardized tasks and datasets that allow the community to track progress. In RL-for-language research, two important benchmark categories are coding/problem-solving platforms and curated reasoning/math problems. Examples that are widely used include Codeforces-style competitive programming problems, the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME) and similar contest mathematics, and domain-specific suites like HumanEval for coding or GSM8K for grade-school math reasoning.

Code-oriented benchmarks vary in structure. HumanEval and MBPP present short programming problems with a reference implementation and hidden unit tests. These benchmarks are convenient for pass@k-style evaluation because correctness is an objective, automated property: a generated program either passes the test suite or not. In contrast, Codeforces and similar competitive programming platforms host large problem sets with diverse difficulty and require more substantial algorithmic reasoning. Evaluating on Codeforces-style tasks often requires full programs, careful handling of input/output formats, and sometimes time/memory constraints. For such problems, benchmarks are typically compiled by crawling public problem statements and splitting them into train/validation/test sets with held-out test cases.

Mathematical reasoning benchmarks include AIME, MATH, and GSM8K. AIME problems are short-form contest math with numeric answers; they require multi-step algebraic reasoning and often clever insights. Because AIME answers are exact integers or rational numbers, automated checking is straightforward, making pass@k applicable. MATH (and similar) contains challenging high-school and undergraduate math problems that require multi-step reasoning, symbolic manipulation, and sometimes proof sketches; some tasks in this space are judged by automated symbolic checks, while others require human verification. GSM8K targets grade-school arithmetic word problems where step-by-step reasoning chains are useful for chain-of-thought evaluations.

When selecting benchmarks, researchers should consider alignment between benchmark properties and their research goals. For example, if your RL method emphasizes sample efficiency and sparse rewards, a benchmark with long-horizon reasoning and sparse success signals (like advanced problem solving) will be more revealing than a trivial unit-test-oriented dataset. Also note that some benchmarks are adversarial or have distributional shifts relative to pretraining corpora; improvements there may reflect better generalization rather than mere memorization.

Benchmark usage requires strict protocols. Always use held-out hidden test cases when available, avoid manual tuning on test data, and report which test splits were used. For benchmarks that permit multiple valid outputs (common in program synthesis or natural-language generation), use appropriate sampling-based metrics (e.g., pass@k) and define precise equivalence checks. For human-evaluated benchmarks, describe the annotation interface, rater qualifications, and quality-control measures.

# Statistical considerations: variance across seeds, significance testing

Neural training procedures, and RL in particular, can produce high run-to-run variance due to stochastic optimization, environment randomness, data shuffling, and reward noise. Consequently, a single run is rarely sufficient to establish that one method is truly better than another. Proper statistical practice involves running multiple random seeds, reporting measures of variability, and conducting hypothesis tests or estimation with confidence intervals.

A common minimum is to run at least three to five independent seeds for each experimental condition, but more is preferable when variance is large. For each seed, compute the primary metric of interest (for instance, mean reward, pass@k, or Elo rating) and then summarize across seeds with the sample mean and standard deviation. To report uncertainty on the mean, compute a standard error $\text{SE} = s/\sqrt{m}$ where $s$ is the sample standard deviation and $m$ is the number of seeds, and provide a confidence interval, e.g., a 95% interval using the $t$-distribution:
\[
\text{CI}_{95\%} = \bar{x} \pm t_{m-1,0.975}\cdot\frac{s}{\sqrt{m}}.
\]

For pairwise comparisons between two methods, a two-sample t-test (Welch's t-test) can test the null hypothesis that the methods have equal means. However, t-tests assume approximate normality of the sample means and independence among runs; when these assumptions are suspect or sample sizes are small, nonparametric tests or bootstrap methods are safer. The bootstrap constructs an empirical distribution of the difference in means by resampling seeds with replacement and computing the statistic repeatedly, yielding a bootstrap confidence interval that does not rely on normality.

Multiple comparison corrections are necessary when testing many hypotheses (for instance, testing multiple metrics, multiple datasets, or multiple ablations). Simple methods include Bonferroni correction, which controls the family-wise error rate but can be conservative, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, which controls the false discovery rate and is often more powerful in exploratory research. Always pre-specify primary metrics and hypotheses to avoid p-hacking.

Power analysis is often neglected but valuable. If you have an expectation of the effect size $\delta$ (difference between method means) and an estimate of variance $\sigma^2$, you can compute the number of seeds $m$ required to achieve a desired power (probability of detecting an effect) at a given significance level $\alpha$. For normally distributed means, the required $m$ roughly scales like
\[
m \approx 2\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}{\delta/\sigma}\right)^2,
\]
where $\beta$ is the desired Type II error rate and $z_{p}$ denotes the $p$-quantile of the standard normal. In practice, pilot runs can provide variance estimates to guide seed counts. If running many seeds is infeasible, report the observed effect sizes and their confidence intervals clearly, and treat claims as provisional.

Finally, report details that influence variance: random seeds, software versions, hardware, and any nondeterministic operations (for instance, certain GPU kernels or multithreaded data loaders). Transparency about experimental variability is essential for reproducibility and fair comparisons.

# Ablation studies and hyperparameter sweeps

Ablation studies and hyperparameter sweeps are the primary tools for diagnosing what components of a model or training pipeline actually matter. An ablation systematically removes or replaces one component while keeping others fixed to measure its causal contribution to performance. Hyperparameter sweeps explore how sensitive results are to choices like learning rates, entropy bonuses, batch sizes, or reward shaping.

Good ablation studies follow the principle of controlled variation: change only one factor at a time and run multiple seeds for each variant so observed differences are not due to noise. When possible, perform both “single-factor” ablations (removing one component) and “additive” studies (starting from a base model and adding components incrementally). For RL-trained language models, natural ablations include turning off reward shaping, varying the KL penalty strength, replacing off-policy updates with on-policy, or removing auxiliary losses such as supervised pretraining during RL fine-tuning.

Hyperparameter sweeps should be performed over ranges informed by prior work and pilot experiments. Rather than reporting a single “best” setting found via an extensive search, it's more informative to report the full response surface or at least how sensitive final performance is to hyperparameter choices. For example, show how pass@k or mean reward changes as you vary learning rate, KL coefficient $\beta$ (in a KL-regularized RL objective), or temperature during sampling. If the performance is highly brittle, that is an important empirical finding and should inform claims about method robustness.

Design of sweeps benefits from efficient search strategies. Grid search is straightforward but scales poorly with dimensionality. Random search often outperforms grid search in practice for the same budget, because it explores more unique combinations. For more efficient use of compute, consider successive-halving or Bayesian optimization, which allocate resources adaptively. When reporting sweep results, include the total compute cost, the number of evaluations, and the seed count per configuration.

In the context of RL for language, specific hyperparameters often warrant careful attention: learning rate for policy gradient or actor-critic updates, batch size and replay buffer parameters for off-policy methods, weight of KL or supervised loss that prevents divergence from pretraining, clipping thresholds in PPO-style objectives, and entropy bonuses or temperature parameters that control exploration. Report not only the chosen values but the rationale for them and any interactions observed in sweeps.

# Reporting training curves: reward, clipped fractions, KL, and wall-clock compute

Training curves are the visual narrative of learning dynamics. A single final metric is rarely sufficient; plotting how metrics evolve over time (or compute) reveals stability, convergence speed, and potential failure modes. For RL-trained language models, several traces are particularly informative: raw reward, clipped reward fractions, KL divergence from the reference policy, and wall-clock or FLOP-based compute.

Raw reward plots show the mean episodic reward (or task-specific score) over training steps. Because reward scales can be task-specific and heavy-tailed, it's often helpful to plot both mean and percentile bands (e.g., 10th–90th percentiles) across seeds to show variance and instability. Reward curves can be smoothed for readability, but always display the unsmoothed underlying points or report the smoothing window explicitly to avoid masking oscillations or divergence.

Clipped fractions are useful diagnostics for methods that clip policy probability ratios (as in PPO) or otherwise limit per-step updates. A clipped fraction is the proportion of updates or time steps where the probability ratio between new and old policy exceeds the clipping threshold. High clipped fractions indicate that the optimizer is frequently trying to make large policy changes and that the clipping is active; this can signal poor learning rate choice or a mismatch between reward scales and update rules. Plotting clipped fraction over time helps identify regimes where learning is constrained by the clipping mechanism versus regimes where the policy is stable.

KL divergence between the learned policy $\pi_\theta$ and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (often the pretrained base model) is critical in RL fine-tuning for language because large deviations from the base language model can lead to degraded language quality or undesirable behavior. The per-step or per-batch KL can be computed as
\[
\text{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D}\left[ \sum_{t} \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a\mid x_{<t}) \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid x_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid x_{<t})} \right].
\]
In practice this expectation is approximated on minibatches. Plotting KL over training steps alongside reward shows trade-offs: improvements in reward sometimes come at the cost of growing KL. Reporting both quantities and ideally the joint distribution across seeds allows readers to judge whether gains are due to meaningful behavior changes or catastrophic distributional shift.

Wall-clock compute and FLOP-normalized plots are essential to connect efficacy with cost. Two methods that achieve similar final performance may differ drastically in compute efficiency. Common x-axes for training curves include training steps, gradient updates, tokens processed, floating-point operations (FLOPs), and wall-clock time. Of these, wall-clock time and FLOPs are most informative about practical cost; tokens processed is useful for language models where data throughput matters. Always report the hardware used (GPU/TPU generation, number of devices), batch sizes, and whether mixed precision was used, because these influence wall-clock results.

Finally, annotate training curves with key events: learning rate changes, checkpointing, hyperparameter adjustments, or reward-shaping schedule changes. Include confidence bands over seeds and tabulate numerical values at checkpoints so readers can extract exact performance numbers. If releases include checkpoints, provide the steps corresponding to each reported point.

Conclusion

Careful evaluation is as important as model architecture or algorithmic innovation. For RL-trained language models, sampling-based metrics like pass@k, pairwise methods and Elo-like aggregation, and a chosen suite of reasoning and coding benchmarks together form a multi-faceted evaluation ecosystem. Above all, rigorous statistical practice (multiple seeds, confidence intervals, and correcting for multiple comparisons), thorough ablation and hyperparameter studies, and transparent reporting of training dynamics and compute costs are necessary for trustworthy and reproducible claims. Applying these principles will make your experiments interpretable, your results comparable with others, and your contributions meaningful to the research community.
