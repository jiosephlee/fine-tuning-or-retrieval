Introduction

Likelihood-based reinforcement learning (RL) methods — including policy gradient variants, importance-weighted off-policy corrections, and offline RL objectives — rely heavily on computing and manipulating probabilities and log-probabilities. When these methods are implemented in modern deep learning frameworks and run on accelerators, numerical issues from finite-precision arithmetic can materially change behavior, training stability, and the statistical correctness of estimators. This chapter explains the main numerical challenges that arise when computing likelihoods and importance weights, and it provides principled tools and practical heuristics for preventing or diagnosing instabilities.

We first review the floating-point formats commonly used in training neural policies and their consequences. We then derive and justify stable ways to compute log-probabilities and log-sum-exp expressions so you avoid underflow and overflow. Next we examine sequence-length effects: why raw sequence log-likelihoods grow (in magnitude) with length, what normalization options exist, and how they interact with importance weights. That leads into a discussion of how to pick clipping and scaling ranges for importance ratios and related quantities, since clipping ranges that ignore sequence length produce biased or inconsistent updates. We then introduce diagnostic metrics (including effective sample size) that make numerical problems visible. The chapter closes with a compendium of practical heuristics — the actionable rules you can apply immediately to control numerical instability in likelihood-based RL.

# Floating-point precision: fp16/bfloat16 vs fp32 and consequences

Floating-point formats trade off dynamic range and precision. The two formats that are commonly used for faster training on GPUs and TPUs are half-precision floating point (fp16, IEEE 754 binary16) and bfloat16; the standard full-precision format in deep learning is fp32 (IEEE 754 binary32). Understanding their differences explains why some operations explode or vanish in lower precision and which quantities need to be kept in higher precision.

fp32 has a 23-bit mantissa and an 8-bit exponent, giving ~7 decimal digits of precision and a dynamic range of approximately $10^{\pm38}$. fp16 has a 10-bit mantissa and a 5-bit exponent, providing roughly 3 decimal digits of precision and a much smaller dynamic range around $10^{\pm5}$. bfloat16 (brain floating point) keeps the same 8-bit exponent as fp32 but sacrifices mantissa bits: it has a 7-bit mantissa and an 8-bit exponent, so it preserves dynamic range close to fp32 (about $10^{\pm38}$) while having only about 2 decimal digits of precision. In practice this means:

- Underflow/overflow: fp16 will underflow values much more easily (setting them to zero) or overflow (to infinity) than fp32, because the exponent range is tiny. bfloat16 avoids most exponent-related underflow/overflow issues because it keeps a wide exponent range similar to fp32.
- Rounding noise: both fp16 and bfloat16 have coarse mantissas relative to fp32. Small differences that would be resolved in fp32 will be rounded away in these 16-bit formats, especially when adding numbers of very different scales.

Consequences for likelihood-based RL

Likelihoods (probabilities) are typically very small for sequences or for large action spaces. When you compute a product of many probabilities or a sum of log-probabilities, the values quickly fall outside the safe dynamic range of fp16. If you're using fp16, you can see zero log-probabilities or NaNs because of underflow, and importance weights computed as exponentials of log-differences will be zero or infinite. bfloat16 is safer for magnitude (because of the wide exponent) but still suffers from poor precision when subtracting similar large values (catastrophic cancellation) or when accumulating many small gradients.

Mixed precision training mitigates some costs while preserving stability. Common practice is to keep model parameters and master gradients in fp32 while performing many matrix multiplies in fp16 or bfloat16. Loss scaling (multiplying the loss by a large constant before backprop and unscaling gradients) prevents fp16 gradients from underflowing to zero. However, for log-probability arithmetic, you should perform reductions and sensitive scalar computations (for example, computing log-sum-exp, normalizers, or clipping thresholds) in fp32 where possible. Modern frameworks and hardware support automatic mixed precision (AMP) but leave it to you to choose which operations must remain in fp32.

Practical rules from precision differences

- Always compute reductions (sums, log-sum-exp, log probabilities aggregated over sequence length) in fp32. Reduce accumulation error and avoid catastrophic cancellation by casting inputs to fp32 before summing.
- Use bfloat16 in preference to fp16 on hardware that supports it if you require wide dynamic range but can tolerate lower precision.
- Keep scalars controlling clipping ranges, normalization constants, and importance-weight computations in fp32.
- Use loss scaling with fp16 to prevent gradient underflow; but still do final parameter updates in fp32.

# Computing log-probabilities safely: log-sum-exp and underflow/overflow

Many probabilistic computations reduce to operations of the form $\log \sum_i \exp(a_i)$ or $\log \sum_i p_i$ where the $a_i$ are log-probabilities or unnormalized log-scores. Directly exponentiating $a_i$ can overflow if some $a_i$ is large positive, or underflow to zero if all $a_i$ are large negative. Furthermore, subtracting nearly equal large numbers causes catastrophic cancellation. The canonical solution is the log-sum-exp (LSE) trick, which rewrites the expression to stabilize it numerically.

Derivation and stable formula

Let $s = \log \sum_{i} \exp(a_i)$. Choose $m = \max_i a_i$. Then
s = \log \sum_i \exp(a_i) = \log \left( \exp(m) \sum_i \exp(a_i - m) \right) = m + \log \sum_i \exp(a_i - m).
Because $a_i - m \le 0$, each $\exp(a_i - m) \in (0,1]$, so the sum is between $1$ and $n$, and computing $\log \sum_i \exp(a_i - m)$ is safe from overflow and much less likely to underflow. In floating point you should implement: compute $m = \max_i a_i$ in fp32, subtract $m$ from each $a_i$ in fp32, exponentiate (optionally in lower precision), sum in fp32, then take the log and add $m$.

Log-softmax is simply the normalized version:
$\log \text{softmax}(a)_i = a_i - \text{LSE}(a)$,
which you should compute using the same subtraction of the maximum to ensure stability.

Underflow and importance weights

Importance weights are often computed as exponentials of log-likelihood differences: $w = \exp(\log \pi(a|s) - \log \mu(a|s))$. If the difference is a large negative number, direct exponentiation gives zero in low precision. Working in the log domain is safer: keep and combine log-weights, and only exponentiate when necessary and after clamping. In many algorithms you can do updates using log-weights and normalize using log-sum-exp to obtain normalized weights:
$\log \tilde w_i = \log w_i - \log \sum_j w_j = (\log \pi_i - \log \mu_i) - \text{LSE}\big((\log \pi_j - \log \mu_j)\big)$.
Compute these in fp32 to avoid loss of accuracy.

Stable accumulation and Kahan summation

Even when you use log-sum-exp where appropriate, there are places where you must sum many small values (for example, totaling per-token probabilities across long sequences). Floating-point sums can accumulate error proportional to the condition number of the operation. Kahan summation or pairwise summation reduces rounding error: pairwise (tree) summation reduces error to $O(\log n)$ relative to naive left-to-right summation. Implement reductions in fp32 and, for long sequences or large batches, prefer pairwise sum algorithms provided by libraries or implement an explicit reduce tree.

Taking derivatives through log-sum-exp

When you backpropagate through LSE, be mindful that exponentials inside the operation can underflow or overflow in lower precision. During the forward pass, compute and store stabilized intermediates (for example, $a_i - m$ or normalized softmax probabilities in fp32) so the backward pass can rely on numerically sound quantities. Automatic differentiation frameworks do much of this, but when writing custom ops, perform the same stabilization in both forward and backward code paths.

# Length normalization of sequence likelihoods and motivations

Sequence log-likelihoods scale with sequence length. If you have a per-token conditional policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$ and a trajectory or sequence of length $T$, the joint log-likelihood is
$\log \pi_\theta(\tau) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t)$.
As $T$ increases, this sum tends to grow in magnitude linearly with $T$ (assuming typical per-step log-prob magnitudes). This creates at least three problems:

1. Importance-weight explosion or collapse: Importance weights are often exponentials of differences of such sums, so they scale like $\exp(\Delta \text{sum}) = \prod_t \exp(\Delta \log \pi_t)$. For long sequences, small per-step biases multiply and yield extremely large or small weights, making estimators high variance or numerically zero.

2. Clipping ranges lose meaning: If you choose a fixed clipping range for importance weights (e.g., clip $w$ to $[1/10, 10]$), that range may be appropriate for single-step probabilities but not for multi-step sequences. A fixed range will either be too permissive (if sequences are short) or overly restrictive (if sequences are long), causing biased updates or vanishing gradients.

3. Optimization prioritization: When losses are unnormalized cumulative log-likelihoods, the gradient magnitude is proportional to sequence length. Long sequences will dominate learning dynamics, possibly biasing the policy toward optimizing long trajectories at the expense of short ones.

Several normalization strategies exist. The two most common are per-token (average) normalization and length-dependent scaling.

Per-token average log-probability

Divide the sequence log-likelihood by the length $T$ to obtain an average per-step log-probability:
$\bar \ell(\tau) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$.
Using $\bar \ell$ yields per-step importance weights
$w = \exp\big( \bar \ell_\pi - \bar \ell_\mu \big) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \big( \log \pi_t - \log \mu_t\big) \right)$,
which compresses the multiplicative effect: instead of multiplying $T$ per-step ratios, you take the geometric mean. This reduces variance and makes weights more comparable across sequences of different lengths.

Length-normalized log-probabilities with temperature

A more general approach is to scale the sum by a factor $\alpha(T)$:
$\ell_\alpha(\tau) = \alpha(T) \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_t$,
where $\alpha(T) = 1/T$ gives the per-step average. Other choices can be used: $\alpha(T)=1/\sqrt{T}$ trades off between fully normalized and raw sums; $\alpha(T)=1$ recovers the unnormalized sum. The choice of $\alpha$ affects bias and variance: stronger normalization reduces variance but may bias the estimate of the total-return-weighted objective if the original problem depends on joint likelihoods.

When not to normalize

If the underlying objective or importance correction requires the true joint likelihood (for example, when correcting an estimator derived from the joint density), normalization changes the estimator and may introduce bias relative to the original objective. Consequently, one must be explicit about whether fairness across lengths or statistical validity of the estimator is the priority.

Practical decision factors

- If sequences have widely varying lengths and your algorithm uses importance weights multiplicatively across time, per-token normalization ($\alpha=1/T$) is a reasonable default to control variance and ensure comparability.
- If the target objective inherently depends on joint probabilities (e.g., some likelihood-ratio tests or exact off-policy corrections), consider using the true joint likelihood but compensate by smaller learning rates, aggressive clipping, or resampling to equalize sequence lengths.
- Hybrid approaches: normalize by $T$ for computing importance ratios used for weighting gradients, but use the raw sum in estimators that require total probability, with corrections or debiasing applied.

# Scaling and choosing clipping ranges for different likelihood definitions

Clipping importance weights is a standard variance-control technique. But the appropriate clipping thresholds depend on how you represent likelihoods (raw joint log-likelihood, per-token average, or some scaled version) and on sequence length. A bad choice of clipping range can either nullify the correction (if too tight) or provide no benefit (if too loose). Here we examine how to choose clipping ranges coherently and how to scale them with sequence length.

Importance weights and their log representation

Recall unnormalized importance weight for a trajectory $\tau$:
$w(\tau) = \frac{\pi(\tau)}{\mu(\tau)} = \exp\big( \log \pi(\tau) - \log \mu(\tau)\big)$.
If you operate in log-space, let $\delta(\tau) = \log \pi(\tau) - \log \mu(\tau)$. Clipping $w$ to $[c_{\min}, c_{\max}]$ corresponds to clipping $\delta$ to $[\log c_{\min}, \log c_{\max}]$. Working in log-space is numerically safer and makes scaling with sequence length explicit.

Scaling with length

If you switch to per-token average log-likelihoods, then differences become
$\bar \delta(\tau) = \frac{1}{T} \delta(\tau)$
and clipping $w$ in the per-token case corresponds to clipping $\bar \delta$ to $[\frac{1}{T}\log c_{\min}, \frac{1}{T}\log c_{\max}]$. Therefore fixed $[\log c_{\min}, \log c_{\max}]$ in the joint-sum sense is equivalent to shrinking the per-token clip window by $1/T$.

Heuristics for choosing clipping thresholds

- Use log-space clipping: choose bounds $\ell_{\min}$ and $\ell_{\max}$ and clip $\delta(\tau)$ directly, i.e., $\delta_{\text{clipped}} = \max(\ell_{\min}, \min(\delta(\tau), \ell_{\max}))$. Only exponentiate after clipping if necessary.
- Make thresholds proportional to length: set $\ell_{\max} = \beta T$ for some per-step tolerance $\beta$. In other words, allow the per-step log-ratio to vary by at most $\beta$, so the joint allowed range grows with $T$.
- If you normalize by $T$ when computing $\bar \delta$, use fixed per-step thresholds (e.g., $\bar \delta \in [-\beta, \beta]$), which naturally adapt to length without extra scaling.
- Choose $\beta$ based on expected per-step log-ratio magnitudes. If typical per-step log-prob differences are on the order of $10^{-2}$, choose $\beta$ a small multiple of that. If you lack prior, start with conservative thresholds (small $\beta$) and loosen if ESS or training signal is too small.

Clipping strategies beyond hard clipping

Rather than hard clipping, soft clipping or downweighting schemes can be numerically and statistically preferable. For instance, use a sigmoid-based damping:
$w_{\text{damped}}(\tau) = w(\tau) \cdot \sigma\big( ( \log w(\tau) - u ) / s \big)$,
where $u$ and $s$ control center and softness. In log-space this is equivalent to adding a smooth penalty to large $\delta$. Another option is to use weight normalization like:
$\tilde w_i = \frac{\min(w_i, c)}{\sum_j \min(w_j,c)}$,
which truncates but stays in the normalized family. When using these methods, compute everything stably in log-space and perform reductions in fp32.

Choosing clipping ranges adaptively

Adaptive clipping chooses bounds based on batch-level statistics. For example, compute the sample standard deviation $\sigma_\delta$ of $\delta(\tau)$ in a batch and set $\ell_{\max} = \mu_\delta + k \sigma_\delta$ and $\ell_{\min} = \mu_\delta - k \sigma_\delta$ for some $k$ (often $2$ or $3$). This makes clipping relative to the observed spread. To avoid instability caused by a single batch outlier, smooth $\mu_\delta$ and $\sigma_\delta$ using an EMA (exponential moving average) computed in fp32.

# Diagnostic metrics: magnitude and variance of importance ratios

To manage numerical issues you must measure what goes wrong. Several diagnostic metrics are particularly informative about the scale and variability of importance ratios and about whether numerical precision is adequate.

Raw and log statistics

Compute both raw importance weights $w_i$ and log-weights $\delta_i = \log w_i$ for each sample/trajectory in a batch. Report the mean, median, min, max, and several quantiles of $\delta_i$ (e.g., 5th, 25th, 75th, 95th). Seeing extremes in $\delta_i$ reveals whether exponentiating will overflow or underflow. If $\max \delta_i$ is >> 100 in fp32 you risk overflow when exponentiating; if $\min \delta_i$ is << -100 you risk underflow to zero in fp32 (and at much smaller magnitudes in fp16).

Variance and effective sample size (ESS)

High variance in importance weights implies unreliable weighted estimators. Compute the coefficient of variation (CV) and the effective sample size:
- CV of weights: $\text{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(w)}}{\mathbb{E}[w]}$.
- Effective sample size: $\text{ESS} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}$.
ESS ranges from $1$ to $N$ (the batch size); low ESS indicates that only a few samples contribute meaningfully. In log-space, compute ESS via log-sum-exp:
$\log \sum_i w_i = \text{LSE}(\delta)$ and $\log \sum_i w_i^2 = \text{LSE}(2\delta)$,
then $\log \text{ESS} = 2\cdot \text{LSE}(\delta) - \text{LSE}(2\delta)$. Evaluate these in fp32 and report ESS as a diagnostic. If ESS is very small relative to batch size, importance weighting is unstable.

Gradient-weighted statistics

Often we care about the effective contribution to gradient updates. Compute weighted gradient norms:
- The weighted average gradient norm: $\left\| \sum_i w_i g_i \right\|$ and its constituent statistics. Compare the norm of the mean-weighted gradient to the mean of gradient norms $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|g_i\|$, and to the norms when weights are clipped.
- Track the relative change in parameter updates when using clipped vs unclipped weights to detect whether clipping distorts the direction heavily.

Numerical anomaly checks

Report the incidence of NaN or Inf in log-probabilities, weights, and gradients. For fp16/bfloat16 training, also monitor the proportion of fp16 values that were flushed to zero or saturated. Monitoring gradients that are exactly zero or extremely small can signal underflow.

Time-series and length-conditioned diagnostics

Plot diagnostics conditioned on sequence length $T$: histograms of $\delta$ broken down by length show how weights explode with $T$. Monitor ESS as a function of $T$. If ESS decays rapidly with length, apply length-aware normalization or per-step clipping.

# Practical heuristics to control numerical instability

Here are precise, actionable heuristics that combine the theory laid out above into best practices for implementing and debugging likelihood-based RL. Each recommendation includes motivation and implementation notes.

1. Compute and store log-domain quantities in fp32

Always compute per-step log-probabilities, log-weights $\delta$, and LSE reductions in fp32. When accumulating across sequence length, cast per-step logs to fp32 then sum. Avoid converting to fp16 until after unstable reductions are complete.

2. Use log-sum-exp everywhere you would sum exponentials

When normalizing weights, computing partition functions, or forming softmax/log-softmax, implement the numerically stable LSE formulation: subtract the max, exponentiate, sum, then add the max back after taking the log. Store intermediates for backprop in fp32.

3. Prefer per-token (or otherwise normalized) likelihoods when sequences vary in length

If trajectories have heterogeneous lengths and you use importance weights multiplicatively over time, use $\bar \delta = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \delta_t$ for weighting or at least for computing clipping thresholds. This reduces variance and makes thresholds length-independent.

4. Clip in log-space with length-aware thresholds

Clip $\delta$ instead of $w$. If you work with per-token averages, use fixed per-step clip $\beta$ (e.g., $\beta \in [0.02, 0.5]$ depending on problem). If you work with raw sums, set $\ell_{\max} = \beta T$ and $\ell_{\min} = -\beta T$. Compute these thresholds in fp32; implement clipping before exponentiating.

5. Use adaptive clipping based on batch statistics with momentum

Compute batch mean $\mu_\delta$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\delta$ of $\delta$ in fp32. Maintain EMA estimates $\hat \mu$ and $\hat \sigma$ and set clip bounds to $\hat \mu \pm k \hat \sigma$ (typical $k\in[2,4]$). This adapts to changing distribution and avoids clipping to a fixed obsolete range.

6. Maintain master fp32 copies and use loss scaling for fp16

When training with fp16 (or bfloat16 with coarse mantissa), keep master weights in fp32 and use automatic mixed precision for matmuls but compute losses and reductions in fp32. Apply static or dynamic loss scaling to prevent gradient underflow in fp16.

7. Avoid exponentiating large magnitudes — normalize first

If you must exponentiate, subtract a stable constant first (e.g., the maximum of the logits) and exponentiate the shifted values. When normalizing weights across a batch, compute log-normalized weights via LSE and exponentiate only if required for a specific downstream op; otherwise operate in log-space.

8. Use soft clipping or damped weighting when abrupt truncation is harmful

If hard clipping introduces bias or unstable behavior, use smooth damping such as multiplying by $\tanh$ or a sigmoid kernel on the log-scale to smoothly reduce extreme weights. This often gives better gradients while retaining variance control.

9. Monitor ESS and use it as a signal for algorithmic interventions

If ESS falls below a threshold (for example, $0.1N$), consider performing one of: increase batch size, increase entropy regularization, apply stronger normalization, reduce learning rate, or resample to shorten sequences. Tagging ESS thresholds to automated interventions helps maintain stability during long runs.

10. Use double reduction safeguards for large sums

When summing extremely long sequences or large batches, accumulating in blocks and using pairwise (tree) aggregation reduces rounding error. If your framework permits, use higher-precision accumulation (fp64) for critical aggregate statistics such as running totals used in diagnostics.

11. Test in a controlled micro-benchmark

Before scaling to full training, construct small tests that exercise extreme cases: very long sequences, very peaky policies, and very different behavior and target policies. Evaluate log-prob min/max, ESS, and gradients; adjust normalization and clipping until these micro-benchmarks behave stably.

12. Record and act on numerical anomalies

Log counts of NaNs, Infs, and zero-weighted trajectories. If the frequency of anomalies increases, trigger rollback or reduce the learning rate. Where possible, save debug traces (log-probs per timestep) for offline inspection.

Summary and recommended defaults

A reasonable default configuration for likelihood-based RL in modern hardware is:

- Use bfloat16 for matrix ops if available, otherwise fp16 with AMP and loss scaling, and keep reductions and scalar quantities in fp32.
- Compute per-step log-probabilities in fp32 and average over sequence length to produce per-token log-likelihoods unless your estimator explicitly needs joint likelihoods.
- Clip log-weights (not raw weights) with per-step clip $\beta$ in log-space (e.g., $\beta \in [0.05, 0.5]$) or adaptive EMA-based bounds, and perform normalization via log-sum-exp in fp32.
- Monitor ESS, CV, and the distribution of log-weights and take remedial action when ESS is low.

By following these principles — favoring log-domain arithmetic, performing sensitive reductions in fp32, normalizing by sequence length when appropriate, and using length-aware clipping — you will substantially reduce numerical instability and obtain more reliable gradient estimates and ultimately more stable training in likelihood-based RL.
