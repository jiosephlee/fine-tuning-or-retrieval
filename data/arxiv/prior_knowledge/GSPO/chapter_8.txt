Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms often suffer from high variance and unstable training dynamics, particularly when applied to large models, high-dimensional observation spaces, or long-horizon decision problems. Variance manifests in noisy gradient estimates, which slow learning and can lead to divergence. Instability shows up as sudden policy collapse, unpredictable performance regressions, or sensitivity to hyperparameters. This chapter surveys practical techniques that researchers and practitioners use to reduce estimator variance and to stabilize RL training. The goal is to provide both the mathematical intuition and the practical insights needed to apply these methods effectively and to interpret diagnostics when they fail.

We begin with baselines and critic networks, which are fundamental for variance reduction in policy-gradient methods. Then we examine reward scaling and normalization techniques that shape numerical properties of learning signals. Entropy regularization and exploration control address the trade-off between exploring to discover better actions and exploiting known good behavior. Gradient clipping and learning-rate schedules are standard optimizers' tools adapted to the RL setting. Trust-region and proximal methods—most notably TRPO and PPO—provide principled constraints on policy updates to maintain stability. Replay strategies, such as experience replay and prioritized sampling, change data correlation and sample efficiency and thus affect stability. Finally, we discuss diagnostics that practitioners should monitor, including importance weights, KL divergence, and clipped fractions, which provide early warnings of instability or policy collapse.

# Baselines and critic networks

Policy-gradient methods estimate the gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters by sampling trajectories. A canonical estimator for the policy gradient is the REINFORCE estimator:
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\tau\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \, G_t \right],
$$
where $G_t$ is the return from time $t$. A major source of variance in this estimator is the randomness in $G_t$. A classical variance-reduction trick is to subtract a baseline $b(s_t)$ from the return without introducing bias. Concretely,
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\tau\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \, (G_t - b(s_t)) \right].
$$
The baseline can be any function of the state; it does not change the expectation because $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\, b(s_t)] = b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[\;1\;] = 0$. Intuitively, subtracting $b(s_t)$ recenters the learning signal so that actions that produce above-baseline returns get positive weight, and below-baseline returns get negative weight; this reduces variance because it removes components of $G_t$ that are independent of the action.

A common and powerful choice for the baseline is to use a critic network that estimates the state value function $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}[G_t|s_t=s]$. Using the critic yields the advantage estimator
$$
A_t = G_t - V_\phi(s_t),
$$
and the policy gradient becomes
$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \mathbb{E}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\, A_t \right].
$$
Because $V^\pi(s)$ captures the expected return from the state, subtracting it removes state-dependent variance. Training the critic usually proceeds by minimizing a squared-error loss,
$$
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \left( G_t - V_\phi(s_t) \right)^2 \right],
$$
or by using temporal-difference (TD) targets such as $r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$ for bootstrapped updates. The choice between Monte Carlo targets and TD targets trades bias against variance: Monte Carlo targets are unbiased but high-variance, while TD targets are lower variance but biased. In practice, TD($\lambda$) and generalized advantage estimation (GAE) blend multi-step returns to tune this bias–variance trade-off.

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is an important technique for producing low-variance advantage estimates while allowing some bias. Let $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$ be the one-step TD residual. GAE constructs
$$
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},
$$
which is an exponentially-weighted sum of TD errors. The parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$ interpolates between high-variance, unbiased estimates ($\lambda=1$) and low-variance, biased estimates ($\lambda=0$). Choosing $\lambda$ near $0.95$ or $0.97$ is common in practice for large-scale problems.

Architectural and optimization considerations for the critic matter. A poorly trained critic can introduce bias into policy updates (through biased advantage estimates) or amplify variance if its outputs are noisy. Regularization, target networks (to stabilize bootstrapped targets), and careful learning-rate tuning for the critic are practical necessities. It is also common to share representations between the policy and the critic to improve sample efficiency; however, sharing must be handled carefully, because the policy and value losses may have competing gradients. Empirically, small-weighting factors or separate optimization schedules for the actor and critic are used.

# Reward scaling and normalization

The scale and distribution of reward signals directly affect learning. If rewards are very large or very small, gradients can explode or vanish; if rewards shift during training (nonstationarity), the agent may chase noise or oscillate. Reward scaling and normalization are practical recipes to keep the optimization well-conditioned.

One simple technique is to rescale rewards by a constant factor so that typical returns fall into a reasonable numerical range. Since policy-gradient methods are invariant to linear rescaling of the reward when combined with appropriate baseline subtraction only in certain cases, naive rescaling can change learning speed. However, rescaling is often necessary to match the optimizer's effective learning rate. For example, if rewards are on the order of $10^3$, dividing them by $10^2$ can make gradients numerically stable and allow larger learning rates.

A more adaptive approach is to normalize returns or advantages online. For advantages, a common step is to compute
$$
\tilde{A}_t = \frac{\hat{A}_t - \mu_A}{\sigma_A + \epsilon},
$$
where $\mu_A$ and $\sigma_A$ are the running mean and standard deviation of advantage estimates, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. Normalizing advantages makes the effective update scale roughly independent of environmental reward magnitudes and stabilizes learning across tasks. When using a learned value function as baseline, normalizing returns before fitting the critic can improve its conditioning.

Another normalization strategy is observation normalization: centering and scaling input features (including pixel values or proprioceptive sensor readouts) so that each channel has zero mean and unit variance. This prevents some inputs from dominating the representation and often accelerates learning. In partially observable or nonstationary settings, running statistics with appropriate decay factors are used to adapt normalization over time.

Care must be taken when normalizing across episodes in off-policy or replay-buffer contexts, where the distribution of experiences changes as the policy improves. Statistics computed over a mixture of old and new data can introduce bias. Therefore, practitioners often maintain separate normalization statistics for on-policy rollouts versus replay buffers, or use per-batch normalization that resets for each update when using strictly on-policy algorithms.

# Entropy regularization and exploration control

Exploration is essential for RL. Without sufficient exploration, a policy may prematurely converge to a suboptimal deterministic policy that avoids trying potentially better actions. Entropy regularization is a straightforward way to encourage exploration by adding an entropy bonus to the policy objective. For a policy $\pi_\theta$, the entropy is
$$
\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) = -\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)}\left[ \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \right].
$$
Adding an entropy term yields the augmented objective
$$
J_{\text{entropy}}(\theta) = J(\theta) + \beta \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^\pi}\left[ \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) \right],
$$
where $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the entropy bonus and $d^\pi$ denotes the state visitation distribution. The entropy term directly favors higher-variance (more random) policies, which helps exploration and can prevent premature convergence to deterministic policies.

The choice and schedule for $\beta$ are influential. A large fixed $\beta$ keeps the policy stochastic and encourages thorough exploration but can prevent fine exploitation once good behaviors are discovered. Conversely, making $\beta$ decay over time or adapt depending on metrics (such as KL divergence or performance plateauing) allows the algorithm to explore early and focus on exploitation later. Some implementations use an adaptive entropy coefficient, learned by framing the entropy target as a constraint: maximize expected return subject to achieving a desired average entropy. Optimizing a Lagrangian yields an update rule for $\beta$ that adjusts to maintain target exploration levels.

Beyond entropy, explicit exploration strategies include adding parameter noise, using intrinsic motivation bonuses (e.g., curiosity-driven rewards), and using directed exploration mechanisms like Thompson sampling in bandit-like settings. Entropy regularization remains a simple and effective baseline: it is easy to implement, differentiable, and integrates naturally with policy-gradient updates.

However, entropy is not a panacea. In high-dimensional action spaces, entropy computed per action dimension can be misleading; encouraging independent high entropy across dimensions may not produce diverse joint actions if dependencies matter. Additionally, in discrete action spaces, entropy drives uniformity unless combined with other mechanisms that bias exploration toward novel or uncertain actions.

# Gradient clipping and learning-rate schedules

Optimization stability depends strongly on how gradients are handled and how step sizes are chosen. Gradient clipping is a widely used technique to prevent occasional large gradient magnitudes—often caused by rare high-reward trajectories, outliers, or approximation errors—from destabilizing parameter updates. Two common forms are elementwise gradient clipping and global norm clipping. With global norm clipping, if the gradient vector $g$ has norm $\|g\|_2$ greater than a threshold $c$, it is rescaled:
$$
g \leftarrow g \times \frac{c}{\max(\|g\|_2, c)}.
$$
This preserves gradient direction while bounding its magnitude. Clipping is especially useful when combining actor and critic losses or when using high learning rates.

Learning-rate schedules govern how the step size changes over time. Constant learning rates can work for short runs but often lead to instability or inability to converge for long training runs. Decay schedules—such as exponential decay, step decay, or cosine annealing—reduce the learning rate over time, allowing large steps early for exploration of parameter space and smaller steps later for fine-tuning. In RL, it is common to tune separate learning rates for actor and critic, because their loss landscapes and convergence rates differ.

Adaptive optimizers like Adam, RMSProp, and variants are popular in RL because they normalize updates per-parameter based on running estimates of first and second moments of the gradients. However, adaptive methods can interact poorly with nonstationary RL targets, and careful tuning of hyperparameters (learning rate, epsilon, beta1, beta2) is required. Recent empirical studies recommend modest learning rates with Adam and combining it with gradient clipping to handle occasional spikes.

A practical refinement is to use trust-region inspired step-size control or line-search heuristics: compute candidate updates using current gradients and accept them only if a surrogate objective or a measured improvement meets certain criteria (this idea anticipates trust-region methods discussed next). Overall, gradient clipping and thoughtful learning-rate scheduling are low-effort, high-impact techniques for preventing divergence and improving sample efficiency.

# Trust-region methods (TRPO) and proximal methods (PPO)

Aggressive policy updates can change action distributions so much that value estimates and advantage calculations become invalid, causing catastrophic performance drops. Trust-region and proximal methods were developed to constrain policy updates and thereby ensure more stable improvement.

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) formulates policy update as a constrained optimization: maximize a surrogate objective subject to a bound on the average KL divergence between the new policy and the old policy. The TRPO problem can be written as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_\theta \quad & \mathbb{E}_{s,a\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|s)} A^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s,a) \right] \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}}\left[ \operatorname{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)\big) \right] \le \delta,
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\delta$ is a small scalar. TRPO uses a second-order approximation and conjugate gradient methods to compute a step that approximately satisfies the constraint. The KL constraint limits the magnitude of the update in distribution space rather than parameter space, which better reflects the true change in policy behavior.

While TRPO tends to be stable and reliable, it is computationally heavy because of the required second-order computations and line searches. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was introduced as a simpler and more practical family of algorithms that approximate the trust-region idea. The most common PPO variant uses a clipped surrogate objective:
$$
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[ \min\left( r_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t,\, \operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_t \right) \right],
$$
where $r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the probability ratio and $\epsilon$ is typically $0.1$ or $0.2$. The clipping prevents $r_t(\theta)$ from moving too far from $1$ in a direction that would increase the surrogate objective beyond the clipped value. Intuitively, the objective takes the pessimistic value between the unclipped and clipped terms, so policy updates that would excessively boost the advantage-weighted probability are not rewarded.

PPO achieves many of the same stability benefits as TRPO but with first-order optimization and mini-batch stochastic gradient descent, making it simple and scalable. Nevertheless, PPO's hyperparameters—especially the clipping parameter $\epsilon$, the number of epochs per update, and the minibatch size—must be tuned. Too large $\epsilon$ or too many epochs can cause overfitting to the sampled trajectories and large policy shifts; too small $\epsilon$ can slow learning. Some variants add an explicit KL penalty instead of clipping and adapt the penalty coefficient to keep the KL near a target.

Both TRPO and PPO require estimating importance sampling ratios $r_t(\theta)$, and when those ratios become extreme, effective sample size diminishes and variance increases. Thus, trust-region thinking—keeping successive policies sufficiently close—reduces estimator variance indirectly by keeping importance weights well-behaved.

# Replay strategies and their role in stability

Experience replay stores past transitions and reuses them for multiple updates. Originally introduced in the context of off-policy value-based methods like DQN, replay buffers improve sample efficiency by breaking temporal correlations and allowing more gradient steps per collected sample. However, replay interacts with stability in subtle ways.

Using a replay buffer creates an off-policy learning problem: the policy used to generate stored transitions differs from the current policy. Off-policy algorithms that correct for this mismatch (e.g., Q-learning, importance-weighted actor-critic, or off-policy policy gradient methods) can still learn but must handle distribution shift. Experience replay can stabilize learning by smoothing updates—since batches sample from a mixture of past behaviors, sudden changes in policy are tempered by the stored experience. This reduces variance in gradient estimates compared to using only fresh on-policy rollouts.

Prioritized experience replay is a variant that samples transitions with probability proportional to some measure of "surprise", commonly the TD error magnitude. This focuses updates on more informative transitions and often accelerates learning. However, prioritization skews the distribution and can introduce bias unless importance-sampling corrections are used. In practice, using a partial prioritization factor and annealing importance-sampling weights helps balance bias and variance.

Replay buffers raise issues when used with policy-gradient methods that rely on on-policy samples. On-policy algorithms like PPO typically gather fresh rollouts and optimize using only that data for a few epochs; using older data without correction breaks the theoretical guarantees and can cause instability because the advantage estimates and value targets are stale. Some modern actor-critic methods (e.g., off-policy actor-critic with experience replay) combine off-policy corrections such as Retrace, V-trace, or importance sampling with target networks to stabilize learning while reaping replay's sample efficiency.

Another replay-related technique is episodic memory or hindsight experience replay (HER), which reframes sparse-reward problems by relabeling goals in stored episodes to generate more informative learning signals. HER dramatically reduces variance in sparse-reward tasks by creating additional successful examples, improving stability and sample efficiency.

Finally, the buffer size, sampling strategy, and whether to prioritize recent samples affect stability. Very large buffers may contain outdated transitions that slow adaptation; very small buffers yield highly correlated batches. Practitioners often tune buffer size as a trade-off between data heterogeneity and recency.

# Diagnostics: monitoring importance weights, KL, and clipped fractions

Robust training requires monitoring diagnostics that reveal how stable and trustworthy your updates are. Three critical quantities to monitor, especially for TRPO/PPO-style algorithms, are importance weights (probability ratios), KL divergence between successive policies, and the fraction of clipped updates.

Importance weights measure how much the new policy has changed the probability assigned to the actions in the sampled data:
$$
r_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}.
$$
Large variance or extreme values in $r_t$ indicate that the policy has shifted dramatically, reducing the effective sample size and inflating gradient variance. A heavy-tailed distribution of $r_t$ often precedes instability. Monitoring the mean and variance of $r_t$, as well as percentiles (e.g., 5th and 95th), helps diagnose when updates are too aggressive. If ratios routinely exceed the clipping bounds, consider reducing learning rate, decreasing the number of epochs per batch, or tightening the clipping threshold or KL target.

The KL divergence between the old and new policies, averaged over states from the rollout distribution,
$$
\overline{\text{KL}} = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ \operatorname{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)\big) \right],
$$
is a direct measure of how far the policy moved in distribution space. Many algorithms either constrain or penalize this KL. A sudden spike in KL is a red flag: it means the policy has changed enough that previously computed value functions or advantages may no longer be valid. Tracking KL over time helps decide whether to reduce step size or to perform early stopping during multi-epoch updates. In adaptive schemes, KL is used to automatically adjust learning rates or penalty coefficients; in such cases, logging the adaptive coefficient and its evolution is also informative.

For clipping-based objectives like PPO's, the clipped fraction is the proportion of sampled timesteps where the unclipped objective was larger than the clipped surrogate, i.e., where clipping was active. If a large fraction of updates are clipped, the surrogate objective may be constraining the optimizer too tightly, and the effective gradient signal could be vanishing, slowing learning. Conversely, if the clipped fraction is near zero while KL is large, it may indicate that clipping is ineffective at preventing harmful updates—perhaps because updates are driven in directions that do not change the ratio beyond the clip but still change the policy drastically in other senses. Monitoring clipped fraction together with KL and with the distribution of $r_t$ gives a fuller picture.

Other important diagnostics include the value-function loss and its variance, which signal whether the critic is learning; gradients' norms to detect exploding or vanishing gradients; and episode return distributions over time to identify mode collapse or nonstationary performance. Logging per-batch and per-epoch statistics and visualizing them with tools like tensorboard or Weights & Biases makes it easier to spot trends such as performance plateaus, spikes in variance, or correlations between hyperparameter changes and instability.

In addition to monitoring these metrics, using sanity checks helps: compute effective sample size estimates based on importance weights, examine action entropy to ensure exploration is neither too high nor too low, and, if possible, visualize policy behavior in environment-specific ways. When diagnosing instability, revert to simpler settings (smaller networks, shorter horizons, deterministic seeds, and lower learning rates) to isolate the problem before scaling up.

Concluding remarks

Variance reduction and stability techniques are indispensable in modern RL. Baselines and critics reduce estimator variance and form the backbone of actor–critic methods, while reward scaling and normalization keep numerical values in a favorable range. Entropy regularization and exploration control balance discovery and exploitation. Gradient clipping and learning-rate schedules offer straightforward protections against extreme updates. Trust-region and proximal methods provide principled constraints that greatly improve reliability in practice. Replay strategies trade off recency for sample efficiency and must be used with algorithms that properly correct for off-policy data. Finally, diagnostics—especially importance weights, KL divergence, and clipping statistics—are essential for detecting and remedying instability early.

When building RL systems, these techniques are not mutually exclusive; they are typically combined. The art lies in tuning them together: for example, larger entropy bonuses can reduce variance in returns by promoting exploration but may require smaller policy learning rates; experience replay increases sample efficiency but often needs off-policy corrections and more conservative trust-region constraints. Learning to read the diagnostics and to intervene appropriately is vital for scaling RL to large models and long-horizon tasks.
