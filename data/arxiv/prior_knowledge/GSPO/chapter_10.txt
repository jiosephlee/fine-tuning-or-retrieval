Introduction

Sequence models—such as autoregressive language models, sequence-to-sequence models, and recurrent neural networks—are trained and analyzed using objectives that involve probabilities of entire sequences. A fundamental observation is that the log-likelihood of a sequence factorizes as a sum of per-token log-probabilities when the model is autoregressive. This decomposition makes it possible to express sequence-level gradients as sums of token-level gradients, which in turn clarifies how different weighting schemes and importance sampling corrections affect learning. In this chapter we develop the algebra and intuition behind these ideas, examine how weighting choices change gradient magnitudes and variance, discuss how noise accumulates across long sequences, and cover practical implementation details such as stop-gradient semantics, detaching, and numerical stability. We also compare two important weighting paradigms: per-step importance-sampling (IS) weighting and trajectory-level weighting.

# Gradient of log-likelihood for a sequence: sum over token gradients

Consider an autoregressive sequence model parameterized by $\theta$ that assigns probabilities to sequences $x_{1:T}=(x_1,\dots,x_T)$ via the factorization
\[
p_\theta(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
The log-likelihood of a particular observed sequence is therefore
\[
\log p_\theta(x_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
Differentiating with respect to $\theta$ yields the exact decomposition
\[
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{1:T})=\sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
This identity is simple but powerful: the gradient of the sequence log-probability splits neatly into a sum of per-token gradients, each of which depends on the conditional probability at a single time step and on the prefix $x_{1:t-1}$. In practice this means that when computing gradients using backpropagation through the autoregressive model, each token contributes an additive gradient term to the overall update.

There is an immediate interpretation in terms of credit assignment. If you increase the log-probability of token $x_t$ at time $t$, you directly increase the sequence log-probability by that amount; therefore only the token-level log-probability gradients are required to adjust the model to better explain the full sequence. This provides the mathematical basis for token-level loss functions that sum token negative log-likelihoods during training. Moreover, this decomposition also underlies how per-token losses (e.g., cross-entropy at each step) compose into a sequence-level objective: the sequence loss is simply the sum of per-token losses, and its gradient is the sum of per-token gradients.

# Relationship between per-token weighting and overall sequence gradient

Weighting schemes modify how different parts of a sequence influence the overall gradient. Suppose we introduce nonnegative weights $w_t(x_{1:t})$ for each time step $t$, possibly depending on the prefix, and define a weighted sequence objective
\[
L_w(\theta; x_{1:T}) = -\sum_{t=1}^T w_t(x_{1:t}) \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}),
\]
so the negative log-likelihood is scaled at each token by $w_t$. The gradient is
\[
\nabla_\theta L_w(\theta; x_{1:T}) = -\sum_{t=1}^T w_t(x_{1:t}) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}).
\]
Thus, per-token weighting simply rescales each token gradient term. Conceptually, the weights determine how much credit or blame a token receives relative to others when computing parameter updates. If $w_t$ depends on the parameter $\theta$ (for example, through normalized probabilities when performing importance sampling), then additional terms arise from differentiating the weights themselves; one must carefully treat such dependency if the weights are not treated as constant with respect to $\theta$.

Often weights are introduced for variance reduction or to correct for a sampling distribution different from the training target. For instance, in importance sampling we might sample tokens under a behavior policy $q$ while optimizing an objective for the target policy $p_\theta$. A per-token importance weight would be the ratio
\[
r_t = \frac{p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})}{q(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})},
\]
which multiplies the per-step contribution. The weighted gradient estimator then becomes a sum of weighted per-token gradients. The immediate benefit is that token-level adjustments can account for the mismatch in sampling distributions locally, but this comes at the cost of increased variance unless controlled.

When weights depend only on observed data and are treated as constants during gradient computation, they simply rescale the contribution each token makes to the parameter update. But when weights depend on $\theta$, e.g., when using self-normalized importance sampling or when the behavior policy depends on $\theta$, derivatives of the weights add terms of the form $(\nabla_\theta w_t)\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})$, which can complicate the estimator and its bias properties. In practice, frameworks often use stop-gradient to prevent backpropagating through such weights; the implications of that choice are discussed below.

# Effect of weighting/importance ratios on gradient magnitudes

Weights directly control gradient magnitudes. For a token at time $t$ with base gradient vector $g_t=\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})$, scaling by $w_t$ changes its norm to $\|w_t g_t\|=|w_t|\cdot\|g_t\|$. Thus large weights amplify gradient components coming from corresponding tokens, while small weights damp them. Importance weights—ratios of target to behavior probabilities—can be particularly extreme: a single token with a large importance ratio can dominate the entire gradient sum, producing high-variance updates and potentially destabilizing training.

Consider importance sampling more concretely. If sequences (or tokens) are sampled under some distribution $q$, and we wish to estimate gradients for the target distribution $p_\theta$, the unbiased estimator for the gradient of expected log-likelihood under $p_\theta$ typically involves weights proportional to $p_\theta/q$. For token-level IS, the per-step ratio is $r_t=p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})/q(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})$. Because probabilities are small, ratios can be arbitrarily large when $q$ assigns extremely small probability to tokens that $p_\theta$ believes are likely, and vice versa. The consequence is twofold: (1) the estimator remains unbiased (if computed correctly and weights are applied consistently), but (2) its variance can be enormous. Large variance translates into noisy gradient steps and slow or unstable convergence.

One common strategy is to use self-normalized importance sampling, where weights are normalized over sampled trajectories or tokens: let raw weights be $w_t^{\text{raw}}$ and normalized weights be $\tilde w_t = w_t^{\text{raw}} / \sum_{s} w_s^{\text{raw}}$. Normalization reduces variance by limiting the sum of magnitudes, but it introduces bias. Another approach is clipping weights to some maximum value, which bounds gradient magnitude at the cost of bias. A further method is to work in log-space and use log-weights with softmax-like normalization to avoid exploding multiplicative factors. The design of weighting mechanisms always trades bias and variance, and the best choice depends on the problem specifics and acceptable bias for variance control.

# Accumulation of noise across long sequences

When sequences are long, noise in per-token gradient estimates can accumulate. Because the sequence gradient is the sum of token gradients, variance adds across timesteps (for uncorrelated noise) or compounds in more complex ways when noise components are correlated. If each token gradient estimate has variance roughly $\sigma_t^2$, and noise is approximately independent across tokens, then the variance of the total gradient is approximately $\sum_t \sigma_t^2$. Thus, lengthening sequences increases total variance linearly in the number of tokens, which can harm training stability.

There are several sources of noise. First, stochastic sampling of tokens (e.g., sampling from the model during reinforcement learning or off-policy updates) injects sampling variance. Second, Monte Carlo approximations for expectations, such as approximating an expectation over future tokens or rewards, contribute additional noise. Third, importance weights themselves can be noisy, and as discussed above, extreme weights can produce heavy-tailed noise distributions where a few samples dominate the estimator.

Long sequence lengths also exacerbate issues with temporal credit assignment. A gradient term for early tokens influences the model's entire subsequent trajectory and therefore may interact in complex ways with later tokens’ gradients. Moreover, when intermediate computations are approximate or truncated (for example, truncated backpropagation through time), errors at one point in the sequence can propagate and amplify. Practically, several mitigations are used: using per-token baselines to reduce variance, controlling importance weights by clipping or normalization, using multiple independent samples and averaging, and designing model training objectives that focus on local predictions (e.g., teacher forcing with supervised targets) rather than solely on long-horizon trajectory objectives.

An additional phenomenon is the accumulation of bias when employing biased variance-reduction techniques repeatedly across a long sequence. For instance, weight clipping per-step introduces bias at each step, and the total bias across the sequence may grow with length. Understanding whether bias accumulates additively, multiplicatively, or in more complex ways is essential for predicting long-horizon behavior of the trained model.

# Practical computation: detaching, stop-gradient semantics, and numerical concerns

Implementations often need to combine gradients, importance weights, and additional computations such as baselines or normalizers. Two practical concerns are whether certain quantities should be differentiated through (i.e., whether they should be treated as functions of $\theta$) and numerical stability when dealing with small probabilities and large products.

Stop-gradient (detach) semantics are used to prevent backpropagation through values that are not supposed to influence gradients. For example, if using importance weights computed from a past snapshot of a policy or from a behavior policy that should not receive gradient updates, one typically detaches those weights from the computational graph. Concretely, if $r_t(\theta)$ is an importance weight but we want to treat it as a constant for gradient purposes, we replace it with $\text{stop\_grad}(r_t)$ in frameworks like TensorFlow or PyTorch. This avoids adding derivative terms like $(\nabla_\theta r_t)\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})$, which could otherwise produce biased or unintended updates.

When weights are normalized across tokens or trajectories, it is crucial to be deliberate about whether normalization should be included in differentiation. Normalizing can introduce dependence on $\theta$ through the denominator. If that dependence is undesired (e.g., when using self-normalized IS but wanting the numerator-only gradient), one can detach the denominator so gradients only flow through numerators. The choice affects bias and variance and must align with the theoretical estimator being implemented.

Numerical stability is another practical matter. Since token probabilities can be extremely small, directly computing products of probabilities for trajectories or direct ratios can underflow or overflow. Standard mitigations include working in log-space: replace products with sums of log-probabilities and importance ratios with exponentials of log-differences. For example, represent an importance ratio for a trajectory as
\[
r_{1:T} = \frac{\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})}{\prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})} = \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1}) - \log q(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})\right).
\]
Computing the exponent of a large-magnitude log difference can overflow, so it is common to subtract a maximum before exponentiating (the log-sum-exp trick) or to clip log-weights to a safe range. When normalizing weights across a batch of trajectories, do normalization in log-space robustly to prevent numerical errors.

Another numerical concern is gradient explosion due to large weights. Weight clipping, gradient clipping (by norm or value), and adaptive optimization methods (e.g., Adam with appropriate learning rates) help mitigate instability. If using second-order or natural-gradient methods, conditioning may change how weights affect updates and require additional scaling or preconditioning.

Finally, be mindful of in-place operations and autograd semantics; detaching a tensor in a computational graph prevents gradients from flowing to its upstream operations but still allows using its numerical value for downstream computations. Properly detaching quantities you do not wish to be differentiated through is essential to implement theoretical estimators correctly.

# Comparing per-step IS weighting vs trajectory-level weighting

There are two common ways to apply importance sampling when correcting for mismatch between a behavior policy $q$ (used to sample) and a target policy $p_\theta$ (whose gradients we want): per-step (per-token) weighting and trajectory-level (trajectory-wise) weighting. Each has different statistical and computational properties.

Trajectory-level weighting uses the full-trajectory importance ratio
\[
R_{1:T} = \prod_{t=1}^T \frac{p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})}{q(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})},
\]
and weights the entire trajectory's gradient contribution by $R_{1:T}$. For the sequence log-likelihood gradient, an unbiased estimator using trajectory-level IS for the expected gradient under target distribution can be written as
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\log p_\theta(X_{1:T})] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N R_{1:T}^{(n)} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{1:T}^{(n)}),
\]
where samples $x_{1:T}^{(n)}$ are drawn from $q$. This estimator is unbiased (assuming exact ratios and support condition) but typically has very high variance because $R_{1:T}$ is a product of many ratios and hence can be extremely concentrated: most trajectories have minuscule weights and a few have enormous weights. This heavy-tailed behavior leads to unstable estimates and slow convergence when sequences are long.

Per-step weighting applies importance correction at each timestep instead of to the whole trajectory. Two variants are common: 1) weight each per-token gradient by the per-step ratio $r_t$, giving an estimator
\[
\sum_{t=1}^T r_t \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1});
\]
or 2) use cumulative partial ratios up to time $t$, i.e., $R_{1:t}=\prod_{s=1}^t r_s$, weighting partial contributions accordingly. The first variant corrects only locally and is often biased for trajectory-level expectations but can dramatically reduce variance. The second variant interpolates between local and global corrections: weighting by $R_{1:t}$ provides an unbiased correction for expectations involving prefixes up to $t$, but still suffers from multiplicative growth in variance as $t$ increases.

Intuitively, per-step local weighting reduces variance because each ratio is only for one step and is therefore less extreme than the product over many steps. It also allows credit assignment to focus on local mismatches between $p_\theta$ and $q$; if $q$ is a moderately different policy, local ratios remain manageable. However, per-step weighting may not correctly reweight the joint distribution of entire sequences, which matters if the goal is to estimate expectations under the full target distribution. Trajectory-level weighting is correct for joint expectations but is often impractical except for very short sequences or when $q$ is very close to $p_\theta$.

A common compromise is to use per-step weighting with normalization or clipping, or to use control variates and baselines to adjust for the introduced bias. Another approach is to use truncated importance sampling where ratios are multiplied only for a window of recent steps, which limits variance growth at the cost of bias for long-range dependencies.

Choosing between per-step and trajectory-level weighting depends on the objective: if you must produce unbiased estimates for trajectory-level expectations and sequences are short or $q$ is close to $p_\theta$, trajectory-level IS may be appropriate. If variance control is the priority and local corrections suffice for learning, per-step methods (possibly with careful normalization) are often more practical.

# Summary and practical recommendations

The mathematical decomposition of sequence log-likelihood gradients into per-token gradients gives a clear framework for designing learning algorithms for sequence models. Weighting schemes can be applied per-token or per-trajectory; they rescale gradient contributions and thus control which tokens have more influence. Importance sampling weights correct distributional mismatch but tend to increase variance, and products of ratios over long sequences make trajectory-level IS particularly high-variance.

From a practical standpoint, prefer methods that limit variance growth: use per-token corrections when exact trajectory-level correctness is not essential, normalize or clip importance weights to keep gradient magnitudes bounded, work in log-space to avoid numerical underflow/overflow, and use stop-gradient/detach to avoid unintended differentiation through weights or normalizers. When sequences are long, be aware that noise accumulates approximately additively across timesteps, and mitigate by variance reduction techniques (baselines, control variates, averaging, gradient clipping). Finally, always check the bias-variance trade-off your chosen method introduces and validate empirically whether the bias introduced by normalization or clipping is acceptable for your application.
