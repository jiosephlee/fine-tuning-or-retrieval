Chapter 5: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Clipped Surrogate Objectives

Introduction

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a class of policy-gradient algorithms designed to give high sample efficiency with stable and simple updates. PPO became widely used in both continuous-control and discrete problems because it balances the need to change a policy toward higher-reward actions while avoiding destructive large updates that invalidate the gradient estimates. In practice this is achieved by optimizing a surrogate objective that limits how far the new policy can move from the old policy in one update. This chapter explains the surrogate objective, the role of the importance ratio, the clipped objective that is the most common variant of PPO, and alternatives such as KL-penalties or trust-region motivations. It then focuses on practical matters that arise when applying PPO to language models (LMs): whether to operate at the per-token or per-sequence level, how advantages are estimated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), and how mini-batching and multiple epochs interact with off-policy concerns.

# PPO surrogate objective and intuition

The core idea of policy-gradient methods is to directly optimize the expected return by adjusting the policy parameters to increase the probability of actions that resulted in higher-than-expected returns. If the policy is parameterized by $\theta$ and produces probabilities $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$, the canonical policy-gradient objective for a single state-action sample is to change $\theta$ in proportion to the advantage $A(s,a)$ times the gradient of the log-probability: $\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) A(s,a)$. Aggregating over data, this yields an unbiased gradient estimator for the expected advantage.

PPO reframes the update as optimizing a surrogate objective that measures how much the new policy improves expected advantage relative to the old policy while constraining how far $\pi_\theta$ moves from the previous policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. The simplest surrogate objective is the expected importance-weighted advantage:
\[ L^{\text{PG}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a\sim \text{data}}\left[ r(\theta)\, A(s,a) \right], \]
where $r(\theta)$ is the importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}$. This objective aligns with the policy-gradient theorem: taking the gradient of $L^{\text{PG}}$ recovers the policy-gradient estimator (assuming data sampled from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and ignoring baseline/value terms). Intuitively, if $A(s,a)>0$, we want $r(\theta)$ to be greater than 1 so that the update increases the probability of the action; if $A(s,a)<0$, we want $r(\theta)$ to be less than 1 to decrease its probability.

However, directly optimizing $L^{\text{PG}}$ can be unstable because the importance ratio $r(\theta)$ may become very large for rarely sampled actions, or the optimizer may take a large step that improves the surrogate but drastically changes the policy so that subsequent gradient estimates are no longer valid. PPO addresses this by modifying the surrogate so that improvements are trusted only within a "proximal" region around the old policy. This encourages small, well-behaved updates: enough to improve expected returns, but not large enough to invalidate the data distribution or cause catastrophic changes.

# Importance ratio r(θ) = π_θ(a)/π_θ_old(a) and its role

The importance ratio
\[ r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)} \]
is central in PPO because it connects the new policy to the data collected under the old policy. When we compute expectations under the old policy (the data distribution) but want to reason about the new policy, importance weighting is the standard technique to correct for distribution mismatch. In the surrogate objective, $r(\theta)$ rescales advantages computed under the old policy so they reflect how much the new policy would favor those actions.

There are several important intuitions and practical considerations about $r(\theta)$:

- When $r(\theta)>1$, the new policy places more probability mass on that action than the old policy. If the action had positive advantage $A>0$, increasing $r(\theta)$ increases the surrogate objective, which is desirable. Conversely, if $A<0$, a large $r(\theta)$ is penalized by the objective.
- When $r(\theta)$ is extreme (very large or very small), it indicates that the policy change is substantial at that state-action pair. Large importance ratios inflate the contribution of a single sample to the objective and can destabilize learning because the variance of gradient estimates increases.
- Since importance sampling is unbiased but can have high variance, controlling $r(\theta)$ is essential for stable optimization. PPO uses clipping (or alternative regularization) to bound the effective influence of large importance ratios while still allowing small, informative policy changes.
- The ratio is computed per sample (per state-action). In sequential tasks such as RL for LMs, the state may be the full history and the action a chosen token; hence $r(\theta)$ can vary token-by-token and can compound over sequences when considering sequence-level probabilities.

The importance ratio is also a diagnostic: monitoring its distribution during training helps detect whether the policy is changing too fast (many ratios far from 1) or too slowly. Typical PPO implementations log statistics like the mean, standard deviation, and the fraction of ratios clipped to assess training behavior.

# Clipped objective: min(r(θ)A, clip(r(θ),1-ε,1+ε)A)

The canonical PPO objective proposed by Schulman et al. uses a clipped surrogate that prevents excessively large policy updates for any single sample. The clipped objective for a single sample is
\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \min\big( r(\theta) A, \; \text{clip}\big(r(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\big) A \big) \right], \]
where $\epsilon>0$ is a small hyperparameter (commonly $0.1$ or $0.2$) and the expectation is over data collected under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

To unpack this formula, consider two cases depending on the sign of the advantage $A$:

- If $A>0$, the unclipped term $r(\theta) A$ increases with $r(\theta)$. The clipped term caps $r(\theta)$ at $1+\epsilon$, so the minimum selects the smaller of the unclipped and clipped values. Thus, the objective does not benefit from increasing $r(\theta)$ beyond $1+\epsilon$ for positive-advantage actions. This prevents the policy from overly privileging particular high-advantage actions in a single update.
- If $A<0$, the unclipped term $r(\theta) A$ becomes more negative when $r(\theta)$ increases, which is bad because we want to reduce the probability of this action. The clipping on the ratio at $1-\epsilon$ ensures that for negative advantages, the objective does not reward making $r(\theta)$ too small (i.e., decreasing probability too much) beyond $\epsilon$. Thus, it prevents extreme probability reductions for negative-advantage actions.

The min(.,.) construction makes the clipped surrogate a lower bound on the unclipped surrogate. At each sample the objective takes the conservative value: either the true importance-weighted advantage if that is within the trust region, or the clipped proxy if outside. This has two major consequences. First, it leaves the gradient unaffected when $r(\theta)$ is within $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$, so natural small updates behave like standard policy gradient. Second, when $r(\theta)$ would move outside the interval, the gradient is zero beyond the clipping point for that sample in the direction that would further increase the deviation. The clipping is thus directly capping the incentive for updating probabilities too far in a single step.

Clipping achieves a simple, computationally cheap trust-region-like behavior without needing expensive second-order estimations. It is easy to implement and tends to work well across tasks. However, clipping is blunt: once a sample's ratio is outside the interval, additional beneficial information in the gradient is ignored for that sample. This is usually acceptable because PPO can make multiple small updates and gradually move the whole distribution while respecting the clipping at each mini-update.

# Trust-region motivation and KL penalty alternatives

PPO's clipping mechanism is motivated by the idea of a trust region: do not move the policy too far from the current policy in a single update. A previously-proposed method, Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), formalized this as a constrained optimization:
\[ \max_\theta \ \mathbb{E}[r(\theta) A] \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}\big[ \text{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s), \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)) \big] \le \delta, \]
where KL denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the old and new policies averaged over states. TRPO enforces this constraint with a second-order optimization step and is theoretically principled, but computationally heavier.

PPO provides two practical approximations to the trust-region idea: clipping (the variant above) and a KL-penalty variant. The KL-penalty variant optimizes a penalized objective:
\[ L^{\text{KL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[ r(\theta) A \big] - c\, \mathbb{E}\big[ \text{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s), \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)) \big], \]
where $c>0$ weights the KL penalty. The penalty discourages large KL divergences but does not hard bound them. One can adaptively tune $c$ to meet a target average KL, increasing $c$ if the observed KL exceeds the target and decreasing it if the KL is too small.

Each approach has tradeoffs. Clipping is simpler, cheap, and robust; it avoids hyperparameter tuning of an adaptive KL coefficient in many settings. The KL-penalty offers a smoother constraint: rather than abruptly cutting off incentives beyond $\epsilon$, it continuously penalizes divergence, which can sometimes yield more stable or efficient progress when the right penalty weight is known. The KL-penalty also provides a more direct link to TRPO's theoretical guarantee. In practice, many implementations combine both strategies (clipping for per-sample robustness and an overall KL penalty or monitoring to keep the policy in a reasonable trust region).

A key perspective is that both clipping and KL penalties aim to constrain the expected change in policy measured either implicitly per sample (clipping) or explicitly via KL divergence. The choice between them depends on computational constraints, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and task-specific behavior.

# Per-action (token-level) vs per-episode (sequence-level) implementations

In many RL tasks a single action is selected in a single state, but in sequential tasks like language modeling (autoregressive generation) the policy repeatedly selects tokens, so we must decide the granularity at which PPO is applied. Two common approaches are per-action (token-level) and per-episode (sequence-level) implementations.

Per-action (token-level) PPO treats each autoregressive step as a separate state-action sample and computes advantages, importance ratios, and surrogate losses at the token level. For a sequence $x_{1:T}$, the policy probability factorizes as
\[ \pi_\theta(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^T \pi_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}). \]
Token-level PPO computes ratios
\[ r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x_t\mid x_{<t})} \]
and optimizes a surrogate that aggregates per-token clipped terms, using token-level advantages $A_t$. The main advantages of the token-level approach are better sample efficiency (each sequence yields many training samples), more localized credit assignment (which token choices contributed to a reward), and compatibility with common RL baselines and GAE computed over time steps. Token-level updates also let the policy learn to prefer or avoid particular tokens given different contexts.

However, token-level PPO has pitfalls. The per-token probabilities multiply to produce a sequence probability; changing many tokens slightly can lead to large sequence-level changes. If rewards are provided only at the end of a sequence (e.g., a scalar quality score), token-level advantage estimation becomes noisy because the same sequence-level reward is attributed to many token actions. Careful advantage estimation (using temporal-difference and GAE) is required in this case to avoid high variance and poor credit assignment. Another complication is that many token choices are extremely common or deterministic under the base language model; clipping per-token may either be too permissive or too restrictive depending on the desired degree of divergence from the reference.

Per-episode (sequence-level) PPO treats the entire generated sequence as a single action and computes a single ratio
\[ r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(x_{1:T})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x_{1:T})} = \prod_{t=1}^T r_t(\theta). \]
The advantage is evaluated per-sequence (e.g., reward from human preference or a scalar annotator), and the surrogate objective operates on sequence-level ratios and advantages. Sequence-level PPO directly optimizes the probability of whole sequences that produce high rewards, which is natural when the reward is only meaningful at sequence completion and when dependencies across tokens are strong.

The sequence-level approach has its own challenges: the ratio is a product of many per-token ratios and can therefore be numerically unstable or extremely large/small, increasing variance severely. Moreover, because each sequence yields a single sample, sample complexity decreases relative to token-level training. In practice, many LM RL setups (such as RL from Human Feedback) use a hybrid approach: compute per-token ratios and advantages but derive the advantage from a sequence-level reward signal distributed across tokens using GAE or simple temporal assignment. Another pragmatic approach is to include an explicit KL penalty to the reference model at the sequence level to prevent runaway divergence.

Choosing between token-level and sequence-level implementations depends on the structure of the reward, the desired regularization relative to the base LM, and computational considerations. Sequence-level choices are appropriate for global rewards with strong inter-token dependencies, while token-level approaches are preferable when token-level credit assignment is feasible or when sample efficiency is paramount.

# Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) and advantage inputs

A central ingredient in PPO's surrogate objective is the advantage estimate $A(s,a)$, which measures how much better an action $a$ at state $s$ performed compared to the expected value of being in state $s$. Accurate advantage estimates reduce variance and improve learning speed. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is a popular method to construct low-variance, low-bias advantage estimates by combining multi-step temporal-difference (TD) errors.

Given a value function estimate $V_\phi(s)$ (a learned critic parameterized by $\phi$), define the TD residual at time $t$ with discount factor $\gamma$ as
\[ \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t). \]
GAE defines the advantage as a weighted sum of k-step TD residuals:
\[ \hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l \, \delta_{t+l}. \]
The hyperparameter $\lambda\in[0,1]$ interpolates between high-variance, low-bias Monte Carlo returns ($\lambda=1$) and low-variance, high-bias one-step TD ($\lambda=0$). When $\lambda$ is closer to 1, longer-term rewards are taken into account, reducing bias at the cost of variance. In practice values like $\lambda=0.95$ are common.

GAE is particularly important for sequential tasks and for RL with sparse or delayed rewards (common in LM reward settings), because it provides a way to distribute sequence-level rewards across time steps in a principled way. When rewards are only at the end of a sequence, the TD residuals will propagate back that final reward, and GAE will smooth the advantage signal across tokens depending on $\gamma$ and $\lambda$.

Implementation-wise, GAE requires a value function that can estimate $V(s)$ for each time step. For language models, the critic can be a separate network (or a copy of the LM with heads) that takes the same context and outputs scalar value estimates per token. Stability of the critic matters: a poor value baseline increases variance and can mislead policy updates. Techniques to stabilize the critic include using target networks, value clipping, high-quality supervised pretraining, and regularization.

When using GAE in PPO, practitioners often normalize advantages (subtract mean and divide by standard deviation) within a batch to stabilize learning across varying reward scales. The normalized $\hat{A}$ is then used in the surrogate objective and for updating the policy. For LM applications where reward scales can be heterogeneous (e.g., human preference scores, classifier outputs), normalizing the advantage is often essential.

# Mini-batching, epoch updates, and off-policy considerations in PPO

PPO is typically implemented with repeated passes (epochs) over collected data using mini-batches, which raises questions about on-policy vs off-policy behavior. The simplest loop is:

1. Collect a set of trajectories (or dialogues, sequences) by rolling out the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for a certain number of steps or episodes.
2. Compute returns and advantages (e.g., with GAE) using a value function $V_\phi$.
3. Freeze $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and perform $K$ epochs of optimization on the surrogate objective using mini-batches drawn from the collected dataset, updating policy parameters $\theta$ and possibly the critic $\phi$.
4. After the epochs, use the final $\theta$ to collect new data and repeat.

This repeated-epochs approach improves sample efficiency because each collected sample contributes multiple gradient updates. But it implicitly uses data that is no longer exactly on-policy after the first parameter update. The clipped objective and small step sizes mitigate the mismatch; the importance ratio $r(\theta)$ captures the deviation and clipping caps its effect. In practice, PPO treats the collected data as "approximately on-policy" for a few epochs. The choice of epochs $K$ and mini-batch size determines how off-policy the optimization becomes; larger $K$ and smaller mini-batches increase the effective off-policy usage and can harm stability.

Important practical points:

- Use a reasonable number of epochs (e.g., 3–10) and a batch size that provides a good balance between gradient estimate variance and the number of parameter update steps per sample. The original PPO papers often used 4–10 epochs.
- Use shuffle-and-minibatch: shuffle the collected dataset each epoch and iterate through mini-batches, which helps reduce correlations among samples and improves convergence.
- Learning rate scheduling and gradient clipping for the optimizer are helpful because repeated epochs can amplify optimizer updates and lead to overshooting.
- Monitor the fraction of clipped samples and the average KL divergence between the old and new policy. If the KL grows too large, reduce learning rate, reduce the number of epochs, or increase clipping $\epsilon$.

Off-policy considerations: PPO is not designed as a fully off-policy method. Applying it to arbitrary replay buffers where data may be generated by much older policies can lead to high variance in importance weights and degrade performance. If off-policy data must be used, techniques like weighted importance sampling with truncation, retrace-style corrections, or trusting only recent data can help. Alternatively, use algorithms tailored for off-policy learning (e.g., Q-learning based methods or actor-critic algorithms with off-policy corrections).

In language-model RL specifically, collected sequences are often expensive (e.g., human feedback), so practitioners attempt to reuse data more aggressively. Careful tuning of epochs, conservative clipping, and explicit KL penalties to the base model are common strategies to avoid instability from reusing older data.

# Practical issues when applying PPO to language models

Applying PPO to large autoregressive language models has become a standard approach for fine-tuning models to human preferences and other sequence-level objectives (e.g., RL from Human Feedback). However, LMs introduce several distinctive challenges compared to typical RL domains.

Large action space and probability factorization. The action space (vocabulary) can be large (tens of thousands). Per-token probabilities are small and have complex structure conditioned on large contexts. Because the sequence probability factorizes, small token-level changes accumulate over time, which can lead to substantial sequence-level shifts even when each token's ratio is within the clipping range. This amplifies the need for global regularization (e.g., a KL penalty to the original pretrained LM) so that the fine-tuned policy does not drift far from the pretrained distribution, preserving fluency and factuality.

Credit assignment with sparse sequence rewards. Many LM objectives are provided at the sequence level (e.g., human preference, style scores). Token-level PPO must distribute these rewards across time steps. GAE helps by propagating the reward back through time, but when sequences are short or rewards are highly sparse, variance can remain high. Common practical tricks include shaping the reward to give per-token signals, using intermediate critic predictions that estimate per-token value, or using reward models that provide denser feedback.

KL regularization to reference model. When fine-tuning a pretrained LM, it is common to include an explicit KL penalty against a reference policy (the pretrained LM) to prevent the policy from diverging into degenerate text that achieves high reward but is uninterpretable or adversarial. This takes the form of subtracting $\beta\,\text{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}, \pi_\theta)$ from the reward or incorporating it into the PPO objective as an extra loss term. The KL penalty also helps keep the expected token log-probabilities close to those learned during pretraining, preserving language quality.

Value function and critic design. The critic must estimate values for the same states (contexts) encountered by the policy. In LM settings, the critic is typically another network that consumes the context and possibly model hidden states and outputs per-token scalar values. Training the critic is tricky because the state space is huge and distributions shift as the policy updates. Practitioners often tie the critic architecture closely to the policy network to leverage shared representations, or use separate heads on the LM. Stabilizing the critic with weight decay, gradient clipping, and limiting learning rate can improve PPO performance.

Handling variable-length sequences and padding. Language tasks often involve variable-length trajectories. When batching, one must mask out padding tokens when computing losses, ratios, and advantages. Masking is also important for GAE and value computation to avoid propagating rewards or TD errors past sequence ends.

Entropy bonus and exploration. Entropy regularization (adding $-c_H \mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)]$ as an objective term) encourages the policy to be stochastic, aiding exploration and preventing premature collapse to deterministic responses. In LM fine-tuning, too much entropy regularization can conflict with the desire to adhere to preferences; too little can cause mode collapse. Entropy is often weaker relative to continuous-control tasks because pretrained LMs already have strong stochastic priors.

Batching and sequence-level KL monitoring. Because sequences are expensive (e.g., human feedback), large-batch collection is less common. Practitioners carefully monitor the mean KL between old and new policies across entire sequences as an additional safety metric. If KL increases beyond thresholds, training can be paused or hyperparameters adjusted.

Reward scaling and advantage normalization. Rewards from human preference models or classifiers may be arbitrarily scaled. Before computing advantages and optimizing PPO, it is common to normalize rewards and advantages within a batch to keep gradient magnitudes stable. This affects how clipping and KL penalty hyperparameters should be tuned.

Numerical stability of per-sequence ratios. If sequence-level ratios are used, their product form can underflow/overflow. Implementations often compute log-probabilities and work with log-ratios
\[ \log r(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}), \]
and exponentiate carefully if needed, or directly use log-domain clipping schemes.

Practical heuristics. Common "rules of thumb" that help PPO in LM settings include: using small policy learning rates, limiting number of epochs per batch, monitoring KL to a reference LM and enforcing a maximum, employing per-token clipping combined with a sequence-level KL penalty, using GAE with $\lambda$ near 0.95, normalizing advantages and rewards, and including an entropy bonus and/or value loss with clipping.

# Conclusion: Putting it together

PPO and its clipped surrogate objective provide a practical, effective way to perform policy-gradient optimization while limiting destructive updates. The importance ratio $r(\theta)$ plays the central role of connecting new policies to data from the old policy, and clipping that ratio yields a simple trust-region mechanism. Alternatives such as KL penalties or TRPO-style constraints offer other ways to control divergence. When applying PPO to language models, the choice between token-level and sequence-level implementations, careful advantage estimation via GAE, and mindful mini-batching and epoching are all crucial to stable, sample-efficient learning. Practical engineering choices—value-function architecture, KL regularization to a reference LM, advantage normalization, and monitoring of ratio and KL statistics—often determine whether PPO training succeeds in producing high-quality, aligned language model behavior.
