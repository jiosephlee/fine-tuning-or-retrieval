Introduction

Modern deep learning architectures rely heavily on two building blocks: convolutional layers, which are dominant in vision models, and Transformer layers, which power language models and increasingly are used for vision and multimodal tasks. Understanding the weight structure of these layers—how parameters are organized, what each parameter does, and how we can reinterpret those parameters as collections of neuron- or channel-wise vectors—is essential both to analyze model behavior and to design effective finetuning strategies. Finetuning large pretrained models typically avoids updating all weights because full updates are expensive in compute, memory, and data demand, and may overfit. Instead, practitioners often target a subset or a low-dimensional reparameterization of weights such as adapters, low-rank updates, or orthogonal transforms. This chapter explains the canonical parameterizations of convolutional, depthwise/pointwise, Transformer attention, and fully connected layers, and then connects those structures to practical finetuning targets and trade-offs in memory and compute.

# Standard convolutional layer weights and interpretation as channel-wise neuron vectors

A standard 2D convolutional layer maps an input tensor of shape $(C_{\text{in}}, H, W)$ to an output tensor of shape $(C_{\text{out}}, H', W')$ using a bank of learnable convolutional kernels. The weight tensor is typically denoted as $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w}$, where $K_h \times K_w$ is the spatial kernel size. There is also often a bias vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$, one bias per output channel. The standard forward operation for a given output channel $o$ is
\[
y_o = \sum_{i=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} W_{o,i} * x_i + b_o,
\]
where $W_{o,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_h \times K_w}$ and $*$ denotes spatial convolution.

A useful perspective is to interpret each output channel as a neuron with a flattened weight vector that connects to all input channels and spatial positions. If we flatten the spatial dimensions and input channels, each output channel $o$ corresponds to a vector
\[
w_o \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w}
\]
obtained by concatenating all $W_{o,i}$ for $i=1,\dots,C_{\text{in}}$. The convolution then computes correlations between this flattened filter and corresponding patches of the input. This per-output-channel view aligns convolutional layers with fully connected layers applied patch-wise: each spatial location of the input provides a vector of size $C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w$, and the convolution applies the same linear map (the matrix with rows $w_o^\top$) at every spatial location. This re-interpretation is especially valuable when thinking about which parameters to adapt: changing a single output-channel vector $w_o$ modifies the linear response of one "neuron" across all spatial positions and across all input channels and positions inside the kernel.

From an adaptation or pruning standpoint, the per-output-channel view suggests natural granularities for parameter modification. One can adapt biases $b_o$ to change channel gains independently. One can adapt entire $w_o$ vectors to change the receptive-field filters for particular channels. One can also think of per-input-channel slices $W_{:,i}$ (all output channels to a particular input channel) as input-side projection vectors, which affect how information from a specific input channel is distributed across outputs. Both views—output-channel-centric and input-channel-centric—are valid and useful depending on the target: to modify feature extraction performed by a few filters, adapt output-channel weights; to remap feature channels before combination, adapt input-channel slices.

# Depthwise and pointwise convolutions and channel-wise parameterization

Depthwise separable convolutions decompose a standard convolution into two (often cheaper) operations: depthwise convolution followed by pointwise convolution. This factorization underlies many efficient architectures (e.g., MobileNet) and also produces a parameter structure that is extremely amenable to channel-wise adaptation.

A depthwise convolution has a weight tensor $W^{\text{dw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} \times 1 \times K_h \times K_w}$ when we choose to keep the number of channels equal and perform one spatial filter per input channel. More often written as $W^{\text{dw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w}$, a depthwise convolution applies an independent $K_h \times K_w$ filter to each input channel, producing $C_{\text{in}}$ intermediate channels. Concretely,
\[
\tilde{y}_i = W^{\text{dw}}_i * x_i + b^{\text{dw}}_i,
\]
for each channel $i$. There is no mixing across channels in this step.

A pointwise convolution is a $1\times 1$ convolution, with weight tensor $W^{\text{pw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times 1 \times 1}$, which reduces to a matrix $W^{\text{pw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}}}$. This performs a learned linear combination across channels at each spatial location:
\[
y_o = \sum_{i=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} W^{\text{pw}}_{o,i}\, \tilde{y}_i + b^{\text{pw}}_o.
\]

The depthwise layer offers an especially natural channel-wise parameterization: each input channel has its own spatial filter. If we want to modify the spatial filtering behavior of a particular channel without affecting cross-channel mixing, we need only update the corresponding depthwise kernel $W^{\text{dw}}_i$. Conversely, the pointwise matrix governs channel mixing; each column of $W^{\text{pw}}$ is a "channel selector" describing how one input channel contributes to all output channels. Viewing $W^{\text{pw}}$ column-wise, column $i$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$ that says how the $i$th input feature is redistributed among outputs. Viewing it row-wise, row $o$ is a vector that readouts a linear combination of all intermediate channels to produce the $o$th output channel.

This factorization suggests two axes for compact finetuning. If the objective is to alter spatial response patterns, one can adapt only depthwise filters, which have parameter count proportional to $C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w$. If the objective is to alter channel interactions or downstream feature recombination, one can adapt only the pointwise weights, which is often much cheaper: $C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}}$ for pointwise versus $C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w$ for full convs. In many cases with large $C_{\text{in}}, C_{\text{out}}$ and small kernels (e.g., $3\times 3$), pointwise weights dominate parameter count; targeting them with low-rank adapters yields big gains for little cost. Depthwise layers are also naturally compatible with per-channel scalars (channel-wise affine transforms) that rescale or bias each channel independently, an approach used in lightweight adapter designs.

# Transformer attention: query/key/value/projection matrices and where to apply adapters

Transformer attention layers implement a content-based weighted sum of value vectors where weights are computed from queries and keys. For multi-head attention, the input is a sequence of vectors $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$, where $T$ is sequence length and $d$ is model dimension. For each attention head $h$ we have linear projection matrices for queries, keys, and values:
\[
Q_h = X W^Q_h,\qquad K_h = X W^K_h,\qquad V_h = X W^V_h,
\]
with $W^Q_h, W^K_h, W^V_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}$ and $d_h$ usually equals $d / H$ for $H$ heads. Concatenating across heads, practitioners often use combined matrices $W^Q, W^K, W^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ by stacking head projections. The attention weights for head $h$ are computed by scaled dot-product:
\[
A_h = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_h K_h^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right),
\]
and the head output is $O_h = A_h V_h$. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected by an output matrix $W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$:
\[
\text{Attention}(X) = \operatorname{Concat}(O_1,\dots,O_H) W^O.
\]

Thus, the attention layer parameters consist mainly of $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ (plus biases if used). Each of these matrices has a distinct role. $W^Q$ and $W^K$ set the space in which content similarity is measured; small changes to these can drastically change attention patterns because they affect the dot products that form softmax logits. $W^V$ determines what content is retrieved once attention is computed; modifying $W^V$ affects the information placed into outputs but does not directly change which positions attend to which. $W^O$ mixes the concatenated head outputs back into the model dimension; adapting $W^O$ is often used to change how head outputs are recombined.

When designing finetuning interventions (adapters), we need to consider both expressivity and stability. Many practical techniques insert small adapter modules or low-rank updates into one or more of these matrices. For example, practitioners often place adapters into the output projection $W^O$ or into $W^V$ because these affect what information is written into the residual stream and are less likely than $W^Q$ or $W^K$ to trigger large, destabilizing shifts in attention patterns. Low-rank updates to $W^Q$ or $W^K$ are also used (e.g., LoRA applied to attention) when one needs to change attention behavior in a targeted, parameter-efficient way. The choice reflects a trade-off: altering $W^Q/W^K$ can redirect attention and may give stronger downstream effects for tasks requiring attention reconfiguration, but risks unwanted perturbation of attention stability; altering $W^V/W^O$ tends to be safer and can still implement many task-specific transformations.

A common parameter-efficient strategy is to apply low-rank adapters to these matrices. For a given matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (representing one of $Q,K,V,O$), one can learn a pair of small rank-$r$ matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ and add their product to $W$ during finetuning:
\[
W' = W + \alpha A B,
\]
where $\alpha$ is a scaling hyperparameter. This keeps inference cost nearly identical (since $W$ is unchanged at inference if one folds the update, or the low-rank adapter can be applied on the fly) while allowing expressive adaptation with only $O(2 d r)$ parameters per adapted matrix. Choosing which attention matrix to adapt—$Q$, $K$, $V$, or $O$—depends on the task and the desired behavior: $Q/K$ for attention pattern changes, $V/O$ for output-space changes.

# Fully connected (MLP) layers and per-neuron parameterization

The Transformer architecture also contains position-wise feedforward (MLP) blocks, typically consisting of two fully connected layers with a nonlinearity in between. A canonical form is
\[
\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2,
\]
where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}} \times d}$ expands dimensionality from $d$ to $d_{\text{ff}}$ (often $d_{\text{ff}} \approx 4d$), $\phi$ is a nonlinearity like GELU, and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{ff}}}$ projects back to model dimension.

We can interpret $W_1$ and $W_2$ in per-neuron terms. $W_1$ defines $d_{\text{ff}}$ hidden neurons, each with an input weight vector $w^{(1)}_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and bias $b^{(1)}_j$. The pre-activation for neuron $j$ is $z_j = (w^{(1)}_j)^\top x + b^{(1)}_j$. The nonlinearity produces activation $a_j = \phi(z_j)$, and $W_2$ contains $d$ readout vectors $w^{(2)}_o \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}}$, which combine the hidden activations $a$ to produce output components. Thus each hidden neuron can be seen as a basis function, and $W_2$ forms coefficients that recombine these basis functions to produce output coordinates.

This per-neuron view clarifies where to target finetuning: modifying certain rows of $W_1$ (i.e., particular hidden neurons) changes the basis functions available; modifying columns of $W_2$ changes how those basis functions affect each output coordinate. Many adapter approaches insert small bottleneck layers inside the FFN block, for example replacing $W_2$ by $W_2 + A B$ with low-rank $A,B$ or inserting an adapter between $W_1$ and the nonlinearity to alter hidden activations. Another common lightweight choice is to learn per-neuron biases or scalings. For instance, adding a per-hidden-unit scale $\gamma_j$ gives $a_j = \gamma_j \phi(z_j)$; this introduces only $d_{\text{ff}}$ extra parameters but can significantly redirect the MLP output by modulating contributions of individual neurons.

Adapting entire $W_1$ or $W_2$ is parameter-expensive because $d_{\text{ff}}$ is typically large; therefore per-neuron parameterizations and low-rank modifications are attractive. Importantly, because FFNs operate position-wise, adapting these parameters affects each token independently and thus commonly suffices for tasks requiring changes in token-level transformations without changing attention patterns.

# Practical considerations: memory, compute, and where low-rank or orthogonal transforms can be applied

When choosing which weights to finetune, one must weigh expressivity versus resource cost and stability. Memory and compute constraints directly limit how many parameters can be updated and how much additional temporary memory (for optimizer states) is required. Updating all weights of a large model multiplies memory cost by the number of optimizer moment tensors (e.g., Adam requires storing first and second moment estimates), often requiring many gigabytes. Parameter-efficient approaches reduce this burden by updating only a small fraction of parameters or by introducing compact learnable modules.

Low-rank adapters (LoRA-style) are widely used because they add only $O(d r)$ parameters per adapted matrix and do not require storing full optimizer states for the frozen base model. They are particularly effective for large linear maps such as attention $W^Q,W^K,W^V,W^O$ and MLP matrices $W_1,W_2$. Applying low-rank updates in the factorized form $A B$ shifts the memory–compute trade-off: training computes gradients for $A$ and $B$ (small), but at inference time one can fold $A B$ into $W$ if one wants to produce a single dense matrix and avoid extra runtime cost. The rank $r$ controls expressivity: small $r$ can implement many meaningful changes, but tasks that demand complex reparameterization may need larger $r$.

Orthogonal transforms and structured parameterizations are another route. Instead of arbitrary low-rank updates, one might constrain adaptation to rotations or orthogonal matrices, which preserve certain norms and can stabilize training. For example, an adapter can be implemented as $W' = U W$, where $U$ is near-orthogonal and parameterized via a skew-symmetric matrix exponentiation or via a small set of parameters that generate orthogonal transformations. These preserves feature norms and often avoid collapsing or amplifying activations, which is beneficial when stability is a concern. Orthogonal adapters are more expensive to parameterize than simple low-rank layers but can be regularized to remain close to identity, offering a safe, structured way to adapt.

Compute considerations include both additional FLOPs introduced by adapters during training and inference, and the overhead of dealing with sparse or block updates. Depthwise or channel-wise adapters tend to be cheap in compute because they operate channel-wise or per-element and don't introduce large matrix multiplications. Low-rank adapters add small matrix multiplications of shapes $(d \times r)$ and $(r \times d)$ which cost $O(d r)$ per token instead of $O(d^2)$. In mixed precision training, adapter parameters and optimizer states can be stored in reduced precision to reduce memory. Another practical trick is to freeze most of the model and only enable gradient computation (and optimizer states) for targeted adapters; frameworks usually let you exclude parameters to save optimizer memory.

Where to apply adapters depends on the task. For language-model finetuning aimed at style or domain adaptation without changing syntax-sensitivity, inserting adapters into FFN layers often suffices. For tasks that require changing how context is aggregated—e.g., long-range dependency emphasis—adapting $W^Q$ and $W^K$ or their low-rank surrogates is appropriate. When the model already learns powerful attention patterns and one only needs to nudge outputs, adapting $W^V$ or $W^O$ is a good compromise. In vision, pointwise convolutions (the $1\times 1$ mixing layers) are often the heaviest parameter blocks, so low-rank updates applied there or channel-wise scalings in depthwise separable designs provide much of the benefit at low cost.

Finally, it is important to consider layer normalization and residual connections. Adapters inserted after normalization or inside residual branches may have different effects because normalization rescales activations. Simple learned scale-and-bias terms around normalization layers or at residual branch entrances are often highly effective and extremely cheap. Residual pathways mean that small adapter outputs are combined with the original signal additively; therefore small, low-rank adapters can have outsized influence due to iterative compounding across layers, especially in deep networks.

Conclusion

Understanding the internal structure of convolutional and Transformer layers—interpreting convolutional filters as per-channel neuron vectors, distinguishing depthwise and pointwise roles, and decomposing attention into $Q,K,V,O$ matrices and MLPs into per-neuron basis functions—provides a principled foundation for targeted finetuning. Choices such as adapting depthwise filters, pointwise matrices, attention value/output projections, or FFN bottlenecks map directly to different behaviors and resource profiles. Practical finetuning methods like low-rank adapters, channel-wise scalings, and orthogonal transforms let practitioners trade parameter efficiency for expressivity and stability. Careful selection of which matrices to adapt, how to parameterize adapters, and where to place them relative to normalization and residual connections yields strong finetuning performance while controlling memory and compute demands.
