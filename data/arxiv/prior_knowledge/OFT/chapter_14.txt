# Introduction

Evaluating generative image models is one of the most challenging but essential tasks in modern computer vision. Unlike standard supervised learning where a single scalar label or a well-defined loss can indicate success, generative models must be assessed across multiple axes: the realism or perceptual quality of images, the diversity and distributional match to a target dataset, the degree to which generated images follow conditioning inputs such as text prompts or masks, and the consistency or controllability of generated outputs under varied constraints. No single metric captures all of these aspects, so researchers rely on a suite of complementary quantitative measures together with careful human evaluation protocols and statistical analysis.

This chapter presents the most commonly used metrics and practical protocols for evaluating generative image models. We will develop intuition for why each metric is used, how it is computed, and what its limitations are. We start with widely used perceptual and distributional metrics—Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Inception Score (IS)—then move to representation-based similarity metrics, especially CLIP-based scores for text–image alignment. Next we cover feature-matching metrics such as DINO and LPIPS that are useful for diversity and perceptual distance, and then task-specific control metrics like intersection-over-union (IoU), F1, and landmark L2 used when generation is conditional on structure or pose. Finally, we discuss robust human evaluation protocols, the importance of seed averaging, and statistical significance tests that make comparisons defensible.

Throughout, we aim for clarity in how each metric is computed (including mathematical definitions where appropriate), practical caveats, and best practices for reporting results so that research is reproducible and meaningful.

# Perceptual and distributional metrics: FID, IS (overview and caveats)

Two of the most widely used automatic metrics for assessing generative image models are the Inception Score (IS) and the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). Both use a pretrained convolutional neural network as a feature extractor—commonly the Inception-v3 network—and operate on large sets of generated images to produce scalar summaries meant to reflect sample quality and diversity.

The Inception Score was introduced to capture two desirable properties of generated images: that individual images be classifiable into distinct, confident categories, and that across samples the model covers many different categories. Given a generative model that produces samples \(x\), the Inception network provides a conditional label distribution \(p(y \mid x)\) where \(y\) indexes classes (in practice the 1000 ImageNet classes). The IS is defined as
\[
\mathrm{IS} = \exp\left( \mathbb{E}_{x} \; \mathrm{KL}\big( p(y\mid x) \,\|\, p(y) \big) \right),
\]
where \(p(y) = \mathbb{E}_x p(y\mid x)\) is the marginal class distribution over generated samples. Intuitively, if for each image \(p(y\mid x)\) is low-entropy (the model produces images that look like recognizable objects) and the marginal \(p(y)\) is high-entropy (samples span many classes), then the KL divergence inside the expectation will be large and the IS high.

Despite its simplicity, IS has important caveats. First, it relies heavily on the Inception classifier’s biases: models that produce images looking like ImageNet classes will score highly even if those images are implausible to humans in other respects. Second, IS does not compare to a real data distribution; it only measures properties of generated samples, so a generative model could cheat by producing highly classifiable but unrealistic images. Third, the IS cannot capture intra-class diversity beyond what the Inception network can detect. For these reasons, IS is best treated as a rough heuristic and not a definitive measure of sample quality.

Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) was proposed to directly compare the distribution of generated images with that of real images in the feature space of a pretrained network. Let \( \phi(x) \) denote the features extracted from the pool3 (or another chosen) layer of an Inception network. If real data features have mean \( \mu_r \) and covariance \( \Sigma_r \), and generated data features have mean \( \mu_g \) and covariance \( \Sigma_g \), then FID is
\[
\mathrm{FID} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\big( \Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2} \big).
\]
FID treats both real and generated feature distributions as Gaussians and computes their Fréchet distance (a.k.a. 2-Wasserstein distance for Gaussians). Lower FID indicates generated images resemble the real distribution more closely.

FID has advantages over IS: it directly measures distance to real data, is sensitive to both quality and diversity, and correlates relatively well with human judgments in many settings. However, FID also has limitations and pitfalls. The Gaussian assumption can break down if features are highly non-Gaussian. FID is sensitive to the choice of feature extractor and to preprocessing (image resizing, cropping, color channels). Crucially, FID estimates are biased and have high variance for small sample sizes; recommendations are to use at least several thousand samples (commonly 5k–50k) and, when comparing models, to use the same sample size and random seed protocol. In addition, FID can be “gamed” by models that optimize toward lowering FID without improving perceptual realism, and it is not well suited for conditional generation where the marginal distribution is not the primary concern.

In practice, report both IS (when relevant) and FID, but always specify the feature extractor, the number of samples, the preprocessing steps, and confidence intervals or standard deviations. When evaluating conditional models, complement these metrics with conditional and alignment metrics discussed later.

# Representation-based similarity metrics: CLIP score for text-image alignment

Conditional generative models, particularly text-to-image systems, require evaluation of how well generated images match conditioning signals, which can be free-form text. CLIP, which jointly trains an image encoder and a text encoder on large amounts of image–text pairs, offers a practical tool for measuring image–text alignment.

The CLIP score for a generated image \(x\) and a text prompt \(t\) is built from cosine similarity of the encoded representations. Let \(f_{\mathrm{img}}(x)\) and \(f_{\mathrm{text}}(t)\) be the normalized feature vectors produced by CLIP’s image and text encoders. Their cosine similarity is
\[
s(x,t) = f_{\mathrm{img}}(x)^\top f_{\mathrm{text}}(t).
\]
A common aggregate CLIP score across a set of \(N\) image–text pairs is the average similarity \(\frac{1}{N} \sum_i s(x_i, t_i)\) or the softmax-normalized retrieval likelihoods that reflect how well an image matches its matched caption compared to other captions in a batch. Many works report the mean cosine similarity; some compute a recall-style metric where the correct text is among the top-\(k\) retrieved captions for a given image.

CLIP scores have strong practical advantages. They are flexible across open-ended text, capture many semantic attributes, and correlate reasonably with human judgments of relevance in many domains. However, CLIP has important limitations. First, CLIP was trained on web-scale image–text pairs and thus inherits dataset biases; it may reward or penalize attributes that reflect spurious correlations in its training data. Second, CLIP can be insensitive to fine-grained correctness: it might give similar scores to an image with minor but critical mistakes (wrong color, extra objects) if global semantics are right. Third, CLIP’s scores are bounded by the capacity and training domain of the model: out-of-distribution styles, non-photorealistic art, or text involving unusual tokens may not be well represented. Finally, CLIP score alone does not reflect visual quality: a blurry but semantically correct image may get a high CLIP score while being undesirable.

Because of these issues, CLIP scores are best used as one component of a multifaceted evaluation. For text–image alignment, report both CLIP-based metrics and human judgments focused on prompt fidelity. When comparing models, use the same CLIP model and preprocessing settings, and where possible complement cosine similarity with retrieval-style evaluations that control for batch effects (see below on human and statistical protocols).

# Feature-matching metrics: DINO, LPIPS for diversity and perceptual distance

Beyond global distributional metrics and semantic alignment, researchers often need measures that quantify perceptual similarity and diversity at a finer level. Two commonly used feature-based metrics are LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity) and metrics derived from self-supervised features like DINO. Both operate by comparing deep features that capture perceptually relevant structure rather than raw pixel differences.

LPIPS was designed to measure perceptual distance between two images in a way that correlates with human judgments. Given a pretrained convolutional network (VGG, AlexNet, or others), LPIPS extracts features from several layers, rescales and weights them, then computes a weighted L2 distance. Formally, if \(\phi_l(x)\) are normalized features from layer \(l\) and \(w_l\) are learned scalar weights, LPIPS between images \(x\) and \(y\) is
\[
\mathrm{LPIPS}(x,y) = \sum_l w_l \lVert \phi_l(x) - \phi_l(y) \rVert_2^2.
\]
LPIPS correlates well with human perceptual judgments of image similarity and is particularly useful for evaluating similarity of generated images to reference images in paired tasks (e.g., image-to-image translation) and for measuring diversity by computing pairwise distances across samples.

DINO is a self-supervised representation learned with contrastive or clustering-based objectives that yields rich semantic features suitable for many downstream tasks. DINO features are often used to compute clustering-based metrics or average cosine distances to quantify diversity and mode coverage. For example, one can extract DINO embeddings for many generated images and compute the coverage of k-means clusters derived from real data, or compute the mean pairwise cosine distance as a summary of diversity. These features are sensitive to mid- and high-level semantic content, making them useful when pixel-level distances (like mean squared error) are misleading.

When using LPIPS or DINO-based metrics, keep in mind their domain sensitivity. The pretrained networks were trained on particular datasets and may under- or over-emphasize certain visual properties. LPIPS is designed to measure perceptual similarity; low LPIPS indicates images are perceptually close, but a low LPIPS to a poor-quality reference does not imply generative quality. Conversely, high pairwise LPIPS among generated samples indicates diversity, but diversity without fidelity is useless. Computational cost can be nontrivial for large sample sets because pairwise distances scale quadratically when computing metrics like average unique distances; approximate sampling is common.

Best practices include reporting both fidelity (e.g., LPIPS to nearest reference) and diversity (e.g., mean pairwise LPIPS or feature-space coverage) and combining these with distributional metrics like FID and alignment metrics like CLIP score. For conditional generation, compute these metrics conditioned on the same control inputs (e.g., evaluate diversity across different random seeds for the same conditioning signal) so that they reflect controllability rather than global diversity.

# Task-specific control metrics: IoU, F1 for structure consistency, landmark L2 for pose control

When generative models are used in controlled or conditional generation tasks—such as image editing with masks, semantic synthesis from segmentation maps, or pose-guided generation—evaluation must reflect how well the generated images adhere to the conditioning structure. Generic metrics like FID or CLIP cannot capture fine-grained structural fidelity. For structure-oriented conditions, task-specific metrics such as intersection-over-union (IoU), F1 score, and L2 distance of predicted landmarks are critical.

IoU (intersection-over-union), also called the Jaccard index, is a standard metric for measuring overlap between two binary or multi-class segmentation masks. Given a predicted mask \(M_p\) and a ground-truth mask \(M_{gt}\), both defined over pixels and possibly per-class, the IoU for class \(c\) is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{p,c} \cup M_{gt,c}|}.
\]
For multi-class segmentation one typically report mean IoU (mIoU) averaged across classes. In generation tasks where the model is conditioned on a segmentation map or is expected to preserve object layout, one can run a semantic segmentation model on the generated image to produce \(M_p\) and compare it to the target mask using IoU. A high IoU implies the generated image respects the target spatial arrangement and class labels.

IoU has intuitive appeal but also practical subtleties. It depends on the quality of the segmentation model applied to generated images; if the segmentation network fails on generated styles, IoU will be unreliable. For fine-grained boundaries or thin structures, IoU can be overly harsh; in such cases reporting boundary-aware metrics or F1 scores (which combine precision and recall) can be helpful. The F1 score for a class is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\[
\mathrm{Precision}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{p,c}|}, \quad
\mathrm{Recall}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{gt,c}|},
\]
\[
\mathrm{F1}_c = 2 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{Precision}_c \cdot \mathrm{Recall}_c}{\mathrm{Precision}_c + \mathrm{Recall}_c}.
\]
F1 can be informative when false positives (spurious objects) and false negatives (missing objects) are both important and need to be balanced.

For pose control and landmark consistency, evaluating how well a generated image matches a specified pose often uses encoded keypoints detected on both generated and real images. Suppose the conditioning pose is specified as a set of 2D keypoints \(\{(x_i^{(c)}, y_i^{(c)})\}_{i=1}^K\}\) and a pose detector returns predicted keypoints on the generated image \(\{(x_i^{(p)}, y_i^{(p)})\}_{i=1}^K\}\). A common metric is mean squared error or mean Euclidean distance over landmarks:
\[
\mathrm{Landmark\ L2} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^K \sqrt{ (x_i^{(p)} - x_i^{(c)})^2 + (y_i^{(p)} - y_i^{(c)})^2 }.
\]
This L2 distance can be normalized by image size or inter-ocular distance (for faces) to make comparisons scale-invariant. Lower landmark L2 indicates better pose adherence.

Again, caveats apply: landmark metrics depend on the reliability of the detector used on generated images, which may fail on stylized outputs or occlusions. It is also possible for a model to achieve low landmark L2 by producing degenerate outputs that mimic keypoint locations but lack photorealism. Therefore landmark metrics should be used alongside fidelity and perceptual metrics.

Other task-specific metrics arise in specialized contexts: for instance, in structured editing one might measure edge consistency, in text-conditional generation one might use object-counting accuracy when prompts mention counts, and in face synthesis identity preservation metrics (embedding cosine similarity between generated and reference faces) are common. The key principle is to choose metrics that directly quantify the intended controllable property, verify assumptions about detectors or classifiers used to compute them, and always report multiple complementary metrics.

# Human evaluation protocols, seed averaging, and statistical significance in generative evaluations

Automatic metrics are invaluable, but many aspects of image quality and alignment are inherently subjective, requiring human judgments. Well-designed human evaluation protocols and rigorous statistical analysis are essential to draw reliable conclusions.

Human evaluation can take many forms. Pairwise preference tests ask annotators to choose which of two images better matches a prompt or looks more realistic. Absolute rating scales ask annotators to score images on quality, fidelity, or other attributes (e.g., a 1–5 Likert scale). Binary correctness tasks ask whether an image satisfies a given constraint (yes/no). Each format has trade-offs: pairwise comparisons reduce scale ambiguity and are more sensitive to small differences, but require \(O(n^2)\) comparisons if all pairs are considered; rating scales are cheaper but more susceptible to inter-annotator variability.

Designing human studies requires careful controls. Use clear and consistent instructions, include qualification tests to ensure annotators understand the task, and provide training examples. Randomize image order and anonymize model sources to avoid bias. Define the evaluation question precisely: for example, “Which image better depicts ‘a red bird perched on a branch’?” is preferable to an ambiguous “Which image is better?” Ideally, collect multiple independent judgments per item (commonly 3–10) and aggregate via majority vote or mean rating. Report inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s kappa, Krippendorff’s alpha, or percent agreement) as an indicator of evaluation reliability.

Seed averaging and repeatability are important in generative modeling because randomness in initialization, training order, and sampling can cause nontrivial variation in results. When reporting quantitative metrics, it is best practice to run experiments with multiple random seeds (both for training and for generation, when feasible) and report mean and standard deviation across runs. For sampling variability at evaluation time, generate multiple sets of images with different RNG seeds and average the metric, or report confidence intervals. For metrics like FID and CLIP score that depend on finite sample draws, present bootstrap or standard-error estimates reflecting sampling noise.

To establish whether observed differences between models are statistically significant, perform appropriate hypothesis tests. For paired evaluations—e.g., when comparing scores of two models on the same set of prompts—use paired tests that account for correlation. For continuous metrics that are approximately normally distributed across items, a paired t-test can be used to test whether the mean difference is zero. If normality is suspect or sample sizes are small, use nonparametric paired tests like the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For binary outcomes aggregated across items (e.g., success/failure per prompt), McNemar’s test can test differences on paired binary outcomes. For human preference data, analyze proportions with a binomial test or compute confidence intervals using the Wilson score interval.

Bootstrapping is a powerful and widely used approach: resample the evaluation dataset with replacement many times (e.g., 10,000 bootstrap samples) and compute the metric difference for each sample to produce an empirical distribution of differences and a confidence interval. This approach makes minimal distributional assumptions and naturally accommodates complex metrics.

When conducting multiple comparisons (e.g., comparing several models or several metrics), adjust for multiple hypothesis testing to control family-wise error rates. Simple methods include Bonferroni correction, but for many hypotheses the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate is often more powerful.

Finally, report effect sizes and practical significance, not just p-values. A difference can be statistically significant but practically negligible. Always include the number of samples, number of annotators, standard errors, and confidence intervals alongside point estimates. Provide ablation studies that isolate the contribution of architectural changes vs. hyperparameter variations, and share code and seeds to support reproducibility.

# Conclusion and practical recommendations

Evaluating generative image models requires a toolbox rather than a single instrument. Use distributional metrics like FID to assess how closely generated images match the overall real-data manifold, but recognize FID’s sensitivity to preprocessing and sample size. Use IS cautiously and primarily as a historical baseline. For conditional alignment, use representation-based metrics such as CLIP score while acknowledging CLIP’s biases and blind spots. Use perceptual metrics like LPIPS and self-supervised features (DINO) to measure diversity and perceptual similarity, and apply task-specific metrics like IoU, F1, and landmark L2 to quantify structural and pose adherence. Complement all automatic measures with human evaluations designed with clear prompts, multiple annotators, and robust statistical analysis. Average across multiple random seeds and report confidence intervals; use paired tests or bootstrap methods to assess statistical significance, and correct for multiple comparisons when needed.

When presenting results, be transparent: document the feature extractors and their versions, preprocessing pipelines, number of generated samples, random seeds, and human evaluation details (instructions, annotator qualifications, inter-annotator agreement). This transparency enables fair comparisons and advances the field by ensuring that reported improvements reflect real, reproducible gains in image quality and controllability.
