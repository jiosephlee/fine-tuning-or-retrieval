Introduction

Orthogonal matrices play a central role in numerical linear algebra, signal processing, and machine learning. They preserve inner products and norms, which makes them useful for stable transformations, parametrizations of rotations, and for regularizing networks. When learning orthogonal matrices by gradient-based optimization, one must choose a convenient parameterization that enforces orthogonality while being numerically stable and efficient to compute and differentiate. This chapter studies a family of practical parameterizations centered on skew-symmetric matrices and the Cayley transform, explains their theoretical properties and limitations, and describes engineering choices such as block-structured parameter reductions and initialization strategies that make these parameterizations useful in practice.

We begin by reviewing the relationship between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices. We then introduce the Cayley parameterization, derive its orthogonality and determinant properties, and highlight important limitations (notably, its inability to represent reflections). Next we investigate numerical approximations to the Cayley transform using Neumann series expansions when the skew-symmetric parameter is small. We then discuss block-diagonal and block-sharing parameterizations that reduce parameter counts while preserving desirable properties. Finally, we collect practical considerations for initialization and numerical stability, including how to avoid singularities and how to trade off accuracy and cost.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and their relationship to orthogonal matrices

A real matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is skew-symmetric if $Q^T = -Q$. Skew-symmetric matrices have several convenient algebraic and spectral properties that make them natural parameters for orthogonal maps.

First, for any skew-symmetric $Q$, the quadratic form $x^T Q x$ is zero for all real vectors $x$, since
$x^T Q x = - x^T Q^T x = - (x^T Q x)$, so $x^T Q x = 0$. This indicates that $Q$ generates infinitesimal rotations—motions that preserve length to first order—rather than scalings.

Second, the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix are purely imaginary and come in conjugate pairs. More precisely, the eigenvalues are of the form $\pm i\lambda_j$ with $\lambda_j \in \mathbb{R}$ (and possibly zeros when $n$ is odd). The spectral decomposition in the real field can be expressed via a block-diagonal form using $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks (and possibly a zero row/column), which connects skew-symmetric matrices to plane rotations.

Third, the matrix exponential $\exp(Q)$ of a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ is orthogonal. To see this, use the identity $(\exp Q)^T = \exp(Q^T) = \exp(-Q)$. Then
\[
(\exp Q)^T \exp Q = \exp(-Q)\exp(Q) = \exp(0) = I,
\]
so $\exp(Q)$ is orthogonal. Furthermore, $\det(\exp(Q)) = \exp(\mathrm{tr}(Q))$, and since $\mathrm{tr}(Q) = 0$ for skew-symmetric $Q$, we have $\det(\exp(Q)) = 1$. Thus exponentiating skew-symmetric matrices yields elements of the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, which are rotations (orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$).

These observations motivate parameterizing orthogonal matrices via skew-symmetric matrices. A skew-symmetric parameterization has minimal redundancy: a real $n \times n$ skew-symmetric matrix is determined by the $n(n-1)/2$ entries strictly above (or below) the diagonal. Representing an orthogonal matrix with the same number of degrees of freedom requires enforcing orthogonality constraints; using skew-symmetric parameters plus a map to orthogonal matrices gives a convenient unconstrained parameter space for optimization.

# Cayley parameterization: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with Q = -Q^T

One useful explicit mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices is the Cayley transform. Given a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ for which $(I-Q)$ is invertible, the Cayley transform produces
\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]
This map has a number of attractive properties and is computationally convenient because it requires just one matrix inverse (or the solution of a linear system) and some matrix multiplications.

Why does this produce an orthogonal matrix? The proof is straightforward. Assuming $Q^T = -Q$ and that $(I-Q)$ is invertible,
\[
R^T R = \bigl((I-Q)^{-T}(I+Q)^T\bigr) (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]
Using $(I+Q)^T = I + Q^T = I - Q$ and $(I-Q)^{-T} = (I-Q^T)^{-1} = (I+Q)^{-1}$, we get
\[
R^T R = (I+Q)^{-1} (I-Q)(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} = I.
\]
Thus $R^T R = I$ and $R$ is orthogonal.

The Cayley transform has a geometric interpretation: it maps skew-symmetric matrices representing infinitesimal rotations into finite rotations. It can be seen as a rational approximation to the exponential map. Indeed, if $Q$ is small (in norm), then $(I-Q)^{-1} \approx I + Q + Q^2 + \dots$, so
\[
R \approx (I+Q)(I + Q + Q^2 + \dots) = I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2),
\]
whereas the exponential map gives $\exp(Q) \approx I + Q + \tfrac{1}{2} Q^2 + \dots$. Both maps agree to first order in $Q$, and both map skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$.

Practically, implementing the Cayley transform requires solving a linear system or computing an inverse. Instead of forming $(I-Q)^{-1}$ explicitly, it is usually better to solve $(I-Q)X = (I+Q)$ for $X$, producing $X = R$. This avoids numerical amplification from forming the inverse and enables use of efficient linear solvers.

# Limitations of Cayley: inability to represent reflections (determinant -1) and numerical considerations

Despite its convenience, the Cayley transform has important limitations that must be understood if it is to be used for parameterizing orthogonal matrices in learning systems.

A primary algebraic limitation is that the Cayley transform cannot represent orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ (i.e., reflections or improper rotations). The image of the Cayley map from the space of real skew-symmetric matrices (connected, with the zero matrix) lies entirely inside the connected component of the orthogonal group containing the identity, namely the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. Concretely, the Cayley transform at $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, and as $Q$ varies continuously the determinant cannot jump to $-1$. More directly, for skew-symmetric $Q$ with $(I-Q)$ invertible, both $\det(I+Q)$ and $\det(I-Q)$ are complex conjugates of each other when eigenvalues are paired, so their ratio is positive real; the map preserves orientation. Thus Cayley cannot produce reflections.

For applications where both rotation and reflections (i.e., both components of $O(n)$) are needed, the Cayley parameterization alone is insufficient. There are several workarounds: one can allow a separate diagonal factor with entries $\pm 1$ to incorporate reflections, or use a different parameterization (for example, the full $n(n-1)/2$ parameters through Householder products can represent the whole $O(n)$). A simple hybrid in learning is to parameterize an orthogonal matrix as $R = D (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ where $D$ is a fixed or learned diagonal matrix with $\pm 1$ entries; this allows determinant sign control at the cost of a nondifferentiable discrete structure (but in practice you can allow $D$ to be a learned real diagonal then project to $\pm 1$).

Another practical numerical concern is that $(I-Q)$ may be singular or nearly singular. The Cayley transform requires that $-1$ is not an eigenvalue of $Q$, or equivalently that $I-Q$ is invertible. Since skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues, this singularity occurs only if $Q$ has an eigenvalue equal to $1$ times $i$, which cannot happen for real $Q$; however, when discretized via finite precision arithmetic or when $Q$ is not perfectly skew-symmetric due to parameterization errors, near-singularity is possible. More concretely, when approximating via series expansions or when $Q$ has a large norm, numerical instability in solving the linear system for $R$ may occur: $(I-Q)$ may be ill-conditioned, and small perturbations in $Q$ can lead to large changes in $R$. This is especially relevant when $Q$ contains large entries for which the rational mapping can amplify those entries.

In summary, Cayley provides an efficient map to orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices but cannot represent reflections and may suffer numerical instability when $Q$ is large or when inversion is ill-conditioned.

# Neumann series approximation for matrix inverse and small-Q expansion

When $Q$ is small in operator norm, the inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ admits a Neumann series expansion
\[
(I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k,
\]
which converges when the spectral radius $\rho(Q) < 1$ (and in practice it suffices that the operator norm $\|Q\| < 1$). Substituting this into the Cayley formula yields
\[
R = (I+Q) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^{k+1} = I + 2Q + 2Q^2 + 2Q^3 + \dots,
\]
so formally
\[
R = I + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} Q^k.
\]
Truncating the series after a few terms gives a polynomial approximation to the Cayley transform that is cheap to compute and differentiable. The leading-order approximation is
\[
R \approx I + 2Q,
\]
which is orthogonal only to first order (i.e., fails to exactly satisfy $R^T R = I$ beyond linear order) but often serves as a useful linearization for small updates.

Higher-order truncated approximations, such as $R \approx I + 2Q + 2Q^2$, yield better accuracy while still avoiding an explicit matrix inverse. Truncation order should be chosen based on a tradeoff between computational cost, accuracy, and stability. For moderate-sized $Q$ satisfying $\|Q\| \le \alpha < 1$, the truncation error can be bounded: the tail of the series behaves like $\mathcal{O}(\|Q\|^{m+1})$ after truncation at order $m$.

However, relying on the Neumann series has its own limitations. The convergence condition requires $\rho(Q) < 1$, so this approach is valid only when $Q$ is small in spectral radius. In learning settings this suggests constraining or regularizing the magnitude of $Q$ (for example, by rescaling) if one intends to use low-order approximations. When $Q$ is not small, it is safer to compute the inverse via a stable linear solver or a direct factorization (LU, QR, or Cholesky on symmetric perturbations) rather than relying on a poorly convergent series.

An advantage of exploiting the Neumann series is that it turns the Cayley map into a sequence of matrix powers, which can be implemented efficiently when the parameterization of $Q$ has structure (e.g., block-sparsity or low-rank). Truncated polynomial maps are also easier to backpropagate through than a matrix inverse in some automatic differentiation frameworks and may be faster on hardware that favors matrix-matrix multiplications.

# Block-diagonal and block-sharing parameterizations to reduce parameter count

A full skew-symmetric $n \times n$ matrix has $n(n-1)/2$ free parameters. For very large $n$, this can create parameter and computational bottlenecks. One strategy is to restrict $Q$ (or $R$) to have block-diagonal or block-structured form, which reduces the number of parameters while retaining useful transformation capabilities.

The simplest structured choice is a block-diagonal skew-symmetric matrix composed of $2 \times 2$ blocks and possibly a singleton zero for odd $n$. Each $2 \times 2$ skew-symmetric block has the form
\[
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\]
which exponentiates to a planar rotation by angle $\theta$. The corresponding Cayley transform or exponential confined to this block yields a rotation in the corresponding plane. By arranging multiple such blocks along the diagonal, we parameterize an orthogonal matrix that performs independent rotations on disjoint 2D subspaces. This block-diagonal parameterization uses only one parameter per 2D block and thus uses about $n/2$ parameters rather than $n(n-1)/2$.

Block-sharing is a further compression: the same small block (or a small set of blocks) can be shared across many positions. For instance, one can tile the skew-symmetric parameter with repeated $k \times k$ blocks that are identical across blocks. This drastically reduces parameter count and is useful when the same local transformation should be applied across many coordinates (analogous to weight sharing in convolutional neural networks). Block-sharing can be implemented by defining a small prototype skew-symmetric matrix $Q_0$ and then constructing a larger $Q$ as a direct sum or Kronecker product with identity: $Q = I_m \otimes Q_0$ or a block-diagonal repetition. The resulting orthogonal matrix becomes blockwise repeated rotations.

Another common choice is to use a sequence (product) of sparse rotation blocks (Givens rotations). A Givens rotation affects only two coordinates and can be parameterized by a single angle. Composing a sequence of Givens rotations can approximate any orthogonal matrix, and restricting to a small number of such rotations reduces parameters. The tradeoff is expressivity: fewer blocks reduce capacity but lower the parameter count and computational cost. Designing the sequence (which pairs of coordinates to rotate) is an important architectural decision; some learn the coordinates or use structured patterns (but learning coordinate indices requires combinatorial choices, so fixed patterns are common).

When applying the Cayley transform with block-diagonal $Q$, the computation simplifies drastically: each diagonal block can be transformed independently, and the inverses required are small. This improves numerical stability and computational efficiency.

# Practical considerations: initialization to identity, numerical stability

Several practical engineering points make skew-symmetric and Cayley parameterizations effective in learning systems.

Initialization: A common and principled initialization is to start with $Q = 0$, which yields $R = I$ via the Cayley transform. This is often favorable because the identity matrix is a neutral transformation that preserves signal magnitudes and avoids initial distortions. Starting near the identity also keeps $Q$ small so that linear approximations are valid at the beginning of training, which stabilizes gradients. If a nontrivial initial orthogonal matrix is desired, one can initialize $Q$ to a small random skew-symmetric matrix so that $R$ is near but not exactly the identity, or use the exponential of a block-symmetric random matrix followed by a Cayley mapping to introduce controlled rotations.

Numerical stability and conditioning: Avoid explicitly computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ when possible. Instead solve the linear system $(I-Q)X = (I+Q)$ for $X$ using a stable linear solver (LU with partial pivoting or an iterative solver) or use matrix factorizations tailored to skew-symmetric structure. Regularize $Q$ magnitude by clipping or applying norm penalties if the solver indicates ill-conditioning. When using the Neumann series approximation, ensure that $\|Q\|$ is comfortably less than 1; otherwise raise the order of series truncation or compute the exact inverse.

Handling the inability to represent reflections: If your application requires orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$, incorporate an explicit discrete reflection factor. A simple approach is to learn a diagonal sign vector $s \in \{\pm 1\}^n$ and set $R = S (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $S = \mathrm{diag}(s)$. Learning $s$ directly is discrete; in practice you can predecide whether reflections are allowed or use a continuous relaxation. Alternatively, use parameterizations that can generate all of $O(n)$, such as products of Householder reflections (which can represent any orthogonal matrix exactly with at most $n$ reflectors).

Differentiation and computational cost: When backpropagating through the Cayley transform, differentiating through the linear solve is numerically preferable to differentiating through an explicit inverse. Many automatic differentiation frameworks provide stable and efficient adjoint rules for linear solvers. Pay attention to the cost of matrix inversions or solves: these scale as $O(n^3)$ for dense matrices, which may be prohibitive for large $n$. Using block or sparse structures reduces cost and memory.

Robust implementation tips: enforce skew-symmetry explicitly by parameterizing only the upper triangular entries and constructing $Q = A - A^T$ for a learned matrix $A$. Clamp or rescale $A$ to control $\|Q\|$. Use double precision when inverting near-singular matrices during initialization or debugging. Monitor condition numbers of $(I-Q)$ during training and apply penalties or reparameterizations if they grow too large.

Summary

Skew-symmetric matrices provide a compact and natural parameter space for orthogonal maps. The Cayley transform turns skew-symmetric parameters into orthogonal matrices with a simple rational formula that is efficient and differentiable, but it only covers orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices and may become numerically unstable when the skew-symmetric parameter is large. For practical learning systems, one often constrains $Q$ (via block-diagonal structure or sharing) to reduce parameters and improve computational cost, initializes $Q$ to zero to get the identity map, and uses numerically stable linear solves or controlled Neumann series approximations for inversion. Understanding these tradeoffs allows you to choose a parameterization that balances expressivity, efficiency, and stability for your application.
