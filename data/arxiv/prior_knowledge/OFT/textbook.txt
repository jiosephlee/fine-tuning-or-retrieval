Introduction

Generative modeling is the branch of machine learning that studies how to learn probability distributions from data and how to draw new samples that resemble the observed data. Where discriminative models answer questions like "given this image, what is the class label?", generative models answer "what images are likely under the data distribution?" A good generative model yields two capabilities: it provides a mechanism to sample new synthetic datapoints and it often provides some representation of the data distribution (for instance, a density function or an implicit sampling process). Understanding generative models is important for many applications: image and audio synthesis, data augmentation, anomaly detection, simulation, and as building blocks inside larger systems that require modeling uncertainty or creating counterfactuals.

This chapter introduces the foundational ideas and families of generative models that are widely used in modern machine learning, with particular emphasis on the distinctions you will need to understand why diffusion models became a leading approach for high-quality image synthesis. We will define what we mean by generative modeling, describe the different training objectives that arise in the field, survey the major model families (variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, autoregressive models, and energy-based models), and explain the basic training criteria used in practice. We will also discuss qualitative trade-offs — such as fidelity versus diversity and the phenomenon of mode collapse — and conclude with practical issues in evaluating generative models: how to measure sample quality and diversity, and what pitfalls to avoid when interpreting evaluation metrics.

# Definition of generative modeling and objective types (likelihood-based, implicit)

At its heart, generative modeling is about approximating an unknown data distribution. Suppose we have a dataset of samples drawn from some unknown distribution $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ where $x$ denotes a data point (for example, an image). A generative model provides a parameterized family of distributions $\{p_\theta(x)\}_{\theta}$ or a parameterized procedure that produces samples resembling $p_{\text{data}}$. The two broad classes of generative modeling approaches are likelihood-based methods and implicit methods.

Likelihood-based models explicitly define or approximate a probability density or mass function $p_\theta(x)$. When the model gives a tractable or tractably approximable density, we can directly evaluate $p_\theta(x)$ (or evaluate log-likelihoods) and train by maximizing the likelihood of the training data. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the canonical criterion in this class: given samples $\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, the MLE objective is to find parameters $\theta$ that maximize the average log-likelihood
\[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(x^{(i)}).
\]
Equivalently, MLE minimizes the Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)$ in the limit of infinite data, because MLE pushes $p_\theta$ to place probability mass where $p_{\text{data}}$ has mass. Likelihood-based models include autoregressive models and many forms of variational autoencoders (VAEs) and normalizing flows.

Implicit generative models do not necessarily provide a tractable density function. Instead they define a stochastic procedure for generating samples, often by transforming random noise with a parameterized function. Classic implicit models accept a simple source of randomness, typically $z \sim p(z)$ where $p(z)$ is a simple prior such as a standard multivariate normal $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and pass it through a generator function $G_\theta$ so that $x = G_\theta(z)$. The model defines an implicit distribution of $x$ but does not usually make $p_\theta(x)$ available in closed form. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are the canonical example of implicit modeling: training is done without direct likelihood estimation, but instead via an adversarial loss that assesses whether samples look real.

A middle ground exists where models are partially implicit: they may permit approximate densities via variational bounds (as in VAEs) or evaluate unnormalized densities up to a normalization constant (as in energy-based models). Each of these objective types leads to different algorithms, trade-offs, and diagnostic tools.

# Overview of major families: VAEs, GANs, autoregressive models, energy-based models

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are likelihood-based in the sense that they seek to model $p_\theta(x)$, but they use variational approximations because the marginal likelihood is typically intractable. The VAE introduces a latent variable $z$ and defines a generative model $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)$. The marginal likelihood $p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz$ is often intractable for expressive decoders, so VAEs train using the evidence lower bound (ELBO). With a recognition or encoder network $q_\phi(z\mid x)$, the ELBO for a data point $x$ is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x) = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big),
\]
which is a lower bound on the log-likelihood $\log p_\theta(x)$. VAEs are attractive because they produce both an encoder and a decoder and provide a principled density estimator via the ELBO. However, basic VAEs often produce blurry samples when used with pixel-wise decoders, because maximizing likelihood or the ELBO tends to prefer averaging over multiple plausible outputs when the decoder has insufficient capacity or the assumed output noise model (e.g., Gaussian pixel noise) does not match perceptual metrics.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are implicit models trained via an adversarial game between a generator and a discriminator (or critic). The generator $G_\theta(z)$ attempts to produce realistic samples from noise $z$, while the discriminator $D_\phi(x)$ attempts to distinguish real data samples from generated samples. The original GAN loss sets up a minimax optimization
\[
\min_\theta \max_\phi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D_\phi(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}\big[\log\big(1 - D_\phi(G_\theta(z))\big)\big].
\]
When trained well, GANs can produce extremely sharp, high-fidelity samples because the discriminator can learn to reward samples that are perceptually plausible even if they have low likelihood under a pixel-level model. However, GAN training is notoriously unstable and susceptible to mode collapse, where the generator maps many $z$ to a small set of outputs, capturing only a subset of the data modes.

Autoregressive models explicitly decompose the joint distribution $p_\theta(x)$ into a product of conditional distributions using the chain rule,
\[
p_\theta(x) = \prod_{i=1}^D p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{<i}),
\]
where $x$ is treated as a sequence of $D$ elements (for images, $x_i$ might be pixels or sub-pixel tokens). Autoregressive models parameterize each conditional $p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{<i})$ using powerful sequence models such as recurrent neural networks, convolutional architectures with masking, or the Transformer. Examples include PixelRNN, PixelCNN, and language models. These models provide exact likelihoods and typically achieve excellent likelihood scores on benchmarks. Sampling from autoregressive models can be slow because sampling each $x_i$ depends on previously sampled components, and they can produce samples that are good in terms of likelihood but sometimes lack the perceptual sharpness of GAN samples for images.

Energy-Based Models (EBMs) model an unnormalized density $p_\theta(x) \propto \exp(-E_\theta(x))$ where $E_\theta(x)$ is an energy function. Since the partition function (normalization constant) $Z_\theta = \int \exp(-E_\theta(x))\,dx$ is typically intractable, training EBMs requires special techniques that either avoid computing $Z_\theta$ directly (such as contrastive divergence or score matching) or that use MCMC to estimate gradients. EBMs are highly flexible and conceptually simple: they place low energy on data-like configurations and higher energy elsewhere. However, they suffer from the practical difficulty of sampling from the model and from unstable training when MCMC sampling is approximate.

There are hybrids and newer families as well. Normalizing flows are models that transform a simple base density into a complex one using an invertible mapping with tractable Jacobian determinant; they provide exact likelihoods and efficient sampling but impose architectural constraints. Diffusion models and score-based generative models will be discussed in later chapters; they are related to EBMs and have become very successful for image synthesis by modeling and reversing a gradual noising process.

# Basic training objectives: maximum likelihood, adversarial loss, reconstruction loss

Maximum likelihood is the fundamental objective for likelihood-based models. When we can compute or tightly approximate $\log p_\theta(x)$, maximizing the average log-likelihood over data corresponds to minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)$. Maximum likelihood is attractive because it has well-understood statistical properties: under standard regularity conditions, MLE is consistent and asymptotically efficient. From a practical perspective, training with MLE encourages the model to cover the support of the data distribution because placing near-zero probability where data exist results in a large penalty.

Adversarial loss arises in GANs where the objective is to fool a discriminator. Rather than directly matching densities, the adversarial framework sets up a critic that learns a surrogate objective that distinguishes generated samples from real ones. Variants of the adversarial loss have been proposed to improve stability: Wasserstein GANs replace the binary classification objective with an approximation of the Earth-Mover distances using a critic constrained to be Lipschitz; least-squares GANs modify the logistic loss to reduce vanishing gradients. The adversarial losses are powerful in that they can implicitly encode perceptual criteria: the discriminator can learn to penalize artifacts that simple pixel-wise metrics miss. However, because adversarial training minimizes divergences that are not symmetric (and are often undefined when distributions do not overlap), the resulting $p_\theta$ may concentrate on a subset of modes — which leads directly to the phenomenon known as mode collapse.

Reconstruction loss is commonly used in autoencoder-type models and appears explicitly in VAEs as the expected log-likelihood term $\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]$. In deterministic autoencoders, reconstruction loss measures how well the decoded output matches the input, usually via mean squared error (MSE) or cross-entropy. Reconstruction losses encourage the model to preserve input information through the bottleneck, but if used alone without a proper probabilistic constraint, a degenerate solution is possible (for instance, an identity mapping in an overcomplete autoencoder). In VAEs, the reconstruction term is combined with a regularization term (the KL divergence between the approximate posterior and the prior) to both reconstruct well and shape the latent space. The choice of reconstruction metric affects perceptual quality: pixelwise MSE tends to produce blur because it averages multiple plausible outcomes; perceptual losses based on features from pretrained networks can mitigate blurriness but complicate probabilistic interpretation.

Other objectives include score matching, used in score-based and some EBM training regimes, which directly trains a model to approximate the score function $\nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x)$ rather than the density itself. Contrastive objectives and noise-contrastive estimation provide ways to learn unnormalized models by discriminating real data from noise samples, converting density estimation into classification problems.

# Qualitative trade-offs: fidelity vs diversity, mode collapse, likelihood vs perceptual quality

When evaluating generative models, several qualitative trade-offs commonly arise. One central trade-off is fidelity versus diversity. Fidelity refers to how realistic individual samples are — that is, whether samples look like plausible images with sharp, coherent structure. Diversity refers to the model's ability to capture the full variety of modes present in the data distribution: different object classes, poses, colors, textures, and so on. Some training objectives or model families bias toward high fidelity at the expense of diversity, and vice versa.

GANs are prototypical of models that often prioritize sample fidelity. Because the discriminator focuses on producing realistic-looking images, generators can be pushed to produce very sharp, photorealistic outputs. But this focus can come at the cost of neglecting some data modes. Mode collapse occurs when the generator maps many different latent inputs to the same or a few outputs, thereby reducing diversity. Mode collapse can be partial (missing some modes) or severe (collapsing to a single output). Researchers have proposed many techniques to mitigate mode collapse: improved architectures, minibatch discrimination, unrolled GANs, diversity-promoting regularizers, and alternative divergence measures, but it remains a central challenge for adversarial training.

Likelihood-based models such as autoregressive models and flows often excel at diversity and mode coverage because MLE penalizes assigning low probability to any data point. As a result, such models tend to cover more of the data distribution and perform well in log-likelihood metrics. However, a model trained to maximize likelihood under a simple pixelwise noise model may produce samples that are perceptually poor. The reason is that maximizing likelihood encourages matching the data distribution under the chosen measurement of closeness (usually per-pixel), and pixelwise distances do not align well with human perception. For natural images, minimizing expected squared error can lead to blurring when multiple plausible solutions exist: the model hedges between possibilities by averaging, which increases likelihood but reduces perceptual sharpness.

The choice of divergence implicit in the training objective also affects behavior. MLE minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)$, which heavily penalizes $p_\theta$ that puts low mass where $p_{\text{data}}$ has mass (hence promoting coverage). By contrast, adversarial objectives often correspond to minimizing divergences (or distance-like measures) such as Jensen–Shannon divergence or Wasserstein distance, which can behave more like symmetric or reverse divergences under some formulations. Reverse-KL, $\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\text{data}})$, penalizes the model for putting mass where the data have none and can encourage mode-seeking behavior; some adversarial or approximate adversarial objectives exhibit mode-seeking tendencies. Understanding these asymmetries helps explain why certain training criteria favor fidelity (mode-seeking) whereas others favor coverage (mode-covering).

In practice, choosing a model involves balancing these trade-offs based on application: in data augmentation and simulation you may prioritize coverage and controllable density estimates; in creative image synthesis you may prefer high visual fidelity even if some modes are underrepresented; in anomaly detection you need accurate likelihood estimates. Recent advances such as diffusion models have provided strong performance in both fidelity and diversity by modeling the data distribution in continuous time via a forward noising process and a learned reverse denoising process, which we will study in later chapters.

# Evaluation basics: sample quality, diversity, and evaluation pitfalls

Evaluating generative models is notoriously difficult. There is no single metric that perfectly captures both sample quality and diversity, and different metrics can disagree. A robust evaluation requires multiple complementary measures and careful experimental design.

Qualitative evaluation via human studies remains the gold standard for perceptual quality: ask human raters to compare real and generated images or to rate realism. Human studies are expensive and slow, so researchers have developed automated proxies. For images, two widely used automatic metrics are the Inception Score (IS) and the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). The Inception Score measures whether generated images are both sharp (their predicted label distribution is low entropy) and diverse (the marginal label distribution is high entropy). It uses the pretrained Inception network to produce label distributions $p(y\mid x)$ and computes $\exp(\mathbb{E}_x \mathrm{KL}(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)))$. The Fréchet Inception Distance compares the multivariate Gaussian statistics (mean and covariance) of activations from a pretrained Inception network on real and generated images, computing the Fréchet distance between two Gaussians. FID correlates better with human judgment than IS in many settings and penalizes both poor sample quality and low diversity.

Coverage metrics and log-likelihoods measure how well the model assigns high probability to real data. Likelihood-based evaluations are principled when the model provides true likelihoods, but likelihoods computed with pixelwise decoders may not reflect perceptual quality. Moreover, comparing likelihoods across different model classes can be misleading because likelihood is sensitive to the choice of data representation and preprocessing (e.g., bit-depth, dequantization). Exact log-likelihoods are not available for implicit models like GANs, which complicates cross-family comparisons.

Diversity can be measured directly by estimating the support or by computing precision and recall-like metrics that separately quantify sample fidelity (precision) and coverage of data modes (recall). Precision measures whether generated samples are realistic (near the data manifold); recall measures whether the generator produces the full variety of data modes. Methods to estimate precision and recall often rely on feature embeddings from pretrained networks and geometrical comparisons between sets in feature space. While these metrics are informative, they depend heavily on the embedding and on thresholds used to define "near."

There are several pitfalls to be aware of when evaluating generative models. First, many automatic metrics rely on features from networks trained on ImageNet or other datasets; if your data are substantially different, these features may be inappropriate or biased. Second, models trained with different image resolutions, preprocessing steps, or truncation/sampling heuristics can yield misleading comparisons. Truncation sampling, for instance, deliberately reduces output diversity by restricting latent draws; it can improve FID or perceptual scores at the expense of coverage, and thus a model that uses truncation should not be directly compared to one that does not. Third, overfitting to evaluation metrics is possible: model developers can inadvertently (or deliberately) tune models to optimize a metric rather than to improve generalization, leading to inflated and non-representative results. Finally, reporting only single-number summaries such as FID hides failure modes; visual inspection and per-class or per-mode analyses are important for a complete picture.

A practical evaluation protocol combines multiple approaches: report quantitative metrics that capture both quality and diversity (e.g., FID plus precision/recall), provide log-likelihoods if available, perform human studies where feasible, and include qualitative samples and nearest-neighbor comparisons to real data to check for memorization. Also document sampling procedures, preprocessing, and any heuristics like truncation to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison.

Conclusion

Generative modeling encompasses a variety of model families and training objectives, each with strengths and limitations. Likelihood-based methods offer principled density estimation and encourage mode coverage, while implicit models such as GANs can produce outstanding perceptual quality but face challenges with stability and diversity. Reconstruction-based objectives like those in VAEs trade off sharpness for probabilistic interpretation, and energy-based approaches provide flexible unnormalized models at the cost of challenging sampling and training. Evaluating generative models requires careful use of multiple metrics and awareness of their limitations. Understanding these fundamentals — the goals, common architectures, training criteria, trade-offs, and evaluation challenges — prepares you to study contemporary approaches such as diffusion and score-based models, which build on these ideas and address many of the practical issues discussed in this chapter.

Chapter 2: Diffusion and Score-Based Generative Models

Introduction

Diffusion and score-based generative models have become foundational tools in modern generative modeling, particularly for high-fidelity image synthesis. At a high level these models create complex data samples by starting from simple noise and gradually transforming it into structured data through a learned reverse process. Two complementary viewpoints exist: the discrete-timestep diffusion view (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, DDPMs) and the continuous-time stochastic differential equation (SDE) / score-based view. Both rely on understanding a forward "noising" process that progressively destroys structure in the data, and a learned reverse "denoising" process that reconstructs the data by estimating score functions (gradients of log-densities) or conditional denoising distributions. This chapter explains the forward diffusion process, the reverse process and its connection to score matching, the derivation of the DDPM loss, continuous-time SDE formulations, practical sampling algorithms (including ancestral sampling, deterministic samplers like DDIM, and classifier-free guidance), and the rationale and differences between pixel-space and latent-space diffusion.

# Forward noising (diffusion) process and marginal statistics

The forward diffusion process is a deliberately chosen Markov chain that gradually corrupts data with noise. The aim is to establish a simple tractable distribution at the final time (often Gaussian white noise) that the model can sample from easily, while making each transition analytically simple. In the discrete-time DDPM setup, we define time steps $t=0,1,\dots,T$. The data distribution is $q(x_0)$ and we define a conditional forward process $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ that incrementally adds noise:

$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\,\beta_t\mathbf{I}\big)$

where $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ is a small variance schedule. Intuitively, at each step we scale the previous state by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ and add Gaussian noise of variance $\beta_t$. After many steps, $x_T$ becomes approximately Gaussian regardless of $x_0$.

A crucial property that makes training easier is that we can write the marginal distribution of $x_t$ given the original data $x_0$ in closed form. Because each forward transition is linear and Gaussian, the $t$-step marginal is also Gaussian:

$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0,\, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\big)$

where we define $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$. This closed-form marginal is extremely useful: it means we can sample $x_t$ at any time $t$ directly from $x_0$ by drawing

$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}).$

This expression reveals how much of the original signal remains at time $t$ and how much is noise. For small $t$, $\bar{\alpha}_t\approx 1$ so $x_t$ is close to $x_0$; for large $t$, $\bar{\alpha}_t\to 0$ so $x_t$ is dominated by noise. The typical schedule for $\beta_t$ is chosen so that the noise accumulates gradually and $x_T\approx\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$.

The forward process has other useful conditional forms. For instance, the conditional distribution of $x_{t-1}$ given $x_t$ and $x_0$ is also Gaussian and analytically tractable, which underpins the derivation of the reverse dynamics. The forward construction also generalizes to continuous-time formulations where we define a time-dependent diffusion coefficient and obtain an SDE for $x(t)$; this continuous perspective connects naturally to score-based modeling and to probabilistic interpretations via Fokker–Planck equations. But in the discrete setup, the closed-form marginal $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ allows us to train models that predict either noise or the original $x_0$ from a noisy input.

# Reverse denoising process and connection to score matching

The forward noising process is easy to sample from and analyze. The generative task is to learn the reverse of this process: starting from noise, produce clean samples that approximate $q(x_0)$. Formally, we seek a reverse-time Markov chain with conditional distributions $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ that approximate the true reverse posteriors $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$. In general, Bayes' theorem gives

$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \frac{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\,q(x_{t-1})}{q(x_t)}.$

Because all involved distributions are Gaussian and linear in the DDPM forward process, $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$ is Gaussian with a known mean and variance. The true marginal reverse $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ is a mixture over $x_0$, but the model approximates these posteriors with parametric distributions.

A core insight tying reverse denoising to score matching is that for small noise levels, knowledge of the score function $\nabla_{x}\log q(x)$ — the gradient of the log-density — is sufficient to define a reverse diffusion (or define a denoising vector field). The score tells you in which direction in data space the density increases; starting from noise, following the score gradient (or a modified version) moves you towards high-probability data regions.

Score matching is a learning objective that trains a model $s_\theta(x,t)$ to approximate the score $\nabla_x\log q_t(x)$ at various noise levels $t$. One form of score matching minimizes the expected squared error between the model score and the true score under the noisy marginal $q_t(x)$:

$\mathbb{E}_{t}\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q_t}\big[\|s_\theta(x,t) - \nabla_x\log q_t(x)\|^2\big].$

Although the true score is unknown, denoising score matching provides an alternative: because $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ is Gaussian, we can obtain an analytic expression for $\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$ and use it as a target. Denoising score matching minimizes the expected squared error between $s_\theta(x_t,t)$ and $\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$ averaged over $x_0\sim q(x_0)$ and $x_t\sim q(x_t\mid x_0)$. This is connected to denoising autoencoders and yields a practical training objective: predict the noise component added to produce $x_t$ from $x_0$.

In the continuous-time SDE view, reverse-time SDEs are constructed using the score function. Consider a forward SDE

$dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW_t,$

with $W_t$ a standard Wiener process. The probability density evolves according to a Fokker–Planck equation. The corresponding reverse-time SDE that samples from the data distribution backwards uses the score $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$:

$dx = \big[f(x,t) - g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar{W}_t,$

where $\bar{W}_t$ is a reversed-time Wiener process (and time $t$ runs backward). Thus, knowledge of the score across times enables construction of the reverse dynamics. Practically, we approximate $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ with $s_\theta(x,t)$ and plug it into the reverse SDE to sample approximate data.

# DDPM and derivation of training loss

DDPMs (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) cast the generative modeling problem as learning the parameters of reverse transition distributions $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$. The model initially defines a simple prior $p(x_T) = \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ and learns Gaussian reverse conditionals:

$p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_{t-1};\,\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\,\Sigma_\theta(x_t,t)\big).$

Training maximizes a variational lower bound (ELBO) on the likelihood of data under the model. The joint forward distribution factorizes as

$q(x_{0:T}) = q(x_0)\prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}),$

and the model joint is

$p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t).$

The negative ELBO equals the KL divergence between these two joint distributions and can be written as a sum of terms:

$-\log p_\theta(x_0) \le \mathbb{E}_{q}\Big[-\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)\Big] + \sum_{t=2}^T \mathbb{E}_{q}\Big[ \operatorname{KL}\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)\Big] + \operatorname{KL}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\big).$

Each KL term involves matching the model reverse conditional to the true posterior conditioned on $x_0$ and $x_t$. Because $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$ is Gaussian with known mean and variance, one convenient approach is to parameterize $\mu_\theta(x_t,t)$ in terms of a predicted noise value. Using the marginal identity

$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon,$

we can design a neural network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ to predict the noise $\epsilon$. A common parameterization sets

$\mu_\theta(x_t,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big).$

When this parameterization is used and the variances $\Sigma_\theta$ are fixed (or sometimes learned but constrained), the KL term simplifies. A key empirical and theoretical result is that optimizing a simplified mean-squared error (MSE) between the true noise $\epsilon$ and the predicted noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ is equivalent to optimizing a reweighted ELBO; concretely, the simplified training loss becomes

$L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon,t}\big[ \|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2\big],$

where $t$ is sampled uniformly from $\{1,\dots,T\}$, $x_t$ is constructed via the closed-form marginal, and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$. Intuition: predicting the noise added at level $t$ encourages the network to denoise effectively, which corresponds to approximating the reverse conditional mean. In practice, a time-conditional U-Net with attention is used for $\epsilon_\theta$, and positional encodings (sinusoidal or Fourier features) embed $t$.

The DDPM framework also supports different weighting schemes for the MSE loss that better reflect the ELBO decomposition. Early implementations derived per-timestep weighting factors, but subsequent empirical work found that the simple unweighted MSE (or a small time-dependent weighting) yields excellent results and is easier to optimize.

At sampling time, we run ancestral sampling: initialize $x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ and for $t=T,\dots,1$ sample $x_{t-1}\sim p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$. If we use the learned mean and fixed Gaussian variance, sampling requires drawing Gaussian noise at each step; if one sets variance to zero at inference, the process becomes deterministic (which leads to different approximations).

# Score-based generative modeling and stochastic differential equations (SDEs)

Score-based generative modeling reframes diffusion models in continuous time and emphasizes learning the score functions $s_\theta(x,t)\approx\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ for the time-dependent marginals $p_t(x)$. This perspective makes the intimate connection between diffusion processes and stochastic differential equations explicit, and reveals continuous analogues of DDPMs that can be more flexible.

A general forward SDE takes the form

$dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW_t.$

For example, the Variance Preserving (VP) diffusion SDE analogous to DDPM has $f(x,t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t)x$ and $g(t) = \sqrt{\beta(t)}$, where $\beta(t)$ is a noise schedule. The marginal distributions $p_t(x)$ have known Gaussian forms conditioned on $x_0$, paralleling the discrete case. The forward SDE gradually injects noise, and in the limit $t\to T$ the distribution approaches a noise prior, often Gaussian.

The reverse-time SDE for a diffusion of the above form is given by

$dx = \big[f(x,t) - g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar{W}_t,$

where $d\bar{W}_t$ denotes the reverse-time Wiener process and time flows backwards. Crucially, access to $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ lets us simulate the reverse dynamics. Score-based models train $s_\theta(x,t)$ to approximate this score, using denoising score matching or other objective variants. Once $s_\theta$ is learned, one can numerically integrate the reverse SDE (or a deterministic ODE version described below) to generate samples from the learned model.

There is also an associated probability flow ODE that shares the marginal distributions $p_t(x)$ with the forward SDE but is deterministic. Its form is

$dx = \big[f(x,t) - \tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]\,dt.$

Because this ODE is deterministic, integrating it backward from the prior yields a single deterministic sample path per initial noise draw, and it has no stochastic noise terms during integration. Sampling via the probability flow ODE is useful for deterministic samplers and for likelihood computation (via change-of-variables and instantaneous change-of-variables formula), enabling exact likelihood evaluation given a perfect score model.

Training the score network is commonly performed with the denoising score matching objective adapted to continuous time. A practical formulation samples a continuous time $t$ and noise $\epsilon$, constructs $x_t$ from $x_0$, and minimizes

$\mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,\epsilon}\big[\lambda(t)\|\ s_\theta(x_t,t) - \nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)\ \|^2\big],$

where $\lambda(t)$ is a weighting function and $\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)$ is analytically available because $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ is Gaussian. Reparameterizations allow replacing the score target with a scaled noise target, just as in DDPMs.

The continuous SDE viewpoint provides flexibility: different choices of $f$ and $g$ produce different noise paths, which can affect learning and sampling efficiency. It also leads to theoretically principled sampling algorithms where numerical integration schemes (e.g., Euler–Maruyama for SDEs or higher-order solvers for the ODE) can be deployed with controls on discretization error.

# Sampling strategies: ancestral sampling, deterministic samplers (DDIM), and classifier-free guidance

Sampling is where the various formulations meet practice, and several strategies are used depending on trade-offs among sample quality, speed, and control.

Ancestral (stochastic) sampling is the natural algorithm for DDPMs. Starting with $x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$, for $t=T,\dots,1$ we sample from the learned reverse Gaussian $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$:

$x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t,t) + \sigma_t z,\quad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}).$

This requires $T$ network evaluations and $T$ Gaussian noise draws. When the model approximates the true reverse conditionals well, ancestral sampling yields high-quality diverse samples. However, the typical $T$ used in training (hundreds to thousands) can make sampling slow.

DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) introduces a deterministic, non-Markovian sampler that shares the same marginal denoising behavior as DDPM at training time but allows deterministic trajectories with fewer steps. The DDIM update can be derived by considering implicit sampling rules that exactly map $x_t$ to $x_{t-1}$ via the denoising prediction. Let $\hat{x}_0=\hat{x}_0(x_t,t)$ denote the model's prediction of the original sample $x_0$ from $x_t$ (e.g., computed from $\epsilon_\theta$ by rearranging the marginal formula). A generic DDIM step updates via

$x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\hat{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\,\hat{\epsilon},$

where $\hat{\epsilon}$ is computed deterministically from $\hat{x}_0$ and $x_t$. Equivalently, one can parameterize a deterministic map that reduces to the probability flow ODE integration in the continuous limit. Because DDIM is deterministic and non-Markovian, it permits skipping many intermediate timesteps while retaining plausible samples; in practice, it enables a trade-off between sampling speed and quality and often uses far fewer steps than ancestral sampling.

Classifier-free guidance is a powerful method for controlling conditional generation (e.g., class-conditional or text-conditional image synthesis) without a separate classifier. The idea is to train a single model that can operate both conditionally and unconditionally by randomly dropping the conditioning information during training. Concretely, during training we provide condition $y$ (e.g., class labels or text embeddings) with probability $p$ and with probability $1-p$ replace $y$ by a null token; the model thus learns to produce both $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,y)$ and $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,\varnothing)$.

At sampling time, we take a linear combination of the conditional and unconditional score predictions to amplify the influence of the condition. In the score formulation, this means using an adjusted score

$s_{\text{cfg}}(x,t) = s_\theta(x,t,\varnothing) + w\big(s_\theta(x,t,y) - s_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\big),$

where $w\ge 1$ is a guidance weight. For $w>1$ the conditional effect is strengthened, often improving fidelity to the condition at the cost of some diversity. In the DDPM parameterization that predicts $\epsilon$, an analogous formula interpolates (or extrapolates) between conditional and unconditional predictions of $\epsilon$. Classifier-free guidance is simple, often more stable and flexible than classifier guidance (using an external classifier to adjust the score), and widely used in large conditional diffusion models.

Other sampling improvements include noise schedule adjustments, knowledge distillation to train fast samplers, and higher-order ODE/SDE solvers that reduce discretization error. A practical sampling pipeline often combines a judiciously chosen timestep sequence, classifier-free guidance for conditional control, and deterministic samplers (e.g., DDIM) or few-step ancestral samplers to balance speed and quality.

# Latent diffusion vs pixel-space diffusion (motivation and differences)

Diffusion models originally operated directly in pixel space: the model learns to transform Gaussian noise defined over all pixels into images. While successful, pixel-space diffusion has two practical limitations. First, pixel-space sampling is computationally heavy because images are high-dimensional (e.g., 256x256x3 dimensions) and diffusion typically requires many network evaluations. Second, learning to denoise at the pixel level may be inefficient because the model needs to represent fine-grained low-level noise distributions as well as high-level semantic structure.

Latent diffusion models (LDMs) address these issues by performing diffusion in a lower-dimensional latent space. The core idea is to train an encoder-decoder (often a convolutional autoencoder or a variational autoencoder) that maps images into a compact latent representation $z=\mathcal{E}(x)$ and reconstructs via $x\approx\mathcal{D}(z)$. Diffusion modeling is then applied to $z$ rather than $x$: the forward and reverse processes operate on $z$-space, which has much lower dimensionality and often smoother structure. Sampling proceeds by running the learned reverse diffusion in latent space to obtain $z_T\to z_0$ and then decoding $x=\mathcal{D}(z_0)$.

There are several motivations and consequences for this design. Because the latent dimensionality is lower, each diffusion step is cheaper and the neural network can be smaller or operate at smaller spatial scales (e.g., fewer channels and downsampled representations), leading to faster sampling and lower memory usage. Latent spaces obtained via perceptually aware encoders concentrate semantic information and discard high-frequency pixel noise, which can make the denoising learning problem easier and improve sample fidelity. Additionally, when conditioning on text or other modalities, conditioning can be applied in the latent space, where relationships between modalities may be more direct.

However, latent diffusion introduces trade-offs and design choices. The encoder-decoder must be trained to produce a latent space that is suitable for diffusion: it should be invertible (or at least reconstructive), capture semantic variability, and not introduce artefacts. In practice, LDMs use carefully designed autoencoders with perceptual loss terms and optionally adversarial components to ensure high-fidelity reconstructions. The distribution of latents is typically non-Gaussian, so diffusion in latent space models a more complex prior; nevertheless, the same forward noising and reverse modeling machinery applies.

Another difference is that pixel-space diffusion can directly optimize pixel-wise likelihood proxies and can sometimes produce crisper low-level detail, whereas latent diffusion relies on the decoder quality; if the decoder is imperfect, the final samples may be limited by decoder artifacts. Conversely, latent diffusion can enable very large generative models that would be infeasible in pixel space, and is often the architecture choice in state-of-the-art text-to-image systems where large text encoders and attention mechanisms are integrated in latent denoising networks.

Summary and practical tips

Diffusion and score-based models are conceptually elegant: a simple forward noising process turns data into noise, and a learned reverse process, driven by score estimates, reconstructs data from noise. Discrete-time DDPM and continuous-time score-based SDE formulations are mathematically connected; both permit training via denoising objectives that reduce to predicting noise. Practical sampling strategies include stochastic ancestral sampling, deterministic DDIM samplers, and classifier-free guidance for conditioning. Latent diffusion offers a practical and scalable approach for high-resolution generation by moving the diffusion process into a lower-dimensional learned representation.

When learning these models and reading research papers, pay particular attention to: the noise schedule and its impact on training dynamics and sampling; the parameterization chosen (predicting noise, predicting $x_0$, or predicting score) and how it affects loss scaling and numerical stability; the variance parameterization in reverse conditionals (learned vs fixed); and the sampling algorithm (SDE integration vs DDIM vs ancestral) used at inference. Understanding the mathematical equivalences and approximations connecting these choices will help you interpret results, reproduce experiments, and design improvements.

Introduction

Text-to-image generation systems combine natural language understanding with image synthesis to produce images that match a textual description. Modern pipelines typically decompose the problem into modules: a text encoder that converts text into a compact representation, a generative backbone (often a diffusion model) that produces images conditioned on that representation, and auxiliary components such as latent autoencoders and guidance schemes that shape the output. This chapter explains the main building blocks and data flows used in these systems, with an emphasis on intuition and practical details needed to read and implement research papers. We will introduce how multimodal embeddings are created, how conditioning is injected into diffusion denoisers, the role of latent autoencoders, common denoiser architectures, representative high-level system designs (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, DALL·E2), and the runtime considerations of sampling.

# Text encoders and multimodal embeddings (e.g., CLIP encoder basics)

At the heart of a text-conditioned image generator is a text encoder: a model that maps a textual prompt into a vector (or set of vectors) that can be used by the image generator to control content, style, and composition. A widely used family of encoders is based on contrastive multimodal training such as CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining). The CLIP-style encoder is trained to produce embeddings for images and text in a shared latent space where matching image-text pairs are close and mismatched pairs are far apart.

Intuitively, CLIP training uses a batch of text-image pairs and a contrastive loss to align the embeddings. Let $t_i$ and $v_i$ be text and image inputs in the same batch. The text encoder $E_T$ produces a vector $z^T_i = E_T(t_i)$ and the image encoder $E_V$ produces $z^V_i = E_V(v_i)$. The contrastive objective encourages the cosine similarity between $z^T_i$ and $z^V_i$ to be high for matching pairs and low for non-matching pairs. A common loss is the cross-entropy over similarities, e.g. for text-to-image direction:
\[
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i \log \frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z^T_i, z^V_i)/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(\mathrm{sim}(z^T_i, z^V_j)/\tau)}
\]
where $\mathrm{sim}(a,b) = \frac{a^\top b}{\|a\|\|b\|}$ and $\tau$ is a temperature parameter. This trains both encoders to place semantically matching pairs together in the same space.

There are two important structural features of modern text encoders. First, many are transformer-based and produce a sequence of per-token embeddings as well as a pooled global embedding. The pooled vector summarizes the whole prompt; per-token embeddings allow the generator to attend to different parts of the prompt separately. Second, the output dimensionality is chosen to be compatible with the downstream model; for example, if the denoiser uses cross-attention layers, the text encoder typically returns a matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ where $L$ is the number of tokens and $d$ is the attention key/query/value dimensionality.

CLIP-like embeddings have several practical advantages: they are trained on very large, diverse datasets so they capture a wide range of concepts; they are compact and fast to compute; and because image and text live in the same space, they offer natural evaluation metrics (cosine similarity) and enable multimodal retrieval. However, CLIP embeddings are learned for alignment rather than exact captioning fidelity; they can sometimes lose fine-grained structural instructions (e.g., exact counts or spatial arrangements) unless the text encoder or training data explicitly focus on such aspects.

Beyond CLIP, other multimodal encoders exist: larger transformer text encoders (e.g., T5 variants) tuned to generate embeddings for conditioning; encoder-decoder multimodal models that produce distributions over tokens; and specialized trained embeddings for attributes like style. The choice depends on whether the downstream generator expects a single global vector, a sequence of vectors for cross-attention, or a different conditioning format.

# Conditioning mechanisms in diffusion models: concatenation, cross-attention, classifier guidance

Conditioning is the mechanism by which the generator is informed about the desired content. Diffusion models are probability models over images that learn to reverse a noising process. Conditioning modifies the denoising process so that samples are drawn from the conditional distribution $p(x|c)$, where $c$ is the conditioning signal (text embedding, class label, example image, etc.). There are several common conditioning mechanisms: input concatenation, cross-attention, and classifier-based guidance.

Concatenation is the simplest approach: the conditioning signal is concatenated to the input (or to features) of the denoiser. For example, if the denoiser processes an image latent $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W \times C}$, one can tile or spatially broadcast a conditioning tensor $c$ (e.g., a global vector reshaped to a constant channel map) and concatenate along the channel dimension. The denoiser then learns filters that combine local image structure with the global conditioning. Concatenation is easy to implement and works well when the conditioning is low-dimensional and constant across spatial locations. However, it is limited in expressiveness for complex prompts and does not naturally allow token-wise interactions between parts of the prompt and specific spatial regions.

Cross-attention provides a more flexible mechanism. In a denoiser block that uses attention, you can treat the image features as queries and the text embeddings as keys and values. Concretely, let $F \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ be flattened image features (with $N = H\times W$ positions) and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$ be text embeddings. Cross-attention computes
\[
\mathrm{Attention}(F, C) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{F W_Q (C W_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right) (C W_V)
\]
where $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ are learned projections. This lets each spatial location attend to any token in the prompt with a learned, content-dependent weighting. The model can thus learn to place objects mentioned late in the prompt into particular parts of the image by forming spatial queries that match token content. Cross-attention also naturally supports variable-length prompts and fine-grained instruction following. Architecturally, cross-attention blocks are inserted into UNet residual blocks; they add negligible computational cost compared to the convolutions for modest token lengths.

Classifier guidance is a different idea that steers sampling using an auxiliary classifier that predicts conditioning labels from noisy inputs. Introduced for class-conditional diffusion, classifier guidance uses an external classifier $p_\phi(c|x_t)$ trained to predict the conditioning $c$ from a noisy image $x_t$. During sampling, the denoiser’s score function $\nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t)$ is adjusted using the classifier:
\[
\nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t|c) = \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t) + \nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(c|x_t).
\]
In practice, one uses the noise-prediction model to get $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ and modifies it by adding a scaled gradient from the classifier, often implemented by predicting a conditional noise and taking a linear combination:
\[
\tilde{\epsilon} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) + s \cdot \big(\epsilon_{\theta,c}(x_t, t) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big)
\]
where $s$ is a guidance scale. Classifier guidance can produce sharper, more accurate conditional samples but requires a separately trained classifier and can be expensive. Classifier-free guidance is a related and now widely used technique that avoids a separate classifier by training the denoiser to model both conditional and unconditional distributions: during training, random conditioning inputs are sometimes replaced with a null token. At sampling, one combines the conditional and unconditional outputs:
\[
\tilde{\epsilon} = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c_{\text{cond}}) + s \cdot \big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c_{\text{cond}}) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c_{\text{null}})\big).
\]
Classifier-free guidance offers the guidance advantages while keeping training simpler and allowing fine-grained control via the scale $s$.

Each of these mechanisms has trade-offs. Concatenation is simple but limited in expressivity. Cross-attention is powerful for text because it supports token-wise interactions and spatial conditioning. Classifier-based approaches give a direct mechanism to push samples toward a conditioning but add training and inference complexity. Modern text-to-image systems often combine cross-attention conditioning with classifier-free guidance to get both expressive alignment and controlled sampling.

# Latent autoencoders (VAE, VQ-GAN) and their role in latent diffusion pipelines

Working directly in pixel space is expensive: high-resolution images have many pixels, and diffusion models must denoise at every resolution. Latent autoencoders compress images into a lower-dimensional latent space where diffusion models can operate more efficiently. Two common types of autoencoders are Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and vector-quantized autoencoders such as VQ-GAN.

A VAE has an encoder $E$ that maps an image $x$ to a latent distribution $q(z|x)$ (often Gaussian with mean and diagonal covariance) and a decoder $D$ that reconstructs $x$ from $z$. Training minimizes a reconstruction loss plus a Kullback–Leibler divergence regularizer:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}\big[-\log p(x|z)\big] + \mathrm{KL}(q(z|x) \| p(z)).
\]
In practice, VAEs used for latent diffusion are often learned to prioritize perceptual and L2 reconstruction quality rather than strict probabilistic modeling, for example by weighting the KL term or using perceptual losses. The key is that images $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ become latents $z \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times c}$ where $h \ll H$, $w \ll W$. Operating a diffusion model on $z$ reduces compute and memory costs roughly by a factor of $(Hh^{-1})(Ww^{-1})$.

VQ-GANs (vector-quantized generative adversarial networks) combine discrete latent codes with adversarial training. The encoder maps an image to indices in a learned codebook; the decoder reconstructs from codebook embeddings. The discrete nature can encourage compact, semantically meaningful codes and has been popular in autoregressive generation (for example DALL·E). VQ-GAN extends the VQ-VAE idea by adding GAN loss and perceptual loss to improve reconstruction sharpness.

Latent diffusion models (LDMs) like Stable Diffusion adopt the strategy: train an autoencoder $E,D$ to map images $x \leftrightarrow z$, then train a diffusion denoiser on $z$. The diffusion model learns a denoising distribution $p_\theta(z_0)$ (or conditional $p_\theta(z_0|c)$), and at inference time one samples $\hat z_0$ via diffusion and decodes $\hat x = D(\hat z_0)$. The autoencoder’s capacity and the information retained in $z$ are crucial: if the encoder discards important high-frequency detail, the decoded images will be blurry or lack fidelity. So practical systems choose a compression factor (e.g., 8× downsampling) and tune losses to preserve perceptual quality.

Using a latent space also interacts with conditioning: the text embeddings must be compatible with the latent features. Cross-attention typically occurs inside the denoiser that operates on latent features, so the text encoder must provide embeddings in a dimensionality that the denoiser expects. The choice of latent space size, channel count, and tokenizer (for VQ-based methods) all influence the design and performance of the pipeline.

# Typical UNet or encoder-decoder denoiser architectures used in diffusion models

Diffusion denoisers require architectures that can model multi-scale image structure and incorporate conditioning. The UNet architecture is the canonical choice: it combines downsampling and upsampling paths with skip connections, enabling both localized processing and global context aggregation. A UNet processes a noisy image or latent at time step $t$ and produces a prediction for the noise or denoised image.

A typical UNet for diffusion has an encoder path that repeatedly applies convolutions and downsampling (strided conv or pooling) to reduce spatial resolution and increase channels, creating a feature hierarchy. The bottleneck captures global context. The decoder path upsamples and combines features from corresponding encoder levels via skip connections, preserving fine-grained spatial detail. Residual blocks (ResNet-style) are commonly used within each level. Importantly, time-step conditioning is injected into each block: the denoiser needs to know $t$ because the noise level changes across steps. Time is often encoded via sinusoidal embeddings $\gamma(t)$ or a learned MLP that produces a vector which is then added or used to scale activations inside blocks.

In text-conditioned models, cross-attention layers are inserted into UNet residual blocks—typically after normalization and before nonlinearity—so that intermediate features can attend to text embeddings. This allows the denoiser to modulate internal features based on the prompt at multiple scales. Positional encodings for spatial features are sometimes used to allow the model to learn spatial relationships more easily.

Encoder-decoder transformers have also been explored as denoisers, especially in pixel- or patch-based formulations. Here the encoder processes the noisy input into tokens, and cross-attention with conditioning tokens can be used in the decoder. However, for dense image data with large resolution, convolutional UNets remain efficient and very effective.

The architecture choices—depth, number of channels, attention placement, and normalization type—affect sample fidelity and computational cost. Attention becomes expensive at high spatial resolution because it scales quadratically in token count; hence attention is often applied only at lower-resolution bottleneck layers or using efficient attention variants. The balance between convolutional inductive bias and attention's global receptive field is a recurring design trade-off.

# High-level pipelines of systems like Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and DALL·E2

Although the broad components are shared, different systems assemble them differently and make distinct training choices.

Stable Diffusion is a latent diffusion pipeline. It uses a VAE to compress images to a latent space, a CLIP text encoder (typically a transformer) to produce text token embeddings, and a UNet-based denoiser that operates on latents. Cross-attention layers inside the UNet let latent features attend to text tokens. Stable Diffusion is trained on image-caption pairs to model $p(z_0|c)$ with a standard diffusion objective in latent space. At inference, classifier-free guidance is used: the UNet is trained both conditionally and unconditionally by randomly dropping text, and sampling combines these outputs with a guidance scale. This design yields efficient sampling (relative to pixel-space diffusion) and good expressivity from the CLIP-conditioned cross-attention.

Imagen (from Google) emphasized the strength of large, high-capacity text encoders (notably large T5 variants) and higher-resolution training. Imagen uses a cascade of diffusion models that operate at progressively higher resolutions. The text encoder is pretrained for captioning-quality embeddings; the diffusion models are trained in pixel or high-quality latent spaces. Imagen reported that text encoder quality was a primary factor in improved text fidelity, demonstrating that better language understanding directly improves image alignment. Imagen also used classifier-free guidance at sampling time and relied on careful data curation and loss weighting to produce photorealistic outputs.

DALL·E2 (OpenAI’s second-generation system) uses a two-step approach that leverages CLIP in a different way. Instead of conditioning a diffusion model directly on text, DALL·E2 trains a diffusion model to map from CLIP image embeddings to images, and a separate prior model maps text embeddings to image embeddings. Concretely, given text $t$, the prior predicts an image embedding $z_I \approx E_V(v)$ conditioned on the text embedding $z_T = E_T(t)$. Then a decoder (a diffusion model or autoregressive decoder) generates the image conditioned on $z_I$. This modular design separates the language-to-embedding mapping (the prior) from embedding-to-image generation (the decoder), which can ease training and enable better control of semantics via the embedding space. DALL·E2 also explored diffusion-based decoders and used CLIP as a representation bottleneck.

These systems share common elements: a text encoder trained or selected for strong semantic representation, a generative backbone that translates conditioning into image structure, and guidance techniques to balance fidelity and diversity. They differ in where compression or cascading occurs (latent vs. pixel cascades), whether the text-to-image mapping is direct or mediated by an embedding prior, and how large and specialized the text encoder is. Understanding these architectural decisions helps interpret reported trade-offs in image quality, text fidelity, and computational cost across papers.

# Runtime considerations and inference pipeline for text-conditioned sampling

At inference time, the pipeline stitches together the components and runs iterative sampling. The high-level flow is: encode text, sample latent (via diffusion) conditioned on text, and decode latent to image. Each stage has practical performance and implementation considerations.

First, text encoding happens once per prompt. For CLIP-style encoders or large transformer encoders, this can dominate latency if the encoder is very large, but it is typically modest compared to multiple diffusion steps. If you generate many images for the same prompt, encode once and reuse embeddings. Tokenization, prompt engineering (like adding style or negative prompts), and batching of prompts are standard optimizations.

Second, the diffusion sampling loop iteratively updates a latent $z_t$ from noise to a clean sample $z_0$. The number of steps $T$ (e.g., 50–1000 in classic setups) affects both quality and runtime. Recent samplers and schedulers (DDIM, PLMS, DPM-Solver) provide fewer steps with comparable quality; choosing a sampler is a trade-off between speed and fidelity. Classifier-free guidance modifies the denoiser call at each step and requires two forward passes (conditional and unconditional) or one pass with concatenated conditioning depending on implementation, increasing runtime. Cross-attention layers add compute proportional to the number of tokens and spatial positions; for high-resolution latents or long prompts, this can be significant.

Memory usage is another key constraint. Storing activations for backpropagation is unnecessary at inference but storing the denoiser model and text encoder parameters consumes GPU memory. For real-time or constrained environments, one can use mixed precision (FP16), model quantization, or smaller models. Latent spaces reduce memory and compute relative to pixel-space diffusion, allowing larger UNets or more attention layers for the same resources.

The decode step (VAE decoder) is usually a single forward pass but can be heavy depending on decoder architecture. Some pipelines perform multi-stage cascades: generate a low-resolution latent, decode or upsample using another denoiser, and refine at higher resolutions. Cascades improve quality but increase inference time.

Parallelism and batching can help throughput. If generating multiple images, batch multiple noise samples through the UNet and process text encodings for all prompts in parallel. When using classifier-free guidance, batching needs to accommodate both conditional and unconditional pairs (e.g., interleave unconditional embeddings in the batch). However, large batch sizes increase memory pressure.

Practical runtime engineering also addresses non-idealities: preserving aspect ratios, handling long prompts, and managing safety filters. Pre- and post-processing steps include prompt normalization, negative prompt specification, image resizing, and post-decoding color correction. For controlled composition, methods like region-conditioned attention, image-conditioned inpainting, or multi-token spatial control are applied; these may require additional passes or modified conditioning.

Finally, latency-sensitive applications can use cached components, distilled models with fewer sampling steps, or alternative architectures (autoregressive transformers for low-latency generation of small images). Monitoring and tuning sampling hyperparameters (guidance scale, step count, noise schedule) is essential to find a balance that suits quality, speed, and diversity requirements.

Conclusion

Text-to-image systems are modular but tightly integrated: the choice of text encoder affects how conditioning is represented, which in turn shapes the denoiser architecture and where cross-attention should be placed. Latent autoencoders allow computationally efficient diffusion modeling by compressing image information, and UNet-based denoisers with time and cross-attention conditioning are the workhorses of modern pipelines. Different systems like Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and DALL·E2 make different trade-offs in encoder size, latent representation, and modular decomposition to achieve state-of-the-art results. Understanding these components and their interactions is essential for reading research papers, reproducing results, and designing new multimodal generative models.

Introduction

Linear algebra provides the language and intuition for describing what neural networks compute and how their parameters change during training. At its core, a neural network manipulates vectors with linear and nonlinear operations: weights multiply activations, inner products produce pre-activations, and normalization rescales signals. Understanding basic geometric concepts such as vector norms, inner products, angles, and directionality of weight vectors makes many phenomena in deep learning—such as why normalization helps, how neurons specialize, and how scaling affects gradients—clear and predictable. In this chapter we develop those notions carefully and build an intuitive geometric picture of neurons and filters as vectors in Euclidean space. We will emphasize the role of direction versus magnitude, the meaning of cosine similarity, and the practical consequences of constraining or normalizing weights and activations.

# Vectors, norms (L2), inner products and cosine similarity

A vector in this context is an ordered list of real numbers that represent either an input (activation) to a neuron or the parameters (weights) of a neuron. We typically work in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$, so a vector is written as $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d)^\top$. Two fundamental tools for comparing and measuring vectors are the norm and the inner product.

The L2 norm (Euclidean norm) of a vector $x$ is defined by $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_d^2}$. The L2 norm measures the length or magnitude of the vector in Euclidean space. It is useful whenever we care about overall signal strength, such as the amplitude of an activation vector or the size of a weights vector. The squared L2 norm $\|x\|_2^2 = x^\top x$ often appears in optimization (for example as an L2 regularizer) because it is smooth and differentiable.

The inner product (or dot product) between two vectors $x$ and $w$ is defined by $w^\top x = \sum_{i=1}^d w_i x_i$. The inner product has two complementary interpretations. Algebraically, it is a bilinear form used to compute projections and energies; geometrically, it relates to both magnitudes and the angle between vectors. A key identity connects inner product and norms:
\[
w^\top x = \|w\|_2 \, \|x\|_2 \cos\theta,
\]
where $\theta$ is the angle between $w$ and $x$. From this identity we see that the inner product is large when either vector has large magnitude or when the vectors point in similar directions (small $\theta$).

Cosine similarity isolates the directional component by dividing the inner product by the product of norms:
\[
\cos\theta = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|_2 \, \|x\|_2}.
\]
Cosine similarity ranges from $-1$ to $1$ and depends only on the direction of $w$ and $x$, not on their magnitudes. It is widely used in information retrieval and in neural network analysis when we want to compare patterns irrespective of scale. In practice, cosine similarity helps identify whether two vectors have similar orientation in high-dimensional space even when their lengths differ.

Understanding these basic quantities is essential for interpreting neuron activations and for reasoning about how training influences weights. The norm tells you how strong a signal is; the inner product measures alignment scaled by magnitudes; and cosine similarity isolates alignment alone.

# Interpreting neurons/filters as weight vectors in R^d

A single neuron in a fully connected layer computes a weighted sum of its inputs followed by a nonlinearity. If the neuron has weights $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$, and it receives input $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, its pre-activation is $z = w^\top x + b$. Geometrically, $w$ is a vector that defines a linear functional on the input space: it selects a particular direction in input space and measures how much $x$ projects onto that direction, scaled by the length of $w$.

Thinking of neurons as weight vectors provides a geometric view of their function. The set of inputs $x$ that produce the same pre-activation $z$ (ignoring the bias for a moment) lie on hyperplanes orthogonal to $w$. For fixed bias, the decision boundary or level set $w^\top x + b = 0$ is an affine hyperplane perpendicular to $w$. Thus the direction of $w$ determines which features of the input are considered salient by that neuron: inputs that align positively with $w$ produce large positive pre-activations, and inputs that align negatively produce large negative pre-activations.

In convolutional networks, a filter (kernel) can be flattened into a vector and interpreted similarly. A convolutional filter applied to image patches computes inner products between the filter weights and local patches; hence each filter is a template that “matches” certain local patterns. A filter vector that points in the direction of particular edge patterns will produce large activations on patches aligned with that pattern. Filters in early layers often become oriented edge detectors, while filters in later layers correspond to more complex template patterns; in every case, the weight vector defines which patterns are emphasized.

This weight-vector interpretation explains phenomena like selectivity and specialization: neurons that are trained to respond to specific features develop weight vectors that point roughly in the direction of those features in input space. When you visualize a neuron's weights (e.g., as an image), you are visually seeing the template that the neuron correlates with.

# Normalization of weights and activations; effects of unit-norm constraints

Normalization rescales vectors so that they have a prescribed magnitude or distribution. Two common forms of normalization in neural networks are weight normalization (constraining or parametrizing weights to have specific norms) and activation normalization (e.g., batch normalization, layer normalization), which standardize activations across examples or features.

Unit-norm constraints force a vector to have $\|w\|_2 = 1$. There are a few ways to achieve this in practice: one can directly reparameterize the weight as $w = \frac{v}{\|v\|_2}$ where $v$ is an unconstrained vector, or one can apply a projection step after each gradient update to rescale $w$. Enforcing unit-norm removes magnitude as a degree of freedom so only direction matters for the linear computation $w^\top x$ if inputs are also normalized. This has several consequences. First, when $\|w\|_2 = 1$, the pre-activation reduces to $z = \|x\|_2 \cos\theta + b$, making clear that activations depend on input magnitude and cosine similarity only. Second, gradients with respect to direction can become more stable because scaling instabilities (e.g., runaway growth of weight norms) are eliminated.

Activation normalization methods, such as batch normalization, standardize the distribution of activations across a batch by subtracting a mean and dividing by a standard deviation, often followed by learned scaling and shift parameters. From a geometric perspective, this centers and rescales the cloud of activation vectors in the layer so that their coordinate-wise variances are controlled. This reduces covariate shift between layers and stabilizes training by ensuring that the inputs to nonlinearities remain in favorable ranges. For example, if inputs to a ReLU are too large or too small, gradient signals can vanish or explode; normalization keeps inputs in a regime where gradients are informative.

Unit-norm constraints and activation normalization both reduce the degrees of freedom that learning must manage. When weights are constrained to unit norm, the optimization focuses on choosing directions rather than magnitudes. This can improve generalization by preventing a single neuron from simply increasing its norm to trivially scale outputs, which might otherwise interact poorly with regularization or downstream nonlinearities. However, there are trade-offs. If only weights are unit-norm but activations vary greatly in magnitude, the network still can change effective strengths via the activation norms. Conversely, if activations are normalized but weights can change norms freely, the network can still control output scale by adjusting weights. Many normalization schemes combine constraints or learned scale parameters so that the network retains some capacity to adjust magnitudes when needed.

# Angles between vectors and geometric interpretation of similarity

The angle between two vectors provides an intuitive geometric measure of how similar their directions are, independently of their magnitudes. Given vectors $w$ and $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the cosine of the angle between them is
\[
\cos\theta = \frac{w^\top x}{\|w\|_2 \, \|x\|_2}.
\]
If $\cos\theta$ is close to $1$, the vectors point in nearly the same direction; if $\cos\theta$ is close to $-1$, they point in opposite directions; and if $\cos\theta$ is near $0$, they are nearly orthogonal.

In high-dimensional spaces, random vectors tend to be nearly orthogonal: as dimensionality $d$ increases, the distribution of angles between randomly drawn vectors concentrates around $\pi/2$. This fact has practical implications: in a high-dimensional input space, a randomly initialized neuron will, with high probability, have little alignment with any particular structured input. Learning works by rotating weight vectors from random orientations toward directions that align with relevant patterns in the data.

Visualizing angle changes helps to understand learning dynamics. When training a neuron to recognize a pattern, the weight vector rotates in input space toward the pattern direction. Small-angle rotations correspond to minor adjustments in selectivity; large-angle rotations indicate that the neuron’s focus has substantially changed. Because the inner product scales with cosine of the angle, moving a weight from an angle $\theta$ to a smaller angle $\theta'$ increases the activation on inputs aligned with the target by a factor proportional to $\cos\theta'/\cos\theta$, assuming norms fixed.

Angles also explain interference and orthogonality-based strategies. If two neurons have weight vectors that are nearly orthogonal, they respond to largely different components of the input. This orthogonality can reduce redundancy and improve expressiveness by encouraging diverse feature detectors. Conversely, when weight vectors become aligned (small angles), neurons respond similarly and may be redundant unless their biases or subsequent nonlinearities separate their behavior.

A useful geometric relation is the law of cosines for differences: the squared Euclidean distance between normalized vectors relates to angle via
\[
\| \hat{w} - \hat{x} \|_2^2 = 2(1 - \cos\theta),
\]
where $\hat{w} = w/\|w\|_2$ and $\hat{x} = x/\|x\|_2$. This equation shows that small angles correspond to small Euclidean distances on the unit sphere, reinforcing the idea that directional similarity can be quantified either by angle (cosine) or by Euclidean distance after normalization.

# Consequences of scaling vs direction change on linear transforms

A linear transform applied by a neuron depends on both the direction and the scale of its weight vector. Consider the pre-activation $z = w^\top x$. We can factor $w$ into its norm and direction: $w = \|w\|_2 \hat{w}$ where $\hat{w}$ is the unit vector. Then
\[
z = \|w\|_2 (\hat{w}^\top x) = \|w\|_2 \, \|x\|_2 \cos\theta.
\]
This decomposition clarifies how scaling and direction affect the output. Scaling the weight by a positive scalar $\alpha$ multiplies $z$ by $\alpha$, so changing the magnitude of $w$ is equivalent to amplifying or attenuating the neuron's response uniformly across all inputs. Changing the direction $\hat{w}$, however, alters which inputs receive strong responses by changing their cosines with $\hat{w}$.

These two degrees of freedom—scale and direction—have different implications for learning and for stability. Scaling tends to affect the amplitude of activations and gradients. For example, if you double the weights of a neuron (scale $\alpha = 2$) but keep everything else constant, pre-activations double and, depending on the nonlinearity, downstream gradients may also scale, potentially destabilizing training. Directional changes, in contrast, change the pattern selectivity of the neuron without necessarily amplifying all responses.

A concrete consequence appears in networks with homogeneous activation functions, such as ReLU, which satisfy $\sigma(cz) = c\sigma(z)$ for $c \ge 0$. In such networks, scaling the weights of one layer and inversely scaling the next layer can leave the function represented by the network invariant. For instance, if one layer has weight matrix $W_1$ and the next linear layer has weights $W_2$, then replacing $W_1$ by $\alpha W_1$ and $W_2$ by $\frac{1}{\alpha} W_2$ results in the same composition when activations are homogeneous. This shows that scale degrees of freedom can create flat directions in parameter space—many parameter settings correspond to the same function—which can complicate optimization because gradient-based methods navigate a manifold with redundant parameterizations.

Another consequence concerns regularization. L2 weight decay penalizes $\|w\|_2^2$, pushing the optimizer to prefer smaller norms. Since scaling changes norms directly, weight decay effectively biases solutions towards lower-amplitude weights. If the task primarily requires directional alignment rather than large magnitudes, weight decay helps by encouraging the network to use direction changes rather than uncontrolled scaling. Conversely, if the problem needs large signal magnitudes, weight decay may conflict with representational needs unless balanced by learned scaling parameters (for example, batch normalization’s affine gamma parameter).

Gradient behavior also depends on scale and direction differently. Consider the gradient of a loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $w$. If we decompose $w = \|w\|_2 \hat{w}$ and constrain $\hat{w}$ to the unit sphere, the gradient splits into a radial component (changing $\|w\|_2$) and a tangential component (rotating $\hat{w}$). Radial gradients rescale the neuron's output, while tangential gradients change selectivity. Optimization schemes that project gradients to be tangent to the sphere (so-called Riemannian gradient on the sphere) enforce constant norm and focus learning on direction. These projected gradients can have different magnitudes and dynamics than unconstrained gradients, sometimes improving stability because the explosive growth of norms is mitigated.

Finally, the interaction of input scaling and weight direction is important. If inputs $x$ are scaled uniformly (e.g., due to dataset statistics), scaling either input or weights produces the same pre-activation effect, but training dynamics differ: when inputs are large, gradients with respect to weights may also be large, causing noisy updates; activation normalization helps by fixing input scale so that learning can focus on directional alignment.

Summary

Interpreting neurons and filters geometrically as vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ gives powerful intuition for what neural networks do and how training modifies them. Norms quantify magnitudes, inner products combine magnitude and alignment, and cosine similarity isolates direction. Normalization methods and unit-norm constraints alter which degrees of freedom (scale vs direction) the optimizer controls, with important implications for stability and generalization. Angles measure directional similarity and are central to understanding specialization and redundancy among neurons. Finally, scaling and direction changes have qualitatively different effects on linear transforms and on learning dynamics; understanding their roles helps explain phenomena like parameter redundancy, the need for normalization, and the geometry of learned representations.

Introduction

Many modern representation-learning methods benefit from focusing on the directions of vectors rather than their raw magnitudes. When the information that matters about a data point or a classifier is mostly encoded in the direction of a vector, it is natural to represent the vector on the unit hypersphere and use angular measures (angles, cosine similarity) to compare and manipulate representations. This chapter develops the geometry of unit hyperspheres, explains why and how cosine similarity and angular distances are used as semantic measures, and examines how angular-based classification methods such as SphereNet and angular softmax work. We then introduce the formal notion of hyperspherical energy, a scalar quantity that captures pairwise angular relations among a set of vectors on the sphere, and develop the intuition that low or high hyperspherical energy corresponds to particular organization patterns of neurons or features. Finally, we discuss implications of hyperspherical energy for neuron uniformity, redundancy reduction, and the emergent structure of learned representations.

# Unit hypersphere and normalization of feature/weight vectors

The unit hypersphere in $d$ dimensions, denoted $S^{d-1}$, is the set of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with unit Euclidean norm:
$$
S^{d-1} = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : \|x\|_2 = 1\}.
$$
A point on $S^{d-1}$ is determined entirely by its direction; any positive scalar multiple of the point corresponds to the same direction, so restricting vectors to unit length removes scale information and emphasizes angular relationships.

Normalization of feature and weight vectors is a common preprocessing or architectural choice in deep learning. Given a feature vector $f \in \mathbb{R}^d$, normalized features are defined by
$$
\hat{f} = \frac{f}{\|f\|_2},
$$
and likewise for a weight vector $w$, we write $\hat{w} = w / \|w\|_2$. After normalization, both $\hat{f}$ and $\hat{w}$ lie on $S^{d-1}$.

There are several reasons to normalize vectors in representations and classifiers. First, normalization decouples magnitude and direction. In many problems, magnitudes can be arbitrary or dominated by factors unrelated to semantics (batch effects, lighting, or arbitrary scaling by previous layers), while directions carry class or identity information. Normalization therefore reduces variance caused by magnitude and stabilizes training. Second, normalization makes similarity measures bounded and comparable: the inner product of unit vectors equals cosine similarity and lies in $[-1,1]$, which is convenient for loss scaling and for interpreting logits in classification. Third, normalization often leads to better-conditioned optimization landscapes; gradients with respect to normalized parameters do not blow up when weights become large, and the scale invariance of many architectures is explicit when operating on the sphere.

In practice, normalization can be implemented in several ways: a hard constraint (explicitly reprojecting vectors onto the sphere after each gradient step), by adding a normalization layer (e.g., $L_2$-normalization of the feature vector before the classifier), or by parameterizing weights as normalized vectors with separate learnable scalars. For example, in classifier layers we often see the form
$$
s \cdot \hat{w}_c^\top \hat{f},
$$
where $s>0$ is a learnable or fixed scalar that controls the effective temperature or margin of the logits, $\hat{w}_c$ is the $c$-th class weight normalized to unit length, and $\hat{f}$ is the normalized feature. This enforces a purely angular decision rule: the logits depend only on the angle between $\hat{f}$ and class weight directions.

# Cosine similarity and angular metrics as semantic measures

Cosine similarity between two vectors $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is defined as the inner product of their normalized versions:
$$
\operatorname{cosine}(x,y) = \frac{x^\top y}{\|x\|_2 \|y\|_2}.
$$
When $x$ and $y$ are already normalized, the cosine similarity simplifies to $x^\top y$. Because $x^\top y = \cos\theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $x$ and $y$, cosine similarity is directly related to angular separation. The angle itself is given by
$$
\theta(x,y) = \arccos\left( \frac{x^\top y}{\|x\|_2 \|y\|_2} \right).
$$

Why is cosine similarity often a better semantic measure than Euclidean distance in representation learning? Intuitively, cosine similarity ignores vector length and focuses on direction. For many semantic tasks, length can reflect irrelevant factors (feature magnitude correlated with sample difficulty or norm regularization) while direction captures the discriminative information. Consider image or text embeddings trained so that semantically similar items point in similar directions: two embeddings that are scalar multiples of each other represent essentially the same semantic content, and cosine similarity treats them as identical, whereas Euclidean distance would not.

Angular metrics also have desirable geometric properties. The geodesic distance on the unit sphere between two points is proportional to their angle:
$$
d_{\mathrm{geo}}(x,y) = \theta(x,y).
$$
This geodesic distance is intrinsic to the manifold $S^{d-1}$, whereas Euclidean distance measures chord length in the ambient space. For unit vectors $x$ and $y$, the chordal distance is
$$
\|x - y\|_2 = \sqrt{2(1 - x^\top y)} = \sqrt{2(1 - \cos\theta)}.
$$
For small angles, chordal distance and angle are approximately proportional, but for larger angles they differ; if the task cares about rotation-invariant relationships, geodesic (angular) distances are more meaningful.

In information retrieval, face recognition, and metric learning, cosine similarity is widely used to rank nearest neighbors and to define margins. Cosine similarity also leads to better-behaved decision boundaries when weights are normalized: classification boundaries become great circles (hyperplanes intersected with the sphere), and the relative positions of class weight directions fully determine the partitioning of the sphere into class regions.

Finally, cosine similarity provides a simple way to implement contrastive objectives and temperature scaling. In contrastive learning losses (e.g., InfoNCE), similarities are often computed as $s \cdot \hat{z}_i^\top \hat{z}_j$ where $s$ is a temperature parameter; optimization then encourages similar pairs to have large cosine similarity and dissimilar pairs to have small similarity. Temperature controls the softness of the softmax over cosine similarities and hence the effective margin between classes.

# SphereNet and angular softmax ideas (using directions for classification)

SphereNet and angular softmax variants introduce angular geometry explicitly into neural-network classifiers by making the network's outputs and classifier weights live on the unit sphere, and by designing losses and margins that depend on angles rather than raw inner products. The central idea is to make classification depend on the angle between a feature vector and class weight vectors, enforcing that correct-class angles are as small as possible and that different classes occupy distinct directional regions.

A prototypical angular-softmax formulation modifies the usual linear classifier and cross-entropy loss as follows. In a standard linear classifier, logits for a feature $f$ are computed as $w_c^\top f + b_c$. If we normalize both weights and features and optionally remove biases, the logits become proportional to the cosine similarity:
$$
\ell_c(f) = s \cdot \hat{w}_c^\top \hat{f} = s \cos \theta_c,
$$
where $\theta_c$ is the angle between $\hat{f}$ and $\hat{w}_c$ and $s$ is a positive scale (often called temperature). The cross-entropy loss is then applied to these logits:
$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log\frac{\exp(s \cos \theta_{y})}{\sum_{c}\exp(s \cos \theta_c)},
$$
where $y$ is the ground-truth class label.

Angular-softmax (sometimes called A-Softmax, SphereFace, or variants like CosFace, ArcFace) further modifies the target logit to enforce an angular margin. One approach replaces $\cos \theta_{y}$ with $\cos(m \theta_{y})$ for an integer margin $m\ge 1$, which makes the decision boundary require a smaller angle to be classified correctly. The SphereFace A-Softmax does precisely this, encouraging learned features to be closer (in angle) to the correct class weight by multiplying the angle inside the cosine. Alternative approaches such as CosFace and ArcFace impose additive margins: they subtract a margin $m$ from the cosine or add an angular margin to the angle before taking the cosine. For example, ArcFace directly enforces:
$$
\ell_y(f) = s \cos(\theta_y + m),
$$
with $m>0$ an angular margin. These margins increase inter-class separability on the sphere by pushing class directions further apart and forcing features to be more tightly clustered around their class weights.

SphereNet is an umbrella term for architectures that combine normalization (placing features and sometimes weights on $S^{d-1}$), angular margins, and often spherical convolutions or representations organized on the sphere. The key is to treat classification as directional discrimination on $S^{d-1}$ rather than magnitude-based discrimination in Euclidean space.

Design choices in angular-softmax families matter. The scale $s$ controls effective temperature and gradient magnitudes: too small, and the classifier cannot enforce margins; too large, and optimization becomes unstable. The margin parameter $m$ controls discriminative tightness: larger margins yield more separated classes but also make initial optimization harder and risk overfitting or underfitting if set improperly. Empirically, angular softmax variants have produced state-of-the-art results in face recognition and metric learning, where identity is well-captured by direction and intra-class angular variance should be minimized.

# Definition of hyperspherical energy and intuition (pairwise angular relations)

Hyperspherical energy is a scalar measure that quantifies how a set of points on the unit sphere are distributed relative to each other, focusing on pairwise angular relations. There are several related definitions depending on the kernel or potential chosen, but a common form is inspired by electrostatic or Coulomb-like interactions: given $n$ unit vectors $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n \subset S^{d-1}$, define the $s$-energy (sometimes called Riesz $s$-energy) as
$$
E_s(\{x_i\}) = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} \frac{1}{\|x_i - x_j\|_2^s},
$$
for some $s>0$. Because $\|x_i - x_j\|_2$ depends on the angle $\theta_{ij}$ via $\|x_i - x_j\|_2 = \sqrt{2(1 - x_i^\top x_j)}$, this energy is a function of pairwise cosines or angles. In terms of inner products, one may equivalently write kernels such as
$$
K_\alpha(x_i, x_j) = (1 - x_i^\top x_j)^{-\alpha}
$$
for some exponent $\alpha$, and define energy as $\sum_{i\ne j} K_\alpha(x_i,x_j)$.

Another common and analytically convenient variant uses the log potential (a limit as $s\to 0$ in some regimes) or directly the sum over absolute inner products:
$$
E_{\text{dot}}(\{x_i\}) = \sum_{i\ne j} |x_i^\top x_j|^p,
$$
for some $p\ge 1$. When $p=2$, this becomes the sum of squared pairwise inner products. Minimizing this quantity encourages orthogonality among vectors (small inner products). The $p=2$ case is directly related to frame potential and has a simple matrix form. If $X$ is the $d\times n$ matrix whose columns are vectors $x_i$, then
$$
\sum_{i\ne j} (x_i^\top x_j)^2 = \|X^\top X\|_F^2 - n,
$$
since the diagonal entries of $X^\top X$ are ones for unit vectors. Minimizing the sum of squared off-diagonal inner products is equivalent to making the Gram matrix $G = X^\top X$ as close as possible to the identity (or to scaled identity in overcomplete cases).

Intuitively, hyperspherical energy captures repulsion or attraction depending on the chosen kernel. For a repulsive potential like $1/\|x_i - x_j\|^s$, energy decreases when points are further apart (larger pairwise distances), so minimal energy configurations tend to distribute points as uniformly as possible over the sphere. For kernels that penalize large inner products, minimizing energy enforces mutual orthogonality or incoherence. Conversely, maximizing certain energy definitions might encourage clustering or alignment, but in learning contexts one typically minimizes energy to encourage diversity.

There is rich mathematical history behind these energies. The problem of minimizing total pairwise repulsive energy on the sphere is related to the classical Thomson problem for electrons on a sphere (minimizing Coulomb energy), spherical codes, and concepts from discrepancy theory. In many dimensions, characterizing exact minimal-energy configurations is difficult, but general principles apply: points tend to spread out to reduce pairwise interactions, with the nature of spread depending on $n$, $d$, and the kernel exponent.

In representation learning, hyperspherical energy can be interpreted as a regularizer or diagnostic: it measures how dispersed weight or feature directions are. Low energy (under repulsive kernel) implies vectors are well spread out and less redundant; high energy suggests clustering and redundancy. The choice of kernel dictates what aspect of the pairwise relations is being measured: angular closeness, correlation, chordal distance, or some power-law repulsion.

# Implications of hyperspherical energy: neuron uniformity, redundancy, and representation structure

Understanding the hyperspherical energy of a set of neurons (weight vectors) or features provides insight into several practical and theoretical properties of learned representations: uniformity, redundancy, expressivity, and the structure of class partitions.

Neuron uniformity refers to how evenly the directions of neurons (e.g., weight vectors of a fully connected layer) are distributed across the sphere. If the neurons are uniformly distributed, any direction in the feature space has a neuron nearby, which helps in providing diverse detectors. Minimizing repulsive hyperspherical energy tends to promote such uniformity. For example, in a hidden layer where neurons are supposed to detect different directional features, a low-energy configuration discourages many neurons from collapsing to similar directions, thereby preventing redundancy and improving coverage of the representational space.

Redundancy is closely tied to pairwise angular similarity. When two neurons are near-parallel (small angle), they produce highly correlated responses across inputs; they are effectively redundant. High hyperspherical energy under repulsive kernels correlates with high redundancy. Redundancy is not always bad—some redundancy can provide robustness and overparameterization benefits—but excessive redundancy reduces effective model capacity and can hurt generalization. Regularizing toward lower hyperspherical energy (i.e., promoting orthogonality or incoherence) can help reduce redundancy, leading to compact and efficient representations.

Representation structure refers both to the geometry of class clusters in the feature space and to the global organization of features across classes. Angular-based classifiers and hyperspherical regularizers jointly influence this structure. For instance, when weight vectors of different classes are repelled from each other (low pairwise inner products), class decision regions on the sphere become well-separated, which facilitates generalization and robustness to small perturbations in angle. Angular margin methods explicitly create such separation by forcing intra-class features to be closer to class centers and inter-class centers to be farther apart in angle. The combination of angular margins and energy minimization of weights supports well-separated, compact clusters on the sphere.

There are trade-offs and nuances. For a small number of neurons relative to the dimension, perfect mutual orthogonality may be achievable and desirable. When the number of neurons $n$ exceeds the dimension $d$ (the overcomplete regime), perfect orthogonality is impossible; the best achievable configuration trades off equiangularity: vectors arranged to make all pairwise inner products equal in magnitude, known as equiangular tight frames. These frames minimize certain energy measures (e.g., the frame potential) subject to unit norm constraints. Thus, hyperspherical energy minimization naturally connects to frame theory: optimal configurations in the overcomplete setting are not orthogonal but as uniformly spread as possible.

In optimization practice, adding hyperspherical-energy-inspired regularizers to the training objective can promote desirable properties. For example, adding a penalty proportional to the sum of squared pairwise inner products,
$$
\lambda \sum_{i\ne j} (w_i^\top w_j)^2,
$$
with $\lambda>0$, encourages weight orthogonality. This has been used as an orthogonality-promoting regularizer in convolutional filters and fully connected weights. However, one must balance such regularization with the primary training objective: too strong a penalty can harm performance by forcing unrealistic constraints on representation geometry. Moreover, the optimization landscape changes: repulsive regularizers introduce interactions among all pairs of neurons, increasing the complexity of gradients but often improving generalization.

Hyperspherical energy also has implications for interpretability and robustness. Uniformly spread neurons reduce the chance of catastrophic failure modes where many neurons respond identically to inputs; they provide a richer basis for reconstructing or interpreting features. In adversarial settings, angular separation among class centers can increase robustness to small perturbations: an adversarial attack needs to rotate the feature vector by a larger angle to cross the decision boundary into a different class. Conversely, if classes are clustered near each other on the sphere (high angular overlap), small perturbations suffice to change predictions.

Lastly, considering dynamics during training, networks may initially produce weight vectors that are highly correlated due to common initialization and early gradient directions. Over the course of training, gradients induced by classification and contrastive losses tend to spread weight vectors apart implicitly. Augmenting objectives with explicit hyperspherical energy terms or employing normalization and angular margins accelerates this decorrelation and can lead to faster convergence to well-structured representations.

Summary and practical recommendations

Representing features and weights on the unit hypersphere, and using angular measures such as cosine similarity, is a powerful and conceptually clean approach when direction encodes semantics. Angular softmax variants and SphereNet-style architectures exploit this by making classification purely directional and by enforcing angular margins that yield tighter intra-class clusters and better inter-class separation. Hyperspherical energy provides a principled scalar to quantify how well-spread or redundant a set of spherical vectors is; minimizing appropriate energy kernels encourages uniformity and reduces redundancy, with connections to classical problems like the Thomson problem and to frame theory in overcomplete regimes.

In practice, adopt normalization of features and weights when semantics are directional; tune a temperature scale $s$ and angular margin $m$ carefully for angular-softmax methods; and consider mild hyperspherical-energy regularization (for instance, penalizing squared inner products among class weights or filters) to promote diversity while leaving enough flexibility for the network to learn useful correlated detectors. Understanding the geometric and energetic structure of representations on the sphere helps both in designing loss functions and in interpreting the emergent organization of neurons in deep models.

Introduction

Orthogonal transformations form a fundamental class of linear maps on Euclidean space that preserve the geometric structure of vectors: lengths, angles, and inner products. These maps include familiar operations such as rotations and reflections, and they play a central role in geometry, mechanics, numerical linear algebra, and the study of symmetry. In matrix form, orthogonal transformations are represented by orthogonal matrices, i.e., real matrices whose transpose is their inverse. This chapter develops the algebraic and geometric properties of orthogonal matrices, explains why they preserve inner products and norms, and clarifies the distinction between proper rotations and improper orthogonal transformations (those involving reflections). We introduce the orthogonal group $O(n)$ and the special orthogonal group $SO(n)$, and explain how composition and inversion behave in these groups. Throughout, emphasis is placed on intuitive geometric understanding together with precise algebraic statements and short proofs.

# Definition and properties of orthogonal matrices: $R^T R = I$

An $n\times n$ real matrix $R$ is called orthogonal if its transpose equals its inverse. In symbols,
$R$ is orthogonal if and only if
$$R^T R = I_n,$$
where $I_n$ denotes the $n\times n$ identity matrix. Equivalently, $R R^T = I_n$, since taking transpose of $R^T R = I_n$ yields $R R^T = I_n$ as well. The defining equation encapsulates both algebraic and geometric properties: algebraically, it says that the columns of $R$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and geometrically, it says $R$ preserves the standard inner product.

To see the column interpretation, write $R = [r_1\ r_2\ \cdots\ r_n]$ where each $r_j$ is the $j$th column vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then the $(i,j)$ entry of $R^T R$ is $r_i\cdot r_j$, the standard dot product of columns $r_i$ and $r_j$. The condition $R^T R = I_n$ therefore means $r_i\cdot r_j = 0$ when $i\neq j$ and $r_i\cdot r_i = 1$ for all $i$. Thus the columns are pairwise orthogonal unit vectors. The same argument applied to rows shows that the rows of an orthogonal matrix form an orthonormal basis as well.

There are several immediate basic consequences of $R^T R = I$. One is that orthogonal matrices are invertible, with inverse $R^{-1} = R^T$. Another is that entries of an orthogonal matrix are bounded in magnitude: since each column has unit norm, every entry is at most $1$ in absolute value. Furthermore, the set of all $n\times n$ orthogonal matrices has a natural topology and algebraic structure that will be developed later; algebraically it forms a group under matrix multiplication, and topologically it is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$.

It is helpful to check easy examples. The identity matrix $I_n$ is orthogonal because $I_n^T I_n = I_n$. Permutation matrices that shuffle coordinate axes are orthogonal because they simply reorder standard basis vectors, preserving orthonormality. A simple 2-by-2 rotation matrix
$$R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\[4pt]\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$$
satisfies $R(\theta)^T R(\theta)=I_2$ and is therefore orthogonal. A reflection across the $x$-axis in the plane, represented by $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, is also orthogonal.

# Preservation of inner products, norms, and angles under orthogonal transforms

One of the most important geometric consequences of the orthogonality condition is that orthogonal matrices preserve the standard inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $R$ is orthogonal and $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n$, then
$$\langle Ru,Rv\rangle = (Ru)\cdot(Rv) = u^T R^T R v = u^T I_n v = u\cdot v = \langle u,v\rangle.$$
This single algebraic calculation explains a great deal: lengths (norms), angles, and orthogonality relations are all preserved under the linear map $R$.

From inner product preservation, norm preservation follows immediately because the Euclidean norm arises from the inner product:
$$\|Ru\|^2 = \langle Ru,Ru\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle = \|u\|^2.$$
Taking square roots yields $\|Ru\|=\|u\|$ for every vector $u$. Thus orthogonal transformations are isometries of Euclidean space that fix the origin: distances between any two points are preserved because for any $u,v$,
$$\|Ru - Rv\| = \|R(u-v)\| = \|u-v\|.$$
In particular, orthogonal matrices map spheres to spheres and preserve lengths of line segments.

Angles are determined by the inner product via the formula
$$\cos\theta_{u,v}=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\|v\|}.$$
Since orthogonal transforms preserve both inner products and norms, $\cos\theta_{Ru,Rv}=\cos\theta_{u,v}$, so the angle between any two nonzero vectors is preserved. Consequently, if $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal (i.e., $\langle u,v\rangle=0$), their images $Ru$ and $Rv$ are also orthogonal. This is the geometric content of orthogonality: they are exactly the linear maps that preserve the full Euclidean structure up to orientation (orientation will be discussed below).

The converse is also true in the context of linear maps: any linear map that preserves the inner product must be orthogonal. Suppose a linear operator $T$ satisfies $\langle Tu,Tv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle$ for all $u,v$. Using a matrix representation relative to the standard basis, this condition is equivalent to $T^T T=I$, so $T$ is orthogonal. This equivalence shows that orthogonal matrices are precisely the linear isometries of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that fix the origin.

# Determinant ±1 distinction: rotations ($SO(n)$) vs reflections

Orthogonal matrices always have determinant $\pm 1$. To see this, take determinants of the defining relation $R^T R = I_n$. Using multiplicativity and the fact that $\det(R^T)=\det(R)$ for real matrices, one gets
$$\det(R)^2 = \det(R^T)\det(R) = \det(I_n)=1.$$
Therefore $\det(R)=1$ or $\det(R)=-1$. This dichotomy splits orthogonal matrices into two qualitatively different types: those with determinant $1$, called proper orthogonal transformations, and those with determinant $-1$, often called improper orthogonal transformations.

Geometrically, matrices with determinant $1$ are orientation-preserving: they do not reverse the orientation of $\mathbb{R}^n$. In low dimensions this is easy to picture. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, an orthogonal matrix with determinant $1$ is a pure rotation (possibly together with the identity, a rotation by $0$). The rotation matrix $R(\theta)$ above has determinant $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$. On the other hand, a reflection across a line in the plane has determinant $-1$, because reflections reverse orientation. For example, $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ has determinant $-1$.

A reflection can also be described more generally: given a unit vector $n\in\mathbb{R}^n$, reflection about the hyperplane orthogonal to $n$ is the linear map
$$v\mapsto v - 2\langle v,n\rangle n.$$
Its matrix is $I_n - 2 n n^T$, and one checks this matrix is orthogonal with determinant $-1$. In odd dimensions a single reflection reverses orientation; in even dimensions, compositions of reflections may or may not reverse orientation depending on parity.

Matrices with determinant $1$ form a distinguished subgroup of the orthogonal group, the special orthogonal group $SO(n)$; these are the rotations when viewed homotopically and locally. In $\mathbb{R}^3$, for instance, every element of $SO(3)$ is a rotation about some axis by some angle (this is the Euler rotation theorem). However, in higher dimensions the structure is richer: elements of $SO(n)$ can be composed from planar rotations in mutually orthogonal 2-planes, and their classification involves block-diagonal orthogonal matrices consisting of $2\times 2$ rotation blocks and possible $1\times 1$ identity blocks.

It is important to stress that determinant alone does not distinguish between different geometric actions beyond orientation: a matrix with determinant $1$ might still be the identity or a complicated composite of rotations, and a matrix with determinant $-1$ might be a single reflection, or a composition of an odd number of reflections.

# Orthogonal group O(n) and special orthogonal group SO(n)

The set of all $n\times n$ real orthogonal matrices is denoted $O(n)$:
$$O(n)=\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}: R^T R = I_n\}.$$
Under matrix multiplication, $O(n)$ forms a group: the product of two orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, the identity matrix lies in $O(n)$, and inverses are given by transposes. This group captures the symmetries of Euclidean $n$-space that fix the origin and preserve distances.

Within $O(n)$ lies the subgroup $SO(n)$, the special orthogonal group:
$$SO(n)=\{R\in O(n): \det(R)=1\}.$$
$SO(n)$ is a normal subgroup of $O(n)$ of index $2$, because determinant is a continuous group homomorphism $\det:O(n)\to\{1,-1\}$ and its kernel is precisely $SO(n)$. Intuitively, $SO(n)$ consists of the orientation-preserving isometries fixing the origin, and $O(n)\setminus SO(n)$ consists of the orientation-reversing ones.

From a topological viewpoint, $O(n)$ is a compact Lie group of dimension $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$. It is compact because it is closed and bounded in the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ (orthogonality imposes polynomial equations and unit-norm constraints). The dimension count arises because orthogonal matrices are determined by choosing an orthonormal basis, and the space of orthonormal bases has $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, corresponding to the number of independent parameters in skew-symmetric matrices or in infinitesimal rotations.

$SO(n)$ is a connected Lie group for $n\ge 2$ only when $n\ge 2$? To be precise, $SO(n)$ is connected for all $n\ge 2$; $SO(1)=\{1\}$ is trivial. $O(n)$ is disconnected, consisting of two connected components: $SO(n)$ and the other component of determinant $-1$ matrices. The connectivity of $SO(n)$ encodes the idea that any orientation-preserving orthogonal map can be continuously deformed to the identity through orientation-preserving orthogonal maps (think of gradually turning a rotation angle to zero).

In low dimensions the groups have concrete descriptions. $O(2)$ consists of rotations and reflections in the plane; $SO(2)$ is isomorphic to the circle group $S^1$ and consists only of rotations. $O(3)$ consists of rotations and improper rotations (rotations composed with reflections); $SO(3)$ consists of all rotations about some axis. In general, elements of $SO(n)$ can be diagonalized over the complex numbers into blocks that are $2\times 2$ rotation matrices or $1\times1$ entries of $1$, giving a normal form under orthogonal similarity.

The group structure is important both algebraically and for applications: $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$ act on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by matrix multiplication, providing symmetry groups for spheres, tensors, physical laws, and boundary conditions in partial differential equations. They are also central in classification problems: for instance, two symmetric matrices are congruent via an orthogonal matrix if and only if they represent the same quadratic form in an orthonormal basis.

# Composition and inverse properties of orthogonal matrices

Orthogonal matrices behave very well under composition and inversion. If $R_1$ and $R_2$ are orthogonal matrices, then their product $R_1 R_2$ is also orthogonal. This follows algebraically from
$$(R_1 R_2)^T (R_1 R_2) = R_2^T R_1^T R_1 R_2 = R_2^T I_n R_2 = R_2^T R_2 = I_n.$$
Thus $O(n)$ is closed under multiplication. Similarly, since $R^T R = I_n$, one immediately gets $R^{-1}=R^T$ when $R$ is orthogonal. In particular, orthogonal matrices are always invertible and the inverse is again orthogonal because $(R^{-1})^T R^{-1}=(R^T)^{-1}R^{-1}=(R^{-1}R)^{-1}=I_n$.

These algebraic properties have straightforward geometric interpretations. Composition of orthogonal maps corresponds to doing one rigid motion after another: for instance, a rotation followed by another rotation is again a rotation (possibly by a different angle or about a different axis in higher dimensions). Composition may mix rotations and reflections; for example, composing two reflections whose mirror hyperplanes meet at an angle $\varphi$ produces a rotation by $2\varphi$ in the plane spanned by the normal vectors. This last fact is a standard geometric construction: reflecting across two lines in the plane is equivalent to a rotation by twice the angle between the lines.

The inverse of an orthogonal transformation is its transpose: to undo a rigid motion you apply the reversed motion, which is again rigid. This is consistent with the group picture: inverses in a group correspond to reversing the operation sequence. An important corollary is that conjugation by orthogonal matrices preserves many matrix properties. For example, if $A$ is any square matrix, and $R$ is orthogonal, then $R^T A R$ is the representation of the linear transformation corresponding to $A$ in a rotated orthonormal basis. Since $R^T=R^{-1}$, conjugation by $R$ is an orthogonal change of basis that preserves inner products and spectral properties like eigenvalues when $A$ is symmetric or normal.

Because orthogonal matrices form a group, they provide a natural setting for symmetry operations: the product of two symmetries is a symmetry, and the inverse of a symmetry is a symmetry. This is exploited in applications such as rigid-body dynamics, computer graphics (where composing rotations must stay within $SO(3)$), and computational linear algebra (where orthogonal transformations are used for numerically stable algorithms like QR factorization because they preserve norms and do not amplify rounding errors).

Conclusion

Orthogonal matrices connect algebra and geometry tightly: their algebraic definition $R^T R = I$ encodes the geometric idea of preserving inner products, lengths, and angles. The determinant discriminates between orientation-preserving (rotations, determinant $1$) and orientation-reversing (reflections, determinant $-1$) maps, leading to the groups $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$ which capture all linear isometries that fix the origin. The closure of these sets under composition and inversion makes them groups, and their matrix properties have far-reaching consequences in both theory and applications. Mastery of orthogonal matrices and the orthogonal group is essential for understanding symmetry, rigid motions, and a large portion of linear algebra and its applications.

Introduction

Orthogonal matrices play a central role in numerical linear algebra, signal processing, and machine learning. They preserve inner products and norms, which makes them useful for stable transformations, parametrizations of rotations, and for regularizing networks. When learning orthogonal matrices by gradient-based optimization, one must choose a convenient parameterization that enforces orthogonality while being numerically stable and efficient to compute and differentiate. This chapter studies a family of practical parameterizations centered on skew-symmetric matrices and the Cayley transform, explains their theoretical properties and limitations, and describes engineering choices such as block-structured parameter reductions and initialization strategies that make these parameterizations useful in practice.

We begin by reviewing the relationship between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices. We then introduce the Cayley parameterization, derive its orthogonality and determinant properties, and highlight important limitations (notably, its inability to represent reflections). Next we investigate numerical approximations to the Cayley transform using Neumann series expansions when the skew-symmetric parameter is small. We then discuss block-diagonal and block-sharing parameterizations that reduce parameter counts while preserving desirable properties. Finally, we collect practical considerations for initialization and numerical stability, including how to avoid singularities and how to trade off accuracy and cost.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and their relationship to orthogonal matrices

A real matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is skew-symmetric if $Q^T = -Q$. Skew-symmetric matrices have several convenient algebraic and spectral properties that make them natural parameters for orthogonal maps.

First, for any skew-symmetric $Q$, the quadratic form $x^T Q x$ is zero for all real vectors $x$, since
$x^T Q x = - x^T Q^T x = - (x^T Q x)$, so $x^T Q x = 0$. This indicates that $Q$ generates infinitesimal rotations—motions that preserve length to first order—rather than scalings.

Second, the eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix are purely imaginary and come in conjugate pairs. More precisely, the eigenvalues are of the form $\pm i\lambda_j$ with $\lambda_j \in \mathbb{R}$ (and possibly zeros when $n$ is odd). The spectral decomposition in the real field can be expressed via a block-diagonal form using $2 \times 2$ rotation blocks (and possibly a zero row/column), which connects skew-symmetric matrices to plane rotations.

Third, the matrix exponential $\exp(Q)$ of a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ is orthogonal. To see this, use the identity $(\exp Q)^T = \exp(Q^T) = \exp(-Q)$. Then
\[
(\exp Q)^T \exp Q = \exp(-Q)\exp(Q) = \exp(0) = I,
\]
so $\exp(Q)$ is orthogonal. Furthermore, $\det(\exp(Q)) = \exp(\mathrm{tr}(Q))$, and since $\mathrm{tr}(Q) = 0$ for skew-symmetric $Q$, we have $\det(\exp(Q)) = 1$. Thus exponentiating skew-symmetric matrices yields elements of the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, which are rotations (orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$).

These observations motivate parameterizing orthogonal matrices via skew-symmetric matrices. A skew-symmetric parameterization has minimal redundancy: a real $n \times n$ skew-symmetric matrix is determined by the $n(n-1)/2$ entries strictly above (or below) the diagonal. Representing an orthogonal matrix with the same number of degrees of freedom requires enforcing orthogonality constraints; using skew-symmetric parameters plus a map to orthogonal matrices gives a convenient unconstrained parameter space for optimization.

# Cayley parameterization: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with Q = -Q^T

One useful explicit mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices is the Cayley transform. Given a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ for which $(I-Q)$ is invertible, the Cayley transform produces
\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]
This map has a number of attractive properties and is computationally convenient because it requires just one matrix inverse (or the solution of a linear system) and some matrix multiplications.

Why does this produce an orthogonal matrix? The proof is straightforward. Assuming $Q^T = -Q$ and that $(I-Q)$ is invertible,
\[
R^T R = \bigl((I-Q)^{-T}(I+Q)^T\bigr) (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}.
\]
Using $(I+Q)^T = I + Q^T = I - Q$ and $(I-Q)^{-T} = (I-Q^T)^{-1} = (I+Q)^{-1}$, we get
\[
R^T R = (I+Q)^{-1} (I-Q)(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} = I.
\]
Thus $R^T R = I$ and $R$ is orthogonal.

The Cayley transform has a geometric interpretation: it maps skew-symmetric matrices representing infinitesimal rotations into finite rotations. It can be seen as a rational approximation to the exponential map. Indeed, if $Q$ is small (in norm), then $(I-Q)^{-1} \approx I + Q + Q^2 + \dots$, so
\[
R \approx (I+Q)(I + Q + Q^2 + \dots) = I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2),
\]
whereas the exponential map gives $\exp(Q) \approx I + Q + \tfrac{1}{2} Q^2 + \dots$. Both maps agree to first order in $Q$, and both map skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$.

Practically, implementing the Cayley transform requires solving a linear system or computing an inverse. Instead of forming $(I-Q)^{-1}$ explicitly, it is usually better to solve $(I-Q)X = (I+Q)$ for $X$, producing $X = R$. This avoids numerical amplification from forming the inverse and enables use of efficient linear solvers.

# Limitations of Cayley: inability to represent reflections (determinant -1) and numerical considerations

Despite its convenience, the Cayley transform has important limitations that must be understood if it is to be used for parameterizing orthogonal matrices in learning systems.

A primary algebraic limitation is that the Cayley transform cannot represent orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ (i.e., reflections or improper rotations). The image of the Cayley map from the space of real skew-symmetric matrices (connected, with the zero matrix) lies entirely inside the connected component of the orthogonal group containing the identity, namely the special orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(n)$. Concretely, the Cayley transform at $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, and as $Q$ varies continuously the determinant cannot jump to $-1$. More directly, for skew-symmetric $Q$ with $(I-Q)$ invertible, both $\det(I+Q)$ and $\det(I-Q)$ are complex conjugates of each other when eigenvalues are paired, so their ratio is positive real; the map preserves orientation. Thus Cayley cannot produce reflections.

For applications where both rotation and reflections (i.e., both components of $O(n)$) are needed, the Cayley parameterization alone is insufficient. There are several workarounds: one can allow a separate diagonal factor with entries $\pm 1$ to incorporate reflections, or use a different parameterization (for example, the full $n(n-1)/2$ parameters through Householder products can represent the whole $O(n)$). A simple hybrid in learning is to parameterize an orthogonal matrix as $R = D (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ where $D$ is a fixed or learned diagonal matrix with $\pm 1$ entries; this allows determinant sign control at the cost of a nondifferentiable discrete structure (but in practice you can allow $D$ to be a learned real diagonal then project to $\pm 1$).

Another practical numerical concern is that $(I-Q)$ may be singular or nearly singular. The Cayley transform requires that $-1$ is not an eigenvalue of $Q$, or equivalently that $I-Q$ is invertible. Since skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues, this singularity occurs only if $Q$ has an eigenvalue equal to $1$ times $i$, which cannot happen for real $Q$; however, when discretized via finite precision arithmetic or when $Q$ is not perfectly skew-symmetric due to parameterization errors, near-singularity is possible. More concretely, when approximating via series expansions or when $Q$ has a large norm, numerical instability in solving the linear system for $R$ may occur: $(I-Q)$ may be ill-conditioned, and small perturbations in $Q$ can lead to large changes in $R$. This is especially relevant when $Q$ contains large entries for which the rational mapping can amplify those entries.

In summary, Cayley provides an efficient map to orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices but cannot represent reflections and may suffer numerical instability when $Q$ is large or when inversion is ill-conditioned.

# Neumann series approximation for matrix inverse and small-Q expansion

When $Q$ is small in operator norm, the inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ admits a Neumann series expansion
\[
(I - Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k,
\]
which converges when the spectral radius $\rho(Q) < 1$ (and in practice it suffices that the operator norm $\|Q\| < 1$). Substituting this into the Cayley formula yields
\[
R = (I+Q) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^k + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} Q^{k+1} = I + 2Q + 2Q^2 + 2Q^3 + \dots,
\]
so formally
\[
R = I + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} Q^k.
\]
Truncating the series after a few terms gives a polynomial approximation to the Cayley transform that is cheap to compute and differentiable. The leading-order approximation is
\[
R \approx I + 2Q,
\]
which is orthogonal only to first order (i.e., fails to exactly satisfy $R^T R = I$ beyond linear order) but often serves as a useful linearization for small updates.

Higher-order truncated approximations, such as $R \approx I + 2Q + 2Q^2$, yield better accuracy while still avoiding an explicit matrix inverse. Truncation order should be chosen based on a tradeoff between computational cost, accuracy, and stability. For moderate-sized $Q$ satisfying $\|Q\| \le \alpha < 1$, the truncation error can be bounded: the tail of the series behaves like $\mathcal{O}(\|Q\|^{m+1})$ after truncation at order $m$.

However, relying on the Neumann series has its own limitations. The convergence condition requires $\rho(Q) < 1$, so this approach is valid only when $Q$ is small in spectral radius. In learning settings this suggests constraining or regularizing the magnitude of $Q$ (for example, by rescaling) if one intends to use low-order approximations. When $Q$ is not small, it is safer to compute the inverse via a stable linear solver or a direct factorization (LU, QR, or Cholesky on symmetric perturbations) rather than relying on a poorly convergent series.

An advantage of exploiting the Neumann series is that it turns the Cayley map into a sequence of matrix powers, which can be implemented efficiently when the parameterization of $Q$ has structure (e.g., block-sparsity or low-rank). Truncated polynomial maps are also easier to backpropagate through than a matrix inverse in some automatic differentiation frameworks and may be faster on hardware that favors matrix-matrix multiplications.

# Block-diagonal and block-sharing parameterizations to reduce parameter count

A full skew-symmetric $n \times n$ matrix has $n(n-1)/2$ free parameters. For very large $n$, this can create parameter and computational bottlenecks. One strategy is to restrict $Q$ (or $R$) to have block-diagonal or block-structured form, which reduces the number of parameters while retaining useful transformation capabilities.

The simplest structured choice is a block-diagonal skew-symmetric matrix composed of $2 \times 2$ blocks and possibly a singleton zero for odd $n$. Each $2 \times 2$ skew-symmetric block has the form
\[
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\]
which exponentiates to a planar rotation by angle $\theta$. The corresponding Cayley transform or exponential confined to this block yields a rotation in the corresponding plane. By arranging multiple such blocks along the diagonal, we parameterize an orthogonal matrix that performs independent rotations on disjoint 2D subspaces. This block-diagonal parameterization uses only one parameter per 2D block and thus uses about $n/2$ parameters rather than $n(n-1)/2$.

Block-sharing is a further compression: the same small block (or a small set of blocks) can be shared across many positions. For instance, one can tile the skew-symmetric parameter with repeated $k \times k$ blocks that are identical across blocks. This drastically reduces parameter count and is useful when the same local transformation should be applied across many coordinates (analogous to weight sharing in convolutional neural networks). Block-sharing can be implemented by defining a small prototype skew-symmetric matrix $Q_0$ and then constructing a larger $Q$ as a direct sum or Kronecker product with identity: $Q = I_m \otimes Q_0$ or a block-diagonal repetition. The resulting orthogonal matrix becomes blockwise repeated rotations.

Another common choice is to use a sequence (product) of sparse rotation blocks (Givens rotations). A Givens rotation affects only two coordinates and can be parameterized by a single angle. Composing a sequence of Givens rotations can approximate any orthogonal matrix, and restricting to a small number of such rotations reduces parameters. The tradeoff is expressivity: fewer blocks reduce capacity but lower the parameter count and computational cost. Designing the sequence (which pairs of coordinates to rotate) is an important architectural decision; some learn the coordinates or use structured patterns (but learning coordinate indices requires combinatorial choices, so fixed patterns are common).

When applying the Cayley transform with block-diagonal $Q$, the computation simplifies drastically: each diagonal block can be transformed independently, and the inverses required are small. This improves numerical stability and computational efficiency.

# Practical considerations: initialization to identity, numerical stability

Several practical engineering points make skew-symmetric and Cayley parameterizations effective in learning systems.

Initialization: A common and principled initialization is to start with $Q = 0$, which yields $R = I$ via the Cayley transform. This is often favorable because the identity matrix is a neutral transformation that preserves signal magnitudes and avoids initial distortions. Starting near the identity also keeps $Q$ small so that linear approximations are valid at the beginning of training, which stabilizes gradients. If a nontrivial initial orthogonal matrix is desired, one can initialize $Q$ to a small random skew-symmetric matrix so that $R$ is near but not exactly the identity, or use the exponential of a block-symmetric random matrix followed by a Cayley mapping to introduce controlled rotations.

Numerical stability and conditioning: Avoid explicitly computing $(I-Q)^{-1}$ when possible. Instead solve the linear system $(I-Q)X = (I+Q)$ for $X$ using a stable linear solver (LU with partial pivoting or an iterative solver) or use matrix factorizations tailored to skew-symmetric structure. Regularize $Q$ magnitude by clipping or applying norm penalties if the solver indicates ill-conditioning. When using the Neumann series approximation, ensure that $\|Q\|$ is comfortably less than 1; otherwise raise the order of series truncation or compute the exact inverse.

Handling the inability to represent reflections: If your application requires orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$, incorporate an explicit discrete reflection factor. A simple approach is to learn a diagonal sign vector $s \in \{\pm 1\}^n$ and set $R = S (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $S = \mathrm{diag}(s)$. Learning $s$ directly is discrete; in practice you can predecide whether reflections are allowed or use a continuous relaxation. Alternatively, use parameterizations that can generate all of $O(n)$, such as products of Householder reflections (which can represent any orthogonal matrix exactly with at most $n$ reflectors).

Differentiation and computational cost: When backpropagating through the Cayley transform, differentiating through the linear solve is numerically preferable to differentiating through an explicit inverse. Many automatic differentiation frameworks provide stable and efficient adjoint rules for linear solvers. Pay attention to the cost of matrix inversions or solves: these scale as $O(n^3)$ for dense matrices, which may be prohibitive for large $n$. Using block or sparse structures reduces cost and memory.

Robust implementation tips: enforce skew-symmetry explicitly by parameterizing only the upper triangular entries and constructing $Q = A - A^T$ for a learned matrix $A$. Clamp or rescale $A$ to control $\|Q\|$. Use double precision when inverting near-singular matrices during initialization or debugging. Monitor condition numbers of $(I-Q)$ during training and apply penalties or reparameterizations if they grow too large.

Summary

Skew-symmetric matrices provide a compact and natural parameter space for orthogonal maps. The Cayley transform turns skew-symmetric parameters into orthogonal matrices with a simple rational formula that is efficient and differentiable, but it only covers orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices and may become numerically unstable when the skew-symmetric parameter is large. For practical learning systems, one often constrains $Q$ (via block-diagonal structure or sharing) to reduce parameters and improve computational cost, initializes $Q$ to zero to get the identity map, and uses numerically stable linear solves or controlled Neumann series approximations for inversion. Understanding these tradeoffs allows you to choose a parameterization that balances expressivity, efficiency, and stability for your application.

Introduction

Optimization problems with matrix variables arise throughout applied mathematics, statistics, and machine learning. Often these matrix variables are further constrained: for example, columns of a matrix may be required to be orthonormal, or a square matrix may be required to be orthogonal. These orthogonality constraints are not linear constraints in the usual Euclidean sense; instead they define curved sets that are naturally interpreted as matrix manifolds. Optimization methods that ignore the manifold geometry often fail to respect the constraints (producing infeasible iterates) or are inefficient. This chapter explains the central ideas and practical methods for optimization when variables lie on orthogonality-constrained manifolds, focusing on the Stiefel and orthogonal manifolds. We develop intuition for tangent spaces and retractions, describe projected gradient and manifold-aware algorithms, present differentiable orthogonalization procedures used in modern automatic-differentiation pipelines, and discuss trade-offs between strict orthogonality enforcement and computational cost.

# Constrained optimization vs unconstrained optimization

In unconstrained optimization we minimize a function $f(x)$ over $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, and gradient-based methods move in directions informed by the Euclidean gradient $\nabla f(x)$. Convergence theory, line-search rules, and step-size choices are built on the vector-space properties of $\mathbb{R}^n$: we can add any two vectors, scale them, and move arbitrarily far along a direction.

Constrained optimization adds restrictions on the feasible set, for example $x$ must satisfy $g(x)=0$ or lie in some set $C$. When constraints are simple (e.g., box constraints) projection onto the feasible set can be used, but when constraints define a smooth manifold the geometry becomes central. Orthogonality constraints are of the second type: the set of matrices with orthonormal columns is a manifold, not an affine subspace. A common example is the Stiefel manifold

$${\rm St}(p,n)=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}: X^\top X = I_p\},$$

which is the set of $n\times p$ matrices with orthonormal columns ($p\le n$). The orthogonal group

$${\rm O}(n)=\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}: Q^\top Q = I_n\}$$

is the special case $p=n$ (with determinant $\pm 1$). These sets are smooth manifolds embedded in Euclidean spaces, with curvature and tangent spaces. Treating constraints as equalities and using Lagrange multipliers is one route, but manifold optimization exploits the differential geometry to produce algorithms that remain on the manifold (feasible at every iterate) and use gradients projected into tangent spaces.

Why not just parameterize the manifold? One could parametrize an orthogonal matrix with $n(n-1)/2$ angles (e.g., Givens rotations) but these parametrizations are often awkward, numerically ill-conditioned, and lead to complicated gradient formulas. Instead, manifold optimization works directly with matrices and leverages matrix factorizations and projections that are numerically stable and efficient.

# Optimization on Stiefel and orthogonal manifolds: retractions and geodesics

To develop algorithms on manifolds we need two geometric ingredients: an inner product defining gradients and a mechanism to move from a point along a tangent vector while remaining on the manifold. The tangent space at a point encodes allowable infinitesimal moves that keep you on the manifold, while a retraction maps a tangent vector back to a nearby manifold point.

For the Stiefel manifold $X\in{\rm St}(p,n)$, the tangent space at $X$ is

$$T_X{\rm St}(p,n)=\{Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}: X^\top Z + Z^\top X = 0\}.$$

This condition follows from differentiating the constraint $X^\top X = I_p$; for a differentiable curve $X(t)$ with $X(0)=X$ and $\dot X(0)=Z$ we have $(X+\varepsilon Z)^\top (X+\varepsilon Z)=I_p + \varepsilon (X^\top Z + Z^\top X) + O(\varepsilon^2)$, hence the antisymmetry requirement. Equivalently, any tangent vector $Z$ can be represented as

$$Z = X\Omega + X_\perp K,$$

where $\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$ is skew-symmetric ($\Omega^\top = -\Omega$), $X_\perp$ is any orthonormal completion of $X$ (so $[X\;X_\perp]$ is orthogonal), and $K\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}$. The decomposition separates "in-column-space" rotations ($X\Omega$) from moves that change the subspace spanned by the columns ($X_\perp K$).

A Riemannian metric (inner product) on the tangent space is commonly taken as the canonical metric obtained by restricting the Frobenius inner product:

$$\langle Z_1, Z_2\rangle_X = \mathrm{trace}(Z_1^\top Z_2).$$

With this metric the Riemannian gradient of a function $f$ (given its Euclidean gradient $\nabla f$) is the orthogonal projection of $\nabla f$ onto the tangent space: compute

$$G = \nabla f(X),$$

and the tangent-space projection is

$$\mathrm{grad}\,f(X) = G - X\,\mathrm{sym}(X^\top G),$$

where $\mathrm{sym}(A) = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^\top)$. One can verify that $\mathrm{grad}\,f(X)$ satisfies the tangent condition $(X^\top \mathrm{grad}\,f + \mathrm{grad}\,f^\top X)=0$.

How do we move from $X$ in the direction of a tangent vector $Z$? The exact geodesic (shortest path) on the Stiefel manifold can be derived from matrix exponential formulas, but computing matrix exponentials is expensive and often unnecessary. Instead we use retractions: smooth maps $R_X:T_X{\rm St}(p,n) \to {\rm St}(p,n)$ that approximate the exponential map to first order and satisfy $R_X(0)=X$ and $\frac{d}{dt}R_X(tZ)|_{t=0}=Z$. A retraction is any mapping that pulls a tangent vector back onto the manifold in a well-behaved way.

Several common retractions for the Stiefel and orthogonal manifolds are:

1. QR-based retraction (orthonormalization): compute $X+Z = QR$ where $Q\in{\rm St}(p,n)$ and $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal (uses QR with column pivoting or sign choice to make diagonal positive). Then set $R_X(Z)=Q$. This is cheap and numerically stable.

2. Polar decomposition (or SVD) retraction: compute the polar factor of $X+Z$:

$$X+Z = UH,$$

with $U\in{\rm St}(p,n)$ and $H$ symmetric positive definite ($H$ is the positive-semidefinite factor). Then $R_X(Z)=U$. The polar factor can be computed by SVD (economy SVD) or iterative polar methods. This retraction is second-order accurate and preserves orthogonality precisely up to numerical precision.

3. Cayley retraction (for the orthogonal group ${\rm O}(n)$): for skew-symmetric $A$ the Cayley transform

$$\mathrm{Cay}(A) = (I - \tfrac{1}{2}A)^{-1}(I + \tfrac{1}{2}A)$$

maps skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices (for sufficiently small $A$). If we parametrize tangent vectors at $X$ as $Z = X\Omega$ with skew $\Omega$, then updating $X \leftarrow \mathrm{Cay}(\Omega) X$ stays in ${\rm O}(n)$. The Cayley transform is inexpensive for moderate dimensions and avoids expensive SVDs, but it has a limited domain (requires invertibility of $I-\tfrac12A$).

4. Exponential map: for theoretical completeness, if $Z$ is tangent at $X$ one can compute the geodesic using matrix exponentials. For the orthogonal group, the exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is orthogonal: if $X\in{\rm O}(n)$ and $Z = X\Omega$ with $\Omega^\top = -\Omega$, then

$$X(t) = X\exp(t\Omega)$$

is a geodesic on ${\rm O}(n)$. For the Stiefel manifold geodesic formulas are more involved and combine exponentials with additional terms; they are rarely used in practice due to cost.

Retractions trade off computational cost, numerical stability, and approximation quality. QR and polar retractions are very common in implementations (e.g., Manopt, Pymanopt), with polar offering better second-order properties at higher cost, and QR being cheap and robust.

# Projected gradient descent for orthogonality constraints

A straightforward way to adapt gradient descent to orthogonality constraints is projected gradient descent. The idea is simple: take a Euclidean gradient step, then project the result back to the feasible set. Concretely, given current iterate $X_k\in{\rm St}(p,n)$, take an unconstrained step

$$Y = X_k - \eta_k \nabla f(X_k),$$

and then find the nearest point on ${\rm St}(p,n)$ by solving

$$X_{k+1} = \arg\min_{X\in{\rm St}(p,n)} \|X - Y\|_F.$$

The solution to this orthogonal Procrustes problem is given by the polar factor of $Y$: compute the SVD $Y = U\Sigma V^\top$, then set $X_{k+1} = UV^\top$ (or equivalently compute polar decomposition). This is equivalent to retraction by polar decomposition applied to the step $- \eta_k \nabla f(X_k)$. Projected gradient methods are conceptually simple and ensure feasibility of iterates.

However, pure projected gradient using Euclidean gradient can be suboptimal because the Euclidean gradient direction need not be a descent direction with respect to the manifold geometry. A more principled approach is Riemannian gradient descent: first compute the Riemannian gradient $\mathrm{grad}\, f(X_k)$ by projecting the Euclidean gradient onto the tangent space, then take a step along that tangent direction and retract. The iteration is:

1. Compute $G = \nabla f(X_k)$ and the Riemannian gradient $Z = G - X_k\mathrm{sym}(X_k^\top G)$.

2. Choose step size $\eta_k$ (Armijo line search or fixed schedule).

3. Move: $Y = X_k - \eta_k Z$.

4. Retract: $X_{k+1} = R_{X_k}(-\eta_k Z)$ (e.g., QR or polar of $Y$).

This respects the manifold geometry and yields better theoretical convergence properties; many convergence proofs for manifold optimization assume the use of the Riemannian gradient and retraction.

Projected gradient descent and Riemannian gradient descent are similar in practice when using a polar or QR retraction after an appropriate step, but the Riemannian variant uses the tangent-projected gradient which avoids components that would force immediate re-projection and can be more stable and efficient.

Important practical points: the choice of step size $\eta_k$ matters. Backtracking line search using Armijo conditions adapted to manifold objective values works and can be implemented by repeatedly trying $\eta$ and retracting until sufficient decrease in $f$ is obtained. Also, second-order methods (Riemannian trust-region, Riemannian Newton) can be used if Hessians or Hessian-vector products are available; they require more geometric machinery like connections and vector transport, but yield faster convergence near optimal points.

# Differentiable orthogonalization methods (e.g., Gram–Schmidt, QR, Cayley-based updates)

In many modern applications (deep learning layers with orthonormal weights, differentiable PCA, etc.) one needs orthogonalization steps inside a computation graph that must be differentiable so gradients can backpropagate. Not all orthogonalization methods are equally friendly to automatic differentiation, and some have numerical or gradient-stability issues.

Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization takes a collection of vectors and orthonormalizes them sequentially: start with column 1, normalize it; for column 2 subtract its projection onto column 1 and normalize the residual; and so on. The classical Gram–Schmidt (CGS) algorithm suffers from numerical instability in finite precision when columns are nearly linearly dependent; modified Gram–Schmidt (MGS) improves stability by orthogonalizing each remaining vector against the current orthonormal vector immediately. MGS is algorithmically simple and differentiable as long as no exact linear dependence occurs, but the normalization steps introduce division by norms which can be ill-conditioned and produce large gradients when norms are tiny. From the point of view of autodiff, MGS yields straightforward Jacobians, but care is needed to stabilize near-singular cases (e.g., add small epsilon to denominators).

QR factorization using Householder reflections is numerically stable and is the standard method in linear algebra libraries. The QR decomposition $Y=QR$ yields an orthonormal $Q$ (up to sign choices on $R$'s diagonal). Differentiating through a QR decomposition is more delicate because the mapping from $Y$ to $Q$ is not globally smooth: the signs of diagonal entries in $R$ are not unique, and at singular points the Jacobian may be ill-conditioned or undefined. Nonetheless, in practice one can implement differentiable QR by choosing a convention for the signs of $R$'s diagonal (e.g., enforce positive diagonal) and computing gradients via matrix calculus formulas for the SVD/QR derivatives or using autodiff tools that support QR with stable gradient rules. The polar decomposition $Y=UH$ where $U=YH^{-1}$ can be made differentiable; one way is to compute $H = (Y^\top Y)^{1/2}$ and then $U = YH^{-1}$. Differentiation through a matrix square root is well-studied and can be implemented with matrix Lyapunov or Sylvester equation solvers; it is numerically robust if $Y^\top Y$ has well-separated eigenvalues.

The Cayley transform provides an alternative differentiable update particularly apt when you can parametrize tangent vectors by skew-symmetric matrices. If $W$ is skew-symmetric and small, then $\mathrm{Cay}(W)$ is orthogonal. One can parametrize an unconstrained skew matrix $A$ (e.g., lower-triangular free parameters uniquely map to a skew-symmetric $W$), then compute

$$Q = (I - \tfrac12 W)^{-1}(I + \tfrac12 W),$$

and set the new iterate as $QX$ or $XQ$ depending on whether you act on rows or columns. The Cayley computation involves solving linear systems $(I-\tfrac12 W)Z = I + \tfrac12 W$, which are differentiable and can be implemented efficiently. The Cayley map is smooth in its domain and yields exact orthogonality, with gradients computable using implicit differentiation of the linear solve. However, if $I-\tfrac12W$ is ill-conditioned or singular, gradients blow up. So in practice one enforces bounds on the norm of $W$.

Which orthogonalization method to choose inside a differentiable model depends on the trade-offs between numerical stability, differentiation complexity, and computational cost. QR or polar decompositions give precise orthogonality and are stable but require SVDs or matrix square roots for gradients; Gram–Schmidt is cheap and easy to differentiate but less robust; Cayley is attractive when updates are naturally parametrized by skew-symmetric matrices.

Differentiation formulas: suppose $Y=QR$ with $Q^\top Q=I$ and $R$ upper triangular with positive diagonal. If we perturb $Y$ by $\Delta Y$, the first-order change in $Q$ can be computed by solving linear systems that enforce orthogonality and triangular structure. Similar formulas exist for the polar factor $U(Y)$: let $S = Y^\top Y$, $H=S^{1/2}$, $U=YH^{-1}$. Perturb $Y$ and compute $\Delta U$ using

$$\Delta H = \mathcal{L}_H(\Delta S)$$

where $\mathcal{L}_H$ denotes the Fréchet derivative of the matrix square root, often computed by solving a Sylvester equation. Then

$$\Delta U = \Delta Y H^{-1} - U H^{-1}\Delta H H^{-1}.$$

These expressions, while algebraically dense, are implementable and give correct gradients for autodiff systems; libraries such as PyTorch and TensorFlow include QR and SVD with adjoints that handle most cases.

# Trade-offs: computational cost vs strict orthogonality enforcement

Practical algorithms must balance the desire for strict orthogonality with computational efficiency and numerical robustness. Several trade-offs appear:

1. Computational cost: Polar decomposition or SVD-based retractions give the most accurate orthogonality and better theoretical properties (second-order retractions), but an SVD on an $n\times p$ matrix costs about $O(np^2)$ when $p\le n$ (economy SVD). QR-based retractions cost $O(np^2)$ as well but with a smaller constant; Cayley or exponential-map-based updates can be cheaper if the skew-symmetric parametrization reduces the dimension of the expensive linear algebra operations. For very large matrices (large $n$ and $p$), even $O(np^2)$ is expensive; low-rank structure, randomized methods, or incremental orthonormalization (e.g., streaming Gram–Schmidt) may be needed.

2. Orthogonality accuracy and feasibility: Some methods maintain exact orthogonality to machine precision (polar, QR with reorthogonalization), while others (single-pass Gram–Schmidt) can accumulate orthogonality drift. In optimization, small violations of orthogonality may be tolerable if corrected periodically; for example, one can use cheaper approximate orthonormalization every few iterations and an expensive retraction occasionally to restore feasibility. The choice often depends on whether the objective or subsequent computations are sensitive to orthogonality errors.

3. Differentiability and gradient stability: Methods using SVD or polar decompositions are differentiable except at degenerate points (repeated singular values), and their gradients involve solving Sylvester or Lyapunov-like equations which are numerically stable if eigenvalues are well separated. Gram–Schmidt yields simple Jacobians but is sensitive to near-degeneracies where gradients can become large. Cayley transforms have well-behaved derivatives within their domain but can suffer near singularities. In machine learning where gradients are propagated repeatedly, ensuring numerical stability (clipping norms, adding small regularizers like $\varepsilon I$) is crucial.

4. Convergence speed vs per-iteration cost: More accurate retractions and second-order methods (Riemannian Newton, trust-region) can reduce the number of iterations required, but each iteration is more expensive. First-order Riemannian gradient descent with QR retraction is often the best practical compromise for large-scale problems: each iteration is relatively cheap and iterations are cheap to parallelize. For small to moderate problems where high accuracy is needed, trust-region methods with precise polar retractions may be preferred.

5. Implementation complexity: QR and Gram–Schmidt are straightforward to implement and well-optimized in linear-algebra libraries. Polar decompositions and Cayley transforms require either SVD or solving linear systems/matrix square roots, which are available but more complex. Using existing manifold-optimization libraries (e.g., Manopt, Pymanopt, ROPTLIB) can relieve implementation burden.

Practical heuristics combine methods: for example, use tangent-projected gradients for descent, perform cheap QR retraction every iteration, and occasionally (or at the end) do a polar or SVD-based orthogonalization to guarantee high-precision orthogonality. In differentiable models, adding a small regularization term that penalizes deviation from orthogonality, e.g., $\lambda\|X^\top X - I\|_F^2$, can reduce the need for strict enforcement each step and smooth the optimization landscape, trading off exact feasibility for easier gradient flow.

Conclusion

Optimization with orthogonality constraints requires tools beyond standard Euclidean gradient descent. Viewing feasible sets like the Stiefel or orthogonal manifold as Riemannian manifolds yields natural constructions of tangent spaces, Riemannian gradients, and retractions that keep iterates feasible. Projected gradient descent (via polar or QR projection) and Riemannian gradient descent (project gradient to tangent, then retract) are workhorse methods, while more advanced mechanisms (Cayley transforms, exponential maps, Riemannian second-order methods) provide alternatives with different cost-accuracy trade-offs. In differentiable programming contexts, selecting orthogonalization routines with stable and tractable derivatives is critical. Ultimately, the right choice balances the problem scale, required orthogonality accuracy, sensitivity to gradient noise, and available computational resources.

Introduction

Large pretrained models—whether in language, vision, or generative modeling—have become the foundation for many state-of-the-art systems. However, adapting these huge models to new tasks, domains, or user-provided concepts raises practical challenges: full fine-tuning of all parameters is often computationally expensive, requires storing a separate copy of the entire parameter set for each task, and can easily overfit when downstream datasets are small. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) refers to a family of techniques that change only a small fraction of a model’s parameters (or introduce a small number of new parameters) while achieving adaptation quality comparable to full fine-tuning. This chapter surveys the main PEFT approaches used in modern practice—additive adapters, prefix/prompt tuning, low-rank reparameterizations such as LoRA, and specialized methods for image- and diffusion-model adaptation like Pivotal Tuning and DreamBooth-style approaches. We emphasize intuition, concrete parameterizations, and how these methods trade off compute, storage, and generalization.

# Motivation for parameter-efficient finetuning (compute, storage, overfitting)

Why avoid full fine-tuning of a pretrained model? There are three primary motivations: compute cost, storage cost, and generalization / overfitting.

Compute: Large models contain billions of parameters. Updating every parameter requires computing gradients and performing optimizer updates for the entire parameter set. This demands more GPU memory (for activations, optimizer states such as momentum and second-moment accumulators) and longer wall-clock time per step. For resource-constrained practitioners or for deployments that require rapid adaptation to many different tasks, full fine-tuning is costly or infeasible. By only optimizing a small set of added parameters, memory and computation per training step are reduced. Lightweight updates can often be trained with smaller batch sizes and fit on a single GPU, enabling wider experimentation.

Storage: Suppose you want to maintain 100 task-specific variants of a 20B-parameter model. If you store full parameter snapshots, storage requirements explode. Parameter-efficient methods store only the changed parameters (often a few million or tens of millions) or compact update representations. For example, if an adapter introduces 2M parameters per task, storing 100 task adapters requires only 200M parameters’ worth of storage—far less than 100 full copies of the base model. This makes real-world deployment of many-task models tractable: the base model is shared, and task-specific "delta" files are small and loaded on demand.

Overfitting and catastrophic forgetting: Classic fine-tuning can overwrite the general knowledge encoded in pretrained weights, leading to catastrophic forgetting—degraded performance on data distributions the base model covered well. When downstream data is scarce, fully updating billions of weights tends to overfit to the small dataset. PEFT methods constrain the space of allowable updates either by only changing a subset of parameters or by imposing low-dimensional parameterizations. This inductive bias tends to preserve the pretrained model’s general capabilities while allowing targeted specialization. Ideally, the method should allow fast convergence to a good task-specific solution while maintaining the model’s broad competence.

Together, these motivations push researchers and practitioners toward methods that are compact, fast to train, and robust to small-data overfitting. The following sections describe the principal families of such methods.

# Adapter modules, prefix tuning, and prompt tuning

Adapter modules, prefix tuning, and prompt tuning are three related families of techniques that alter a model’s forward computations by inserting or concatenating small, learned modules rather than changing the pretrained weights directly.

Adapters: The adapter idea is simple and widely applicable. For each layer (or selected layers) of a pretrained network, insert a small bottleneck module that transforms the layer’s activations and adds them back to the main pathway, usually with a residual connection. A common adapter design is a two-layer feedforward module with a down-projection followed by a nonlinearity and an up-projection. Concretely, given an activation vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ entering a layer, an adapter computes
$$
\text{Adapter}(x) = W_\text{up} \sigma(W_\text{down} x),
$$
where $W_\text{down} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$, $W_\text{up} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, and $r \ll d$ is the adapter bottleneck dimension. The layer’s output is modified as
$$
y = \text{Layer}(x) + \alpha \, \text{Adapter}(x),
$$
with $\alpha$ optionally a scalar or learned parameter that controls adapter strength. Only $W_\text{down}$ and $W_\text{up}$ (and possibly $\alpha$ and a few normalization parameters) are trained; the pretrained layer parameters remain frozen.

Intuitively, adapters provide a low-dimensional subspace in which the model can adjust its representations. Because adapters are small, they require few trainable parameters and are cheap to store. They tend to work well in language and vision models and can be selectively placed (e.g., in attention layers, MLP layers, or only in higher layers) to balance expressivity and cost.

Prefix tuning: Prefix tuning is specific to transformer-style architectures that use self-attention. Instead of inserting modules inside the layers, prefix tuning prepends a small sequence of virtual tokens (prefixes) to the input sequence at each transformer layer. These prefix tokens have learned key and value vectors for attention, altering how attention attends without changing the original token embeddings. More formally, in a self-attention computation with queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$, prefix tuning extends $K$ and $V$:
$$
K_\text{ext} = [K_\text{prefix}; K], \quad V_\text{ext} = [V_\text{prefix}; V],
$$
where $K_\text{prefix}$ and $V_\text{prefix}$ are learned matrices of shape $(l_p \times d_k)$ and $(l_p \times d_v)$ respectively, with $l_p$ the prefix length. The queries $Q$ remain unchanged. The learned prefixes are optimized while the base transformer is frozen. Because they act as additional context that is free to steer attention at each layer, prefixes can be effective at controlling model behavior with a small number of parameters.

Prompt tuning: Prompt tuning is the most minimalistic of the three and is conceptually similar to prefix tuning but applied at the input layer only. Prompt tuning learns a small set of embedding vectors that are concatenated to token embeddings at the model input. These learned embeddings prime the frozen model to perform a task by shaping the initial activations. Prompt tuning has been shown effective for some NLP tasks, particularly when the pretraining task closely matches the downstream task, but it can be less powerful than adapters or prefix tuning for complex distribution shifts because it only influences the model through the input rather than at each internal layer.

Comparison and trade-offs: Adapters are architecturally intrusive but highly expressive: they can implement nonlinear transformations of the layer activations and are often placed at many layers to provide rich adaptation capacity. Prefix tuning offers a middle ground—it injects learned context at each attention layer but does not add nonlinear transformations. Prompt tuning is the least intrusive, simplest to implement, and smallest in parameter count, but may need larger prompt lengths or careful initialization to match adapter performance. In practice, hybrid strategies are common: small adapters combined with prefix tokens can yield strong results while keeping parameter counts modest.

# LoRA: low-rank additive updates and parameterization details

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is a particularly influential PEFT method that targets linear projection matrices inside a model, such as the weight matrices of attention queries, keys, values, and feedforward layers. The key idea is to model the weight update during fine-tuning as a low-rank additive matrix and to optimize only that low-rank factorization, leaving the original weights frozen. This provides the expressivity of full-rank updates when accumulated over layers and steps, while needing far fewer trainable parameters.

Parameterization: Consider a pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{out} \times d_\text{in}}$ used in a linear map $y = W_0 x$. The full fine-tuning approach would add a change $\Delta W$ and replace $W_0$ by $W = W_0 + \Delta W$. LoRA constrains $\Delta W$ to be low-rank:
$$
\Delta W = A B,
$$
where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{out} \times r}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_\text{in}}$, and $r \ll \min(d_\text{out}, d_\text{in})$. Only $A$ and $B$ are learned; $W_0$ is frozen. This reduces the number of trainable parameters from $d_\text{out} \times d_\text{in}$ to $r(d_\text{out} + d_\text{in})$, which can be orders of magnitude smaller.

Implementation detail: During the forward pass, one can compute
$$
y = W_0 x + (A B) x = W_0 x + A (B x).
$$
This can be implemented efficiently by computing the small projection $B x$ to a vector of size $r$, then projecting back with $A$. In practice, LoRA often incorporates a scaling factor $\alpha / r$ so that initialization and optimization are stable:
$$
\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} A B,
$$
with $\alpha$ chosen by the practitioner (often equal to $r$ or a small constant). A typical initialization sets $A$ to random values (e.g., normal with small variance) and $B$ to zeros, so the initial $\Delta W$ is zero and the pretrained model behavior is preserved before adaptation.

Where to apply LoRA: LoRA is most commonly applied to the projection matrices inside multi-head attention—specifically the query and value projections—because attention layers are crucial for model behavior and represent bottlenecks where small changes can have large effects. But the method is agnostic to layer type and can be applied to feedforward layers as well.

Why low-rank makes sense: Empirically and theoretically, parameter updates required to adapt a large pretrained model to a downstream task often lie in a low-dimensional subspace. The pretrained manifold of useful weight configurations is highly structured: small changes along certain directions can produce the necessary behavioral shifts while most directions are either redundant or harmful. By constraining updates to a low-rank form, LoRA exploits this inductive bias while dramatically reducing parameter counts. Additionally, the factorized form allows efficient storage of each task’s deltas by storing $A$ and $B$ rather than a full dense matrix.

Equivalence to additive adapters: LoRA is effectively an additive adapter in weight space. Instead of inserting a new module in the architecture, the update is applied directly to the weight matrix as an additive low-rank term. This often makes LoRA simpler to deploy: the base model remains unchanged, and the low-rank delta can be merged with the base weights at inference time if desired (i.e., $W = W_0 + \Delta W$), producing a single set of weights without additional forward-pass overhead. Alternatively, to keep the model fully modular, one can compute $\Delta W x$ on the fly using the low-rank factorization.

Multi-head and rank allocation: For multi-head attention, LoRA can allocate rank differently across different projection matrices and heads. A common practice is to apply LoRA to query and value projections across all heads by treating each head separately or by collapsing heads into a single matrix and applying a shared low-rank update. Detailed design choices (per-head vs shared, rank per matrix, initialization and scaling) affect the trade-off between parameter count and adaptation power.

Optimization and regularization: Because LoRA optimizes fewer parameters, it often benefits from higher learning rates and simpler optimizers. Regularization techniques like weight decay are less relevant when the parameter count is tiny, but controlling $\alpha$ and initialization ensures stable training.

# Pivotal Tuning and DreamBooth-style adaptation for image models

Adapting image models, in particular diffusion models for image generation, presents domain-specific challenges. Two influential families of methods—DreamBooth-style fine-tuning and Pivotal Tuning—are widely used for personalizing generative models to specific concepts (e.g., a person’s face, a pet, or a particular object) with a small number of example images.

DreamBooth-style adaptation: DreamBooth (first proposed for text-to-image diffusion models) is an approach for personalizing a pretrained diffusion model to generate images of a specific subject or concept using only a few photos. The core idea is to fine-tune the model (or parts of it) so that a special placeholder token (e.g., "<S1>") in the text prompt becomes associated with the subject. Training proceeds by optimizing the diffusion model parameters to reconstruct the provided images when prompted with captions that include the placeholder, often together with class-level regularization to prevent the model from collapsing the subject to a particular background or overfitting to the few exemplars.

DreamBooth variants: Full DreamBooth fine-tunes many parameters and can require careful regularization to prevent overfitting and identity leakage. To be parameter-efficient, many subsequent works adapt DreamBooth’s idea using adapters or LoRA applied to the U-Net and/or text encoder components. The text encoder can be fine-tuned to produce an embedding for the placeholder token that captures the subject; alternatively, adapters inserted in the U-Net can learn to inject subject-specific appearance into the denoising process. In practice, using a frozen text encoder and adapting only small modules inside the U-Net via LoRA or adapters can achieve convincing personalization with much smaller parameter budgets.

Pivotal Tuning: Pivotal Tuning introduces a different strategy that focuses on aligning the generation manifold rather than solely conditioning on a learned token embedding. In Pivotal Tuning, one first finds an optimal text prompt embedding (or conditioning representation) for each exemplar that, when used with the frozen generator, reconstructs the example image. These per-image embeddings are called "pivotal embeddings" because they pivot the generator to the right region of latent space for that subject. After finding these embeddings, Pivotal Tuning then fine-tunes a small subset of model parameters—often in the decoder or denoiser U-Net—so that the generator reliably maps the text-conditioned latent to the exemplar images. Because the initial search finds good conditioning vectors, the fine-tuning is targeted and can be done with fewer parameter changes, reducing overfitting.

Intuition and advantages: Both DreamBooth and Pivotal Tuning are designed to teach the generator a new concept while retaining generalization: the goal is to generate the subject across novel contexts, poses, and styles. DreamBooth’s token-based approach makes it easy to prompt the subject in arbitrary contexts once the model has associated the placeholder token with the subject. Pivotal Tuning’s conditioning-search step helps decouple optimization of conditioning vectors from model weight changes, which stabilizes learning and can reduce the number of trainable parameters needed. Both approaches benefit from parameter-efficient layers (adapters, LoRA) to reduce storage and avoid catastrophic forgetting of the generator’s general capabilities.

Practical notes for diffusion models: Diffusion models denoise a latent or pixel-level tensor iteratively. Personalization often focuses on weight matrices in the U-Net’s attention blocks, because attention controls how context and conditioning influence denoising. Applying LoRA to attention projection matrices in the U-Net is therefore a common recipe. Additionally, because diffusion models are iterative, small per-step biases can accumulate; hence careful scaling and regularization of adapters/LoRA parameters is important. Finally, training schedules often include class-image regularization or augmentation to maintain diversity and prevent the model from memorizing backgrounds or overfitting to the exemplars.

# Comparison of multiplicative vs additive weight updates and their effects

When modifying a pretrained model, there are two broad families of transformations one might apply to the frozen weights: additive updates and multiplicative updates. Additive updates modify weights by adding a delta matrix, while multiplicative updates change weights by scaling, gating, or otherwise multiplying the original weights or activations. Each approach has different representational properties, optimization behavior, and implications for stability and generalization.

Additive updates: An additive update takes the form
$$
W = W_0 + \Delta W,
$$
with $\Delta W$ typically low-rank or otherwise parameter-efficient. LoRA is an example of an additive update. Adapters, when viewed in weight-space, are also effectively additive: inserting a residual module that computes $f(x)$ and adds it to the layer output is equivalent to adding a certain functional perturbation to the layer.

Advantages of additive updates include conceptual simplicity, ease of merging at inference time (you can precompute $W$), and generally smooth optimization dynamics: small additive changes can be initialized to near-zero, preserving the base model at initialization and allowing gradual adaptation. Additive parameterizations also allow the original linear algebraic structure to remain intact; for some layers, additive changes can implement arbitrary affine transformations when allowed to be full rank.

Multiplicative updates: Multiplicative updates modify weights or activations via elementwise or low-rank multiplicative factors. A simple multiplicative form is weight-wise scaling:
$$
W = D W_0,
$$
where $D$ is a diagonal matrix (or block-diagonal) of learned scalars; or
$$
W = W_0 D.
$$
More complex multiplicative schemes use gating networks or attention-like mechanisms to compute multiplicative modulation conditioned on input. In neural network literature, techniques like feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) and conditional batch normalization use multiplicative and additive modulation of activations, often expressed as
$$
\text{FiLM}(h) = \gamma(h) \odot h + \beta(h),
$$
where $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned scale and shift parameters (possibly conditioned on context).

Advantages and behaviors: Multiplicative updates can be more parameter-efficient in some contexts because scaling can selectively amplify or attenuate existing components of the model without needing large additive deltas. They can be particularly effective at controlling gating or routing properties and are useful when one wishes to keep weights on the same manifold (e.g., preserve rank or sign patterns) while reweighting contributions. Multiplicative modulation can also be dynamically conditioned (e.g., a small controller network outputs scales based on task metadata), enabling runtime adaptation without changing core weights.

However, multiplicative updates have some drawbacks. If scales are not initialized carefully (e.g., set to values far from unity), they can destabilize pretraining behavior dramatically. They also may have limited expressivity: diagonal scaling cannot change the subspace spanned by a weight matrix, only the relative scaling of its columns. To overcome this, multiplicative updates are often implemented in low-rank or structured forms, but then they resemble additive low-rank mechanisms in representational power.

Comparative considerations: Which is better—additive or multiplicative? There is no universally best choice; the decision depends on the task, where updates are applied, and constraints on inference-time computation.

- Expressivity: Additive low-rank updates can change the column space of a matrix (since $W_0 + AB$ can have new columns), while diagonal multiplicative scaling cannot. Thus, additive updates can implement a broader set of linear transformations for a given parameter budget.

- Stability: Additive updates initialized near zero naturally preserve pretrained behavior initially, which makes optimization stable. Multiplicative updates must be initialized near identity to achieve the same property; failure to do so can cause large deviations. For conditional multiplicative schemes, the controller network can be initialized to produce neutral factors.

- Efficiency and sparsity: Multiplicative updates such as per-channel scales are extremely parameter-efficient (one scalar per feature). When task differences are primarily about reweighting features, multiplicative updates are enough. When more complex changes are needed (e.g., adding new directions in representation space), additive low-rank updates excel.

- Interpretability and merging: Additive updates can be merged into weights for inference by summation, at which point no extra computation is needed. Multiplicative updates that are computed dynamically may require extra operations at inference time; but diagonal/static multiplicative matrices can also be merged into the weight matrix by multiplication, if desired.

- Interaction with normalization layers: Multiplicative updates often interact subtly with layer normalization and batch normalization. For example, scaling activations before or after normalization may be canceled out or amplified depending on where normalization sits in the network, so careful placement and initialization matter.

Hybrid approaches: Many practical PEFT methods combine additive and multiplicative ideas. FiLM layers incorporate both scaling and shifting of activations. Some LoRA variants use multiplicative scaling factors on the low-rank components. When applied to attention, prefix tuning and prompt tuning act multiplicatively in the sense that they change attention scores via additional keys/values, but they do so by augmenting the input rather than changing weights directly.

Effects on diffusion and image generators: In diffusion models, multiplicative changes to denoising layers can alter the magnitude of denoising steps across time steps, potentially causing instability in the denoising trajectory. Additive low-rank updates, by contrast, often produce more controlled modifications to the denoiser’s function. Thus, many practitioners prefer LoRA-like additive updates for U-Net attention matrices in diffusion personalization tasks, although carefully designed multiplicative adapters can also work.

Summary and practical guidance

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning is a pragmatic response to the realities of large-model deployment: it reduces compute and storage costs and often improves robustness to overfitting. Adapter modules, prefix tuning, and prompt tuning provide different ways to insert small learned structures into a frozen model and are chosen based on the level of intrusion and expressivity required. LoRA’s low-rank additive weight updates provide a broadly applicable and practically successful parameterization that balances expressivity and compactness; its factorized form is computationally efficient and easy to merge at inference time. For image personalization, methods like DreamBooth and Pivotal Tuning exploit token-based conditioning and targeted weight updates (often via LoRA or adapters) to teach generators new concepts from few examples while maintaining generalization. Finally, the choice between additive and multiplicative updates depends on the nature of the adaptation: additive updates generally offer greater transformational flexibility and optimization stability, while multiplicative updates are extremely parameter- and compute-efficient when reweighting existing features suffices.

When approaching research papers or implementing PEFT methods, keep these questions in mind: which layers to adapt, how many parameters to dedicate, whether to initialize updates near zero/identity, how to regularize to avoid overfitting, and how to store and deploy task-specific deltas. Understanding the trade-offs above will help you interpret experiments in the literature and design effective, efficient fine-tuning strategies for large pretrained models.

Introduction

Modern deep learning architectures rely heavily on two building blocks: convolutional layers, which are dominant in vision models, and Transformer layers, which power language models and increasingly are used for vision and multimodal tasks. Understanding the weight structure of these layers—how parameters are organized, what each parameter does, and how we can reinterpret those parameters as collections of neuron- or channel-wise vectors—is essential both to analyze model behavior and to design effective finetuning strategies. Finetuning large pretrained models typically avoids updating all weights because full updates are expensive in compute, memory, and data demand, and may overfit. Instead, practitioners often target a subset or a low-dimensional reparameterization of weights such as adapters, low-rank updates, or orthogonal transforms. This chapter explains the canonical parameterizations of convolutional, depthwise/pointwise, Transformer attention, and fully connected layers, and then connects those structures to practical finetuning targets and trade-offs in memory and compute.

# Standard convolutional layer weights and interpretation as channel-wise neuron vectors

A standard 2D convolutional layer maps an input tensor of shape $(C_{\text{in}}, H, W)$ to an output tensor of shape $(C_{\text{out}}, H', W')$ using a bank of learnable convolutional kernels. The weight tensor is typically denoted as $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w}$, where $K_h \times K_w$ is the spatial kernel size. There is also often a bias vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$, one bias per output channel. The standard forward operation for a given output channel $o$ is
\[
y_o = \sum_{i=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} W_{o,i} * x_i + b_o,
\]
where $W_{o,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{K_h \times K_w}$ and $*$ denotes spatial convolution.

A useful perspective is to interpret each output channel as a neuron with a flattened weight vector that connects to all input channels and spatial positions. If we flatten the spatial dimensions and input channels, each output channel $o$ corresponds to a vector
\[
w_o \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w}
\]
obtained by concatenating all $W_{o,i}$ for $i=1,\dots,C_{\text{in}}$. The convolution then computes correlations between this flattened filter and corresponding patches of the input. This per-output-channel view aligns convolutional layers with fully connected layers applied patch-wise: each spatial location of the input provides a vector of size $C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w$, and the convolution applies the same linear map (the matrix with rows $w_o^\top$) at every spatial location. This re-interpretation is especially valuable when thinking about which parameters to adapt: changing a single output-channel vector $w_o$ modifies the linear response of one "neuron" across all spatial positions and across all input channels and positions inside the kernel.

From an adaptation or pruning standpoint, the per-output-channel view suggests natural granularities for parameter modification. One can adapt biases $b_o$ to change channel gains independently. One can adapt entire $w_o$ vectors to change the receptive-field filters for particular channels. One can also think of per-input-channel slices $W_{:,i}$ (all output channels to a particular input channel) as input-side projection vectors, which affect how information from a specific input channel is distributed across outputs. Both views—output-channel-centric and input-channel-centric—are valid and useful depending on the target: to modify feature extraction performed by a few filters, adapt output-channel weights; to remap feature channels before combination, adapt input-channel slices.

# Depthwise and pointwise convolutions and channel-wise parameterization

Depthwise separable convolutions decompose a standard convolution into two (often cheaper) operations: depthwise convolution followed by pointwise convolution. This factorization underlies many efficient architectures (e.g., MobileNet) and also produces a parameter structure that is extremely amenable to channel-wise adaptation.

A depthwise convolution has a weight tensor $W^{\text{dw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} \times 1 \times K_h \times K_w}$ when we choose to keep the number of channels equal and perform one spatial filter per input channel. More often written as $W^{\text{dw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w}$, a depthwise convolution applies an independent $K_h \times K_w$ filter to each input channel, producing $C_{\text{in}}$ intermediate channels. Concretely,
\[
\tilde{y}_i = W^{\text{dw}}_i * x_i + b^{\text{dw}}_i,
\]
for each channel $i$. There is no mixing across channels in this step.

A pointwise convolution is a $1\times 1$ convolution, with weight tensor $W^{\text{pw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times 1 \times 1}$, which reduces to a matrix $W^{\text{pw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}}}$. This performs a learned linear combination across channels at each spatial location:
\[
y_o = \sum_{i=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} W^{\text{pw}}_{o,i}\, \tilde{y}_i + b^{\text{pw}}_o.
\]

The depthwise layer offers an especially natural channel-wise parameterization: each input channel has its own spatial filter. If we want to modify the spatial filtering behavior of a particular channel without affecting cross-channel mixing, we need only update the corresponding depthwise kernel $W^{\text{dw}}_i$. Conversely, the pointwise matrix governs channel mixing; each column of $W^{\text{pw}}$ is a "channel selector" describing how one input channel contributes to all output channels. Viewing $W^{\text{pw}}$ column-wise, column $i$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}}}$ that says how the $i$th input feature is redistributed among outputs. Viewing it row-wise, row $o$ is a vector that readouts a linear combination of all intermediate channels to produce the $o$th output channel.

This factorization suggests two axes for compact finetuning. If the objective is to alter spatial response patterns, one can adapt only depthwise filters, which have parameter count proportional to $C_{\text{in}} K_h K_w$. If the objective is to alter channel interactions or downstream feature recombination, one can adapt only the pointwise weights, which is often much cheaper: $C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}}$ for pointwise versus $C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times K_h \times K_w$ for full convs. In many cases with large $C_{\text{in}}, C_{\text{out}}$ and small kernels (e.g., $3\times 3$), pointwise weights dominate parameter count; targeting them with low-rank adapters yields big gains for little cost. Depthwise layers are also naturally compatible with per-channel scalars (channel-wise affine transforms) that rescale or bias each channel independently, an approach used in lightweight adapter designs.

# Transformer attention: query/key/value/projection matrices and where to apply adapters

Transformer attention layers implement a content-based weighted sum of value vectors where weights are computed from queries and keys. For multi-head attention, the input is a sequence of vectors $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$, where $T$ is sequence length and $d$ is model dimension. For each attention head $h$ we have linear projection matrices for queries, keys, and values:
\[
Q_h = X W^Q_h,\qquad K_h = X W^K_h,\qquad V_h = X W^V_h,
\]
with $W^Q_h, W^K_h, W^V_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}$ and $d_h$ usually equals $d / H$ for $H$ heads. Concatenating across heads, practitioners often use combined matrices $W^Q, W^K, W^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ by stacking head projections. The attention weights for head $h$ are computed by scaled dot-product:
\[
A_h = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_h K_h^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right),
\]
and the head output is $O_h = A_h V_h$. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and projected by an output matrix $W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$:
\[
\text{Attention}(X) = \operatorname{Concat}(O_1,\dots,O_H) W^O.
\]

Thus, the attention layer parameters consist mainly of $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ (plus biases if used). Each of these matrices has a distinct role. $W^Q$ and $W^K$ set the space in which content similarity is measured; small changes to these can drastically change attention patterns because they affect the dot products that form softmax logits. $W^V$ determines what content is retrieved once attention is computed; modifying $W^V$ affects the information placed into outputs but does not directly change which positions attend to which. $W^O$ mixes the concatenated head outputs back into the model dimension; adapting $W^O$ is often used to change how head outputs are recombined.

When designing finetuning interventions (adapters), we need to consider both expressivity and stability. Many practical techniques insert small adapter modules or low-rank updates into one or more of these matrices. For example, practitioners often place adapters into the output projection $W^O$ or into $W^V$ because these affect what information is written into the residual stream and are less likely than $W^Q$ or $W^K$ to trigger large, destabilizing shifts in attention patterns. Low-rank updates to $W^Q$ or $W^K$ are also used (e.g., LoRA applied to attention) when one needs to change attention behavior in a targeted, parameter-efficient way. The choice reflects a trade-off: altering $W^Q/W^K$ can redirect attention and may give stronger downstream effects for tasks requiring attention reconfiguration, but risks unwanted perturbation of attention stability; altering $W^V/W^O$ tends to be safer and can still implement many task-specific transformations.

A common parameter-efficient strategy is to apply low-rank adapters to these matrices. For a given matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ (representing one of $Q,K,V,O$), one can learn a pair of small rank-$r$ matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$ and add their product to $W$ during finetuning:
\[
W' = W + \alpha A B,
\]
where $\alpha$ is a scaling hyperparameter. This keeps inference cost nearly identical (since $W$ is unchanged at inference if one folds the update, or the low-rank adapter can be applied on the fly) while allowing expressive adaptation with only $O(2 d r)$ parameters per adapted matrix. Choosing which attention matrix to adapt—$Q$, $K$, $V$, or $O$—depends on the task and the desired behavior: $Q/K$ for attention pattern changes, $V/O$ for output-space changes.

# Fully connected (MLP) layers and per-neuron parameterization

The Transformer architecture also contains position-wise feedforward (MLP) blocks, typically consisting of two fully connected layers with a nonlinearity in between. A canonical form is
\[
\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2,
\]
where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}} \times d}$ expands dimensionality from $d$ to $d_{\text{ff}}$ (often $d_{\text{ff}} \approx 4d$), $\phi$ is a nonlinearity like GELU, and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{\text{ff}}}$ projects back to model dimension.

We can interpret $W_1$ and $W_2$ in per-neuron terms. $W_1$ defines $d_{\text{ff}}$ hidden neurons, each with an input weight vector $w^{(1)}_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and bias $b^{(1)}_j$. The pre-activation for neuron $j$ is $z_j = (w^{(1)}_j)^\top x + b^{(1)}_j$. The nonlinearity produces activation $a_j = \phi(z_j)$, and $W_2$ contains $d$ readout vectors $w^{(2)}_o \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}}$, which combine the hidden activations $a$ to produce output components. Thus each hidden neuron can be seen as a basis function, and $W_2$ forms coefficients that recombine these basis functions to produce output coordinates.

This per-neuron view clarifies where to target finetuning: modifying certain rows of $W_1$ (i.e., particular hidden neurons) changes the basis functions available; modifying columns of $W_2$ changes how those basis functions affect each output coordinate. Many adapter approaches insert small bottleneck layers inside the FFN block, for example replacing $W_2$ by $W_2 + A B$ with low-rank $A,B$ or inserting an adapter between $W_1$ and the nonlinearity to alter hidden activations. Another common lightweight choice is to learn per-neuron biases or scalings. For instance, adding a per-hidden-unit scale $\gamma_j$ gives $a_j = \gamma_j \phi(z_j)$; this introduces only $d_{\text{ff}}$ extra parameters but can significantly redirect the MLP output by modulating contributions of individual neurons.

Adapting entire $W_1$ or $W_2$ is parameter-expensive because $d_{\text{ff}}$ is typically large; therefore per-neuron parameterizations and low-rank modifications are attractive. Importantly, because FFNs operate position-wise, adapting these parameters affects each token independently and thus commonly suffices for tasks requiring changes in token-level transformations without changing attention patterns.

# Practical considerations: memory, compute, and where low-rank or orthogonal transforms can be applied

When choosing which weights to finetune, one must weigh expressivity versus resource cost and stability. Memory and compute constraints directly limit how many parameters can be updated and how much additional temporary memory (for optimizer states) is required. Updating all weights of a large model multiplies memory cost by the number of optimizer moment tensors (e.g., Adam requires storing first and second moment estimates), often requiring many gigabytes. Parameter-efficient approaches reduce this burden by updating only a small fraction of parameters or by introducing compact learnable modules.

Low-rank adapters (LoRA-style) are widely used because they add only $O(d r)$ parameters per adapted matrix and do not require storing full optimizer states for the frozen base model. They are particularly effective for large linear maps such as attention $W^Q,W^K,W^V,W^O$ and MLP matrices $W_1,W_2$. Applying low-rank updates in the factorized form $A B$ shifts the memory–compute trade-off: training computes gradients for $A$ and $B$ (small), but at inference time one can fold $A B$ into $W$ if one wants to produce a single dense matrix and avoid extra runtime cost. The rank $r$ controls expressivity: small $r$ can implement many meaningful changes, but tasks that demand complex reparameterization may need larger $r$.

Orthogonal transforms and structured parameterizations are another route. Instead of arbitrary low-rank updates, one might constrain adaptation to rotations or orthogonal matrices, which preserve certain norms and can stabilize training. For example, an adapter can be implemented as $W' = U W$, where $U$ is near-orthogonal and parameterized via a skew-symmetric matrix exponentiation or via a small set of parameters that generate orthogonal transformations. These preserves feature norms and often avoid collapsing or amplifying activations, which is beneficial when stability is a concern. Orthogonal adapters are more expensive to parameterize than simple low-rank layers but can be regularized to remain close to identity, offering a safe, structured way to adapt.

Compute considerations include both additional FLOPs introduced by adapters during training and inference, and the overhead of dealing with sparse or block updates. Depthwise or channel-wise adapters tend to be cheap in compute because they operate channel-wise or per-element and don't introduce large matrix multiplications. Low-rank adapters add small matrix multiplications of shapes $(d \times r)$ and $(r \times d)$ which cost $O(d r)$ per token instead of $O(d^2)$. In mixed precision training, adapter parameters and optimizer states can be stored in reduced precision to reduce memory. Another practical trick is to freeze most of the model and only enable gradient computation (and optimizer states) for targeted adapters; frameworks usually let you exclude parameters to save optimizer memory.

Where to apply adapters depends on the task. For language-model finetuning aimed at style or domain adaptation without changing syntax-sensitivity, inserting adapters into FFN layers often suffices. For tasks that require changing how context is aggregated—e.g., long-range dependency emphasis—adapting $W^Q$ and $W^K$ or their low-rank surrogates is appropriate. When the model already learns powerful attention patterns and one only needs to nudge outputs, adapting $W^V$ or $W^O$ is a good compromise. In vision, pointwise convolutions (the $1\times 1$ mixing layers) are often the heaviest parameter blocks, so low-rank updates applied there or channel-wise scalings in depthwise separable designs provide much of the benefit at low cost.

Finally, it is important to consider layer normalization and residual connections. Adapters inserted after normalization or inside residual branches may have different effects because normalization rescales activations. Simple learned scale-and-bias terms around normalization layers or at residual branch entrances are often highly effective and extremely cheap. Residual pathways mean that small adapter outputs are combined with the original signal additively; therefore small, low-rank adapters can have outsized influence due to iterative compounding across layers, especially in deep networks.

Conclusion

Understanding the internal structure of convolutional and Transformer layers—interpreting convolutional filters as per-channel neuron vectors, distinguishing depthwise and pointwise roles, and decomposing attention into $Q,K,V,O$ matrices and MLPs into per-neuron basis functions—provides a principled foundation for targeted finetuning. Choices such as adapting depthwise filters, pointwise matrices, attention value/output projections, or FFN bottlenecks map directly to different behaviors and resource profiles. Practical finetuning methods like low-rank adapters, channel-wise scalings, and orthogonal transforms let practitioners trade parameter efficiency for expressivity and stability. Careful selection of which matrices to adapt, how to parameterize adapters, and where to place them relative to normalization and residual connections yields strong finetuning performance while controlling memory and compute demands.

Introduction

Images can be decomposed into sinusoidal building blocks, and that decomposition gives powerful intuition about what parts of an image carry which kinds of information. The Fourier transform (and its discrete implementation, the discrete Fourier transform or DFT) rewrites an image as a sum of sinusoids of different spatial frequencies, amplitudes, and phases. Two quantities emerge from that representation: the magnitude (how strong a sinusoid is) and the phase (the angular shift of that sinusoid). Intuition built from the 2D DFT and a few simple reconstruction experiments explains why phase often carries the bulk of the recognizable structure in natural images, and why changing the magnitude while keeping phase can still preserve semantics. This chapter develops the mathematics of the 2D DFT, explains the difference between magnitude and phase, shows how phase preserves structure, draws analogies to angular or directional information in neural representations, and points out the limits of those analogies.

# 2D discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and frequency domain representation

The 2D discrete Fourier transform takes a discrete image (a 2D array of pixel values) and converts it into a matrix of complex coefficients that represent how much of each two-dimensional sinusoidal pattern is present. For an image \(I\) of size \(M \times N\) with samples \(I[x,y]\) where \(0 \le x < M\) and \(0 \le y < N\), the forward DFT is
\[
F[u,v] \;=\; \sum_{x=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1} I[x,y]\; e^{-2\pi i\!\left(\frac{ux}{M}+\frac{vy}{N}\right)}
\]
for frequency indices \(0 \le u < M\), \(0 \le v < N\). Each complex coefficient \(F[u,v]\) encodes a sinusoid of horizontal frequency \(u\) and vertical frequency \(v\) with a complex amplitude. The inverse transform reconstructs the image:
\[
I[x,y] \;=\; \frac{1}{MN}\sum_{u=0}^{M-1}\sum_{v=0}^{N-1} F[u,v]\; e^{2\pi i\!\left(\frac{ux}{M}+\frac{vy}{N}\right)}.
\]
This pair of equations is an exact, linear change of basis: the set of complex exponentials \(e^{2\pi i(ux/M + vy/N)}\) forms an orthogonal basis for the space of \(M\times N\) images (with complex values).

Each term in the sum is a 2D sinusoid pattern (a plane wave) whose spatial frequency vector is \((u/M, v/N)\). Low values of \((u,v)\) correspond to slowly varying components (broad illumination, large-scale features), while high values correspond to fine detail and rapid variation (edges, texture). The factor \(e^{2\pi i(\cdot)}\) is complex; it can be written in terms of real sinusoids using Euler’s identity:
\[
e^{2\pi i\phi} = \cos(2\pi\phi) + i\sin(2\pi\phi).
\]
Thus each complex coefficient controls both amplitude and relative alignment of cosine and sine components.

Because the DFT coefficients are complex numbers, it is useful to separate them into magnitude and phase. For each frequency index \((u,v)\),
\[
F[u,v] = |F[u,v]|\, e^{i\angle F[u,v]},
\]
where \(|F[u,v]|\) is the magnitude (a nonnegative real number) and \(\angle F[u,v]\) is the phase (an angle in radians). The magnitude tells us how strongly the corresponding sinusoidal pattern contributes to the image; the phase tells us how that sinusoid is shifted relative to the coordinate origin.

Several important properties of the 2D DFT help build intuition. The transform is linear: a sum of images transforms into the sum of transforms. The convolution theorem states that convolution in the image domain corresponds to pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain:
\[
I * K \xleftrightarrow{\text{DFT}} F \cdot H,
\]
where \(F\) and \(H\) are DFTs of \(I\) and \(K\). Shifts in the spatial domain correspond to phase multiplications in the frequency domain: translating \(I\) by \((x_0,y_0)\) multiplies each \(F[u,v]\) by \(e^{-2\pi i(\frac{u x_0}{M}+\frac{v y_0}{N})}\). Parseval’s theorem equates total energy in image and frequency domains. These facts connect spatial structures (edges, patterns) with particular arrangements of frequency coefficients.

# Magnitude spectrum vs phase spectrum and visual reconstruction experiments

Given the DFT matrix \(F[u,v]\), we can separate the magnitude spectrum \(M[u,v]=|F[u,v]|\) and the phase spectrum \(\Phi[u,v]=\angle F[u,v]\). One can experiment with reconstruction by modifying either component and applying the inverse DFT.

A classic experiment is to create two reconstructed images. First, reconstruct using the original phases but replace magnitudes with uniform magnitudes (or random magnitudes). Concretely, set \(\tilde F_1[u,v] = e^{i\Phi[u,v]}\) (or \(\tilde F_1[u,v] = \tilde M[u,v] e^{i\Phi[u,v]}\) with \(\tilde M\) constant across \((u,v)\)). Second, reconstruct using the original magnitudes but set all phases to zero (or random). Let \(\tilde F_2[u,v] = M[u,v]\) (real, nonnegative), or \(\tilde F_2[u,v] = M[u,v] e^{i\cdot 0}\). Taking inverse DFTs gives two images.

Empirically, the first image (original phase, uniform magnitudes) typically looks surprisingly similar to the original image: edges, object boundaries, and recognizable structure remain. The second image (original magnitudes, zero or random phases) becomes a blurry, smeared, texture-like image with almost no recognizable objects. The same effect remains if one swaps the phase spectra between two different images while keeping their magnitudes: the reconstruction tends to take the structural form from the phase donor and the overall global contrast/texture from the magnitude donor. These experiments demonstrate that phase contains critical information for detailed spatial structure, while magnitude contributes more to the global power at different frequency bands.

Why does this happen numerically? Consider that the inverse DFT sums sinusoids weighted by complex amplitudes. The magnitude sets the weights of each sinusoid, but unless the phases align correctly, the sinusoids interfere in ways that do not produce sharp transitions or well-located edges. Phase controls the alignment or timing of the sinusoids so they add up constructively at specific spatial locations to form edges, corners, and localized structure. When phase is scrambled, constructive interference that places contrast at precise positions is lost, and the result becomes an unstructured superposition determined by magnitude alone.

# Role of phase in preserving structure and semantics of images

To understand why phase preserves geometric structure, examine what a sharp edge looks like in the Fourier domain. An ideal step edge localized at a particular position requires the sum of many sinusoids across a wide range of frequencies to line up so that their peaks and troughs coincide at that position. Those alignments are encoded in the phases of the frequency coefficients. If each sinusoid's phase is set so that maxima align at the edge location, their superposition yields a sharp transition. If phases are random, the high-frequency components will not sum coherently at an edge location, and the result is smooth or noisy rather than sharp.

Put differently, magnitude determines how much power at each frequency is available; phase determines where that power is placed in the image. If you imagine each sinusoid as a wave with crests and troughs across the image, magnitude scales the height of the waves, while phase shifts them left or right (and up or down in the 2D sense). The particular set of phase shifts across frequencies determines the constructive interference pattern that creates spatially localized features.

From a semantic point of view — the perception of object identity and shape — edges, contours, and relative positions are vital. Phase carries this positional and alignment information directly. Consider two images that share the same edge map but differ in smooth shading. Their high-frequency content and phases may be quite similar, and swapping magnitudes will maintain the perceived shapes. Conversely, textures and global contrast are often captured in magnitude: broadly speaking, the magnitude spectrum determines how much energy the image has at each spatial scale and orientation, which strongly influences apparent contrast and texture statistics but not the exact placement of object boundaries.

This does not mean magnitude is unimportant: magnitudes encode contrast at different scales and are crucial for texture recognition, global illumination, and some category-specific cues. But for telling whether an object occupies a particular position, whether contours close, and for many semantic identification tasks, phase is often the dominant contributor.

A compact mathematical way to see the centrality of phase for alignment is to write the inverse transform term for a single frequency:
\[
\text{term}_{u,v}(x,y) = |F[u,v]|\cos\!\left(2\pi\!\left(\frac{ux}{M}+\frac{vy}{N}\right) + \angle F[u,v]\right).
\]
The cosine argument includes the phase \(\angle F[u,v]\). The sum over many \((u,v)\) will produce a localized feature at \((x_0,y_0)\) precisely when the arguments for many frequencies align near that location due to their phases. Thus phase controls the spatial registration of multi-frequency interference.

# Analogies between phase information and angular/neuron-direction information

The role of phase as angular alignment in Fourier space invites analogies to neural or angular coding in other domains. In neuroscience, many neurons are directionally tuned: a neuron’s firing rate may depend on the angle of a stimulus or the direction of motion. Similarly, in the Fourier domain each frequency coefficient has an associated phase angle that encodes alignment. One can think of phases as “directional offsets” of sinusoidal basis functions. Both representations use angles to encode relative alignment or orientation.

In primary visual cortex (V1), many neurons behave like localized oriented Gabor filters: they are sensitive to a narrow range of spatial frequencies and orientations and have a preferred phase (polarity) where responses are maximal. A simple-cell neuron's receptive field can be approximated by a localized sinusoid multiplied by a Gaussian envelope. The relative phase of these localized sinusoids determines whether the cell responds to a bright bar on dark or a dark bar on bright — that is, the polarity and precise location of the feature. Thus neuron response patterns across a bank of such filters encode both magnitude-like information (how strongly each oriented frequency component is present) and phase-like information (precisely where along the receptive field the structure lies). The brain can combine outputs from many such neurons to reconstruct contours and identify shapes, in a manner analogous to inverse transforms from frequency components.

A second analogy appears in population codes where angles or directions are meaningfully interpreted: a population vector readout uses preferred directions of neurons and their firing rates to compute a resultant angle or position. In the Fourier phase case, the phase angles across frequencies can be combined to locate spatial events. In both cases the important information is not just the scalar strength but also the relative alignment across units.

Thinking of phase as angular information is helpful because angular representations have properties such as periodicity, wraparound, and modulation under translation. Translating an image imposes a linear change in phase proportional to frequency, just like rotating a vector field imposes a predictable change in angular coordinates. The consistency and predictability of how phase changes under transformations is part of why phase serves as a compact code for positional structure.

# Limitations of the analogy and when it does and does not apply

Analogies are useful but imperfect. Several limitations and caveats must be understood when relating Fourier phase to neural angular codes or semantic content more generally.

First, the Fourier basis is global and sinusoidal. Each Fourier coefficient influences the entire image (excepting special cases like localized transforms). Real neurons are localized in space and frequency, meaning cortical representations are more local and overcomplete relative to the global Fourier basis. Localized bases (wavelets, Gabor dictionaries, learned filters) combine the benefits of frequency and spatial localization, and their “phase-like” parameters are correspondingly local. Thus the DFT phase analogy breaks down when the representation is local and nonlinear.

Second, semantics are not always encoded primarily in phase. There are many tasks where magnitude (power at certain frequencies) is crucial. For texture classification or material recognition, statistical distributions of magnitudes across scales and orientations often provide strong cues. Global lighting and contrast, which affect magnitude, can be essential for recognizing certain categories. Therefore, phase dominance for semantics is context-dependent: phase tends to dominate for shape and positional information, but magnitude can carry semantic content for tasks sensitive to texture, scale, or overall energy distribution.

Third, the linearity of Fourier analysis creates situations that do not reflect perceptual or neural nonlinearity. The brain applies many nonlinear operations (rectification, normalization, thresholding) and pools across units; these operations can extract features that are not simply recoverable by manipulating phase and magnitude alone. In other words, phase and magnitude tell the entire story only for linear reconstructions; biological and engineered vision systems add nonlinear processing that changes which aspects of the input are important.

Fourth, boundary conditions, sampling, and aliasing affect the DFT’s meaning. In discrete images, finite support introduces wraparound (periodic extension) assumptions in the DFT. A phase pattern that correctly aligns features under periodic extension may correspond to different real-space arrangements under other boundary conditions. Aliasing from undersampling mixes frequencies, and thus mixes phase information in ways that can corrupt positional encoding. These technicalities limit how directly phase maps to spatial semantics in practical, sampled imagery.

Fifth, phase is ambiguous up to a global linear phase ramp when images are translated: a pure translation multiplies frequency coefficients by a predictable phase factor, but if a processing pipeline is invariant to translation (as many recognition systems strive to be), the absolute phases may not be meaningful for the task. In that sense, phase encodes absolute position, which may be irrelevant for classification tasks that require translation invariance; here magnitude-based features pooled spatially or invariant representations can dominate.

Finally, for complex natural scenes with occlusion, lighting variation, color, and 3D structure, semantic meaning is distributed across many cues and higher-order statistics that neither magnitude nor phase alone capture. For example, depth cues, shading gradients, and object identity can depend on combinations of local phase relationships, chromatic channels, and nonlinear interactions. Thus the phase-versus-magnitude story is a valuable but partial perspective.

# When the analogy applies: summary and practical implications

Despite limitations, the phase-as-angular-information analogy is illuminating in many practical contexts. When the task depends on spatial arrangement, contours, and precise localization (edge detection, shape recognition in fixed view, registration), phase is crucial. Experiments that swap phases between images show that structure and recognizable form follow phase. In modeling and signal processing, phase must be preserved if you want to keep sharp features; filters that preserve phase relationships (or are linear phase) avoid introducing spatial distortions. In neural modeling, thinking of neurons as encoding both amplitude-like and phase-like aspects clarifies why banks of oriented, phase-sensitive filters are useful primitives for early vision.

When a system needs invariance to translation, rotation, or scale, the raw Fourier phases are less directly useful because they change in predictable but task-irrelevant ways; one often derives invariant descriptors by combining magnitudes across frequencies or computing phase-difference statistics. When representations are localized and nonlinear (as in deep nets), the phase analogy still informs design: convolutional filters have phase-like offsets, and alignment of activations across channels encodes structure.

Conclusion

The 2D DFT provides a clear decomposition of an image into frequency components whose magnitudes and phases play distinct roles. Magnitude controls how much energy each frequency contributes; phase controls how those contributions line up to produce spatial structure. Simple reconstruction experiments illuminate why phase often preserves the recognizable structure and semantic content of images: it encodes alignment and localization. The analogy between phase and angular or directional neuronal information is useful for building intuition about how populations of tuned units represent alignment and position. However, this analogy breaks down in settings with localization, nonlinear processing, invariances, sampling artifacts, and tasks where texture or energy distributions (magnitudes) are the primary cues. Understanding both the power and limits of Fourier phase intuition is important for interpreting signal-processing results and for bridging low-level transforms and higher-level perceptual or neural representations.

Introduction

Differentiation of scalar functions with respect to scalar variables is a topic most students learn early on. In modern machine learning and numerical optimization we frequently need derivatives of scalar loss functions with respect to matrices or of matrix-valued functions with respect to matrix arguments. This chapter develops the matrix calculus and practical differentiation techniques needed when models include matrix-valued operations such as multiplications, transposes, and inverses — operations that arise, for example, when implementing differentiable orthogonal parameterizations (Cayley transforms, orthogonalization by inverse square root, and similar constructions). We will present both the core theoretical identities you will need and the practical recipe for implementing stable, efficient backpropagation through matrix inverses and other matrix-valued functions.

We begin with the calculus building blocks: differentials, product rules, and some useful identities. Then we derive the derivative of a matrix inverse and discuss numerical stability issues that arise when inverting matrices. Next we show how to backpropagate through parameterizations that use an inverse (the Cayley transform as a canonical example), including explicit formulas and computational strategies. After that we discuss efficient automatic-differentiation strategies for matrix operations — how to implement custom backward passes that are numerically stable and cheap. Finally we cover gradient clipping and conditioning techniques that are important when training models whose parameters are matrices, especially when orthogonality or near-singularity are involved.

# Basic matrix derivatives and useful identities (d(Ax), d(A^T), product rules)

A convenient way to reason about derivatives of matrix-valued functions is via differentials. The differential of a function provides a linear approximation to how the function changes in response to a small perturbation of its argument. For a matrix-valued function $F$ of a matrix $X$, we denote the differential by $dF$ and the perturbation of $X$ by $dX$. Many derivative identities are most compactly derived by manipulating differentials and then converting to gradients via inner-product identities.

Start with very simple and frequently used differentials. Let $A$ be a matrix independent of the variable and let $x$ be a vector variable. Then
- If $y = A x$ then the differential is $dy = A \, dx$. The Jacobian with respect to $x$ is therefore linear map $x \mapsto A x$.
- If $y = A^T x$ then $dy = A^T dx$.
These may appear trivial, but when $A$ itself depends on optimization variables the product rule below becomes necessary.

For functions involving matrix multiplication, the product rule is the matrix analogue of the scalar product rule. If $Y = A(X) B(X)$ where $A$ and $B$ both depend on $X$, the differential is
\[
dY = (dA) \, B + A \, (dB).
\]
This is exactly like the scalar product rule but with noncommutativity — the order matters. If either factor is constant then the corresponding term vanishes.

Transposes distribute over differentials in the expected way. If $Y = A(X)^T$ then
\[
dY = (dA)^T.
\]
This fact allows straightforward transfer between derivatives involving transposes.

A frequently useful identity is the differential of a quadratic form. If $y = x^T A x$ with scalar output, then
\[
dy = x^T (dA) x + (A + A^T) x \cdot dx,
\]
where the first term is from the matrix dependence and the second from the vector dependence. For scalar-output functions, the relationship between differentials and gradients is given by the trace form: if $f$ is scalar-valued and $df = \mathrm{tr}(G^T dX)$, then $G$ is the gradient of $f$ with respect to $X$, i.e. $G = \frac{\partial f}{\partial X}$ under the convention that the inner product of matrices is $\langle U, V\rangle = \mathrm{tr}(U^T V)$.

Two other identities that are very useful are those for elementwise (Hadamard) products and for vectorization. If $Y = A \circ B$ (Hadamard product) then
\[
dY = (dA) \circ B + A \circ (dB).
\]
Vectorization, together with Kronecker products, gives a systematic way to represent Jacobians: if $Y = A X B$ with fixed $A$ and $B$, then
\[
\mathrm{vec}(Y) = (B^T \otimes A) \, \mathrm{vec}(X).
\]
Differentiating gives the linear map on $\mathrm{vec}(X)$ immediately. The vectorization/Kronecker representation is powerful for theoretical manipulation but often impractical for large matrices because the Kronecker representation can be very high dimensional, so use it primarily for deriving identities rather than for implementation.

One last identity worth stating explicitly is for traces. If $f(X) = \mathrm{tr}(A^T X)$ then
\[
df = \mathrm{tr}(A^T dX),
\]
so the gradient is simply $A$. Many gradients can be derived by expressing the scalar loss as a trace and applying cyclic permutations inside the trace, e.g.
\[
\mathrm{tr}(U^T V W) = \mathrm{tr}(W U^T V) = \mathrm{tr}(V W U^T),
\]
and so on. These cyclic permutations let you move matrices into convenient positions before matching trace forms to $G$ under $df = \mathrm{tr}(G^T dX)$.

# Derivative of matrix inverse and stability considerations

The derivative of the matrix inverse is a core identity you will use repeatedly. Suppose $A$ is an invertible square matrix that depends smoothly on some parameter(s), and we want the differential of $A^{-1}$. Starting from the identity $A A^{-1} = I$ and differentiating both sides gives
\[
(dA) A^{-1} + A (dA^{-1}) = 0.
\]
Solve for $dA^{-1}$ to obtain the classic result
\[
d(A^{-1}) = -A^{-1} (dA) A^{-1}.
\]
This is a linear map in $dA$; the minus sign is important and reflects the inverse relationship.

If a scalar loss $L$ depends on some variable through $A^{-1}$, you can use this differential to derive gradients. For example, if $L$ is scalar and $dL = \mathrm{tr}\!\big( G^T d(A^{-1}) \big)$ for some upstream matrix $G = \frac{\partial L}{\partial A^{-1}}$, substitute the differential to obtain
\[
dL = \mathrm{tr}\!\big( G^T (-A^{-1} (dA) A^{-1}) \big) = \mathrm{tr}\!\big( -(A^{-1} G A^{-1})^T dA \big).
\]
Matching this to $dL = \mathrm{tr}\!\big( (\partial L/\partial A)^T dA \big)$ yields the gradient
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial A} = - A^{-T} \, G \, A^{-T}.
\]
Equivalently, using transposes,
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial A} = - A^{-T} \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial A^{-1}}\right) A^{-T}.
\]
This formula is frequently used in backpropagation: given an upstream gradient with respect to the inverse, we can compute the gradient with respect to the original matrix by multiplying on both sides by $A^{-T}$ and adding the minus sign.

Stability considerations. Although mathematically straightforward, differentiating through inverses can be numerically treacherous. The conditioning of $A$ plays a central role. The condition number for inversion in the 2-norm is
\[
\kappa(A) = \|A\|_2 \|A^{-1}\|_2.
\]
If $\kappa(A)$ is large (ill-conditioned), small perturbations in $A$ lead to large perturbations in $A^{-1}$. The differential expression $d(A^{-1}) = -A^{-1} (dA) A^{-1}$ makes this explicit: $A^{-1}$ multiplies $dA$ on both sides, amplifying perturbations by roughly $\|A^{-1}\|^2$. In the backward pass, the gradient with respect to $A$ contains $A^{-T}$ factors that can lead to extremely large gradients when $A$ is near singular.

Practical consequences:
- Avoid computing explicit inverses when possible. Instead, solve linear systems (see next sections). This both reduces numerical error and is often computationally cheaper.
- Regularize matrices that will be inverted, for example via $A \leftarrow A + \epsilon I$ for small $\epsilon > 0$ (Tikhonov regularization). This increases the smallest singular value and improves conditioning.
- Monitor singular values or the condition number during training. If the smallest singular value approaches zero, consider reparametrization or stronger regularization.
- Use double precision during sensitive phases or in diagnostics if single precision causes instability.

# Backpropagation through parameterizations that require matrix inverse (Cayley)

Orthogonal matrices are useful in neural networks (RNNs, invertible flows, etc.) because they preserve norms and help control exploding/vanishing gradients. A popular way to parameterize orthogonal matrices smoothly is via the Cayley transform. Given a skew-symmetric matrix $S$ (i.e., $S^T = -S$), the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix $Q$ by
\[
Q = \mathrm{Cay}(S) = (I - S) (I + S)^{-1}.
\]
One can check that $Q^T Q = I$ provided $I + S$ is invertible; the transform maps skew-symmetric matrices (which form a vector space) to orthogonal matrices that have no eigenvalue equal to $-1$.

To backpropagate through this form we must differentiate with respect to $S$. Start from the differential of $Q$:
\[
dQ = d\big((I-S)(I+S)^{-1}\big) = -(dS)(I+S)^{-1} + (I - S) \, d\big((I+S)^{-1}\big).
\]
Apply the inverse differential formula $d(A^{-1}) = -A^{-1} (dA) A^{-1}$ with $A = I+S$ to get $d((I+S)^{-1}) = -(I+S)^{-1} (dS) (I+S)^{-1}$. Substitute:
\[
dQ = -(dS)(I+S)^{-1} - (I - S) (I + S)^{-1} (dS) (I + S)^{-1}.
\]
Factor as
\[
dQ = -\big(I + (I - S) (I + S)^{-1}\big) (dS) (I + S)^{-1}.
\]
Using $Q = (I - S)(I + S)^{-1}$ we can rewrite $I + (I - S) (I + S)^{-1} = (I+S)(I+S)^{-1} + (I - S)(I + S)^{-1} = 2 (I + S)^{-1}$? Be cautious: algebraic simplification yields a more convenient symmetric-looking form but it is fine to continue from the explicit expression.

Now suppose the loss is scalar and we have the upstream gradient $\frac{\partial L}{\partial Q}$ (call it $G$). We want $\frac{\partial L}{\partial S}$. Use the trace inner-product identity:
\[
dL = \mathrm{tr}\!\big( G^T dQ \big).
\]
Substitute $dQ$ and rearrange to expose $dS$:
\[
dL = -\mathrm{tr}\!\Big( G^T \big[ (dS)(I+S)^{-1} + (I - S) (I + S)^{-1} (dS) (I + S)^{-1} \big] \Big).
\]
Use cyclic permutation under the trace to move $(I+S)^{-1}$ factors next to $G$, and then collect terms multiplying $dS$ on the right. After algebraic rearrangement (carried out in detail in many references), one convenient form of the gradient is
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial S} = - \big(I + S\big)^{-T} \left( G + Q^T G Q \right) \big(I + S\big)^{-T},
\]
projected onto the skew-symmetric space if you parametrize $S$ by only its independent elements. One way to see the structure: the Cayley transform creates $Q$ that depends on $S$ through linear and inverse terms; the resulting gradient w.r.t. $S$ involves left- and right-multiplications by $(I + S)^{-T}$ and a skew-symmetric combination of the upstream $G$ and its conjugations by $Q$.

Because $S$ is skew-symmetric, you typically parameterize it in terms of a vector of free variables (the entries above the diagonal). When converting the full matrix gradient $\partial L/\partial S$ to the parameter vector, take only the independent entries (for $i<j$, the variable corresponds to $S_{ij}$ with $S_{ji} = -S_{ij}$), and use the fact that $dS$ must be skew-symmetric, i.e. replace $\partial L/\partial S$ by its skew-symmetric part $\frac{1}{2}(\partial L/\partial S - (\partial L/\partial S)^T)$ before extracting independent entries.

Numerical issues specific to Cayley. The Cayley transform requires invertibility of $I+S$, i.e. $-1$ cannot be an eigenvalue of $Q$. In practice, during optimization $S$ may approach values where $I+S$ becomes nearly singular, causing large gradients due to multiplication by $(I+S)^{-T}$. Remedies include:
- Initialize $S$ small so that $I+S$ is well-conditioned at the start.
- Add a small ridge: replace $(I+S)$ with $(I+S + \epsilon I)$ in the formula and in practice implement $(I+S+\epsilon I)^{-1}$ (this modifies the exact orthogonality but keeps numerics stable).
- Constrain the norm of $S$ (e.g. spectral norm) using clipping or regularization.
- Use alternative parameterizations (exponential map via matrix exponential of skew-symmetric matrices, which always produces orthogonal matrices but is more expensive to compute and differentiate).

A practical computational strategy for the backward pass is to avoid computing explicit inverse matrices; instead compute solutions of linear systems. For expressions involving $(I+S)^{-T} M (I+S)^{-T}$ you can compute these by solving linear systems with $(I+S)^T$ rather than forming inverses explicitly.

# Efficient automatic differentiation strategies for matrix operations

When implementing models with matrix operations, automatic-differentiation (AD) systems like TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX, or autograd will work out gradients automatically if you compose primitive differentiable operations. However, naive AD that differentiates through an explicit inverse computation can be inefficient or unstable. There are several practical strategies to implement efficient and stable gradients for matrix operations.

Avoid explicit matrix inverse. Wherever the forward pass uses an inverse, prefer solving a linear system in both the forward and backward passes. For example, instead of computing $x = A^{-1} b$, compute $x$ by solving $A x = b$ using a numerically stable solver (LU decomposition, Cholesky if $A$ is SPD, or QR). In the backward pass, rather than differentiating the explicit inverse, implement the derivative via differentiating the linear solve. Many AD frameworks implement this automatically, but you should be aware of and prefer linear-solve primitives over explicit inversion.

Use custom backward rules for expensive matrix primitives. Two common primitives are linear solves and Cholesky decompositions. Implementing custom backward (VJP/JVP) rules that reuse intermediate factorizations computed in the forward pass avoids recomputation and improves numerical stability. For instance, if the forward pass computes an LU factorization to solve $A x = b$, store the LU factors. In the backward pass, when the gradient requires multiplies by $A^{-T}$ or similar, use the LU factors to solve triangular systems instead of recomputing factorizations or computing $A^{-1}$.

Exploit structure. If matrices are symmetric, skew-symmetric, triangular, or sparse, exploit these properties. For symmetric positive definite matrices $A = A^T \succ 0$, the Cholesky factorization $A = LL^T$ is numerically stable and the backward pass can be implemented using formulas that operate on $L$ and triangular solves. For orthogonal $Q$, you can use transpose instead of inverse ($Q^{-1} = Q^T$) which is cheap and numerically stable.

Use implicit differentiation for problems defined by linear systems or optimality conditions. Frequently, $X$ is defined implicitly as the solution to a linear system $A(\theta) X = B(\theta)$. Instead of differentiating through an iterative solver, apply implicit differentiation: differentiate the defining equation to obtain a linear system for $dX$,
\[
(dA) X + A (dX) = (dB),
\]
and solve for $dX$ or for its contraction with downstream sensitivities. For reverse-mode AD, one often needs vector-Jacobian products of $dX$ with a vector $v$; you can derive a linear system for the corresponding adjoint and solve using the same factorizations used in the forward pass.

Favor reverse-mode vs forward-mode wisely. For scalar losses depending on many parameters (common in deep models), reverse-mode AD (backpropagation) is efficient. For some matrix computations where you need Jacobian-matrix products in the forward direction, forward-mode can be practical. Be aware which quantities are large: forming full Jacobians (e.g., with Kronecker products) is usually infeasible in high dimensions, so always try to express derivatives as linear solves or matrix-matrix multiplies that scale like $O(n^3)$ or less, not $O(n^4)$.

Use memory-time tradeoffs: store factorizations. When backward requires re-solving with the same matrix, reuse the forward pass factorization (LU or Cholesky). This reduces computational cost and improves numerical stability because factorizations are computed once.

Numerical considerations in AD. Make sure to use numerically stable factorizations (Cholesky for SPD, QR/LS for least-squares) and avoid computing the inverse even when libraries provide it as a primitive. In many frameworks, the primitive "solve" has a standard gradient that uses solving with the transpose rather than explicit inverses; prefer such primitives.

# Gradient clipping and numerical conditioning for matrix-valued parameters

When optimizing matrix-valued parameters, gradients can become large in magnitude — particularly when your parameterization involves inverses or near-singular matrices. Unchecked, such gradients can cause numerical overflow or can push parameters into regimes where the forward computation becomes ill-conditioned or singular. Below are strategies one should know and use.

Gradient clipping. The simplest and most common technique is gradient clipping: scale gradients to keep their norm below a threshold. For matrices one can use different norms:
- elementwise clipping: clamp each element of the gradient matrix to lie in $[-\tau,\tau]$.
- Frobenius norm clipping: if the Frobenius norm $\|G\|_F$ exceeds a threshold $\tau$, scale $G \leftarrow \tau \, G / \|G\|_F$.
- spectral norm clipping: clip the operator norm (largest singular value) of the gradient if that is a more relevant measure for your model.

Clipping can prevent a single large update from sending a matrix into an unstable region. Use the norm most relevant to your parameterization; for orthogonality constraints the spectral norm often controls behavior better than elementwise clipping.

Regularization to improve conditioning. Adding a small multiple of the identity to matrices that will be inverted — $A \leftarrow A + \epsilon I$ — increases the smallest singular value and therefore reduces amplification in the inverse. In optimization this is often referred to as Tikhonov or ridge regularization. When $A$ is intended to be symmetric positive definite, this preserves the SPD property. When dealing with Cayley transforms, adding a small $\epsilon I$ to $I+S$ can be done internally for numerical stability, though it modifies the exact orthogonality.

Constraint-preserving updates and retractions. If you must maintain a constraint such as orthogonality, consider performing unconstrained updates in an ambient parameter space and then projecting (or retracting) back onto the manifold. For orthogonal matrices $Q$, a common retraction is to compute the polar decomposition or to perform a QR decomposition and use the orthogonal factor as the projection. For example, after an unconstrained gradient step on $Q$, compute $Q = \mathrm{polar}(Q)$ or $Q = \mathrm{qf}(Q)$ to obtain the nearest orthogonal matrix in Frobenius norm. These projections prevent drift into ill-conditioned regions and enforce the property exactly (up to numerical error).

Monitor condition numbers and singular values. During training it is often helpful to log the smallest singular value $\sigma_{\min}(A)$ and the condition number $\kappa(A)$ of matrices that will be inverted. If metrics indicate deterioration, increase regularization, reduce step size, or change parameterization.

Use adaptive learning rates and normalization. Per-parameter adaptive methods (Adam, RMSProp) can help, but they can also mask conditioning problems if they compensate for very large gradients without addressing near-singularity. Pair adaptive optimizers with explicit conditioning measures. Weight normalization and spectral normalization are also useful: spectral normalization constrains the spectral norm of a weight matrix by dividing it by an estimate of its spectral norm.

Practical recipe summary:
- Do not compute explicit inverses; use linear solves and factorization reuse.
- Regularize matrices to ensure invertibility and acceptable conditioning.
- Clip gradients using Frobenius or spectral norms appropriate to the parameter.
- Use retractions or projections to keep matrices on constrained manifolds.
- Monitor singular values/condition numbers and adjust hyperparameters when necessary.

Closing remarks

Differentiation through matrix-valued functions is straightforward in principle: rely on differentials, the product rule, and the inverse derivative formula. In practice the main challenges are numerical: avoiding explicit inverses, controlling amplification by ill-conditioned matrices, and implementing efficient AD that reuses factorizations and solves linear systems rather than forming full Jacobian matrices. For orthogonal parameterizations such as the Cayley transform, the backward pass introduces left and right multiplications by inverse-transpose factors and therefore can blow up when the matrix to be inverted approaches singularity; handle this via small initializations, regularization, and custom backward code that solves linear systems.

When you read research papers that involve differentiable matrix factorizations and orthogonal parameterizations, these are the identities and practical strategies you will need to both understand the derivations and implement stable algorithms. Keep the core differential identities at hand, avoid forming explicit inverses, and prefer solvers and factorizations with custom backward implementations to achieve both efficiency and numerical robustness.

Introduction

Fine-tuning a pretrained model is often the most effective route to achieve high performance on a downstream task. However, the process carries two linked risks: (1) the model may overfit to the (often small) downstream dataset and lose general capabilities learned during pretraining, and (2) the optimization may be unstable, leading to sudden degradation of outputs or "collapse" into degenerate behavior. This chapter develops a conceptual and practical toolkit for understanding and mitigating these risks. We first explain why few-shot fine-tuning is especially fragile and describe standard remedies such as weight decay and data augmentation. We then examine how operating in a small learning-rate regime implicitly constrains parameter drift away from the pretrained initialization and how that relates to explicit regularizers. Next we present explicit constraints and regularizers that keep a fine-tuned model close to the pretrained weights or limit parameter norms. We discuss particular stability issues in generative model fine-tuning, including collapse modes and how to prevent them. Finally, we describe how to measure convergence speed and robustness across random seeds, with practical diagnostics and metrics researchers should use when evaluating fine-tuning procedures.

# Overfitting risks in few-shot finetuning and remedies (weight decay, data augmentation)

When you fine-tune on a small dataset, the fundamental statistical problem is that there are many parameter configurations that fit the training examples perfectly, but most of them generalize poorly. Pretraining provides a strong prior: parameters that were shaped by large-scale data tend to encode broadly useful features and inductive biases. Fine-tuning moves the parameters to satisfy the downstream objective, but with scarce data that movement can quickly destroy the useful structure learned earlier.

Overfitting in few-shot regimes manifests in several ways. Loss on the training set can drop rapidly while validation or held-out task performance stagnates or falls. The model may develop brittle behavior: small perturbations of inputs produce nonsensical outputs, or the model may memorize spurious idiosyncrasies of the few examples. A related phenomenon is catastrophic forgetting, in which previously learned capabilities from pretraining are lost after a brief amount of fine-tuning.

Two of the simplest and most effective remedies are weight decay and data augmentation. Weight decay, implemented as L2 regularization on the parameters, modifies the optimization objective from minimizing the empirical loss $L(\theta)$ to minimizing $L(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2$, with regularization coefficient $\lambda>0$. Intuitively, weight decay penalizes large parameter values, which discourages overly complex models and reduces variance. In fine-tuning, weight decay helps keep parameters close to zero, and when combined with a pretrained initialization it often helps retain the broad structure learned during pretraining rather than letting a few parameters blow up to fit training noise.

Data augmentation increases the effective size of the dataset by producing plausible alternative inputs from each example. For vision models, common augmentations are crops, flips, color jitter, and mixup; for text, augmentations are more limited but can include synonym replacement, random masking, back-translation, or paraphrasing. When done carefully, augmentations reduce overfitting because the model must learn invariances that generalize beyond the small original set. For generative models, augmentation can include corruption-then-reconstruction training or adding synthetic noise to inputs during training to encourage robustness.

Both mechanisms are complementary: weight decay constrains the hypothesis class while augmentation increases the diversity of training samples. In practice, combining modest weight decay (tuned on a validation split) with augmentation tailored to the task often produces much more reliable fine-tuning than naively training until training loss is near zero.

# Small learning-rate regimes and implicit constraints on parameter drift

A widely used empirical strategy in fine-tuning is to use a small learning rate. Beyond slowing down optimization, a small learning rate has an implicit regularizing effect: it limits parameter drift from the pretrained initialization. To see this, consider discrete gradient descent updates for parameters $\theta$ initialized at $\theta_0$:
\[
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t),
\]
so after $T$ steps,
\[
\theta_T = \theta_0 - \eta \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla L(\theta_t).
\]
If $\eta$ is small and the gradients are bounded, then $\|\theta_T - \theta_0\|$ will be small. That is, small-step updates implicitly enforce a proximity constraint to the initialization. This implicit bias is often desirable: it keeps the fine-tuned parameters within a basin of the pretraining loss landscape that encodes general capabilities.

More formally, in a quadratic local approximation of the loss near $\theta_0$, $L(\theta) \approx L(\theta_0) + g^\top(\theta-\theta_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta_0)^\top H (\theta-\theta_0)$, gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ converges to a point that balances the gradients and the effective curvature scaled by $\eta$. In continuous-time limit (gradient flow), small step sizes correspond to slow trajectories that require more time to escape local basins. In stochastic gradient descent (SGD), the learning rate also scales the magnitude of parameter noise from minibatch gradients; smaller $\eta$ reduces SGD-born diffusion, which again stabilizes the parameters.

There are practical trade-offs. If the learning rate is too small, the optimizer may not effectively adapt to the downstream objective within resource constraints; convergence will be slow. Conversely, too-large learning rates can produce large parameter jumps that erase pretrained structure or land in narrow minima that generalize poorly. A common approach is to use a small base learning rate for the pretrained layers and a larger learning rate for newly initialized layers (e.g., a randomly initialized classification head). Another common pattern is to use a learning rate schedule that starts small and warms up or to use adaptive optimizers with careful tuning of their effective step sizes.

# Explicit constraints and regularizers (distance to pretrained weights, parameter norms)

Beyond implicit constraints given by small learning rates, explicit regularizers directly penalize deviation from the pretrained parameters. The simplest is an L2 penalty on the difference from the pretrained weights:
\[
R(\theta) = \frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta - \theta_0\|_2^2,
\]
so we minimize $L(\theta) + R(\theta)$. This "L2-from-initialization" regularizer (sometimes known as weight anchoring) explicitly encourages staying near $\theta_0$ while allowing the optimizer to move where necessary. The hyperparameter $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between adherence to pretraining and adaptation to the downstream data.

A more sophisticated variant is elastic weight consolidation (EWC). EWC uses a diagonal approximation of the Fisher information matrix $F$ from the pretrained model to weight penalties for changing each parameter:
\[
R(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i F_{ii} (\theta_i-\theta_{0,i})^2.
\]
Parameters with high Fisher information are those to which the pretraining objective was most sensitive; penalizing changes to them more strongly protects important capacities. EWC and its relatives were developed in continual learning to prevent catastrophic forgetting and can be adapted to finetuning to preserve crucial pretraining features.

Another family of regularizers constrains parameter norms or low-rank structure. For example, nuclear norm or low-rank penalties encourage updates that are concentrated in low-dimensional subspaces, which is comparable to adapters or low-rank-factorized updates (e.g., LoRA). Instead of penalizing all parameters equally, these methods restrict the effective degrees of freedom the fine-tuning process can use, reducing overfitting and preserving generalization.

Regularization can also be applied in function space rather than parameter space. KL regularization on output distributions encourages the fine-tuned model's predictive distribution $p_\theta(y|x)$ to remain close to the pretrained model's distribution $p_{\theta_0}(y|x)$ via a term
\[
R(\theta) = \beta \mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_{\text{train}}}\left[ \text{KL}\big(p_{\theta_0}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\big) \right].
\]
This approach directly limits changes in model behavior and can be particularly useful in generative model finetuning (discussed below). Choosing between parameter-space regularizers and function-space regularizers depends on whether the primary concern is preserving internal representations or preserving observable behavior.

# Stability issues in generative model finetuning and collapse modes

Generative models—language models, diffusion models, and autoregressive models—pose special stability challenges during fine-tuning. Unlike discriminative models where catastrophic forgetting may manifest as reduced accuracy on old tasks, for generative models the symptoms can be abrupt and pathological: the model may "collapse" to repetitive outputs, output trivial sequences, or produce high-confidence but incorrect distributions.

One common collapse mode is mode collapse, borrowed from the GAN literature, where the generator produces a small set of outputs (or even a single output) regardless of the input. In autoregressive models, collapse can appear as excessive repetition or empty answers. Another failure mode is overconfidence: the fine-tuned model becomes overly certain about a narrow set of predictions, losing calibration and diversity. These problems are exacerbated when the fine-tuning objective is misaligned with generation metrics—for example, maximizing likelihood on a small dataset of stylized text may push the model to emulate that style at the cost of fluency or factuality.

Several mechanisms can cause these collapses. High learning rates can push the model into narrow basins of the loss landscape where gradients align with pathological attractors. Unbalanced datasets or teacher forcing with short sequences can encourage degenerate sequence completion strategies. Fine-tuning on synthetic or imperfect labels can let spurious patterns dominate.

To prevent collapse, the following strategies are commonly effective. First, use conservative learning rates and small batch sizes if needed to prevent large, coordinated parameter updates. Second, apply functional regularizers such as KL penalties to keep the fine-tuned distribution close to the pretrained distribution:
\[
\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\big[-\log p_\theta(y|x)\big] + \beta \, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim D}\big[ \text{KL}\big(p_{\theta_0}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\big)\big].
\]
This explicitly prevents the fine-tuned model from assigning tiny probability mass to many tokens it used to consider likely.

Third, consider constrained or modular fine-tuning techniques. Methods like LoRA, adapters, and prompt tuning restrict updates to low-dimensional subspaces or small sets of parameters. Because only a small, structured portion of the network is changed, the risk of disturbing the whole generative process is reduced. Replay and distillation approaches—where some portion of pretraining data (or synthetic samples from the pretrained model) are mixed into the fine-tuning objective—also preserve diversity.

Fourth, monitor generation metrics beyond training loss. Track entropy of the predictive distribution, n-gram repetitiveness, and diversity metrics during training. Use early stopping when validation measures show increased repetition or decreased diversity. Finally, when feasible, use ensemble or temperature-controlled decoding to counteract any residual overconfidence.

# Measuring convergence speed and robustness across seeds

Robust evaluation of fine-tuning methods requires measuring not only final performance but also convergence speed, variance across random seeds, and sensitivity to hyperparameters. Researchers should adopt quantitative metrics and diagnostic plots to capture these aspects.

Convergence speed can be summarized by the number of optimization steps (or wall-clock time) required to reach a target validation performance. If $V_t$ denotes the validation metric at step $t$, we can define the hitting time $T_\epsilon = \min\{t : V_t \ge V^* - \epsilon\}$ where $V^*$ is a target score and $\epsilon$ is slack. Reporting median and percentile statistics of $T_\epsilon$ across seeds gives insight into typical and worst-case training times. Area-under-the-curve (AUC) of the validation metric plotted against steps is another useful scalar: it rewards both fast and steady improvement.

Robustness across seeds is critical because randomness in initialization, data ordering, and augmentation can produce substantial variance in fine-tuning outcomes—especially in few-shot regimes. Always run experiments with multiple independent seeds (commonly 5–10 or more, depending on computational budget) and report the mean and standard deviation of final metrics. Presenting confidence intervals or the full distribution (boxplots or violin plots) helps convey stability.

Beyond scalar performance, measure properties that reflect stability of behavior. The output variance metric measures how variable generated outputs are for the same prompt across different fine-tuned models (different seeds); excessive inter-seed variability suggests brittle optimization. Another useful diagnostic is the Hessian or its top eigenvalues at convergence: flatter minima (smaller top eigenvalues) generally correspond to more robust solutions. Computing the Fisher Information trace or parameter norm changes $\|\theta_T - \theta_0\|$ across seeds provides a picture of how much models depart from pretraining and whether that departure correlates with performance and instability.

When comparing methods (e.g., weight decay versus L2-from-initialization versus LoRA), evaluate them across a grid of hyperparameters and present performance surfaces. Hyperparameter sensitivity can be summarized by the width of hyperparameter ranges that achieve near-optimal performance; methods that perform well over a wider range are preferable in practice.

Finally, for generative models include behavioral diagnostics: entropy of predictive distribution, perplexity on held-out pretraining-like data to detect catastrophic forgetting, repetition rates (e.g., fraction of tokens in repeated n-grams), and human evaluation if possible. These metrics help identify subtle degradation that raw validation loss may miss.

Conclusion

Fine-tuning pretrained models is a powerful technique but requires careful attention to regularization, optimization dynamics, and evaluation protocols to avoid overfitting, preserve pretrained capabilities, and prevent degenerate behavior. Small learning rates and weight decay provide simple but effective controls; explicit regularizers like L2-from-initialization or Fisher-weighted penalties give stronger guarantees of staying near the pretrained solution. For generative models, functional regularization (KL penalties), modular fine-tuning (adapters, LoRA), and replay are particularly valuable to avoid collapse. Finally, rigorous measurement of convergence speed and robustness across seeds is essential: report distributions, not just single numbers, and track behavioral diagnostics alongside loss. Following these principles will make fine-tuning both more reliable and more reproducible in research and deployment.

# Introduction

Evaluating generative image models is one of the most challenging but essential tasks in modern computer vision. Unlike standard supervised learning where a single scalar label or a well-defined loss can indicate success, generative models must be assessed across multiple axes: the realism or perceptual quality of images, the diversity and distributional match to a target dataset, the degree to which generated images follow conditioning inputs such as text prompts or masks, and the consistency or controllability of generated outputs under varied constraints. No single metric captures all of these aspects, so researchers rely on a suite of complementary quantitative measures together with careful human evaluation protocols and statistical analysis.

This chapter presents the most commonly used metrics and practical protocols for evaluating generative image models. We will develop intuition for why each metric is used, how it is computed, and what its limitations are. We start with widely used perceptual and distributional metrics—Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Inception Score (IS)—then move to representation-based similarity metrics, especially CLIP-based scores for text–image alignment. Next we cover feature-matching metrics such as DINO and LPIPS that are useful for diversity and perceptual distance, and then task-specific control metrics like intersection-over-union (IoU), F1, and landmark L2 used when generation is conditional on structure or pose. Finally, we discuss robust human evaluation protocols, the importance of seed averaging, and statistical significance tests that make comparisons defensible.

Throughout, we aim for clarity in how each metric is computed (including mathematical definitions where appropriate), practical caveats, and best practices for reporting results so that research is reproducible and meaningful.

# Perceptual and distributional metrics: FID, IS (overview and caveats)

Two of the most widely used automatic metrics for assessing generative image models are the Inception Score (IS) and the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). Both use a pretrained convolutional neural network as a feature extractor—commonly the Inception-v3 network—and operate on large sets of generated images to produce scalar summaries meant to reflect sample quality and diversity.

The Inception Score was introduced to capture two desirable properties of generated images: that individual images be classifiable into distinct, confident categories, and that across samples the model covers many different categories. Given a generative model that produces samples \(x\), the Inception network provides a conditional label distribution \(p(y \mid x)\) where \(y\) indexes classes (in practice the 1000 ImageNet classes). The IS is defined as
\[
\mathrm{IS} = \exp\left( \mathbb{E}_{x} \; \mathrm{KL}\big( p(y\mid x) \,\|\, p(y) \big) \right),
\]
where \(p(y) = \mathbb{E}_x p(y\mid x)\) is the marginal class distribution over generated samples. Intuitively, if for each image \(p(y\mid x)\) is low-entropy (the model produces images that look like recognizable objects) and the marginal \(p(y)\) is high-entropy (samples span many classes), then the KL divergence inside the expectation will be large and the IS high.

Despite its simplicity, IS has important caveats. First, it relies heavily on the Inception classifier’s biases: models that produce images looking like ImageNet classes will score highly even if those images are implausible to humans in other respects. Second, IS does not compare to a real data distribution; it only measures properties of generated samples, so a generative model could cheat by producing highly classifiable but unrealistic images. Third, the IS cannot capture intra-class diversity beyond what the Inception network can detect. For these reasons, IS is best treated as a rough heuristic and not a definitive measure of sample quality.

Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) was proposed to directly compare the distribution of generated images with that of real images in the feature space of a pretrained network. Let \( \phi(x) \) denote the features extracted from the pool3 (or another chosen) layer of an Inception network. If real data features have mean \( \mu_r \) and covariance \( \Sigma_r \), and generated data features have mean \( \mu_g \) and covariance \( \Sigma_g \), then FID is
\[
\mathrm{FID} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\big( \Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2} \big).
\]
FID treats both real and generated feature distributions as Gaussians and computes their Fréchet distance (a.k.a. 2-Wasserstein distance for Gaussians). Lower FID indicates generated images resemble the real distribution more closely.

FID has advantages over IS: it directly measures distance to real data, is sensitive to both quality and diversity, and correlates relatively well with human judgments in many settings. However, FID also has limitations and pitfalls. The Gaussian assumption can break down if features are highly non-Gaussian. FID is sensitive to the choice of feature extractor and to preprocessing (image resizing, cropping, color channels). Crucially, FID estimates are biased and have high variance for small sample sizes; recommendations are to use at least several thousand samples (commonly 5k–50k) and, when comparing models, to use the same sample size and random seed protocol. In addition, FID can be “gamed” by models that optimize toward lowering FID without improving perceptual realism, and it is not well suited for conditional generation where the marginal distribution is not the primary concern.

In practice, report both IS (when relevant) and FID, but always specify the feature extractor, the number of samples, the preprocessing steps, and confidence intervals or standard deviations. When evaluating conditional models, complement these metrics with conditional and alignment metrics discussed later.

# Representation-based similarity metrics: CLIP score for text-image alignment

Conditional generative models, particularly text-to-image systems, require evaluation of how well generated images match conditioning signals, which can be free-form text. CLIP, which jointly trains an image encoder and a text encoder on large amounts of image–text pairs, offers a practical tool for measuring image–text alignment.

The CLIP score for a generated image \(x\) and a text prompt \(t\) is built from cosine similarity of the encoded representations. Let \(f_{\mathrm{img}}(x)\) and \(f_{\mathrm{text}}(t)\) be the normalized feature vectors produced by CLIP’s image and text encoders. Their cosine similarity is
\[
s(x,t) = f_{\mathrm{img}}(x)^\top f_{\mathrm{text}}(t).
\]
A common aggregate CLIP score across a set of \(N\) image–text pairs is the average similarity \(\frac{1}{N} \sum_i s(x_i, t_i)\) or the softmax-normalized retrieval likelihoods that reflect how well an image matches its matched caption compared to other captions in a batch. Many works report the mean cosine similarity; some compute a recall-style metric where the correct text is among the top-\(k\) retrieved captions for a given image.

CLIP scores have strong practical advantages. They are flexible across open-ended text, capture many semantic attributes, and correlate reasonably with human judgments of relevance in many domains. However, CLIP has important limitations. First, CLIP was trained on web-scale image–text pairs and thus inherits dataset biases; it may reward or penalize attributes that reflect spurious correlations in its training data. Second, CLIP can be insensitive to fine-grained correctness: it might give similar scores to an image with minor but critical mistakes (wrong color, extra objects) if global semantics are right. Third, CLIP’s scores are bounded by the capacity and training domain of the model: out-of-distribution styles, non-photorealistic art, or text involving unusual tokens may not be well represented. Finally, CLIP score alone does not reflect visual quality: a blurry but semantically correct image may get a high CLIP score while being undesirable.

Because of these issues, CLIP scores are best used as one component of a multifaceted evaluation. For text–image alignment, report both CLIP-based metrics and human judgments focused on prompt fidelity. When comparing models, use the same CLIP model and preprocessing settings, and where possible complement cosine similarity with retrieval-style evaluations that control for batch effects (see below on human and statistical protocols).

# Feature-matching metrics: DINO, LPIPS for diversity and perceptual distance

Beyond global distributional metrics and semantic alignment, researchers often need measures that quantify perceptual similarity and diversity at a finer level. Two commonly used feature-based metrics are LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity) and metrics derived from self-supervised features like DINO. Both operate by comparing deep features that capture perceptually relevant structure rather than raw pixel differences.

LPIPS was designed to measure perceptual distance between two images in a way that correlates with human judgments. Given a pretrained convolutional network (VGG, AlexNet, or others), LPIPS extracts features from several layers, rescales and weights them, then computes a weighted L2 distance. Formally, if \(\phi_l(x)\) are normalized features from layer \(l\) and \(w_l\) are learned scalar weights, LPIPS between images \(x\) and \(y\) is
\[
\mathrm{LPIPS}(x,y) = \sum_l w_l \lVert \phi_l(x) - \phi_l(y) \rVert_2^2.
\]
LPIPS correlates well with human perceptual judgments of image similarity and is particularly useful for evaluating similarity of generated images to reference images in paired tasks (e.g., image-to-image translation) and for measuring diversity by computing pairwise distances across samples.

DINO is a self-supervised representation learned with contrastive or clustering-based objectives that yields rich semantic features suitable for many downstream tasks. DINO features are often used to compute clustering-based metrics or average cosine distances to quantify diversity and mode coverage. For example, one can extract DINO embeddings for many generated images and compute the coverage of k-means clusters derived from real data, or compute the mean pairwise cosine distance as a summary of diversity. These features are sensitive to mid- and high-level semantic content, making them useful when pixel-level distances (like mean squared error) are misleading.

When using LPIPS or DINO-based metrics, keep in mind their domain sensitivity. The pretrained networks were trained on particular datasets and may under- or over-emphasize certain visual properties. LPIPS is designed to measure perceptual similarity; low LPIPS indicates images are perceptually close, but a low LPIPS to a poor-quality reference does not imply generative quality. Conversely, high pairwise LPIPS among generated samples indicates diversity, but diversity without fidelity is useless. Computational cost can be nontrivial for large sample sets because pairwise distances scale quadratically when computing metrics like average unique distances; approximate sampling is common.

Best practices include reporting both fidelity (e.g., LPIPS to nearest reference) and diversity (e.g., mean pairwise LPIPS or feature-space coverage) and combining these with distributional metrics like FID and alignment metrics like CLIP score. For conditional generation, compute these metrics conditioned on the same control inputs (e.g., evaluate diversity across different random seeds for the same conditioning signal) so that they reflect controllability rather than global diversity.

# Task-specific control metrics: IoU, F1 for structure consistency, landmark L2 for pose control

When generative models are used in controlled or conditional generation tasks—such as image editing with masks, semantic synthesis from segmentation maps, or pose-guided generation—evaluation must reflect how well the generated images adhere to the conditioning structure. Generic metrics like FID or CLIP cannot capture fine-grained structural fidelity. For structure-oriented conditions, task-specific metrics such as intersection-over-union (IoU), F1 score, and L2 distance of predicted landmarks are critical.

IoU (intersection-over-union), also called the Jaccard index, is a standard metric for measuring overlap between two binary or multi-class segmentation masks. Given a predicted mask \(M_p\) and a ground-truth mask \(M_{gt}\), both defined over pixels and possibly per-class, the IoU for class \(c\) is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{p,c} \cup M_{gt,c}|}.
\]
For multi-class segmentation one typically report mean IoU (mIoU) averaged across classes. In generation tasks where the model is conditioned on a segmentation map or is expected to preserve object layout, one can run a semantic segmentation model on the generated image to produce \(M_p\) and compare it to the target mask using IoU. A high IoU implies the generated image respects the target spatial arrangement and class labels.

IoU has intuitive appeal but also practical subtleties. It depends on the quality of the segmentation model applied to generated images; if the segmentation network fails on generated styles, IoU will be unreliable. For fine-grained boundaries or thin structures, IoU can be overly harsh; in such cases reporting boundary-aware metrics or F1 scores (which combine precision and recall) can be helpful. The F1 score for a class is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\[
\mathrm{Precision}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{p,c}|}, \quad
\mathrm{Recall}_c = \frac{|M_{p,c} \cap M_{gt,c}|}{|M_{gt,c}|},
\]
\[
\mathrm{F1}_c = 2 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{Precision}_c \cdot \mathrm{Recall}_c}{\mathrm{Precision}_c + \mathrm{Recall}_c}.
\]
F1 can be informative when false positives (spurious objects) and false negatives (missing objects) are both important and need to be balanced.

For pose control and landmark consistency, evaluating how well a generated image matches a specified pose often uses encoded keypoints detected on both generated and real images. Suppose the conditioning pose is specified as a set of 2D keypoints \(\{(x_i^{(c)}, y_i^{(c)})\}_{i=1}^K\}\) and a pose detector returns predicted keypoints on the generated image \(\{(x_i^{(p)}, y_i^{(p)})\}_{i=1}^K\}\). A common metric is mean squared error or mean Euclidean distance over landmarks:
\[
\mathrm{Landmark\ L2} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^K \sqrt{ (x_i^{(p)} - x_i^{(c)})^2 + (y_i^{(p)} - y_i^{(c)})^2 }.
\]
This L2 distance can be normalized by image size or inter-ocular distance (for faces) to make comparisons scale-invariant. Lower landmark L2 indicates better pose adherence.

Again, caveats apply: landmark metrics depend on the reliability of the detector used on generated images, which may fail on stylized outputs or occlusions. It is also possible for a model to achieve low landmark L2 by producing degenerate outputs that mimic keypoint locations but lack photorealism. Therefore landmark metrics should be used alongside fidelity and perceptual metrics.

Other task-specific metrics arise in specialized contexts: for instance, in structured editing one might measure edge consistency, in text-conditional generation one might use object-counting accuracy when prompts mention counts, and in face synthesis identity preservation metrics (embedding cosine similarity between generated and reference faces) are common. The key principle is to choose metrics that directly quantify the intended controllable property, verify assumptions about detectors or classifiers used to compute them, and always report multiple complementary metrics.

# Human evaluation protocols, seed averaging, and statistical significance in generative evaluations

Automatic metrics are invaluable, but many aspects of image quality and alignment are inherently subjective, requiring human judgments. Well-designed human evaluation protocols and rigorous statistical analysis are essential to draw reliable conclusions.

Human evaluation can take many forms. Pairwise preference tests ask annotators to choose which of two images better matches a prompt or looks more realistic. Absolute rating scales ask annotators to score images on quality, fidelity, or other attributes (e.g., a 1–5 Likert scale). Binary correctness tasks ask whether an image satisfies a given constraint (yes/no). Each format has trade-offs: pairwise comparisons reduce scale ambiguity and are more sensitive to small differences, but require \(O(n^2)\) comparisons if all pairs are considered; rating scales are cheaper but more susceptible to inter-annotator variability.

Designing human studies requires careful controls. Use clear and consistent instructions, include qualification tests to ensure annotators understand the task, and provide training examples. Randomize image order and anonymize model sources to avoid bias. Define the evaluation question precisely: for example, “Which image better depicts ‘a red bird perched on a branch’?” is preferable to an ambiguous “Which image is better?” Ideally, collect multiple independent judgments per item (commonly 3–10) and aggregate via majority vote or mean rating. Report inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s kappa, Krippendorff’s alpha, or percent agreement) as an indicator of evaluation reliability.

Seed averaging and repeatability are important in generative modeling because randomness in initialization, training order, and sampling can cause nontrivial variation in results. When reporting quantitative metrics, it is best practice to run experiments with multiple random seeds (both for training and for generation, when feasible) and report mean and standard deviation across runs. For sampling variability at evaluation time, generate multiple sets of images with different RNG seeds and average the metric, or report confidence intervals. For metrics like FID and CLIP score that depend on finite sample draws, present bootstrap or standard-error estimates reflecting sampling noise.

To establish whether observed differences between models are statistically significant, perform appropriate hypothesis tests. For paired evaluations—e.g., when comparing scores of two models on the same set of prompts—use paired tests that account for correlation. For continuous metrics that are approximately normally distributed across items, a paired t-test can be used to test whether the mean difference is zero. If normality is suspect or sample sizes are small, use nonparametric paired tests like the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For binary outcomes aggregated across items (e.g., success/failure per prompt), McNemar’s test can test differences on paired binary outcomes. For human preference data, analyze proportions with a binomial test or compute confidence intervals using the Wilson score interval.

Bootstrapping is a powerful and widely used approach: resample the evaluation dataset with replacement many times (e.g., 10,000 bootstrap samples) and compute the metric difference for each sample to produce an empirical distribution of differences and a confidence interval. This approach makes minimal distributional assumptions and naturally accommodates complex metrics.

When conducting multiple comparisons (e.g., comparing several models or several metrics), adjust for multiple hypothesis testing to control family-wise error rates. Simple methods include Bonferroni correction, but for many hypotheses the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate is often more powerful.

Finally, report effect sizes and practical significance, not just p-values. A difference can be statistically significant but practically negligible. Always include the number of samples, number of annotators, standard errors, and confidence intervals alongside point estimates. Provide ablation studies that isolate the contribution of architectural changes vs. hyperparameter variations, and share code and seeds to support reproducibility.

# Conclusion and practical recommendations

Evaluating generative image models requires a toolbox rather than a single instrument. Use distributional metrics like FID to assess how closely generated images match the overall real-data manifold, but recognize FID’s sensitivity to preprocessing and sample size. Use IS cautiously and primarily as a historical baseline. For conditional alignment, use representation-based metrics such as CLIP score while acknowledging CLIP’s biases and blind spots. Use perceptual metrics like LPIPS and self-supervised features (DINO) to measure diversity and perceptual similarity, and apply task-specific metrics like IoU, F1, and landmark L2 to quantify structural and pose adherence. Complement all automatic measures with human evaluations designed with clear prompts, multiple annotators, and robust statistical analysis. Average across multiple random seeds and report confidence intervals; use paired tests or bootstrap methods to assess statistical significance, and correct for multiple comparisons when needed.

When presenting results, be transparent: document the feature extractors and their versions, preprocessing pipelines, number of generated samples, random seeds, and human evaluation details (instructions, annotator qualifications, inter-annotator agreement). This transparency enables fair comparisons and advances the field by ensuring that reported improvements reflect real, reproducible gains in image quality and controllability.

Introduction

Modern generative models—large pretrained transformers and their finetuned variants—are powerful but also expensive and finicky. When evaluating new finetuning methods, architectures, regularizers, or parameter-efficient techniques, we must design experiments that allow fair, informative, and reproducible conclusions. This chapter gives concrete guidance for choosing baselines, creating dataset splits and few-shot protocols, designing ablation studies, reporting variability and failures, and making experiments computationally reproducible. The emphasis is on intuition and practical rules so that your results are credible, comparable, and useful to other researchers.

# Selecting baselines and ensuring fair comparisons (matching parameter budgets/targets)

Good baselines are the backbone of experimental claims. A baseline should represent the best reasonable alternative under comparable resource and design constraints. The key principle is fairness: when claiming superiority of a method, compare to baselines that have matched or transparently documented budgets in the aspects that matter for your claim. Commonly relevant budgets are total trainable parameter count, inference latency, wall-clock/GPU-hours, floating-point operations (FLOPs) during training, and memory usage.

Start by deciding the primary resource your method targets. If you propose a parameter-efficient finetuning recipe (e.g., adapters, LoRA), your central claim likely concerns reductions in the number of trainable parameters. In that case match baselines on trainable parameter count. If your method claims to reduce inference latency, match baselines on wall-clock latency at inference using the same hardware and batch sizes. If the claim is improved sample-efficiency, match baselines on the number of labeled examples and report curves of performance vs examples. Often more than one budget matters; explicitly state which budget you matched and why the matching is the right lens for the claim.

How to match budgets in practice:
- Trainable parameter budget: compute the number of parameters changed during finetuning. For adapter-style methods, explicitly calculate trainable parameters per layer and multiply by the number of layers. For example, a single-layer adapter with input dimension $d$ and bottleneck dimension $b$ has $d\cdot b + b\cdot d = 2bd$ weights (plus biases). If you place two such adapters per transformer layer, multiply accordingly. Compare to full finetuning (all parameters trainable) and to other parameter-efficient baselines with the same or slightly higher/lower counts.
- FLOPs or GPU-hours: If your claim concerns compute efficiency, measure total training FLOPs or GPU-hours and match baselines on that. FLOPs for a forward+backward step can be estimated from model size and sequence length; multiply by number of steps and batch size. Report both wall-clock time on the specific hardware and the estimated FLOPs to allow cross-hardware comparison.
- Latency/memory: For inference claims, measure throughput and latency on the same hardware, with the same batch sizes and implementation tricks (e.g., kernel fusion, quantization). If you compare methods requiring different runtimes (e.g., a sparse method), ensure the deployment environment is consistent.

When you cannot perfectly match a budget, be transparent: report the budgets for each method, and provide sensitivity analyses showing how performance varies with budget (e.g., performance vs parameter count curve). This helps reviewers and readers extrapolate fairness.

Choosing baseline algorithms: include strong, well-tuned versions of standard methods. For finetuning transformers these often include: full finetuning, layer-wise finetuning (top $k$ layers), adapters, LoRA, prompt tuning, and any recent competitive methods on the same task. Use the authors’ recommended hyperparameters for baselines when possible, and if you change them, document why.

Finally, consider an "oracle" or upper bound where appropriate: for example, performance when training with far more data or compute. This contextualizes how much headroom remains and whether a small improvement is meaningful.

# Dataset splits, few-shot protocols, and data-efficiency experiments

Data splits are deceptively important. A standard split has three parts: training, validation (development), and test. The training set is used to fit parameters; the validation set is used to choose hyperparameters and early stopping; the test set is held out and used only for unbiased final evaluation. Never tune or report results on the test set.

For finetuning large generative models, particularly with few-shot or data-limited regimes, you must be explicit about how few-shot examples are selected. There are two common few-shot paradigms:

1. In-context few-shot (prompting): provide $k$ examples in the input to a frozen base model. The selection of in-context examples, their ordering, formatting, and prompt templates can dramatically affect results. To be fair, evaluate over multiple random draws of in-context examples and report mean and variance. If prompts are hand-crafted, include ablations of prompt wording and justify choices.

2. Finetuning few-shot (parameter updates): when you finetune on small numbers of labeled examples, the randomness in example selection matters. Report average performance over multiple randomly sampled training sets of size $k$ (e.g., 5–20 seeds for each $k$). Do not cherry-pick samples that favor your method.

Data-efficiency experiments should be presented as learning curves: plot performance against number of fine-tuning examples on a log or linear scale as appropriate. A single point at one $k$ is weak evidence; a curve shows whether your method systematically improves sample efficiency. When plotting, include standard error bars (see the section on reporting variance) and ensure axes are labeled clearly.

When splitting data for validation and multiple tasks, use nested splits if hyperparameter selection could leak information to the test set. A typical protocol: for each random sample of the $k$-shot training set, set aside a small validation set (or use cross-validation) to choose hyperparameters, then evaluate the selected model on an unseen test set. If the dataset is so small that holding out validation examples is impractical, use cross-validation or repeated holdout with aggregated results.

Additionally, be careful with pretraining overlap: ensure no test examples were seen during pretraining of the base model. This is especially relevant for large models trained on scraped web text. If overlap is suspected, either remove overlapping examples or report them separately.

# Ablation studies: hyperparameters, parameter counts, and block/rank choices

Ablation studies aim to isolate the effect of each design choice. The best ablations are systematic, controlled, and interpretable. The general approach is to vary one factor at a time while holding others fixed (the ceteris paribus principle). When interactions are suspected, consider factorial designs or more modern techniques like random forests or ANOVA to analyze interactions.

Hyperparameter ablations: Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, weight decay, dropout, optimizer type) can dramatically change results. Always perform a hyperparameter search for each baseline and the proposed method under matched tuning budgets. A fair approach is to allocate an equal search budget (number of trials and compute) to each method and use identical search strategies (grid, random, or Bayesian). Report the search space, the number of trials, and how the chosen hyperparameters were selected (e.g., based on validation loss or downstream metric). Present heatmaps or sensitivity plots for learning rate and other crucial hyperparameters to show stability.

Parameter count ablations: When you propose a parameter-efficient technique, evaluate how performance scales with parameter budget. For adapter or LoRA methods, vary the bottleneck size or low-rank $r$ and plot performance vs total trainable parameters. This allows readers to see trade-offs and where diminishing returns begin. Provide formulas for parameter counts so others can reproduce them. For example, adding a LoRA update to a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with rank $r$ costs $\Delta P = 2rd$ parameters (for two matrices $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$), ignoring biases.

Block and rank choices in modular adapters: Many parameter-efficient modules have discrete design choices such as where to insert them (attention vs MLP blocks), whether to tie parameters across layers, and the internal bottleneck rank $r$ or block size. For each such dimension, run ablations varying one design choice while fixing others. Typical axes to explore include:
- Position: insert only in MLPs, only in attention, both, or interleaved.
- Sharing: per-layer independent adapters vs shared adapter across layers vs grouping layers into blocks.
- Rank or bottleneck: ranks $r\in\{1,4,8,16,32,64\}$ or bottleneck sizes $b$ across a geometric range.
- Initialization: zero initialization vs small random initialization; some methods are sensitive to initialization.

Whenever possible, present a 2D surface (e.g., performance vs rank and learning rate) or multiple curves to show trade-offs. If you cannot explore all combinations due to compute limits, prioritize the most plausible design choices and explain why others were deprioritized.

Statistical rigor in ablations: for each ablation point, run multiple seeds (see the next section) to quantify variance. If interactions are complex, consider a small factorial design (e.g., 2-level factorial with core factors) that estimates both main effects and pairwise interactions.

# Reporting standard deviations, multiple seeds, and failure cases

Variability is real and must be reported. Single-seed results are unreliable, especially in low-data regimes and when finetuning large models.

Number of seeds: At minimum, run experiments with at least 3 independent seeds, but 5–10 is preferable for robust estimates if resources permit. The choice depends on the variance observed; if variance is high, more seeds are necessary. When reporting many hyperparameter configurations or ablation points, try to standardize on a fixed number of seeds across all runs.

Reporting format: For each reported metric, give the mean and a measure of spread, typically standard deviation or standard error. For example, report results as: mean ± standard deviation over $N$ seeds. If you display curves, include error bands representing $\pm$ standard error or a bootstrap confidence interval. For a metric $m$ over $N$ independent seeds with sample mean $\bar{m}$ and sample standard deviation $s$, the standard error is
\[
\mathrm{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}.
\]
A 95% confidence interval under approximate normality is $\bar{m} \pm t_{0.975, N-1}\cdot \mathrm{SE}$.

Statistical testing: When claiming superiority, perform a statistical test appropriate for paired or unpaired comparisons. For paired experiments (same random seeds and data splits for both methods), a paired t-test is often appropriate. For more general comparisons or non-normal distributions, a non-parametric test like the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is safer. Always report effect sizes and p-values, and be mindful that small p-values are easier to obtain with large samples; focus on practical significance (magnitude of improvement) and confidence intervals.

Failure cases and negative results: Report failure modes transparently. If some seeds catastrophically diverge or result in collapsed output, note the frequency and conditions (e.g., high learning rate, particular dataset). If a hyperparameter search often fails for a baseline but not your method, that is an important part of the story. Include examples of qualitative failures (e.g., hallucinations, output truncation) and, where relevant, metrics that capture these issues (e.g., calibration, factuality scores, toxicity detectors).

Visual diagnostics: Include plots of per-seed learning curves rather than just final points. This reveals whether improvements are from faster convergence, better asymptotic performance, or reduced variance across seeds.

# Computational considerations and reproducible code/seed reporting

Reproducibility extends beyond seeds and averages to the computational environment and code. Modern ML stacks have many non-deterministic components (cuDNN algorithms, asynchronous kernels, hardware differences). The goal is practical reproducibility: enough information and artifacts that others can reproduce the high-level results or rerun experiments to verify claims.

Environment and code: Tag every experiment with a code commit hash and provide a link to the repository. Include environment specifications (e.g., a requirements.txt, Pipfile, or conda environment.yml). For low-level reproducibility, document:
- Framework and major library versions (PyTorch/TensorFlow, CUDA, cuDNN).
- Hardware used (GPU model(s), CPU, number of GPUs).
- OS and kernel version when relevant.

Random seeds: Document all sources of randomness and how you set seeds. Common seeds include:
- Python's builtin RNG: random.seed(s)
- NumPy: np.random.seed(s)
- Framework RNGs: torch.manual_seed(s), torch.cuda.manual_seed_all(s)
- Any library-specific RNGs (e.g., JAX, TensorFlow)
Record the seed values used for each independent run, and ideally provide a table or file listing seed-to-checkpoint mappings.

Deterministic flags and performance trade-offs: Many frameworks provide deterministic modes (e.g., torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)). These reduce nondeterminism but may degrade performance or prevent certain kernels from running. Document whether deterministic modes were used and, if not, explain the trade-off. If determinism is impossible, provide multiple independent seeds and show that results are robust despite nondeterminism.

Exact scripts and checkpoints: Provide the training and evaluation scripts used to run experiments, with exact command lines for each run. Publish model checkpoints for the best models and for representative seeds where possible. If publishing full checkpoints is impossible due to size or policy, provide fine-grained saved metrics and seed-to-checkpoint mappings.

Compute reporting: For each experiment specify:
- Number of GPUs and type, wall-clock time, and total GPU-hours.
- Batch sizes, sequence lengths, number of training steps, and optimizer settings.
- Any special implementation details that affect runtime (e.g., mixed precision, gradient accumulation, distributed data parallel settings).

Reproducing results across hardware: Provide FLOPs estimates and theoretical compute counts (e.g., number of multiply-adds per forward/backward) in addition to wall-clock times so that others can approximate runtimes on different hardware.

Packaging experiments: Use reproducibility-friendly tools like containerization (Docker), environment lock files (pip-tools, poetry), or workflow definitions (Makefile, shell scripts, or orchestration via reproducibility frameworks). Include a README that gives step-by-step instructions to reproduce key results, e.g., "To reproduce Figure 3, run script experiments/figure3.sh with commit hash X, using 4x A100-40GB GPUs."

Practical checklist
- State precisely which budget(s) you matched for fair comparison, and provide budget numbers for every method.
- Publicly release code, hyperparameter files, and random seeds for all reported runs.
- Use at least 3–5 seeds (preferably more) and report mean±std or confidence intervals.
- Split datasets into train/val/test carefully; use nested validation when tuning on small data.
- Provide learning curves (performance vs examples, vs steps), not just final numbers.
- For ablations, vary one factor at a time and include parameter-count and rank ablations for parameter-efficient methods.
- Report compute in both wall-clock and normalized compute units (FLOPs or GPU-hours).
- Document environment (framework, CUDA, cuDNN), hardware, and commit hash; supply scripts and checkpoints.

Conclusion

Reproducible, fair experimentation in generative model research demands discipline and transparency. Thoughtful baseline selection, rigorous data-splitting protocols, systematic ablations, honest reporting of variance and failures, and careful computational documentation transform research claims from anecdote to evidence. Applying the principles in this chapter will make your results more credible, easier to compare with others, and far more useful for the community.
