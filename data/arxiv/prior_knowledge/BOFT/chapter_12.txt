Introduction

Representation learning often reduces rich data to vector embeddings in Euclidean space, and many learning tasks rely on measuring distances or similarities between those vectors. Understanding the mathematical structures behind distances and similarities clarifies what models can express and how to regularize them. This chapter develops the role of bilinear forms and their special case, quadratic forms, and shows how they underlie the Mahalanobis distance, metric learning algorithms, and bilinear similarity models used for classification and retrieval. We also explain how left-multiplying weights in neural networks implement learned bilinear maps, why enforcing orthogonality is a useful regularizer, and how orthogonal transforms relate to invariances and distance preservation. Throughout, emphasis is on intuition, geometry, and connections to practical modeling choices.

# Bilinear forms and quadratic forms: definitions and properties

A bilinear form is a map that takes two vectors and returns a scalar in a way that is linear in each argument. Formally, for a vector space $V$ over the reals, a function $B: V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ is a bilinear form if for all $u,v,w \in V$ and scalars $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$,
\[
B(au + bv, w) = aB(u,w) + bB(v,w), \qquad B(w, au + bv) = aB(w,u) + bB(w,v).
\]
When $V=\mathbb{R}^d$, every bilinear form can be represented by a $d\times d$ real matrix $M$ so that
\[
B(u,v) = u^\top M v.
\]
This matrix representation follows directly by choosing the standard basis and evaluating the form on basis vectors; the matrix entries are exactly $M_{ij} = B(e_i,e_j)$. The matrix viewpoint makes many properties transparent. Linearity corresponds to matrix multiplication being linear in each factor; symmetry of the bilinear form corresponds to $M$ being symmetric; nondegeneracy corresponds to $M$ being invertible.

A quadratic form is a special case of a bilinear form evaluated with the same vector in both slots. That is, a function $Q: V \to \mathbb{R}$ is a quadratic form if there exists a bilinear form $B$ such that $Q(v) = B(v,v)$. In coordinates, a quadratic form on $\mathbb{R}^d$ has the form
\[
Q(v) = v^\top M v,
\]
for some symmetric matrix $M$ (we can always symmetrize $M$ because $v^\top M v = v^\top \left( \frac{M+M^\top}{2} \right) v$). Quadratic forms encode geometric objects like conic sections and ellipsoids: the set $\{ v : v^\top M v = 1\}$ is an ellipsoid when $M$ is positive definite.

Important properties of bilinear and quadratic forms are tied to the eigenstructure of their matrix $M$. If $M$ is symmetric, it has a real eigen-decomposition $M = U \Lambda U^\top$, where $U$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda$ is diagonal. Then
\[
v^\top M v = v^\top U \Lambda U^\top v = (U^\top v)^\top \Lambda (U^\top v) = \sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i (u_i^\top v)^2,
\]
where $\lambda_i$ are eigenvalues and $u_i$ are the orthonormal eigenvectors. This decomposition shows that directions associated with large positive eigenvalues are emphasized (contribute more) to the quadratic form, while negative eigenvalues reverse signs and can lead to indefiniteness (the quadratic form taking both positive and negative values). Positive semidefiniteness (all $\lambda_i \ge 0$) is a crucial property when the quadratic form is to be interpreted as a squared length or squared distance, because then $v^\top M v \ge 0$ for all $v$.

Another useful viewpoint is the factorization of positive semidefinite matrices. If $M$ is symmetric positive semidefinite (PSD), then there exists $L \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ with $r = \text{rank}(M)$ such that $M = L^\top L$. Consequently,
\[
v^\top M v = \| L v \|^2.
\]
This shows every PSD quadratic form is a squared Euclidean norm after applying a linear map $L$. This factorized view is central to metric learning: learning $M$ is equivalent to learning an embedding $v \mapsto L v$ under which Euclidean distances are measured.

Symmetry and bilinear forms also appear in similarity computations. The symmetric bilinear form $B(u,v) = u^\top M v$ generalizes the standard dot product $u^\top v$ by inserting $M$ to reweight and correlate coordinates. If $M$ is PSD and one uses $S(u,v) = u^\top M v$, then $S$ is a valid inner product (a positive-definite symmetric bilinear form) and induces a norm $\|v\|_M = \sqrt{v^\top M v}$. This inner product geometry is the foundation for generalized notions of angle, projection, and orthogonality.

# Mahalanobis distance and linear metric learning

The Mahalanobis distance is a natural generalization of Euclidean distance that accounts for scaling and correlation among dimensions. Given a symmetric positive definite matrix $M$, the Mahalanobis distance between vectors $x$ and $y$ is defined as
\[
d_M(x,y) = \sqrt{(x-y)^\top M (x-y)}.
\]
If $M = I$ this reduces to the standard Euclidean distance. The Mahalanobis distance can be interpreted in several complementary ways. First, via factorization $M = L^\top L$, we have
\[
d_M(x,y) = \| L(x-y) \|,
\]
so measuring distance after a linear transformation $L$ that scales and rotates the space. Second, if $M$ is the inverse covariance matrix of a distribution, $M = \Sigma^{-1}$, then $d_M$ measures how unlikely the deviation $x-y$ is under that distribution; this is the classical use of Mahalanobis distance in statistics.

Linear metric learning seeks to learn the matrix $M$ (or its factor $L$) from data so that distances in the learned metric reflect semantic similarity. In supervised metric learning, training data often come in the form of pairs or triplets that express similarity or dissimilarity. For instance, given pairs $(x_i,x_j)$ labeled as similar, and pairs labeled as dissimilar, one objective is to minimize distances for similar pairs and maximize distances for dissimilar pairs. A common formulation is to learn $M \succeq 0$ that minimizes a loss like
\[
\sum_{(i,j)\in S} \ell\big( d_M(x_i, x_j) \big) + \sum_{(i,k)\in D} \ell\big( -d_M(x_i, x_k) \big) + \lambda R(M),
\]
where $S$ is a set of similar pairs, $D$ is dissimilar pairs, $\ell$ is a margin-based loss, and $R(M)$ is a regularizer. Because $M$ must be PSD to ensure nonnegative squared distances, optimization must preserve that constraint; classic algorithms like Large Margin Nearest Neighbor (LMNN) or Information Theoretic Metric Learning (ITML) include PSD constraints or parameterize $M$ via $L$.

Parameterizing via $L$ has advantages: instead of optimizing over $d\times d$ symmetric PSD matrices, one directly optimizes over a matrix $L \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ with $r$ chosen (often $r \ll d$ for dimensionality reduction). The induced metric is $d_M(x,y)=\|Lx-Ly\|$. Optimizing $L$ is convenient for gradient-based learning and integrates smoothly with neural networks. The choice of $r$ controls the rank of $M$ and therefore the dimensionality of the effective embedding space.

Probabilistic interpretations motivate particular objective choices. For example, maximum likelihood estimation under a Gaussian model leads to optimization over $M$ when assuming different covariances for classes. ITML uses a log-determinant Bregman divergence to impose that the learned $M$ stays close to an initial matrix while satisfying distance constraints; this gives both stability and a connection to information geometry.

Crucially, learning $M$ changes the geometry of the space. Directions with large eigenvalues of $M$ become more important for distance computations; learning can suppress noisy directions by assigning them small eigenvalues. This geometric control is why metric learning improves nearest-neighbor classification and retrieval: it reshapes the space so that points from the same class cluster closely under $d_M$, while inter-class distances expand.

# Bilinear similarity models in classification and retrieval

While distances are fundamental, many models work directly with similarity scores. Bilinear similarity models compute a score between two vectors using a bilinear form:
\[
S_M(x,y) = x^\top M y.
\]
This family generalizes the dot product and includes several widely used similarity measures. If $M = I$, then $S_M(x,y) = x^\top y$ is the usual cosine-related similarity (after normalization). When $M$ is symmetric PSD, $S_M$ is a symmetric positive-definite kernel-like similarity. If $M$ is unconstrained, $S_M$ can represent asymmetric relationships, which is useful in modeling directed relations such as query-to-document relevance where the asymmetry matters.

In classification, bilinear models appear in linear classifiers and in metric-based classifiers. Consider a prototype-based classifier where class $c$ is represented by a prototype vector $p_c$. A bilinear classifier scores class $c$ for input $x$ by $s_c(x) = x^\top M p_c$. This is equivalent to a linear classifier if we jointly learn $w_c = M p_c$, but the bilinear view makes explicit that classification depends on the interaction between input features and a learned transformation of prototypes. This perspective clarifies transfer learning and few-shot learning approaches where prototypes are estimated from few examples and $M$ captures a learned similarity structure that generalizes across classes.

In retrieval, bilinear similarity models are natural for scoring query-document pairs. Learning $M$ via supervised signals (clicks, relevance judgments) tailors the similarity to observed preferences. When $M$ is low-rank factorized as $M = A^\top B$ with $A,B \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$, the score becomes $x^\top A^\top B y = (Ax)^\top (By)$, which is the dot product in a shared low-dimensional space after two possibly different linear maps. This factorized bilinear form covers both symmetric embeddings (if $A=B$) and asymmetric embeddings useful in modeling different domains (queries vs documents).

Losses used to train bilinear similarity models include margin-based ranking losses (e.g., hinge loss on differences of scores), logistic losses, and pairwise or listwise losses common in learning-to-rank. Regularization is crucial: without constraints on $M$, trivial solutions that blow up norms can appear. Regularizers may be Frobenius norm penalties on $M$, nuclear norm to promote low rank, or constraints forcing $M$ to be PSD or near-orthogonal, depending on the modeling intent.

Interpreting bilinear models geometrically helps: a bilinear form defines a family of hyperplanes in one variable parameterized by the other. For fixed $y$, the score $x \mapsto x^\top M y$ is linear in $x$ with weight vector $M y$. Hence training may be seen as learning a mapping from example $y$ into classifiers over $x$. This viewpoint is particularly useful in tasks like metric learning for few-shot classification where a learned similarity function compares query and support examples.

# Interpretation of left-multiplying weights as learning a bilinear map

In neural networks, a common operation is left-multiplying an input vector $x$ by a weight matrix $W$ to produce a new representation $Wx$ or a linear score $W x$ before a nonlinearity. When such a weight matrix is shared or used in computing similarities with other vectors, it implements a bilinear map. To see this, consider computing a similarity between input $x$ and another vector $y$ via
\[
S(x,y) = (W x)^\top y = x^\top W^\top y,
\]
which is bilinear in $(x,y)$ with matrix $M = W^\top$. Alternately, if we map both sides, $S(x,y) = (W_1 x)^\top (W_2 y) = x^\top W_1^\top W_2 y$, giving $M = W_1^\top W_2$. Thus left-multiplication by weights followed by dot-product or cross-attention computes bilinear similarity scores.

This equivalence gives intuition for why learned linear layers can implement sophisticated relationships between vectors beyond simple scaling. The weight matrix captures how dimensions of $x$ should be combined and reweighted before interacting with $y$. In attention mechanisms, query, key, and value projections are linear maps $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$: attention scores are typically $QK^\top$ where $Q = X W_Q$ and $K = Y W_K$, so the score between a query vector $x$ and a key vector $y$ is $x^\top W_Q W_K^\top y$, a bilinear form with $M = W_Q W_K^\top$. Learning $W_Q$ and $W_K$ therefore learns a bilinear similarity for attention.

Viewing left-multiplying weights through the bilinear lens clarifies parameter sharing and expressivity. For instance, tying $W_Q = W_K$ enforces symmetric similarity; allowing them to differ permits asymmetry. When $W$ has low rank or block structure, the induced bilinear map inherits that structure and so the model's ability to discriminate along certain directions is constrained. Additionally, using a factorized parameterization $M = A^\top B$ corresponds to implementing two separate left multiplications, one on each side, which can be more parameter-efficient and has computational advantages.

This interpretation also clarifies gradient flow during training. When a loss involves $x^\top M y$ and $M$ factorizes as $W^\top$, backpropagation updates $W$ based on how the bilinear interaction contributes to the loss. The left-multiplication view reveals that changing $W$ reorients the embedding space that participates in the similarity, which is effectively learning a metric or similarity transform.

# Regularization via orthogonality in metric learning

Regularization is essential in metric learning to avoid overfitting, to impose desirable structure (such as invariance or interpretability), and to stabilize optimization. One commonly used form of regularization is orthogonality-related constraints on the embedding transform $L$ (or on factors of $M$). There are several motivations and forms of orthogonality regularization.

First, orthogonality preserves lengths and angles: if $U$ is orthogonal (i.e., $U^\top U = I$), then $\|Ux\| = \|x\|$ for all $x$. Using an orthogonal $L$ avoids introducing arbitrary scaling of distances and maintains isotropy. In metric learning, enforcing $L$ to be orthonormal (or nearly so) prevents degenerate solutions where the model simply scales certain directions to satisfy constraints without learning meaningful structure. When one wants to learn a low-dimensional embedding that preserves as much of the original geometry as possible, imposing $L$ to be a partial isometry (rows orthonormal so that $L L^\top = I_r$) is natural: it yields an orthogonal projection onto an $r$-dimensional subspace.

Second, orthogonality aids optimization by reducing parameter coupling. Orthogonal matrices have well-conditioned Jacobians and often result in more stable gradients. In deep networks, orthogonal initialization and orthogonality constraints on weight matrices help mitigate exploding and vanishing gradients. In the metric learning context, enforcing $W$ to be approximately orthogonal can limit the norm growth of $W$ and reduce the tendency of the metric to overfit by uniformly distributing representational capacity across dimensions.

Third, from a regularization perspective, penalizing deviation from orthogonality is equivalent to encouraging $M$ to be close to a scaled identity in certain senses. For example, adding a penalty $\| L L^\top - I_r \|_F^2$ pushes the rows of $L$ toward orthonormality. If $M = L^\top L$, then $L L^\top = I_r$ implies that $M$ has eigenvalues equal to 1 on the subspace spanned by rows of $L$, which can prevent pathological eigenvalue explosion where large eigenvalues dominate and produce unstable distances.

There are variants of orthogonality regularizers tailored to different goals. One may regularize towards orthogonality of the column space ($L^\top L \approx I_d$) or the row space ($L L^\top \approx I_r$), depending on which dimensions are fewer. When $r<d$, the condition $L L^\top = I_r$ is natural: $L$ is an isometry from $\mathbb{R}^d$ to $\mathbb{R}^r$ onto its image. Another form is to constrain $M$ directly, for instance by penalizing $\|M - I\|_F^2$ to discourage large deviations from Euclidean geometry.

Empirically, imposing orthogonality often improves generalization in metric learning. It prevents collapse (all points mapping to the same vector), promotes uniform use of embedding dimensions, and can yield more discriminative and stable embeddings. There is a trade-off: too strict orthogonality may limit the model's ability to reshape geometry for task-specific discrimination, so practitioners commonly use a softness parameter or weight the orthogonality penalty to balance flexibility and regularity.

# Connections between orthogonal transforms and invariant similarity measures

Orthogonal transforms play a fundamental role in constructing invariant and distance-preserving similarity measures. An orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies $Q^\top Q = I$, so it preserves inner products: for any vectors $x,y$,
\[
(Qx)^\top (Qy) = x^\top Q^\top Q y = x^\top y.
\]
Consequently, an orthogonal transform is an isometry of Euclidean space — it preserves distances, angles, and norms. This property is central for designing invariances in representation learning. If we wish a similarity measure to be invariant to certain transformations (e.g., rotations, permutations, sign flips), mapping inputs by orthogonal matrices corresponding to those transformations leaves inner products and Euclidean distances unchanged.

In practice, orthogonality can implement equivariance or invariance depending on whether the transform is applied to both query and key vectors or only to one. If both sides are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix $Q$, the similarity $x^\top y$ is unchanged. If only one side is transformed, the similarity changes in a controlled way; for instance, $ (Qx)^\top y = x^\top (Q^\top y)$, so left-multiplying by $Q$ is equivalent to right-multiplying by $Q^\top$ on the other vector. This flexibility explains why orthogonal parametrizations are natural when wanting to represent invariances or to change coordinate systems without altering geometric relationships.

The spectral decomposition viewpoint clarifies how orthogonal transforms relate to invariant similarity measures. Consider a bilinear similarity $S(x,y) = x^\top M y$ with symmetric $M$. Decompose $M = U \Lambda U^\top$. Then
\[
S(x,y) = (U^\top x)^\top \Lambda (U^\top y).
\]
The orthogonal matrix $U$ rotates coordinates to the eigenbasis of $M$, in which interactions are weighted by the diagonal $\Lambda$. If we desire invariance to rotations in a subspace, we can set corresponding eigenvalues equal, making the similarity insensitive to rotation inside that subspace. In the extreme, if $\Lambda$ is a scalar multiple of the identity, $M = \lambda I$, the similarity reduces to a scaled dot product and is fully rotation invariant: $x^\top (\lambda I) y = \lambda x^\top y$ depends only on the relative orientation of $x$ and $y$, not on particular coordinates.

Orthogonal transforms are also central to whitening and PCA-based preprocessing, which are common steps in metric learning. Whitening uses $Q = \Sigma^{-1/2}$ (which is symmetric and orthogonal in the whitened coordinates) to map data so that covariance becomes identity; distances in the whitened space correspond to Mahalanobis distances with $M = \Sigma^{-1}$. PCA applies an orthogonal matrix $U$ to align data along principal axes and then may truncate to the top $r$ axes, resulting in a partial isometry that preserves variance along retained directions while discarding the rest. Both procedures illustrate how orthogonal transforms expose natural coordinates for defining meaningful similarity measures.

Finally, orthogonality connects to invariance beyond linear algebra: group actions that are represented by orthogonal matrices (e.g., rotations SO(n), permutations via permutation matrices) lead to similarity measures invariant under those group's action. Building models whose learned metrics commute with such group actions yields representations that respect known symmetries in the data, improving sample efficiency and robustness.

Conclusion

Bilinear forms and quadratic forms provide the mathematical backbone for similarity and distance measures used in representation learning. The Mahalanobis distance, parameterized by a PSD matrix, is especially important because it embodies learned linear transformations of space. Bilinear similarity models generalize dot products to capture complex interactions between vectors, and left-multiplying weight matrices in neural models are direct mechanisms for learning such bilinear maps. Regularization via orthogonality helps control geometry, stabilize optimization, and impose invariances. Finally, orthogonal transforms link learned metrics to notions of invariance and coordinate changes, giving both theoretical clarity and practical guidance for designing and interpreting metric learning systems. Understanding these connections gives you the tools to read metric learning literature, design meaningful similarity functions, and reason about the geometry induced by learned embeddings.
