Introduction

Numerical linear algebra studies how to perform linear-algebraic computations accurately and efficiently on finite-precision machines. In practice, algorithms operate with floating-point arithmetic: numbers are represented with a fixed number of significant digits and operations introduce rounding errors. Understanding how these errors interact with the algebraic structure of matrices is essential for writing reliable scientific code and interpreting results from large-scale computations, including training deep neural networks. This chapter focuses on conditioning and stability: how small perturbations in inputs or intermediate steps can change outputs, why orthogonal and structured matrices often behave better numerically, how rounding errors accumulate in composed operations, and what practical remedies are available when ill-conditioning threatens optimization and generalization. The chapter also examines how spectral properties influence gradient-based optimization in deep models, and gives guidance for composing many sparse factors while maintaining numeric robustness.

# Matrix conditioning and condition number

The conditioning of a computational problem measures how the output changes in response to small changes in the input. For matrix problems, a canonical notion is the condition number of a matrix. For a nonsingular square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, the condition number with respect to the Euclidean (or spectral) norm is defined as
\[
\kappa(A) = \|A\|_2 \, \|A^{-1}\|_2 = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)},
\]
where $\sigma_{\max}(A)$ and $\sigma_{\min}(A)$ are the largest and smallest singular values of $A$. Intuitively, $\kappa(A)$ quantifies how much relative output error can be produced by a relative input error. Suppose we solve a linear system $Ax=b$ and the right-hand side $b$ is perturbed by a small $\delta b$. A first-order perturbation analysis shows
\[
\frac{\| \delta x \|_2}{\|x\|_2} \lesssim \kappa(A) \frac{\| \delta b \|_2}{\|b\|_2},
\]
so a large condition number amplifies relative error.

Condition numbers are not only about linear systems. Many problems—least squares, eigenvalue computations, singular value decompositions—have problem-specific condition measures often expressible in terms of singular values or gap values between eigenvalues. For example, the sensitivity of eigenvectors associated with a repeated or nearly repeated eigenvalue is large: small perturbations can rotate the eigenspace significantly.

It is important to separate conditioning of the mathematical problem from the stability of an algorithm used to solve it. A well-conditioned problem can be solved by many algorithms with little loss of accuracy. An ill-conditioned problem cannot be solved accurately in finite precision, no matter how good the algorithm, because the data itself does not determine the answer precisely. However, a stable algorithm is one whose computed result equals the exact result for a slightly perturbed input, with the perturbation magnitude comparable to machine precision and algorithmic operations. Backward stability is the gold standard: an algorithm is backward stable if the computed result is the exact solution to a nearby problem, where the nearby problem differs from the original by relative amounts on the order of machine epsilon. Even when a problem is well-conditioned, an unstable algorithm may produce large errors beyond what conditioning predicts.

Condition numbers also depend on the chosen norm. While $\kappa_2(A)$ uses the spectral norm, other norms lead to different condition numbers. In the context of neural networks, one often cares about how singular values of the weight matrices influence gradient propagation and training dynamics, so the spectral condition number or distribution of singular values is particularly relevant.

# Stability of orthogonal transforms and their numerical benefits

Orthogonal (or unitary in complex spaces) transforms play a central role in numerical linear algebra. A matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is orthogonal if $Q^\top Q = QQ^\top = I$. Orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean lengths and angles: for any vector $x$, $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$. This property leads to multiple numerical benefits.

First, orthogonal transformations do not change the condition number of a matrix when applied as left or right multipliers in the spectral norm: $\kappa(QA) = \kappa(A)$ and $\kappa(AQ) = \kappa(A)$ because left or right multiplication by $Q$ preserves singular values. Thus, orthogonal pre- and post-transformations do not introduce additional amplification of errors in the spectral sense. This invariance explains why algorithms like QR factorization rely on orthogonal transformations to introduce structure (e.g., triangular form) while avoiding worsening conditioning.

Second, applying an orthogonal matrix in finite precision is relatively stable. Householder reflections and Givens rotations implement orthogonal transforms with high numerical stability: the computed $Q_{\text{comp}}$ is close to exact orthogonal, and the matrix-vector products $Qx$ are backward stable in the sense that the computed result equals $(Q+\delta Q)x$ with $\|\delta Q\|$ on the order of machine precision. The fact that orthogonal transformations preserve norms prevents the artificial growth or shrinking of vector magnitudes solely due to the transformation; any growth in norms must come from the problem data or nonorthogonal operations.

Third, in the context of iterative algorithms and deep networks, orthogonal weight matrices help control the explosion or vanishing of activations and gradients. If all layer-to-layer linear operations are orthogonal, then the norm of signals propagated forward or backward is preserved (ignoring nonlinearities and scaling factors). This reduces the extreme sensitivity of the network to initial conditions and helps training stability.

Despite these advantages, orthogonality is not a panacea. Numerical orthogonality may be lost when orthogonal matrices are multiplied many times in finite precision or when one approximates orthogonality with parameterizations that are only approximately orthogonal. Nevertheless, the central point is that orthogonal transforms minimize the risk of adding numerical amplification and are therefore often preferred when building numerically robust algorithms.

# Rounding error and accumulation in staged matrix products

Computers represent real numbers with finite precision; for IEEE 754 double precision, the machine epsilon is roughly $2^{-53} \approx 1.11\times 10^{-16}$. When we perform arithmetic operations, each operation incurs a small relative error bounded approximately by machine epsilon. In matrix algorithms, many operations are composed, and rounding errors can accumulate, potentially degrading the accuracy of results.

Consider computing the product of two matrices $C = AB$ in floating point arithmetic. The computed product $\widehat{C}$ typically satisfies a model of the form
\[
\widehat{C} = (A + \Delta A)(B + \Delta B),
\]
where $\|\Delta A\|_F$ and $\|\Delta B\|_F$ are bounded by a small multiple of machine precision times the norms of $A$ and $B$. The algorithm is backward stable: the computed product is the exact product of nearby matrices. However, if we compute a product of many matrices in sequence, e.g., $M = A_k A_{k-1} \cdots A_1$, the backward errors of each multiplication can interact. If each multiplication introduces a relative error on the order of $\epsilon$, then after $k$ multiplications one might expect an error that grows like $O(k\epsilon)$ in a first-order model. But more pernicious is the possibility of catastrophic cancellation in subtractive computations or accumulation of error in unstable recurrences, which can lead to growth much larger than linear in $k$.

An important practical case is repeated application of the same matrix, or staged products in deep networks where an activation is repeatedly multiplied by weight matrices during the forward pass and gradients propagate backward through the same matrices. If the weight matrices are poorly conditioned or have singular values far from one, numerical rounding can cause amplification or attenuation of errors at each stage. Even with orthogonal matrices, finite-precision multiplication may cause the product of many orthogonal factors to drift away from exact orthogonality. For example, forming $Q = Q_k \cdots Q_1$ where each $Q_i$ is orthogonal and each multiplication is carried out in floating point will produce a matrix $\widehat{Q}$ that is not exactly orthogonal; the deviation from orthogonality typically grows with $k$.

To mitigate accumulation effects, algorithms are often designed to avoid unnecessary staged products, to combine operations analytically when possible, or to perform periodic reorthogonalization (discussed later). Error analysis techniques frequently use matrix perturbation theory and backward error models to understand and bound the growth of errors across stages. In practice, one should be aware that deep compositions and long recurrence lengths increase the chance that rounding error will materially affect results, especially if the factors are not numerically benign.

# Preservation of spectral norm under orthogonal transforms

One of the most fundamental properties of orthogonal matrices is that they preserve the Euclidean (spectral) norm: for any vector $x$,
\[
\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2.
\]
At the matrix level, left or right multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values. If $A$ has singular values $\sigma_i(A)$, then $QA$ and $AQ$ have the same singular values, so $\|QA\|_2 = \|A\|_2$ and $\kappa(QA) = \kappa(A)$. This invariance is proved by noting that singular values are the square roots of eigenvalues of $A^\top A$, and
\[
(QA)^\top (QA) = A^\top Q^\top Q A = A^\top A,
\]
because $Q^\top Q = I$. Similarly for right multiplication.

The implication is that orthogonal transformations do not distort the spectral geometry of a matrix. This is why orthogonal preconditioning interacts differently from diagonal scaling: orthogonal preconditioning can change basis without changing the intrinsic scaling of the operator, while diagonal scaling rescales different directions and can change condition numbers. In algorithm design, many methods exploit orthogonal transforms to simplify structure (for instance, triangularization via QR) while retaining the spectral properties critical for conditioning.

A practical corollary is that spectral norm-based bounds and inequalities are often invariant under orthogonal change of basis. For example, if you wish to bound $\|Ax\|_2$ in relation to $\|x\|_2$, replacing $A$ by $QA$ or $AQ$ does not affect the maximum amplification factor. This invariance also underpins the stability of algorithms that use orthogonal transformations: they can transform problems into forms more convenient for computation without increasing worst-case amplification of errors.

However, while orthogonal transforms preserve the spectral norm exactly in exact arithmetic, in floating point arithmetic the implemented transforms may deviate slightly from orthogonality. The degree of deviation depends on the method used to construct or represent the orthogonal matrix and on the number of operations. For example, forming an orthogonal matrix as the product of many computed Householder reflectors may introduce orthogonality loss if not performed carefully. Therefore, in practice, orthogonality is preserved to within small multiples of machine precision, and the preservation of spectral norms holds up to the same small perturbations.

# Effects of ill-conditioning on gradient-based optimization

In gradient-based optimization, conditioning affects both the speed of convergence and the behavior of the computed gradients. Consider minimizing a smooth convex quadratic function
\[
f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} x^\top A x - b^\top x,
\]
where $A$ is symmetric positive definite. Gradient descent with fixed step size converges linearly, and the convergence rate depends on the condition number $\kappa(A) = \lambda_{\max}(A) / \lambda_{\min}(A)$. Specifically, with an optimal constant step size the error after $t$ iterations behaves like
\[
\|x_t - x^\star\|_2 \le \left( \frac{\kappa(A)-1}{\kappa(A)+1} \right)^t \|x_0 - x^\star\|_2.
\]
Thus, a large condition number dramatically slows optimization because directions corresponding to small eigenvalues of $A$ make progress very slowly: along those directions the gradient is tiny and the algorithm takes many steps to reduce error.

In nonconvex problems like deep neural networks, the Hessian at a point plays a similar role locally. Ill-conditioning of the Hessian or of the Jacobian of layers can cause slow convergence, stiff dynamics, and sensitivity to initialization. When weight matrices have singular values far from one, forward signals can explode or vanish exponentially across layers, and backward gradients are similarly affected; this leads to vanishing or exploding gradients, which hamper training.

Beyond convergence speed, ill-conditioning can amplify the effect of stochastic noise and rounding error on gradient estimates. In stochastic gradient descent (SGD), gradients are noisy approximations; if the curvature is high in some directions, the noise in the gradient can be amplified into large parameter updates when using naive scaling, leading to unstable training. Ill-conditioning also makes tuning learning rates more delicate: a step size that is safe for well-conditioned directions might be too large for ill-conditioned directions, causing divergence.

Moreover, ill-conditioning can interact with generalization. Recent theoretical and empirical work suggests that the spectral distribution of weight matrices and Jacobians relates to implicit regularization: for instance, overly large singular values can lead to models that are sensitive to small input perturbations, harming robustness. Conversely, certain spectral constraints—such as bounding the spectral norm of layers—can act as a capacity control and improve generalization.

In practice, ameliorating ill-conditioning in optimization involves techniques like preconditioning (transforming the problem so that the effective Hessian has more clustered eigenvalues), adaptive step-size methods (e.g., AdaGrad, Adam) that rescale updates by historical gradient magnitudes, and architectural choices (e.g., normalization layers, skip connections) that help preserve signal magnitudes across depth. When numerical precision is limited, ill-conditioning also increases the risk that computed gradients are dominated by rounding noise, further degrading optimization.

# Techniques to improve numerical stability (rescaling, reorthogonalization)

There are several practical techniques to improve numerical stability. Two frequently used strategies are rescaling and reorthogonalization, but many other approaches (preconditioning, regularization, careful ordering of operations) are also important.

Rescaling involves adjusting the magnitudes of matrices or vectors so that they lie in a numerically safe range. For example, when multiplying many factors, it is often helpful to renormalize intermediate results to avoid overflow or underflow and to keep relative errors small. In the context of neural networks, weight initialization schemes such as Xavier/Glorot and He initialization aim to keep variances of activations roughly constant across layers, preventing signal magnitudes from exploding or vanishing at initialization. More generally, dividing by the square root of the layer width or scaling by appropriate constants is a form of rescaling.

Reorthogonalization restores orthogonality that has been lost due to rounding errors when accumulating or updating orthogonal factors. Classic algorithms in Krylov subspace methods, such as the Arnoldi and Lanczos processes, suffer from loss of orthogonality among the computed basis vectors when run in finite precision. Full reorthogonalization, where each new vector is orthogonalized against all previous vectors with a stable method (like modified Gram-Schmidt performed twice), can restore orthogonality at extra computational cost. Selective or partial reorthogonalization strategies aim to reorthogonalize only when measures indicate significant loss, balancing accuracy and cost. In QR factorizations built from Householder reflections, backward stability is typically guaranteed without reorthogonalization; however, when forming orthogonal matrices as products of many transformations, periodically reorthogonalizing the product may be necessary to maintain numerical orthogonality.

Other numerical techniques include diagonal rescaling (preconditioning) to reduce condition numbers; using algorithms that are backward stable by design (e.g., Householder QR instead of naive Gram-Schmidt); employing higher precision arithmetic selectively in the most sensitive computations; using compensated summation (Kahan summation) to reduce rounding error in scalar dot products; and maintaining algorithms that use orthogonal factors rather than forming full dense products (for instance, representing a sequence of rotations implicitly so that their application preserves numerical stability).

In optimization, techniques like batch normalization, layer normalization, and residual connections act analogously to rescaling: they normalize activations and gradients, mitigating internal covariate shift and enabling larger learning rates. Gradient clipping limits the size of updates, preventing rare large gradients from causing destructive parameter jumps. Combining these algorithmic remedies with careful numerical practices yields more stable computations and better training behavior.

# Practical numeric considerations when composing many sparse factors

Composing many sparse factors arises in various applications: representing structured matrices as products of sparse matrices (e.g., wavelet transforms, fast transforms), factorized linear layers in neural networks (products of sparse or structured factors to mimic dense behavior), and in iterative solvers where preconditioners are expressed as chains of simple sparse operations. Sparse factors help reduce computational cost and storage, but composing many of them raises numerical issues.

First, although each sparse factor may be well-behaved and cheap to apply, their product can become dense or ill-conditioned. Consider a chain of sparse triangular matrices whose product approximates a dense matrix. Even if each factor has small norm or is well-conditioned, the combined effect can amplify numerical errors. When composing factors, it is often preferable to apply them sequentially to vectors rather than explicitly forming the dense product; forming the product explicitly not only loses the sparsity advantage but also increases rounding errors from many multiply-adds.

Second, the order of operations matters. Sparse matrices often allow multiple equivalent ways to compute a result; choosing an order that minimizes intermediate fill-in, reduces subtraction of nearly equal quantities, or keeps intermediate norms moderate improves numerical accuracy. For example, in evaluating a product of many factors applied to a vector, one should avoid reordering that increases condition numbers of intermediate operands. Automatic strategies such as tree-based associativity (pairwise multiplication following a balanced tree) reduce the number of operations applied to any single intermediate and can limit error accumulation compared to left- or right-associative sequential multiplication.

Third, when factors are parameterized (for instance, as learned sparse rotations or scalings), parameter updates can drift into ranges that make the composed operator numerically problematic. Regularization of parameters, explicit constraints (e.g., enforcing orthogonality of factors), or periodic renormalization can maintain numerical health. If factors are intended to be orthogonal, representing them via stable parameterizations (Householder vectors, Givens rotations) and reorthogonalizing when needed helps preserve their desirable properties.

Fourth, sparsity structures can interact poorly with parallel floating-point reductions (sums) on hardware. Parallel reductions can change the associativity of sums and hence alter rounding accumulation; when composing many sparse factors in parallel implementations, one must be mindful of reproducibility and numerical differences introduced by parallel summation orders. Using pairwise summation or compensated algorithms reduces variance due to reduction order.

Finally, when many sparse factors are used to approximate a target operator, monitor the spectral properties of the composition. Computing or estimating extreme singular values and condition numbers gives early warning of ill-conditioning. Fast randomized techniques can estimate spectral norms without forming dense matrices. If the composed operator loses desirable spectral properties (e.g., growth of the largest singular value), incorporate regularization or constraints into the factor updates.

Taken together, composing many sparse factors requires careful algorithm design: apply factors implicitly when possible, choose numerically favorable operation orders, enforce or restore structure when needed, use stable parameterizations, and monitor spectral behavior. These practices preserve both the computational advantages of sparsity and the numerical integrity of the composed operator.

Conclusion

Numerical stability and conditioning are foundational concerns that influence algorithm design,-the interpretation of computed results, and the behavior of optimization procedures, especially in large-scale machine learning systems. Orthogonal transforms offer strong numerical guarantees: preservation of norms, good backward stability properties, and reduced amplification of rounding errors. Yet composing many operations—particularly when factors are not orthogonal or when the problem is ill-conditioned—can lead to significant error accumulation that degrades both numerical accuracy and optimization performance. Practical techniques such as rescaling, reorthogonalization, careful ordering of operations, and monitoring of spectral properties are essential tools. For practitioners working with deep networks and structured matrix factorizations, combining these numerical tactics with architectural and optimization strategies yields more robust training and more reliable computational results.
