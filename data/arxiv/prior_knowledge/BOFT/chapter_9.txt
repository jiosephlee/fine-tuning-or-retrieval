Chapter 9: Graph Theory and Communication/Information Transmission Models

Introduction

Graphs provide a natural language for describing how information moves through systems. Whether we think about physical communication networks, distributed computation, or the internal structure of algorithms such as factorized matrix constructions, the same graph-theoretic concepts—nodes, edges, paths, and connectivity—let us reason formally about what information can reach where, how fast, and at what cost. This chapter introduces those graph concepts and connects them to models of staged information exchange: layered grids and directed acyclic topologies used to model computations, broadcast and multicast structures that capture common communication patterns, and key metrics such as latency and bandwidth that constrain system performance. We emphasize intuition and build toward interpreting matrix multiplication and other linear algebra computations as computation graphs, explaining how sparsity and connectivity limit expressivity and influence design trade-offs.

# Basic graph concepts: nodes, edges, paths, connectivity

A graph is an abstraction consisting of a set of nodes (also called vertices) and a set of edges that connect pairs of nodes. Formally, a graph is a pair $G = (V, E)$ where $V$ is a finite set of nodes and $E \subseteq V \times V$ is a set of edges. If edges are unordered pairs, the graph is undirected; if edges are ordered pairs, the graph is directed. An undirected edge between nodes $u$ and $v$ is written $\{u,v\}$; a directed edge from $u$ to $v$ is written $(u \to v)$.

Edges represent the potential for direct communication or influence between nodes. In modeling computation, nodes often represent processing units, memory locations, or intermediate values, while edges represent data dependencies—what must be available before a node can compute its output.

A path in a graph is a sequence of nodes $(v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k)$ such that for each $i$ there is an edge between $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$ (for directed graphs, the edge must have the correct orientation). The length of a path is the number of edges $k$. Paths capture the notion of transitive communication: even if two nodes are not directly connected, a path indicates that information can flow between them through intermediate nodes.

Connectivity is about whether nodes can reach each other via paths. In an undirected graph, the graph is connected if there is a path between every pair of nodes. In directed graphs there are more nuanced notions: a node $u$ can reach node $v$ if there exists a directed path from $u$ to $v$. Strong connectivity means every node can reach every other node (i.e., for all $u,v$ there exist directed paths $u \to v$ and $v \to u$). Weak connectivity means the underlying undirected graph is connected, even if directions prevent full reachability.

Other local concepts are degree and neighborhood. The degree of a node in an undirected graph is the number of incident edges; in directed graphs we distinguish in-degree (number of incoming edges) and out-degree (number of outgoing edges). The neighborhood of a node is the set of nodes adjacent to it. These local quantities are the simplest measures of how well connected a node is and often appear in constraints on communication capability.

Intuition: imagine a message placed at node $u$. Whether that message can be delivered to node $v$ depends on whether there exists a path from $u$ to $v$; how quickly it can be delivered depends on the path length and the capacities of edges along the path; and how many copies can be delivered concurrently depends on edge capacities and the existence of disjoint paths. Graph structure is thus a concise way to capture complex constraints on information flow.

# Layered or grid graphs and directed acyclic topologies

Layered graphs are graphs where nodes are partitioned into ordered layers $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_{T}$ and edges only go from earlier layers to later layers. Formally, for every edge $(u \to v)$ we require $u \in L_i$ and $v \in L_j$ with $i < j$. When $j = i+1$ for all edges, the graph is said to be levelled or strictly layered. Layered graphs are a concrete way to model staged computation or time steps: layer $L_0$ holds inputs, each intermediate layer $L_t$ corresponds to the state after $t$ stages of computation or communication, and the final layer $L_T$ holds outputs.

A special and widely used type of layered graph is a directed acyclic graph (DAG). A DAG is a directed graph with no directed cycles. Layering is a common topological ordering of a DAG: every DAG admits at least one ordering of its nodes consistent with edge directions, and that ordering can be grouped into layers. In computation models, DAGs represent dependencies: an operation can be executed only after all its predecessors have completed.

Grid graphs are layered graphs where each layer is arranged in a regular array and edges connect nearby positions according to a fixed neighborhood pattern. For example, a two-dimensional grid of size $m \times n$ is a graph whose nodes are coordinates $(i,j)$ and edges typically connect nodes with adjacent coordinates. Layered grids extend this to multiple layers, often modeling spatially structured computation across time. Grid graphs are useful to capture local information exchange: in many physical systems and neural architectures, nodes only exchange with near neighbors, reflecting locality constraints.

Layered and DAG topologies make it easy to reason about causality and information latency. The length of the minimal layer index difference across a path gives a lower bound on the number of stages required for information to propagate. If all edges go from layer $t$ to $t+1$, then any path from layer $0$ to layer $T$ must have at least $T$ edges, so the latency in stages is exactly the layer depth. This strict structure allows straightforward counting of paths, edges, and bottlenecks between layers, which we will use to reason about communication constraints and computational expressivity.

# Broadcast and multicast trees, butterfly networks as communication topologies

Broadcast and multicast are fundamental communication patterns. A broadcast operation is one-to-many: a single source node wants to deliver the same message to many receivers. A multicast is similar but may involve delivering different messages to different groups; here we focus on the tree structures that efficiently accomplish one-to-many transfers.

The canonical broadcast topology is a tree rooted at the source, with edges directed away from the root. In a balanced binary tree, for example, the root sends to two children, each child sends to two grandchildren, and so on. The depth of the tree determines latency: a binary tree of $n$ leaves has depth $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$; thus broadcast can be done in logarithmic time assuming each edge transmission takes one time unit and nodes can forward immediately. Trees minimize redundant transmissions (an edge is used at most once per message), so they are efficient in total edge usage, but they may create bottlenecks at nodes with high degree or at the root's immediate outgoing edges.

Multicast trees generalize broadcast: different subsets of leaves may be interested in different messages, so the tree can be pruned or augmented to optimize delivery for each group. In network design, finding an optimal multicast tree subject to capacity constraints is a classic optimization problem.

Butterfly networks are structured DAGs designed to route many different messages simultaneously with good congestion and dilation properties. A $d$-dimensional butterfly network has $d+1$ layers, each of size $2^d$, and very regular connection patterns. Each input node connects to two nodes in the next layer according to the bits of its index, and this structure ensures that for any permutation of inputs to outputs there exists a set of node-disjoint or edge-disjoint paths with bounded congestion. The butterfly network is a central example in parallel computation and network design because it achieves small depth while keeping degree low and enabling efficient load balancing.

To illustrate, consider a 3-dimensional butterfly with layers $L_0, L_1, L_2, L_3$, each containing $8$ nodes labeled by 3-bit strings. From each node in layer $t$, there are edges to two nodes in layer $t+1$; the choice depends on flipping the $t$-th bit or keeping it, implementing a controlled routing mechanism. The combinatorial structure allows many disjoint paths between sets of inputs and outputs, which is why butterfly networks are used for parallel permutations and collective communication primitives.

Intuitively, broadcast trees are optimal when the same message must reach many targets and when minimizing the number of transmissions matters. Butterfly-like structures are optimal when we need the network to support many different simultaneous communication patterns with low depth and bounded degree. Both structures are examples of how topology tailors information flow properties like latency, bandwidth, and resilience.

# Connectivity requirements for complete transmission between layers

When modeling staged computation, a basic question is: what connectivity between consecutive layers is necessary to guarantee that information from the input layer can reach all nodes in a later layer? Suppose we have a layered DAG with layers $L_0, L_1, \dots, L_T$, and we want every node in $L_T$ to be computable from some subset of nodes in $L_0$. The connectivity requirement depends on whether we need each output to depend on all inputs, or only on some inputs.

A minimal necessary condition for complete transmission of information is that for every cut separating early layers from later layers, there must be enough edge capacity crossing the cut to carry the information. Formally, consider a cut between layers $S = \bigcup_{i=0}^t L_i$ and $V \setminus S$. The set of edges $E(S, V\setminus S)$ that cross the cut determines the maximum amount of information per unit time that can flow from the first $t$ layers to the rest. If the inputs are $n$ independent scalar values, then to transmit all $n$ degrees of freedom across that cut, the edge capacities summing over $E(S, V\setminus S)$ must allow at least $n$ scalar units to pass. In information-theoretic terms, the min-cut capacity bounds the max-flow. If capacity constraints are uniform and each edge carries at most one scalar per time step, then the number of crossing edges must be at least the number of scalars to be moved per time step.

For combinatorial analysis, we often abstract capacity out and ask purely about connectivity: is there a path from each input node to each output node? This is stronger than necessary for transmitting all information if we allow mixing: if nodes can compute linear combinations, then a network can achieve full-rank transmission even when no single input reaches a given output, as long as the set of paths together provides sufficient independence. A classical result is the network coding theorem: in directed acyclic networks, allowing intermediate nodes to encode (i.e., form linear combinations) can achieve multicast capacity equal to the min-cut. Thus the requirement for complete transmission can be relaxed from per-pair paths to cut capacities when coding is allowed.

When coding is not allowed and nodes only forward messages, the existence of vertex- or edge-disjoint paths becomes relevant. If we need to simultaneously transmit $k$ independent messages from $k$ sources to $k$ distinct sinks, Menger's theorem links this to the existence of $k$ pairwise edge- (or vertex-) disjoint paths. For layered networks with limited degree, disjointness imposes strong combinatorial constraints: to have $k$ disjoint paths crossing a layer boundary, that boundary must have at least $k$ nodes (or edges) available.

In layered DAGs designed for computation (e.g., factorized matrix multiplication), the designer must ensure that across every cut corresponding to computational stages there is sufficient connectivity or coding capacity to carry the necessary degrees of freedom. Counting cuts and verifying min-cut capacities are therefore central to proving lower bounds on time or edge usage, or to designing topologies that meet performance targets.

# Counting edges and degrees of freedom under constraints

Counting edges and the available degrees of freedom is a concrete method to analyze what a network can compute or transmit. In many problems we represent information as vectors of dimension $n$. If each edge can carry at most one scalar (or one degree of freedom) per time step, then the total number of independent scalars that can cross a cut in one time step is at most the number of crossing edges. For networks that iterate over stages, the cumulative transfer over multiple time steps is bounded by the sum over cuts weighted by the number of time steps the cut is effective.

Consider a layered network that attempts to compute a linear transformation $y = Ax$ where $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix. Suppose inputs $x$ are placed in layer $L_0$ and outputs $y$ appear in $L_T$. If intermediate nodes are restricted to forwarding or taking simple linear combinations with limited fan-in/fan-out, each edge corresponds to a scalar flow. To realize $m$ independent output scalars that depend on $n$ independent inputs, there must exist enough capacity in each relevant cut. For a network with static edges and single-step transmissions between layers, a necessary condition for full-rank mapping is that for every partition of inputs and outputs induced by a cut, the number of crossing edges be at least the rank needed to connect the portions. Counting edges thus yields lower bounds on required network width or number of parallel channels.

Degrees of freedom here refers to linear independence in the space of messages arriving at a node or layer. Suppose the inputs are $n$ independent variables. At some intermediate layer, node states are linear functions of the inputs. The dimension of the linear span of these states is bounded by both the number of nodes and by the number of distinct incoming edge “channels” used to form them. If there are fewer edges into a layer than $n$, then the layer cannot represent all degrees of freedom of the input; it can only represent a subspace of dimension at most the number of independent channels available. This observation underlies lower bounds in circuit complexity: to compute certain functions, a circuit must have enough wires crossing certain boundaries to carry the necessary information.

Concrete example: suppose we want every output in layer $L_T$ to depend on all $n$ input variables. If the total number of edge-disjoint paths from the input layer to the output layer is at most $r < n$, then at most $r$ independent pieces of information can be carried without coding. Hence, to achieve dependence on all inputs, we need either more paths (increase edges), more time (allow multiple sequential transfers), or coding at intermediate nodes (allow linear combinations to enrich the information content crossing fewer physical edges). Counting edges and path multiplicities is thus a combinatorial tool for proving impossibility results or for calculating minimum resource requirements.

# Graph-theoretic interpretations of matrix multiplication (unrolled computation graph)

Matrix multiplication provides a rich case study for interpreting algebraic operations as graph computations. Consider multiplying an $m \times k$ matrix $A$ by a $k \times n$ matrix $B$ to form $C = AB$, where $C$ is $m \times n$ with entries
$C_{i,j} = \sum_{\ell=1}^k A_{i,\ell} B_{\ell,j}$.
We can represent this computation as a layered DAG: inputs (entries of $A$ and $B$) reside in layer $L_0$, intermediate products $A_{i,\ell} B_{\ell,j}$ or partial sums reside in subsequent layers, and final outputs $C_{i,j}$ are in the final layer. Each multiplication is a node that depends on a pair $(A_{i,\ell}, B_{\ell,j})$, and each addition is a node that sums contributions into the corresponding $C_{i,j}$.

An "unrolled" computation graph explicitly lists the multiplication nodes and the addition reduction tree for each $C_{i,j}$. The structure of this graph makes the data dependencies and communication requirements transparent. For instance, if the computation is arranged so that all multiplications producing $C_{i,j}$ are performed locally and then aggregated, the aggregation tree creates a multicast-like pattern in reverse: many multiplication nodes funnel into a single root (the final sum). If hardware or algorithmic constraints restrict communication (for example, locality or limited fan-in), the shape of these reduction trees and the placement of multiplication nodes determine latency and bandwidth.

Interpreting matrix multiplication graphs is useful for designing factorized matrix constructions. A factorized representation of a matrix or of a matrix-vector multiply can be seen as imposing a sparse computation graph: not every input contributes to every output directly; instead, there are intermediate factors with limited connectivity. For example, a product of three sparse factors $X = U V W$ induces a three-stage layered graph where sparsity of factors limits edges between neighboring layers. Analyzing how these sparsity patterns restrict the set of possible $X$ matrices is equivalent to analyzing which output nodes (entries of $X$ or products applied to vectors) can receive information from which input nodes (entries of original variables).

Moreover, communication lower bounds for matrix multiplication often arise by counting necessary data movements across cuts in the computation graph. Classical I/O lower bounds show that to compute $C = AB$ on a machine with limited fast memory, data must move between fast and slow memory at least a certain number of times. Translating memory transfers into graph edge crossings across a partition between "in-cache" and "out-of-cache" nodes gives a combinatorial counting argument: since each multiplication requires access to two input operands, the number of multiplications that can be done without moving data is limited by how many operands are resident, leading to lower bounds on data movement. The same reasoning extends to distributed settings where network edges are the constrained resources.

# Implications of graph sparsity on information flow and expressivity

Sparsity in a graph—nodes having few incident edges—has immediate implications for the network's ability to transmit and represent information. Sparse connectivity reduces the number of independent channels available between parts of the graph, thereby limiting degrees of freedom and expressivity of computations carried out on the network.

In the context of factorized matrices or layered computation graphs, sparsity corresponds to matrices with many zero entries or factors with low nonzero patterns. If a layer has only $d$ edges incident to it, then the linear span of signals passing through that layer is at most $d$-dimensional (assuming edges carry scalar channels). Consequently, outputs downstream cannot depend on more than $d$ independent linear combinations of upstream inputs. This is the core reason why low-rank or sparse decompositions restrict the class of representable transformations: they compress the available degrees of freedom.

Consider the extreme case of tree-like aggregation with limited branching. If every intermediate node aggregates from at most two children, then the number of distinct linear combinations available at the root grows at most exponentially in tree depth but is still ultimately bounded by structural constraints. Sparse patterns can impose "bottleneck" cuts: small sets of edges whose removal drastically reduces connectivity. These bottlenecks fix the maximum rank or capacity of mappings achievable by the network.

Sparsity also affects robustness and resilience. Dense graphs often admit many disjoint paths between regions, enabling redundancy and fault-tolerance; sparse graphs may have few or unique paths and hence single points of failure. From an expressivity viewpoint in machine learning architectures, sparsity can be a feature (enforcing inductive biases like locality, translation invariance) but also a limitation if the task requires complex global coupling.

Nevertheless, there are subtleties. Sparse graphs can sometimes emulate dense behavior via depth and coding. For example, repeated application of sparse layers (a deep network) can implement transformations that are dense in the input-output mapping, because composition increases expressive power even if each layer is individually sparse. Network coding likewise can convert sparse physical links into effective high-capacity logical channels by mixing messages. But these tricks typically require additional depth, time, or computational overhead; the sparsity still imposes combinatorial lower bounds on resources needed to simulate dense interactions.

In summary, sparsity reduces the instantaneous degrees of freedom and can create bottlenecks that prevent full information propagation unless compensated by additional stages, coding, or higher multiplicity of passes. Understanding these trade-offs is central when designing factorized representations or communication-efficient computation schemes.

# Network design metrics: latency, bandwidth, and redundancy parallels

Designing a communication or computation topology involves making trade-offs among several key metrics: latency, bandwidth, and redundancy (or reliability). Graph theory provides clean ways to formalize and reason about these metrics.

Latency measures how quickly information propagates from sources to sinks. In layered graphs and DAGs, latency is naturally captured by path length or depth. The diameter of a graph, defined as the maximum shortest-path distance between any pair of nodes, gives an upper bound on worst-case latency (under unit per-edge transmission time). In strictly layered topologies where edges go from layer $t$ to $t+1$, latency from $L_0$ to $L_T$ is at least $T$ and possibly larger if edges skip layers unevenly. Minimizing latency often pushes designs toward shallow networks (small depth), which may require higher degrees to maintain connectivity.

Bandwidth captures capacity or throughput: how much information can be moved per unit time between regions. In graph terms, bandwidth is linked to cut capacities and min-cut values. For a pair of node sets $S$ and $T$, the maximum sustainable throughput from $S$ to $T$ is bounded by the size (or capacity) of the minimum cut separating them. Bandwidth also relates to node degrees when edges have unit capacity: a node with high degree can send or receive more parallel streams, increasing aggregate bandwidth. In layered computation graphs, the total number of edges crossing a layer acts as the layer's bandwidth: how many independent scalars can be transmitted in a single stage.

Redundancy (or resilience) measures the extent to which the network can tolerate failures or congestion without losing functionality. Graph-theoretic redundancy is quantified by connectivity measures: edge-connectivity (the minimum number of edges whose removal disconnects the graph) and vertex-connectivity (minimum number of vertices whose removal disconnects). High connectivity means multiple disjoint paths exist between nodes, enabling alternative routing and load balancing. Butterfly networks, for example, have good redundancy properties for permutation routing: even though each node has small degree, the structured multiple paths prevent single-edge failures from crippling many routes.

These metrics interact. Increasing redundancy by adding edges typically improves bandwidth and reduces single points of failure, but may increase cost or per-node degree. Decreasing latency by reducing depth often requires increasing degree or adding long-range edges (which may be expensive or violate locality constraints). Bandwidth can be increased by parallelism (more edges) or by using coding to increase the effective information per physical edge. The design space includes constraints like energy, hardware degree limits, and locality requirements; graph models let us trade these off quantitatively by counting edges, paths, and cuts.

For algorithm designers, these parallels guide choices. If the goal is to perform matrix multiplication distributedly with minimal rounds, using topologies with low depth but larger per-node degree (e.g., broadcast-like or fast direct routing) is appropriate. If node degree must be bounded, then layered or butterfly topologies that leverage multiple stages provide a compromise: low depth relative to simple chains, bounded degree, and sufficient path diversity for throughput.

Concluding remarks

Graph-theoretic models give a powerful vocabulary to reason about staged information exchange. Nodes and edges capture computation and communication resources; layers and DAG structure capture causality and staged operations; broadcast trees and butterfly networks illustrate canonical trade-offs between depth, degree, and throughput. Counting edges and cuts yields rigorous constraints on degrees of freedom and informs lower bounds on what can be computed or transmitted without extra resources. Interpreting algebraic computations like matrix multiplication as unrolled computation graphs clarifies how sparsity and topology together determine expressivity and necessary resources. Finally, latency, bandwidth, and redundancy provide practical lenses to optimize designs for specific applications. Mastery of these graph concepts is essential for reading and contributing to research that blends algorithmic linear algebra, communication complexity, and distributed systems.
