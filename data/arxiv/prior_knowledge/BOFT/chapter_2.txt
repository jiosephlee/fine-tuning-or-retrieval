# Introduction

Orthogonal matrices are central objects in linear algebra with deep geometric, algebraic, and numerical significance. They represent linear maps that preserve length and angles, and they form a compact group under matrix multiplication. In many applications — from classical mechanics to numerical linear algebra to modern machine learning — preserving norms and orthogonality is valuable both for stability and for imposing desirable constraints on parameters. This chapter develops the fundamental definitions and properties of orthogonal matrices, explores their geometric interpretations as rotations and reflections, explains spectral and singular value behavior, presents common parametrizations used in numerical methods, and connects orthogonal matrices to their Lie algebra of skew-symmetric matrices. We also study the Cayley transform as a bridge between skew-symmetric and orthogonal matrices, and discuss practical implications of left-multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix, including consequences for finetuning and reparameterizations.

# Definition of orthogonal matrices and O(n) / SO(n) groups

An $n \times n$ real matrix $Q$ is called orthogonal if its columns (equivalently, rows) form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Algebraically, this is expressed by the condition
\[
Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I_n,
\]
where $Q^\top$ denotes the transpose of $Q$ and $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix. The set of all $n \times n$ real orthogonal matrices is denoted by $O(n)$ and is a group under matrix multiplication; that is, it is closed under multiplication and inversion and contains the identity matrix.

Two important substructures of $O(n)$ are worth highlighting. First, the determinant of an orthogonal matrix must satisfy
\[
\det(Q) = \pm 1,
\]
so $O(n)$ splits into two disjoint subsets according to determinant sign. The subgroup of orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ is denoted $SO(n)$ and is called the special orthogonal group. Matrices in $SO(n)$ represent proper rotations — transformations that preserve orientation. The matrices in $O(n)$ with determinant $-1$ include reflections or rotations composed with a reflection, and they reverse orientation.

Both $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$ are compact Lie groups: they are smooth manifolds with group structure. The compactness follows from the fact that orthogonality constraints put $O(n)$ inside the closed, bounded set of $n \times n$ matrices with operator norm 1. The structure as a manifold will be important later when we discuss parametrizations through exponentials of skew-symmetric matrices and properties of the tangent space at the identity.

# Properties: inverse equals transpose, determinant ±1, preservation of inner products and norms

From the defining relation $Q^\top Q = I$, it follows immediately that the inverse of an orthogonal matrix is its transpose:
\[
Q^{-1} = Q^\top.
\]
This simple identity has many consequences. Computationally, orthogonal matrices are numerically stable to apply and invert because inversion reduces to a transpose operation, which does not amplify floating-point errors in the same way that a generic inversion may. Conceptually, it encodes the idea that an orthogonal transformation is distance-preserving and angle-preserving.

Because $\det(Q^\top Q) = \det(I) = 1$ and $\det(Q^\top) = \det(Q)$, we have $\det(Q)^2 = 1$, hence $\det(Q) = \pm 1$. The sign distinguishes orientation-preserving versus orientation-reversing transformations, as noted in the previous section.

One of the most important features of orthogonal matrices is that they preserve inner products. For any vectors $x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and orthogonal $Q$,
\[
\langle Qx, Qy \rangle = (Qx)^\top (Qy) = x^\top Q^\top Q y = x^\top y = \langle x,y \rangle.
\]
This single identity implies several useful corollaries. In particular, it preserves norms:
\[
\|Qx\|_2^2 = \langle Qx, Qx \rangle = \langle x,x \rangle = \|x\|_2^2,
\]
so orthogonal matrices are isometries of Euclidean space. They also preserve pairwise angles and orthogonality: $x$ is orthogonal to $y$ if and only if $Qx$ is orthogonal to $Qy$.

These preservation properties explain why orthogonal matrices are often used in numerical linear algebra: Householder reflections and Givens rotations are standard building blocks in QR factorization precisely because they eliminate elements of a matrix while preserving Euclidean norms, leading to numerically stable algorithms.

# Geometric interpretation: rotations and reflections

Geometrically, orthogonal transformations are the rigid motions of Euclidean space that fix the origin. In two dimensions, any orthogonal matrix is either a rotation about the origin or a reflection across a line through the origin, depending on its determinant.

Concretely, in $\mathbb{R}^2$ an orthogonal matrix with determinant $+1$ has the form
\[
R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix},
\]
which rotates every vector counterclockwise by angle $\theta$. If the determinant is $-1$, the matrix can be written as a rotation composed with reflection, for example
\[
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{pmatrix},
\]
which reflects across a line through the origin and possibly rotates.

In higher dimensions, the geometric picture generalizes: elements of $SO(n)$ are combinations of rotations in mutually orthogonal 2D planes. Any rotation in $n$ dimensions can be decomposed (not uniquely) into plane rotations acting on coordinate 2-planes; this is formalized by the real Schur or real spectral decomposition for orthogonal matrices. Reflections are linear maps that flip one direction while leaving an $(n-1)$-dimensional hyperplane unchanged; their matrices have determinant $-1$. A general orthogonal matrix with determinant $-1$ can be seen as a reflection combined with a rotation (or vice versa).

It is useful to think of an orthogonal transformation as rigidly moving a rigid body in space without stretching or compressing it, and without translating its center (since the origin is fixed). Because lengths and angles remain unchanged, orthogonal matrices are the natural symmetries of Euclidean geometry and appear in many physical contexts where orientation and distances matter.

# Spectral properties and singular values of orthogonal matrices

Studying eigenvalues and singular values clarifies the algebraic structure of orthogonal matrices. The singular values of a matrix $A$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$. For an orthogonal matrix $Q$,
\[
Q^\top Q = I,
\]
so all eigenvalues of $Q^\top Q$ are equal to $1$, and therefore all singular values of $Q$ are $1$. In matrix terminology, orthogonal matrices are exactly the square matrices with spectral norm equal to 1 and all singular values equal to 1.

Eigenvalues of orthogonal matrices, however, can be complex. If $Q \in O(n)$ is real and $\lambda$ is a (possibly complex) eigenvalue with eigenvector $v \in \mathbb{C}^n$, then from orthogonality we have
\[
\|v\|^2 = \|Qv\|^2 = \|\lambda v\|^2 = |\lambda|^2 \|v\|^2,
\]
so $|\lambda| = 1$. Thus every eigenvalue lies on the complex unit circle. Real eigenvalues must therefore be either $+1$ or $-1$. The $+1$ eigenvalue corresponds to directions fixed by $Q$, while $-1$ corresponds to directions reversed by $Q$.

A canonical real form for orthogonal matrices is given by block-diagonalization using $1 \times 1$ and $2 \times 2$ blocks. Specifically, an orthogonal matrix $Q$ can be orthogonally diagonalized into blocks of the form $[1]$, $[-1]$, or rotation blocks
\[
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.
\]
This expresses the idea that rotations in higher dimensions act as independent rotations in orthogonal 2D subspaces and possibly flip signs in 1D subspaces. Because the rotation blocks have no real eigenvalues unless $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$, many orthogonal matrices have complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs.

The presence of only unit-modulus eigenvalues and all singular values equal to 1 means orthogonal matrices are perfectly conditioned in the sense that they do not amplify or attenuate vector lengths. This property is one reason orthogonal reparameterizations are attractive in learning: they avoid exploding or vanishing norms when applied to weight matrices.

# Common parametrizations: Givens rotations, Householder reflections

Parametrizing orthogonal matrices is both theoretically interesting and practically important for numerical algorithms. Two canonical building blocks are Givens rotations and Householder reflections. Both provide constructive ways to build or manipulate orthogonal matrices with controlled sparsity and numerical stability.

A Givens rotation is an orthogonal matrix that performs a rotation in a selected 2D coordinate plane and acts as the identity on the orthogonal complement. For indices $i < j$ and angle $\theta$, the Givens rotation $G(i,j,\theta)$ is the identity matrix except for the $2 \times 2$ block in rows and columns $i,j$:
\[
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Multiplying by a Givens rotation from the left or right rotates the $(i,j)$ plane of a matrix, and sequences of Givens rotations can zero out selected entries below or above the diagonal. A great advantage of Givens rotations is their locality: each rotation affects only two coordinates, which makes them efficient for introducing sparsity or implementing incremental updates.

Householder reflections are orthogonal matrices that reflect across a hyperplane perpendicular to a chosen vector. Given a nonzero vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the (unnormalized) Householder matrix is
\[
H = I - 2\frac{uu^\top}{u^\top u}.
\]
One can check that $H$ is symmetric ($H^\top = H$), orthogonal ($H^\top H = I$), and has determinant $-1$ (it is a reflection). Householder reflections are widely used to transform a vector into a multiple of a basis vector; for example, in QR factorization, a sequence of Householder reflections can zero out entire columns below the diagonal in a numerically stable way. Compared to Givens rotations, a single Householder reflection can eliminate multiple entries at once, but it is dense in general. Householder reflections are convenient for forming compact representations of orthogonal matrices: a product of $k$ Householder reflections can be stored using the $k$ vectors that define them.

Both Givens rotations and Householder reflections are constructive in the sense that any orthogonal matrix can be expressed as a product of such elementary transformations. For example, QR factorization using Householder reflections expresses an arbitrary matrix as $A = QR$ with $Q$ a product of $n-1$ Householder reflections. Similarly, sequences of Givens rotations can reproduce any orthogonal transformation through appropriate choices of planes and angles. These parametrizations are widely used in numerical linear algebra and are also attractive in machine learning when limited or structured parameterizations of orthogonality are desired.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and the Lie algebra so(n)

The set of skew-symmetric matrices plays the role of the tangent space (Lie algebra) at the identity of the orthogonal group. A matrix $A$ is skew-symmetric if
\[
A^\top = -A.
\]
The set of all $n \times n$ real skew-symmetric matrices is denoted $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, and it forms a real vector space of dimension $n(n-1)/2$. This dimension matches the number of free parameters needed to specify an orthogonal matrix up to connected components, which foreshadows the exponential map linking $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ to $SO(n)$.

Why is $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ the tangent space of $SO(n)$ at the identity? Consider a smooth path $Q(t)$ in $SO(n)$ with $Q(0) = I$. Differentiating the orthogonality condition $Q(t)^\top Q(t) = I$ with respect to $t$ gives
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(Q(t)^\top Q(t)\bigr)\big|_{t=0} = \dot Q(0)^\top I + I^\top \dot Q(0) = \dot Q(0)^\top + \dot Q(0) = 0,
\]
which implies $\dot Q(0)^\top = -\dot Q(0)$, so the velocity $\dot Q(0)$ is skew-symmetric. Conversely, any skew-symmetric matrix generates a curve $Q(t)$ in $SO(n)$ through the matrix exponential, as we explain next.

The Lie bracket on $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the matrix commutator $[A,B] = AB - BA$, and $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is closed under this bracket. This algebraic structure captures the infinitesimal behavior of rotations and underlies the smooth manifold structure of $SO(n)$.

Skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues: if $A$ is real skew-symmetric, then its eigenvalues are of the form $\pm i\theta$ or 0. This is consistent with the fact that exponentials of skew-symmetric matrices produce orthogonal matrices with eigenvalues on the unit circle.

# Cayley transform: mapping skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices (and limitations)

The Cayley transform provides a rational mapping from the space of skew-symmetric matrices to the space of orthogonal matrices with no eigenvalue equal to $-1$. Given a real skew-symmetric matrix $A$ (so $A^\top = -A$) for which $I - A$ is invertible, the Cayley transform is defined as
\[
C(A) = (I - A)^{-1}(I + A).
\]
One can verify that $C(A)$ is orthogonal:
\[
C(A)^\top C(A) = (I + A^\top)(I - A^\top)^{-1}(I - A)^{-1}(I + A) = (I - A)(I - A)^{-1}(I - A)^{-1}(I + A) = I,
\]
using $A^\top = -A$ and some algebraic manipulation. Thus the Cayley transform sends skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices, and it is a bijection between the set of skew-symmetric matrices for which $I - A$ is invertible and the set of orthogonal matrices with no eigenvalue equal to $-1$.

The Cayley transform has several attractive properties. It is rational rather than transcendental, which can be convenient numerically. It also maps zero to the identity and maps neighborhoods of zero in $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ to neighborhoods of the identity in $SO(n)$. One practical advantage is that it gives a parameter-free, differentiable mapping that enforces orthogonality exactly when used as a reparameterization.

However, the Cayley transform has important limitations. It cannot represent orthogonal matrices that have an eigenvalue exactly equal to $-1$, because for such matrices $U$ the matrix $I + A$ would need to be singular when solving for $A$ in $U = (I - A)^{-1}(I + A)$, or equivalently $(I - A)U = (I + A)$ would fail to have a solution with $A$ skew-symmetric. In terms of coverage, the Cayley transform maps $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ onto the connected component of $O(n)$ containing the identity except for those orthogonal matrices possessing eigenvalue $-1$. Since many important orthogonal matrices do have $-1$ eigenvalues (for example, reflections), the Cayley transform is not surjective onto all of $O(n)$ or even all of $SO(n)$ in general.

Because of this limitation, when using the Cayley transform as a parameterization in optimization, one must ensure the optimization path stays within the image of the transform (i.e., avoids orthogonal matrices with eigenvalue $-1$). For many practical purposes, this is not a severe constraint: the set of orthogonal matrices with a $-1$ eigenvalue has measure zero, so randomly initialized parameters will almost surely avoid it; however, numerical or optimization trajectories could reach matrices arbitrarily close to this set, causing ill-conditioning of the inverse $(I - A)^{-1}$.

Another common mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices is the matrix exponential:
\[
\exp(A) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} A^k,
\]
which maps $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ onto the connected component $SO(n)$ (i.e., all orientation-preserving orthogonal matrices) and does not suffer the same exclusion of $-1$ eigenvalues in general. However, computing matrix exponentials can be more expensive than applying a Cayley transform, and controlling the representation of all $SO(n)$ elements might still require careful numerical methods. The exponential map is surjective onto $SO(n)$ for compact groups? In the case of $SO(n)$, the exponential map is surjective onto the connected component of identity for many low dimensions but not always globally surjective for larger $n$; nevertheless, it provides a natural smooth parameterization locally around the identity.

# Implications of left-multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix

In many contexts, especially in machine learning, one considers a weight matrix $W$ that maps input features to hidden representations or outputs. It is common to consider reparameterizations like $\widetilde W = Q W$ for some orthogonal matrix $Q$. Understanding how left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix affects the behavior of the model and training dynamics is important for designing orthogonality-preserving fine-tuning strategies.

Because orthogonal matrices preserve norms and inner products, left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change the singular values of $W$: the singular values of $\widetilde W = QW$ are the same as those of $W$, since
\[
\widetilde W^\top \widetilde W = W^\top Q^\top Q W = W^\top W.
\]
Consequently, operator norm, Frobenius norm, condition number, and other spectral properties that depend on singular values remain unchanged. Geometrically, $Q$ merely rotates (and possibly reflects) the row space of $W$ in the output space while leaving the action on column directions in terms of magnitudes intact. For many learning tasks, where what matters is the relative scaling of outputs or activations, this invariance can be exploited to reparameterize models without changing their functional behavior up to a known transformation.

If a network layer applies $x \mapsto W x$ and then an activation or loss is computed that is invariant to orthogonal transformations on the output side (for example, if a subsequent layer performs another operation that compensates or if the loss depends only on norms), then multiplying $W$ on the left by $Q$ can be functionally neutral. More generally, left-multiplying by $Q$ composes the linear map with a rigid motion in the output space: any downstream processing that is sensitive to coordinate orientation (for example, a loss that compares outputs coordinate-wise to fixed targets) will be affected by $Q$. However, when the rest of the network or the training procedure is adapted accordingly, left-multiplying by an orthogonal $Q$ is a structured way to enforce constraints without altering magnitudes of singular values.

From an optimization standpoint, orthogonal reparameterizations can improve conditioning and regularization. Because orthogonal matrices preserve norms, one can parameterize a factorization $W = QR$ where $Q$ is orthogonal and $R$ has some desirable structure (e.g., triangular or diagonal). This is the idea behind QR-based parameterizations. Optimizing over $Q$ constrained to be orthogonal can be done via parametrizations (Givens, Householder, skew-symmetric exponentials, or Cayley transforms) or by projection methods that orthonormalize $Q$ after each update (e.g., via QR or SVD). These methods ensure that the model does not inadvertently scale parameters in undesirable ways, and they can stabilize gradients by keeping the parameter manifold compact.

In fine-tuning scenarios, people sometimes freeze $W$ and learn an orthogonal adapter $Q$ to left-multiply or right-multiply $W$ to adapt the model. Because $Q$ preserves norms, this can be an efficient way to change directional behavior (rotating the output basis) without changing scale. It limits the capacity of the adapter in a structured way: only rotations and reflections are allowed, which may prevent overfitting and ensures stability.

Finally, because the Jacobian of the mapping $Q \mapsto QW$ is linear in $W$ and orthogonal transformations do not change norms, gradients with respect to $Q$ can have controlled magnitudes. When $Q$ is parameterized via a skew-symmetric matrix $A$ and $Q = \exp(A)$ or $Q = C(A)$ (Cayley), one can compute gradients in the parameter space of $A$ and update $A$ while $Q$ remains orthogonal. This preserves orthogonality exactly (when using the exact exponential or Cayley transform) and allows learning within the compact manifold of orthogonal matrices. The choice of parametrization impacts both computational cost and the coverage of the manifold (e.g., whether determinant $-1$ matrices are reachable), and these choices should be guided by the application.

# Conclusion

Orthogonal matrices are isometries of Euclidean space, forming the compact group $O(n)$ with the connected subgroup $SO(n)$ of rotations. Their algebraic properties — inverse equals transpose, determinant $\pm 1$, singular values all equal to 1, eigenvalues on the unit circle — encode strong geometric constraints: lengths and angles are preserved, and transformations are rigid motions (rotations and reflections). Practical parametrizations like Givens rotations and Householder reflections enable efficient numerical algorithms, while the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ of skew-symmetric matrices provides the infinitesimal viewpoint and a mechanism for smooth parameterizations via exponentials or the Cayley transform. When used as reparameterizations in machine learning, orthogonal multipliers such as left-multiplying a weight matrix preserve singular values and norms, enabling structured, stable adaptations of models. Understanding these properties prepares the reader to reason about orthogonality-preserving techniques used in fine-tuning and other areas where preserving geometric structure is important.
