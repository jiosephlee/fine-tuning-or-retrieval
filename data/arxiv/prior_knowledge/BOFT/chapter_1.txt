Introduction

Linear algebra provides the language and tools for most of the operations that happen inside a machine learning model, especially neural networks. At a basic level, inputs, weights, activations, and gradients are all vectors and matrices; at a more conceptual level, weight matrices are linear maps that transform input-space geometry into output-space geometry. Understanding vector and matrix operations, inner products and norms, orthogonality, eigenstructure, and the singular value decomposition (SVD) is essential to reason about expressivity, stability, optimization, and regularization in neural networks. This chapter develops those core concepts carefully, emphasizing intuition and interpretation for neural-network practitioners. Each section builds on the previous ones, so read in order if this is your first exposure.

# Vectors and matrices: notation and basic operations (transpose, trace, rank)

A vector is a list of numbers arranged as either a column or a row. In this chapter we use column vectors unless otherwise noted: for example $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denotes the column vector with components $x_1,\dots,x_n$. A matrix is a two-dimensional array of numbers: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, with entry $A_{ij}$ in row $i$ and column $j$. Matrices can represent linear maps: the matrix $A$ acts on a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$ to produce $Ax \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

The transpose of a matrix, written $A^{\top}$, flips rows and columns: $(A^{\top})_{ij} = A_{ji}$. Transpose interacts with matrix multiplication by reversing the order: $(AB)^{\top} = B^{\top} A^{\top}$. Transpose is important because symmetric matrices ($A = A^{\top}$) have special properties, and because inner products are often written using transposes, such as $x^{\top} y$ for the standard inner product of two vectors.

The trace of a square matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, denoted $\operatorname{tr}(A)$, is the sum of its diagonal entries: $\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}$. Trace has useful algebraic properties: it is linear, $\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}(A)+\operatorname{tr}(B)$, and cyclic with respect to products, $\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$ (when dimensions permit). In machine learning, trace arises in expressing sums of variances, in expected quadratic forms, and when relating matrix norms to eigenvalues.

Rank measures the dimension of a matrix's range (column space). Formally, the rank of $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, written $\operatorname{rank}(A)$, is the maximum number of linearly independent columns (equivalently, rows) of $A$. A full-rank matrix has rank equal to $\min(m,n)$. Rank captures how many independent directions the linear map $A$ can send input vectors into: if $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$, then $A$ maps the input space onto an at-most $r$-dimensional subspace of the output space. In neural networks, rank of a weight matrix limits the dimension of representable linear transformations and is central when reasoning about expressivity and parameter compression (e.g., low-rank approximations).

# Inner products and norms (L2 norm, Frobenius norm, operator/spectral norm)

The inner product is the basic way to measure similarity between vectors. For two vectors $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ the standard inner product is $x^{\top}y = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$. Inner products induce norms: the standard Euclidean norm (L2 norm) of a vector is $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{x^{\top} x}$. Geometrically, $\|x\|_2$ is the length of vector $x$, and the inner product relates to angle via $x^{\top}y = \|x\|_2 \|y\|_2 \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $x$ and $y$.

For matrices, there are multiple useful norms. The Frobenius norm of $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is defined by
\[
\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^{\top}A)}.
\]
The Frobenius norm is simply the Euclidean norm when the matrix is viewed as a long vector of its entries. It is convenient for regularization (e.g., weight decay is usually an L2 penalty on parameters, equivalent to penalizing the Frobenius norm of a weight matrix).

The operator norm (also called spectral norm) measures how much a matrix can stretch a vector under the Euclidean norm. Formally, the operator norm of $A$ induced by the Euclidean vector norm is
\[
\|A\|_2 = \sup_{x\neq 0} \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} = \max_{\|x\|_2=1} \|Ax\|_2.
\]
This norm equals the largest singular value of $A$ (we will define singular values and the SVD later). Intuitively, $\|A\|_2$ tells you the maximum amplification factor $A$ applies to any unit input: if $\|A\|_2$ is large, $A$ can blow up some inputs, which is significant when considering gradient explosion or stability of forward propagation.

These norms relate to one another. If $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r$ are the singular values of $A$ (with $r=\operatorname{rank}(A)$), then
\[
\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1,
\qquad
\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2}.
\]
Also, $\|A\|_2 \le \|A\|_F \le \sqrt{\operatorname{rank}(A)}\,\|A\|_2$, so Frobenius norm captures total energy while spectral norm captures peak energy.

# Orthogonality and orthonormal bases

Two vectors $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n$ are orthogonal if their inner product is zero: $u^{\top}v = 0$. A set of vectors is orthonormal if every pair is orthogonal and each vector has unit length. An orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a set $\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$ of vectors with $q_i^{\top}q_j = \delta_{ij}$ (Kronecker delta) that spans the whole space. Orthonormal bases are convenient because coordinates with respect to them decompose energy (Parseval's identity): for $x=\sum_i \alpha_i q_i$, we have $\|x\|_2^2 = \sum_i \alpha_i^2$.

A matrix $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ whose columns form an orthonormal basis is an orthogonal matrix, satisfying $Q^{\top}Q = I$ and $Q^{-1} = Q^{\top}$. Orthogonal transformations preserve lengths and angles: for any $x$, $\|Qx\|_2 = \|x\|_2$. In neural networks, orthogonal weight matrices are sometimes used for initialization or regularization to preserve activation variance across layers, which helps with training stability.

Orthogonality also leads to simple projections. Given a subspace spanned by orthonormal columns $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$, the orthogonal projection of $x$ onto that subspace is $QQ^{\top}x$. The matrix $P = QQ^{\top}$ is symmetric ($P^{\top}=P$) and idempotent ($P^2 = P$), and it extracts the component of $x$ that lies in the subspace. Where orthogonality holds, computations simplify a great deal: decomposing a matrix or a linear map along orthonormal bases turns many algebraic problems into coordinate-wise scalar problems.

# Eigenvalues and eigenvectors; diagonalization

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors describe directions in which a linear map acts by simple scaling. For a square matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, a nonzero vector $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is an eigenvector and scalar $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ an eigenvalue if
\[
Av = \lambda v.
\]
The set of eigenvalues is given by the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. If all eigenvalues are real and $A$ admits $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, then $A$ is diagonalizable: there exists an invertible matrix $S$ so that
\[
A = S \Lambda S^{-1},
\]
where $\Lambda$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Diagonalization simplifies powers and exponentials of $A$: $A^k = S \Lambda^k S^{-1}$ and $\exp(A) = S \exp(\Lambda) S^{-1}$.

Diagonalization is particularly clean for symmetric matrices. If $A$ is real symmetric ($A = A^{\top}$), then it has real eigenvalues and admits an orthonormal set of eigenvectors. That is, there exists an orthogonal matrix $Q$ and a real diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ such that
\[
A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}.
\]
This is the spectral theorem. For symmetric $A$, eigenvalues can be interpreted as the amounts by which $A$ stretches orthogonal directions $q_i$; because $Q$ is orthogonal, these directions are decoupled and the action of $A$ is purely scaling in that basis.

In machine learning, eigen-decompositions help analyze quadratic forms, covariance matrices, and second-order structure (Hessian). For example, the eigenvalues of a covariance matrix quantify variance along principal directions; small eigenvalues indicate directions with little variance, which may be eliminated with dimensionality reduction.

# Singular value decomposition (SVD) and its interpretation

The singular value decomposition generalizes diagonalization to arbitrary rectangular matrices. For any matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ there exist orthogonal matrices $U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, and a diagonal matrix $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with nonnegative entries on the diagonal, such that
\[
A = U \Sigma V^{\top}.
\]
The diagonal entries $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r >0$ of $\Sigma$ (with $r=\operatorname{rank}(A)$) are the singular values of $A$. The corresponding columns $u_i$ of $U$ are the left singular vectors, and the columns $v_i$ of $V$ are the right singular vectors.

SVD has a precise geometric interpretation. For a unit vector $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the product $Av$ is some vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$. The singular vectors $v_i$ are orthonormal input directions that are mapped by $A$ into orthonormal output directions $u_i$, scaled by singular values: $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$. Thus $A$ can be viewed as the composition of three operations: rotate (by $V^{\top}$) into the right-singular basis, scale each coordinate by $\Sigma$, then rotate (by $U$) into the output basis. This decomposition reveals how $A$ stretches and compresses different directions, and which input directions get mapped to which output directions.

SVD is central to many tasks in machine learning because it provides the best low-rank approximations of a matrix. The Eckart-Young theorem states that the rank-$k$ matrix closest to $A$ in Frobenius norm is obtained by truncating the SVD to its top $k$ singular values:
\[
A_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top} = U_{:,1:k} \Sigma_{1:k,1:k} V_{:,1:k}^{\top}.
\]
This property underlies principal component analysis (PCA), dimensionality reduction, and low-rank compression of neural network weights.

# Relationships between SVD and spectral norm

SVD directly connects to the spectral (operator) norm and other matrix measures. The largest singular value equals the spectral norm:
\[
\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1.
\]
This follows from the variational characterization $\|A\|_2 = \max_{\|x\|_2=1} \|Ax\|_2$ and the fact that the maximum achieved is $\|Av_1\|_2 = \sigma_1$ for the top right singular vector $v_1$. The smallest nonzero singular value $\sigma_r$ characterizes the minimum amount by which $A$ stretches any vector in the row-space's orthogonal complement; if $A$ is square and invertible, the condition number of $A$ with respect to the Euclidean norm is $\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$, which quantifies numerical sensitivity of solving linear systems and the stability of gradient propagation in deep networks.

There are also exact identities linking the Frobenius norm and singular values: $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2$. Thus, the Frobenius norm measures total squared energy across all singular directions, while the spectral norm measures peak energy in the dominant direction. For matrix regularization and compression, these distinctions matter: penalizing $\|A\|_F^2$ shrinks all singular values, while constraining $\|A\|_2$ only limits the largest singular value and therefore limits maximum amplification.

Because singular vectors diagonalize $A$ up to orthogonal transformations, many inequalities and operator behaviors are most naturally expressed via SVD. For instance, for any unit vectors $x,y$,
\[
|y^{\top} A x| \le \|A\|_2,
\]
with equality when $x=v_1$ and $y=u_1$. This gives a tight sense of how large bilinear forms can be under $A$.

# Column/row spaces, nullspace, and implications for linear layers

The column space (or range) of a matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, denoted $\mathcal{C}(A)$, is the subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ spanned by its columns. The row space is the column space of $A^{\top}$ and lives in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The nullspace (kernel) of $A$ is the set of vectors mapped to zero:
\[
\mathcal{N}(A) = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : Ax = 0\}.
\]
Rank-nullity theorem connects these spaces: for $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$,
\[
\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim \mathcal{N}(A) = n.
\]
Thus the number of degrees of freedom in inputs that vanish under $A$ equals the input dimension minus the rank.

In the context of linear layers in neural networks, a layer implements the map $y = W x + b$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $y\in\mathbb{R}^m$. The column space $\mathcal{C}(W)$ is precisely the set of possible pre-activation outputs (before adding bias) as $x$ ranges over all inputs. If $\operatorname{rank}(W)=r$, then no matter how high-dimensional the input space is, the outputs (without bias) lie in an at-most $r$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$. This limits representational capacity: a linear layer with low rank cannot express arbitrary linear maps between $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$.

The nullspace of $W$ describes directions in input space that the layer ignores. If $x\in\mathcal{N}(W)$ then changes in $x$ along that direction produce zero change in the pre-activations. This is important when thinking about information flow: if certain input directions lie in $\mathcal{N}(W)$, the network cannot distinguish changes along those directions at that layer. During training, gradients cannot recover components that lie entirely in the nullspace unless later layers reintroduce them, because $Wx$ is insensitive to those inputs.

Row space interpretation also matters. Each row of $W$ is the weight vector for one output neuron (if we use the common convention that the $i$th output is $w_i^{\top} x + b_i$ where $w_i^{\top}$ is the $i$th row). The row space is the subspace of input-space directions that the layer can sense linearly. If the row space is low-dimensional, the layer effectively projects input onto that low-dimensional space before further processing.

These linear-algebraic viewpoints explain common practices: bottlenecks (layers with small width) force rank constraints that encourage the network to learn low-dimensional embeddings; low-rank factorization of large weight matrices reduces parameters and imposes capacity limits; skip connections and residual connections can help preserve information that would otherwise fall into a nullspace across many layers.

# Interpretation of neurons as column vectors of weight matrices

It is useful to connect algebraic definitions back to the concrete elements of neural networks: neurons and their weights. Consider a linear layer $y = W x + b$ with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. There are two common ways to view the weights in relation to neurons, depending on whether we treat neurons as rows or columns.

The most common convention in many textbooks and codebases is to view each output neuron $i$ as associated with the $i$th row $w_i^{\top}$ of $W$. Under this view, the pre-activation for neuron $i$ is $y_i = w_i^{\top} x + b_i$, and the row $w_i^{\top}$ is a vector of weights connecting all input features to the $i$th neuron. This interpretation is helpful when you think of each neuron as computing a dot product between the input and a weight template, then applying a nonlinearity. Training adjusts these row vectors so that each neuron responds strongly to certain input patterns.

Alternatively, one can view columns of $W$ as the weight vectors associated with individual input features and how they are distributed across output neurons. If $W = [w^{(1)} \; w^{(2)} \; \dots \; w^{(n)}]$ where $w^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ is the $j$th column, then the linear map can be written as
\[
Wx = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j w^{(j)}.
\]
Under this column interpretation, each input coordinate $x_j$ scales the corresponding column vector $w^{(j)}$, and the output is the linear combination of these column vectors. This view is helpful for understanding how input features are mixed to produce outputs and how sparsity in $x$ or in the columns affects activations. When considering how a perturbation in a single input feature propagates, the corresponding column tells the direction in output space the perturbation will follow.

Both interpretations are useful in practice: rows are natural when thinking per-neuron templates and activations; columns are natural when thinking how input features are mapped and combined. In convolutional layers, the same idea applies to filters and channels: filters act as templates whose inner products with patches produce activations, and channels can be viewed as columns describing how a given input channel contributes across output channels.

Combining this with SVD and rank insights: if $W$ has low rank, its columns lie in a low-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$, meaning the image of any input lives in that subspace. Training can thus reweight columns but cannot escape the subspace spanned by them. Conversely, if the rows lie in a low-dimensional subspace, the layer effectively senses only a low-dimensional projection of inputs.

# Closing remarks

This chapter collected the linear algebra building blocks you will repeatedly see in machine learning papers: how vectors and matrices are manipulated, how inner products and norms quantify similarity and size, why orthogonality and orthonormal bases simplify analysis, and how eigenvalues and SVD reveal the intrinsic action of linear maps. Understanding column/row spaces and nullspaces lets you reason about what linear layers can and cannot represent, and thinking of neurons either as rows or columns of weight matrices provides practical intuition for how information flows through networks. With these concepts in hand you are prepared to read analyses of initialization, expressivity, stability, compression, and optimization that rely heavily on linear-algebraic structure.
