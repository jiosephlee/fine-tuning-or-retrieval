Introduction

Signal processing transforms are mathematical tools that change the representation of data by re-expressing signals in terms of basis functions. In machine learning and statistical prediction, choosing a representation is more than a convenience: it is a way to inject prior assumptions about the data into an algorithm. These priors—called inductive biases—favor some hypotheses over others and are essential for learning from finite data. This chapter develops a signal-processing perspective on inductive biases. We connect classical transforms such as the Fourier transform, the discrete cosine transform (DCT), and the Hadamard transform to the kinds of structure they impose on learning systems. We explain why some transforms compact energy, why sparsity in transform domains matters, how locality versus globality shapes what a model can learn, and how structured linear operators like convolutions and circulant matrices embed strong and useful priors. We also show how fast transforms are used for feature extraction and kernel approximation, and finally we relate transform structure to generalization in learning systems. Throughout, emphasis is on intuition, with mathematical notation used where it clarifies ideas.

# Frequency-domain intuition and basis transforms

A transform changes how we describe a signal by projecting it onto a set of basis functions. Consider a discrete signal $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$. A linear transform can be written as $y = T x$, where $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is invertible and its rows are the analysis basis functions. If $T$ is orthonormal, then $x = T^\top y$ and the transform preserves energy: $\|x\|_2 = \|y\|_2$. The frequency-domain intuition originates from the Fourier transform, whose basis functions are sinusoids of varying frequencies. Projecting a signal onto sinusoids reveals how much of each frequency is present: low-frequency projections capture slowly varying content, and high-frequency projections capture rapid changes or fine detail.

The key idea is that many natural signals are more simply described in a basis other than the canonical one. For instance, an audio waveform that looks complicated in the time domain may concentrate most of its energy in a few Fourier coefficients corresponding to pitch frequencies. Similarly, an image with broad smooth regions and sharp edges will have a representation in a frequency-like basis that places different kinds of content into different coefficients. The transform therefore exposes latent structure: correlations in the original coordinates often become simpler, sometimes diagonalizable, in the transform domain.

Think of a transform as a change of coordinates that aligns axes to directions of variability or structure. Principal component analysis (PCA) is the archetypal example: it finds orthogonal directions (eigenvectors of the covariance) that capture maximal variance. Fourier and related transforms are predefined bases that capture particular types of structure—periodicity, smoothness, piecewise smoothness—without needing data to estimate them. Using such a basis can be seen as asserting that the data are well described by these structural motifs: smooth components, localized bumps, or sparse spikes in frequency.

Mathematically, frequency content is often characterized by differential or shift operators. For a time series, the discrete-time derivative has large magnitude on high-frequency components and small on low-frequency ones. The Fourier basis diagonalizes linear shift-invariant operators: convolution with a fixed kernel becomes pointwise multiplication in the Fourier domain. This diagonalization is why frequency-domain analysis simplifies convolutional systems, filter design, and the study of stability: complicated convolutional mixing becomes independent scalar operations per frequency.

# Sparsity in transform domains and energy compaction (e.g., DCT for images)

One of the most important benefits of transforms is their ability to compact energy—concentrating most of a signal's energy into a relatively small number of coefficients. When most of the energy or information is concentrated in a few components, the representation is called sparse or compressible. Sparsity in a transform domain both enables efficient coding and serves as a strong inductive bias for learning: if we believe the signal has a sparse representation, algorithms that enforce or exploit sparsity will generalize better and require less data.

The discrete cosine transform (DCT) illustrates energy compaction powerfully for natural images. Images typically contain large smooth regions with gradual intensity changes and only limited high-frequency detail. The DCT basis functions are cosines with increasing spatial frequency, and when applied patchwise to images (e.g., 8x8 blocks used in JPEG), most of the energy concentrates in a few low-frequency DCT coefficients. That concentration means one can quantize or discard many high-frequency coefficients with minimal perceived loss in image quality. The DCT is closely related to the Fourier basis but uses symmetric boundary conditions that generally yield better energy compaction for finite, smooth image patches.

Sparsity is not binary; signals can be exactly sparse (only a finite number of nonzero transform coefficients) or compressible (coefficients decay rapidly). Compressibility is often what matters in practice: the sorted magnitudes of coefficients decay, and truncating small coefficients yields good approximations. This property underlies compressed sensing and many denoising methods: if the true signal is sparse or compressible in a known transform basis, one can recover it from fewer measurements or denoise by thresholding transform coefficients. Popular denoising algorithms like wavelet shrinkage rely on the fact that natural images are sparse in wavelet bases: edges are localized, and wavelets capture edge structure with relatively few nonzero coefficients.

From a learning viewpoint, sparsity in a transform domain encodes a prior that the hypothesis class consists of functions with simple structure in that domain. Penalizing the $\ell_1$ norm of transform coefficients or enforcing hard sparsity reduces the effective capacity of the model and biases it toward solutions that are either smooth or composed of a small number of basis elements. This prior is often more appropriate than generic smoothness because it aligns with the typical structure of the data—smooth surfaces, sharp edges, repetitive textures—captured efficiently by transforms such as DCT, wavelets, or learned dictionaries.

# Localized vs global transforms and their inductive biases

Transforms differ in how they localize information in space and frequency. Global transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) or the Hadamard transform, use basis functions that extend across the entire domain: each basis vector has nonzero support over the whole signal. Localized transforms, like wavelets or localized cosine transforms, use basis functions that are concentrated in space (or time) and frequency simultaneously. The degree and nature of localization determine what kinds of structures the transform naturally represents sparsely.

Global transforms favor modeling global patterns and periodicities. The Fourier transform is ideal when the signal is stationary and best described by frequency content that does not vary over time or space. But real-world signals are often non-stationary: an audio recording might have changing pitches and transients; an image has both smooth regions and localized edges. For such signals, a global sinusoidal basis will spread a localized feature across many coefficients (the Gibbs phenomenon), yielding poor localization and less effective sparsity.

Localized transforms like wavelets provide a multi-scale decomposition: they use basis functions that are localized in both space and frequency (e.g., compactly supported wavelets). This localization makes them particularly effective at representing signals with localized singularities, such as edges in images or transients in audio. In a wavelet basis, an edge corresponds to a few significant coefficients at several scales, rather than many nonzero Fourier coefficients. Thus wavelets yield sparser, more interpretable representations for many natural signals.

The inductive biases implied by localization are crucial. A localized transform encodes a belief that important structure is local and often occurs at multiple scales. This encourages models that pool or share information across scales and neighborhoods. In contrast, a global transform imposes a bias toward long-range, global structure—favoring periodicity and stationarity. The right choice depends on the data: for repeated textures and periodic signals, global bases may perform well; for piecewise-smooth images or signals with transient events, localized bases are better.

Hybrid approaches, such as windowed Fourier transforms or short-time Fourier transforms (STFT), attempt to compromise between locality in time and frequency. The STFT uses localized windows but still relies on sinusoids within each window, trading off time and frequency resolution. In machine learning architectures, convolutional neural networks implicitly adopt a localized bias through small receptive fields and weight sharing, which we will make precise in later sections.

# How structured linear operators encode priors for natural data

Structured linear operators are matrices with constraints (Toeplitz, circulant, low-rank, convolutional, sparse, etc.) that reduce degrees of freedom relative to arbitrary dense matrices. These constraints reflect assumptions—priors—about the data. When we restrict learned operators to be structured, we constrain the hypothesis space to functions that respect the structure appropriate to the data.

A simple example is shift invariance. If the data-generating process is translation-invariant (e.g., the appearance of an object in an image does not depend on its absolute position), then enforcing shift-equivariant operations via convolutions is natural. A convolutional layer uses a Toeplitz or doubly-block Toeplitz matrix with repeated parameter sharing across spatial positions. This structure drastically reduces the number of parameters and enforces that the same local filter applies everywhere, encoding the prior that local patterns are useful regardless of location.

Low-rank structure encodes the prior that data lie near a low-dimensional subspace. Principal component analysis imposes a low-rank linear mapping to capture dominant directions of variation. Using low-rank approximations as structured operators forces the model to ignore high-dimensional noise and focus on the principal components that explain most variance. Similarly, structured sparsity constraints (e.g., group sparsity) encode beliefs about which groups of features interact or which bases are relevant, enabling interpretable and data-efficient models.

Another perspective is that structured operators correspond to choosing a particular transform or dictionary. For example, choosing a basis formed by eigenvectors of a shift operator or Laplacian corresponds to assuming smoothness with respect to that operator. Graph Laplacian eigenvectors provide a transform tuned to graph-structured data, encoding a prior of smoothness across graph edges. The operator structure thus both determines the computational complexity (through fast algorithms) and the inductive bias.

From a Bayesian viewpoint, structured linear operators relate to prior distributions on functions. A Gaussian process with a particular covariance kernel implicitly defines a transform (Mercer decomposition) that diagonalizes the covariance. Choosing a kernel imposes a prior favoring functions that are smooth, periodic, or otherwise structured. Likewise, restricting linear predictors to particular structured forms is equivalent to specifying a prior over parameter matrices that concentrates mass on those structures.

# Examples: convolution as structured linear map, circulant matrices

Convolution is a central example of a structured linear map. A 1-D discrete convolution of a signal $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with a kernel $h \in \mathbb{R}^k$ can be written as $y = H x$, where $H$ has a Toeplitz structure: each row is a shifted version of the kernel. If we assume circular boundary conditions, $H$ becomes a circulant matrix. A circulant matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is defined by its first column $c$, and every subsequent column is a cyclic shift of that column. The key algebraic property of circulant matrices is that they are diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform: $C = F^\ast \Lambda F$, where $F$ is the unitary DFT matrix and $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix whose entries are the DFT of $c$. This means convolution becomes elementwise multiplication in the Fourier domain, which is both computationally and analytically convenient.

The structured parameter sharing in convolutions drastically reduces the number of learnable parameters. Instead of learning $n^2$ entries of a dense matrix, a convolutional kernel of width $k$ uses only $k$ parameters per channel pair. Beyond parameter efficiency, the shared local filters impose the inductive bias of translation equivariance and locality: the filter responds the same way to a local pattern regardless of position. This bias matches many image and audio tasks, where patterns like edges or harmonic structures are useful everywhere.

Circulant matrices are also useful in randomized linear algebra. Random circulant projections can approximate dense random matrices while allowing fast multiplication via FFTs: multiplying by a circulant matrix costs $O(n \log n)$ rather than $O(n^2)$. This property is exploited in algorithms for dimensionality reduction and sketching, where structured randomness gives computationally efficient approximate embeddings.

More generally, linear operators with Toeplitz or block-Toeplitz structure capture linear shift-invariant systems in higher dimensions. In learning, enforcing such structure yields models that are stable under shifts and that share statistical strength across positions. The mathematical convenience of diagonalization by transforms (e.g., Fourier) makes analysis of stability, spectrum, and inverse problems tractable.

# Use of fast transforms in feature extraction and kernel approximation

Fast transforms are computationally efficient algorithms for applying structured linear operators. The FFT (fast Fourier transform) computes the DFT in $O(n \log n)$ time, the fast Hadamard transform computes multiplications by a Hadamard matrix in $O(n \log n)$ time, and similarly efficient algorithms exist for wavelets and DCT. In machine learning pipelines these transforms serve both as feature extractors and as building blocks for approximating kernel methods.

Feature extraction: Many classical feature extractors are simply transform coefficients or statistics derived from them. In audio, Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) are computed from windowed Fourier magnitudes and summarize spectral envelope information. In images, blockwise DCT coefficients or wavelet coefficients are used as compact descriptors. Fast transforms make these features practical at scale and enable real-time processing.

Kernel approximation: Kernel methods rely on inner products in a high-dimensional feature space. Random Fourier features provide an approximation to shift-invariant kernels (like the Gaussian kernel) by sampling frequencies from the kernel's spectral density and computing random cosine features. The original approach uses dense random matrices to project inputs into the feature space. Replacing dense random projections with structured random matrices—such as products of diagonal random sign matrices and Hadamard matrices, or random circulant matrices—yields approximate random features that can be computed with $O(n \log n)$ complexity while preserving approximation quality. These structured random features are sometimes called fastfood or structured random features. They reduce both computation and storage while maintaining the kernel approximation properties necessary for downstream learning.

Fast transforms also accelerate convolutional operations with large kernels using FFT-based convolution. In deep learning for large receptive fields or in signal processing pipelines, computing convolutions via FFT multiplication is often asymptotically faster than direct spatial convolution. Using transform-domain operations further allows filtering, deconvolution, and spectral regularization to be implemented efficiently.

Crucially, using fast transforms often brings additional implicit biases. For example, random Hadamard-based projections have different distributional properties than Gaussian projections: they are structured and balanced, and for some data they lead to better conditioning and numerical stability. Hence, computational shortcuts introduced via fast transforms can also change the inductive bias of the system in subtle ways.

# Relation between transform structure and model generalization

The structure of transforms used in a model influences generalization by constraining the hypothesis space and by interacting with data geometry. When a transform aligns well with the true generative structure of the data, it concentrates relevant information into fewer parameters or features and reduces variance in estimation. Conversely, a mismatch between transform and data can scatter signal across many components, increasing the sample complexity required to learn.

From a statistical learning viewpoint, imposing a transform-based prior reduces model complexity. Consider learning a linear predictor after transforming inputs by $T$: we fit $w$ to predict from $y = T x$. If $T$ is chosen so that the target function depends primarily on a few coordinates of $y$, then we can regularize or select those coordinates and achieve sample-efficient learning. For example, if images are sparse in the DCT domain and the target depends largely on low-frequency coefficients, learning in the DCT domain with weight decay or sparsity constraints yields faster convergence and better generalization than learning in the pixel domain.

Structure also affects inductive bias through parameter tying and locality. Convolutional networks generalize well on image tasks not only because they have fewer parameters but because the convolutional architecture encodes translation equivariance and locality—properties of natural images. These priors reduce the effective hypothesis class to functions that are plausible for images, thereby improving generalization. Similarly, wavelet-based features that capture multi-scale localization bias a model toward representing edges and textures compactly, which aligns with how image content is structured.

Spectral properties of transforms relate directly to stability and inductive smoothing. Many regularizers can be expressed as penalties on transform coefficients. For example, Tikhonov regularization with a smoothness operator penalizes high-frequency components: if $L$ is a discrete Laplacian and diagonalizes in an appropriate basis, then penalizing $\|Lx\|_2^2$ suppresses components associated with large eigenvalues (high-frequency modes). This gives a frequency-dependent bias toward smooth solutions. Understanding the spectrum of structured operators enables precise control of where the model will prefer to put mass—low-frequency, localized, or particular graph-frequency components—thus shaping generalization.

There is also an implicit regularization effect from optimization and parameterization. Overparameterized neural networks with convolutions often learn solutions that are smoother or aligned with the architecture's inductive bias, even without explicit regularization. Parameter sharing and locality constrain the set of possible solutions that stochastic gradient descent traverses, making certain low-complexity functions more reachable.

Finally, computational structure can enable learning algorithms to scale to sizes where statistical benefits appear. Fast transforms permit using richer feature maps or larger datasets without prohibitive cost, which indirectly improves generalization by enabling better approximation of complex functions given ample data. However, care must be taken: using structured random features introduces bias in the approximation that can affect downstream generalization, so the trade-off between computational efficiency and inductive appropriateness must be considered.

Conclusion

Transforms sit at the intersection of signal processing and statistical learning. They are not mere computational tricks: the choice of transform encodes priors about smoothness, locality, sparsity, periodicity, and invariance. Understanding the spectrum, locality properties, and sparsity behavior of transforms such as Fourier, DCT, wavelets, circulant and Hadamard bases clarifies why particular architectures and feature extraction methods succeed on natural data. Structured linear operators like convolutions or circulant matrices both reduce parameter count and impose meaningful inductive biases that align with many real-world signals. Fast transforms make these biases computationally practical and enable efficient kernel approximations and feature computations. Ultimately, the match between transform structure and data geometry governs how well a learning model can generalize from limited data, making transform design and selection a core concern in principled machine learning and signal processing.
