Introduction

Empirical evaluation is central to understanding how structured parameterizations — such as low-rank factors, sparse masks, block-diagonal decompositions, or multi-stage factorizations — trade off parameter count, compute, and approximation quality. For researchers and practitioners aiming to measure expressivity of these parameterizations, careful experimental design is required: the choice of target matrices, error metrics, ablation controls, statistical protocol, and visualization all influence the conclusions. This chapter provides a practical, methodological guide to designing matrix approximation experiments, measuring approximation error, running ablations that isolate the effects of architecture choices, and interpreting results in the context of generalization. Throughout, the focus is on reproducible experiments that yield actionable insight into when and why a structured parameterization can approximate a dense transformation well.

# Designing matrix approximation experiments (random orthogonal targets, error metrics)

The simplest controlled setting to probe expressivity is matrix approximation: given a dense target matrix $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$, how well can a structured parameterization produce a matrix $\widehat{T}$ using a constrained number of parameters or operations? Matrix approximation experiments decouple issues of data, optimization, and generalization by focusing purely on representational capability and approximation error under idealized recovery.

A common choice for targets is random orthogonal (or orthonormal) matrices. For square matrices ($n=m$), a uniformly random orthogonal matrix sampled from the Haar measure provides a maximally "generic" dense linear operator that has full rank and evenly distributed singular values (all equal to 1). For rectangular matrices, one can sample $T = U \Sigma V^\top$ with $U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}$ having orthonormal columns and $\Sigma$ a diagonal matrix of singular values chosen to reflect decay patterns you expect in practice. Random orthogonal targets are useful because they avoid special structure that could bias an evaluation in favor of a particular parameterization.

However, not all practical matrices behave like random orthogonals. To probe different regimes, include targets with controlled singular value spectra: for example, low effective rank matrices with fast-decaying singular values, or matrices with a heavy-tailed spectrum. Constructing $T = U \, \mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_r) \, V^\top$ with chosen singular values $s_i$ lets you test whether a parameterization captures principal components well or struggles with finer-scale detail. Also include structured targets such as circulant, banded, or sparse matrices when those are relevant to your application.

Design the experiment to vary the key resource budgets: number of free parameters, number of nonzero weights, multiplication count (FLOPs) or depth of composition (multiple stages). For each budget, optimize the structured parameterization to approximate $T$. Optimization can be direct least squares (analytical when possible), gradient-based minimization of $\lVert \widehat{T} - T \rVert$ under a chosen norm, or by fitting on random inputs (see next sections). When optimization is non-convex (e.g., factorized products), run multiple restarts and record best and typical outcomes.

Lastly, always report how the target matrices are sampled and include a range of target types and sizes. A single target instance can be misleading; use ensembles of targets to summarize typical behavior.

# Measuring approximation error: Frobenius norm, spectral norm, angle-based metrics

Choosing error metrics is crucial because different norms emphasize different failure modes. The two most common matrix norms used in approximation studies are the Frobenius norm and the spectral norm.

The Frobenius norm of the error matrix is
\[
\lVert \widehat{T} - T \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} (\widehat{T}_{ij} - T_{ij})^2}.
\]
This norm treats each matrix entry equally and is equivalent to the Euclidean norm of the flattened matrix. It is sensitive to total squared error and is convenient analytically: when approximating $T$ by a rank-$k$ matrix, the Eckart–Young theorem gives the best Frobenius approximation via truncated SVD. Use the Frobenius norm when you care about average-case reconstruction quality across many input directions.

The spectral norm (operator 2-norm) is
\[
\lVert \widehat{T} - T \rVert_2 = \max_{\lVert x\rVert_2=1} \lVert (\widehat{T} - T) x\rVert_2,
\]
equal to the largest singular value of the error. It captures worst-case amplification of inputs and is meaningful when a single large direction of error dominates performance. The spectral norm is stricter than the Frobenius norm: $\lVert A\rVert_2 \le \lVert A\rVert_F$ with equality only when the matrix has rank one.

Angle-based metrics examine alignment of subspaces rather than raw magnitude. If you care whether a parameterization captures the leading subspace of $T$ (i.e., the top-$k$ singular vectors), principal angles are appropriate. Given column spaces spanned by $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$ (true) and $\widehat{U}$ (approximate), compute singular values of $U^\top \widehat{U}$; the principal angles $\theta_i$ satisfy $\cos\theta_i$ as these singular values. The largest principal angle indicates worst misalignment of the captured subspace; the average or squared sines of principal angles quantify subspace distance.

Which metric to use depends on downstream aims. If the matrix will be applied to random inputs, Frobenius error averaged over random unit vectors corresponds to expected squared error: since for isotropic input $x$, $\mathbb{E}\lVert (\widehat{T}-T)x\rVert_2^2 = \frac{1}{n}\lVert \widehat{T}-T\rVert_F^2$ up to constants, Frobenius ties to average-case behavior. If worst-case stability or Lipschitz properties matter, use spectral norm. Angle metrics are essential when principal directions are the signal-carrying components (e.g., low-rank structure). In practice, report multiple metrics to provide a fuller picture.

Also consider relative error metrics: normalize by the norm of $T$ so values are interpretable across sizes:
\[
\mathrm{rel\_err}_F = \frac{\lVert \widehat{T}-T\rVert_F}{\lVert T\rVert_F},\qquad
\mathrm{rel\_err}_2 = \frac{\lVert \widehat{T}-T\rVert_2}{\lVert T\rVert_2}.
\]
Relative error communicates fraction of signal preserved. For angle metrics, report $\sin\theta$ or $\cos\theta$ to indicate closeness.

# Ablation studies: controlling parameter counts, block sizes, and stages

Ablations are controlled experiments where one variable is changed while others are held constant to isolate its effect. For structured parameterizations, common axes of variation are total parameter count, block size (in block-diagonal or block-sparse designs), number of stages (sequential composition depth), and degree of sparsity or rank. A careful ablation controls for confounders so that observed performance differences can be attributed to the variable of interest.

Controlling parameter counts is critical: comparing a full dense matrix with $nm$ parameters to a low-rank factorization with $k(n+m)$ parameters is only meaningful when you state $k$. When varying block size or depth, adjust the other dimensions so that the parameter budgets are equal where appropriate. For instance, to compare a block-diagonal matrix with $b$ blocks to a low-rank factorization at the same parameter count, compute parameters precisely and match budgets in separate experiments. Where exact matching is impossible, run sweeps over parameter budgets and report performance curves as a function of parameter count.

Block size affects both expressivity and computational locality. Small blocks enforce independence between coordinate groups, which can dramatically reduce approximation power for matrices with global interactions. Conversely, large blocks approach dense behavior. When evaluating block-diagonal designs, vary block sizes and plot error vs block size for fixed parameter counts to reveal trade-offs. Similarly, for stage-based parameterizations (e.g., product of several structured matrices), vary the number of stages. More stages often increase expressivity because composition can generate complex linear maps, but they also increase depth and possibly optimization difficulty.

Another useful ablation is the location where structure is applied. For example, in transformer fine-tuning, small adapters can be inserted in different layers or the same adapter repeated across layers. Hold the per-layer parameter budget fixed and vary which layers receive structure to assess whether expressivity depends more on depth or width. When possible, isolate structural properties (rank vs sparsity) by constructing hybrid baselines: a sparse low-rank model or a block-sparse product-of-factors model. These hybrids help disentangle which structural constraint drives performance.

When performing ablations, run multiple repeats per configuration to account for optimization variance, and visualize performance surfaces rather than just single numbers. Present curves of error vs parameter count for each ablation axis, and include confidence intervals.

# Statistical considerations: repeatability, random seeds, confidence intervals

Empirical claims must be supported by statistically sound protocols. Non-convex optimizations and random target sampling make single-run results noisy. To ensure repeatability and to quantify uncertainty, follow best practices.

First, fix and report random seeds for all sources of randomness: target matrix generation, initialization of parameters, random mini-batches if trained on data, and algorithmic nondeterminism where possible. But do not overinterpret single-seed results; instead, run multiple seeds (10 or more when feasible) to estimate variability. For small experiments where running many seeds is cheap, use 30 or more to obtain stable estimates.

Report means and confidence intervals (CIs). For metric values that are approximately Gaussian across seeds, a 95% CI for the mean can be computed as
\[
\bar{x} \pm t_{0.975,\,\nu} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},
\]
where $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $s$ the sample standard deviation, $n$ the number of seeds, and $t_{0.975,\,\nu}$ the appropriate Student's $t$ quantile with $\nu=n-1$. For bounded or skewed metrics (e.g., angles near 0 or 90 degrees), consider bootstrap confidence intervals that do not rely on normality assumptions. Report both the mean and CI in tables and plots, and avoid overclaiming differences that are within noise.

When comparing two methods, perform statistical tests to check whether observed differences are significant. Paired tests are appropriate when the same target instances and seeds are reused between methods; an unpaired test is appropriate otherwise. Use nonparametric tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) if metric distributions are non-Gaussian or sample sizes are small.

Document the optimization protocol in full: number of epochs or iterations, learning rates and schedules, weight decay, optimizer type, initialization scheme. These factors can interact with structure in subtle ways. If possible, run hyperparameter sweeps and report best-per-method results under a fixed tuning budget. If hyperparameter tuning is asymmetric between methods, report both tuned and untuned results.

# Visualization of sparsity patterns and information flow

Visualizations convey structure that scalar metrics hide. For matrix approximations, plot sparsity patterns (nonzero entries) as heatmaps or binary masks to reveal locality, block structure, or repeated patterns. For dense approximations, display the magnitude of entries on a logarithmic color scale to show scale variation and reveal whether approximations concentrate energy in certain rows or columns.

Plot singular value spectra of the target and approximation side by side. Spectra show whether a parameterization captures leading singular values and whether it underestimates or overestimates tail energy. Cumulative energy plots (fraction of Frobenius norm explained by top-$k$ singular values) are useful to summarize how quickly the approximation loses signal with budget.

When studying multi-stage compositions (products of matrices), visualize "information flow": for a fixed input basis vector, plot how its representation evolves after each stage. This can be shown by displaying the row or column activation patterns after each composition. Sankey-like diagrams can indicate how mass moves between coordinates across stages, showing whether the composition mixes well or remains localized.

For angle-based analyses, visualize principal angles with a bar plot of $\cos\theta_i$ or $\sin\theta_i$ across modes. Also show histograms of error across input directions by sampling many random unit vectors $x$ and plotting the distribution of $\lVert (\widehat{T}-T)x\rVert_2$. These distributions indicate whether approximation errors are concentrated in a few directions or spread uniformly.

When presenting visualizations in papers, include multiple target instances and annotate plots with parameter budgets and seeds to help interpretation.

# Benchmarks and baselines for PEFT comparisons

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA (low-rank adapters), adapters (small added layers), BitFit (bias-only tuning), or pruning-based methods require fair benchmarks and baselines for comparison. Use diverse baselines: full fine-tuning (if affordable), random feature baselines (same parameter count but random frozen weights with trivial trainable scaling), and simple parameterized baselines like diagonal or block-diagonal adjustments. Baselines should match parameter budgets and, ideally, compute cost.

For language or vision tasks, evaluate both onheld-out test sets and on synthetic matrix-approximation tasks that isolate representation power. Benchmark across model sizes: small, medium, and large models might interact differently with parameterization; scaling laws can change expressivity outcomes. Where the goal is transfer learning, evaluate on multiple downstream tasks to assess generalization of learned updates.

When comparing methods, ensure that training protocols are equivalent: same optimizer, same number of update steps, same data augmentation, and same learning-rate tuning effort per method. If one method requires more tuning to succeed, document and, if possible, quantify tuning cost.

Establish simple baselines that expose trivial failure modes: e.g., "no training" (zero-shot), "random initialization and train small head only", or "only train a single last-layer matrix". These help interpret whether gains are due to structure or merely added capacity.

# Interpreting results: expressivity vs generalization trade-offs

Expressivity measures the ability of a parameterization to approximate target matrices when optimized on the target (training-time expressivity). Generalization concerns how well the optimized parameterization performs on unseen inputs or tasks. High expressivity does not guarantee good generalization: a very flexible parameterization may overfit specific inputs or noise, especially when training data is limited.

When you observe lower approximation error for a structured parameterization at equal parameter count, consider two interpretations. First, the structure might be inductively biased toward matrices similar to your targets (e.g., low-rank targets). Second, the optimization landscape given the structure might be easier to traverse, enabling better recovery. Disentangling these requires additional experiments: test on multiple target distributions (random orthogonal vs low-rank), evaluate optimization trajectories, and measure generalization by applying the approximate matrix to held-out input distributions.

Trade-offs often appear as a Pareto frontier: for a given parameter budget, increasing expressivity (e.g., by adding parameters or stages) can reduce training error but increase variance and decrease robustness. Conversely, constrained parameterizations may generalize better on small-data problems due to stronger inductive bias. Report both training-time approximation error and generalization metrics (e.g., downstream test loss or error on held-out inputs) to capture this duality.

Also consider computational and memory trade-offs: a parameterization that uses fewer parameters but increases runtime (e.g., deep composition) may be impractical. Interpret results in the context of deployment constraints and training/serving budgets.

Finally, be cautious about extrapolating from matrix-approximation experiments to behavior in full models. While matrix experiments isolate representational capacity, real networks include nonlinearities, data distributions, and optimization dynamics that interact with parameterization structure. Use matrix results to form hypotheses and then validate them in end-to-end experiments.

Conclusion

Careful experimental design and measurement are essential when evaluating structured parameterizations. Use a variety of target matrices, multiple error metrics, rigorous ablations that control parameter counts and other confounders, and statistically sound protocols. Visualizations and well-chosen baselines illuminate the sources of improvement, while a nuanced interpretation distinguishes expressivity from generalization. Following the practices in this chapter will produce reproducible, interpretable experiments that advance understanding of when and why parameter-efficient designs work.
