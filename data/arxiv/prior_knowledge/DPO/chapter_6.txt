Introduction

Fine‑tuning pretrained language models (LMs) has become a central step in adapting large, general models to specific tasks or to follow human instructions. In this chapter we walk through supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) and instruction tuning in detail: the learning objectives used, how to collect and structure datasets, practical pipelines for training and deploying fine‑tuned models, and how to evaluate whether a model has genuinely learned to generalize to new instructions. We also cover initialization strategies and continual fine‑tuning patterns that practitioners use to preserve and build capabilities, and we discuss common pitfalls such as overfitting to surface format or suffering distribution shift when the deployment data differ from the fine‑tuning data.

The goal is not only to explain how to run fine‑tuning technically, but also to provide intuition for why particular choices (objectives, dataset formats, initialization) succeed or fail, and how to read research papers that report instruction‑tuning experiments.

# Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) objectives and data collection

Supervised fine‑tuning means taking a pretrained LM and continuing to optimize its parameters on a labeled dataset of input–output pairs that capture the task(s) you want the model to perform. The most common objective is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) using teacher forcing: given an input context $x$ and a desired target output token sequence $y = (y_1,\dots,y_T)$, you minimize the negative log‑likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}(x,y;\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
\]
where $p_\theta$ is the conditional token distribution produced by the model with parameters $\theta$, and $y_{<t}$ denotes the previously generated gold tokens. This objective matches the pretraining autoregressive objective except that the conditional context includes a task‑specific input. It directly encourages the model to place high probability on the ground‑truth outputs conditioned on the provided inputs.

The MLE objective is simple and stable, and it is sample‑efficient when your dataset contains many high‑quality examples. It is also straightforward to combine examples from multiple tasks by concatenating them into a single dataset and interleaving examples during training. However, MLE has limitations: it only teaches the model to reproduce the training outputs, not to weigh different outputs according to human preference or to produce helpful conversational behavior. Those limitations motivate further steps such as preference learning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which can be applied after SFT.

Data collection for SFT is one of the most important practical considerations. Training data can come from multiple sources:

- Human‑annotated datasets where labelers produce target outputs in response to prompts. This is often the highest quality but the most expensive route. Annotation schemes must be carefully designed so labelers understand scope, desired style, and failure modes.
- Synthetic data generated by other models (self‑training), where a stronger or similarly sized model produces outputs for a set of prompts. Synthetic generation can cheaply expand dataset size but risks amplifying the generator’s biases and errors.
- Public benchmarks (e.g., GLUE, SuperGLUE, SQuAD, etc.) converted into prompt–response pairs. These enable evaluation and comparison but may not reflect the style of in‑the‑wild instructions.
- Instructional datasets collected from user logs when the model is already deployed, subject to privacy and safety considerations.

An important design decision is how to pair inputs and outputs: in many tasks outputs are short labels (classification), sequences (translation), or free‑form text (summarization, instruction following). For tasks with multiple valid outputs (e.g., many paraphrases), you can include multiple target outputs for the same input to teach the model the variety of acceptable responses. For noisy or ambiguous tasks, it is helpful to include both positive and negative examples to shape model confidence.

Quality control matters: inconsistent labels, ambiguous instructions, or mismatched formatting can confuse the model and hurt generalization. Data cleaning practices include removing or rewriting low‑quality examples, normalizing tokens and whitespace, and curating a validation set that reflects the intended deployment distribution.

# Instruction tuning: datasets, formats, and generalization

Instruction tuning is a specific kind of SFT where the dataset comprises instruction–response pairs: each input explicitly frames a user instruction (e.g., "Summarize the following article" or "Explain gradient descent to a beginner"), and the target is the desired response that follows that instruction. The goal is to make the model respond appropriately to novel natural‑language instructions at inference time. Instruction tuning differs from task‑specific fine‑tuning in its emphasis on natural‑language task descriptions and on teaching the model to follow the intent encoded by those instructions.

Datasets for instruction tuning vary in size and origin. Some are relatively small human‑curated collections of diverse instruction–response pairs, while others are large compilations aggregated from public sources and synthetic generations (often referred to as "instruction datasets"). Famous examples in research include InstructGPT’s human‑labeled instruction dataset and follow‑up releases that combine human and synthetic data. When building or choosing an instruction dataset, pay attention to three characteristics:

First, diversity of instructions. Models trained on a broad range of tasks and styles of instruction are better able to generalize to new instructions. Diversity spans domains (technical, creative, conversational), formats (questions, commands, transformations), and complexity (single‑step vs. multi‑step tasks). A dataset that only includes, for example, short Q&A style prompts will leave the model ill‑prepared for multi‑paragraph instruction following.

Second, format and structural variability. If every example follows the same rigid format (e.g., "Input: ... Output: ..."), models may learn to pay attention to format cues rather than instruction semantics. Including varied phrasing and structural templates reduces overfitting to superficial formatting and encourages semantic understanding of instructions. On the other hand, some controlled structure can be helpful when your deployment has a stable interface—if you expect every user to provide a "system" line and then an "instruction" field, then tuning on that format yields better empirical performance.

Third, quality and correctness of responses. Instruction tuning aims to teach the mapping between instructions and desired outputs. If targets are incorrect, biased, or inconsistent, the model learns those harmful patterns. Human vetting or high‑quality synthetic filtering is essential to ensure that the model learns useful behaviors.

Dataset formats are central to instruction tuning. Two common approaches are template prompts and free‑form prompts. Template prompts place instructions into a fixed template, for instance:
"User: {instruction}
Assistant: {response}"
Free‑form prompts include more natural variability: "Can you explain {topic} for a high school student?" or "I have the following code snippet — can you find the bug?" The choice affects both training efficiency and how the model generalizes to unseen prompt styles.

Evaluation of instruction tuning should not be limited to the training set tasks. The hope is that instruction tuning induces a general "instruction following" capability: the model learns to interpret the intent and constraints within an instruction and produce a relevant output, even for tasks not explicitly present in the training set. Studies show that training on many diverse instructions can produce substantial generalization — models can follow novel combinations of skills (e.g., translate and then summarize) or adjust verbosity and tone on request — but generalization is not guaranteed. Understanding what types of generalization occur requires careful evaluation (discussed below).

# Initialization strategies and continual fine‑tuning

How you initialize the weights before fine‑tuning has a large effect on performance, speed, and stability. The most straightforward approach is to start from a pretrained base LM checkpoint (e.g., a foundation model pretrained on large text corpora). This makes sense because pretraining encodes general linguistic knowledge and world facts that SFT can leverage to map instructions to outputs with far fewer labeled examples.

However, there are alternatives and refinements that trade off compute and plasticity:

Full‑parameter fine‑tuning. This updates all model parameters starting from the pretrained checkpoint. It typically achieves the best performance if you have enough labeled data and compute, but it is expensive to store separate copies for many downstream tasks, and it risks catastrophic forgetting of pretraining knowledge when fine‑tuning on narrow data.

Head re‑initialization and task heads. For classification or other structured outputs, practitioners sometimes append a small task‑specific head (e.g., an MLP) and fine‑tune just the head (or fine‑tune the head and a subset of layers). This reduces storage and computation, but it often underperforms full fine‑tuning for complex instruction following tasks that require changing internal representations.

Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT). Methods such as low‑rank adaptation (LoRA), adapters, prefix tuning, or tuning only layer normalization scale factors enable updating a small set of trainable parameters while keeping most pretrained weights frozen. LoRA, for instance, injects low‑rank matrices into attention projections and trains only those matrices. PEFT is attractive because it drastically reduces the number of parameters to store per task, enables fast checkpointing, and often preserves more of the base model’s capabilities, which helps when you want to maintain generality across many fine‑tuned variants.

Continual fine‑tuning refers to sequentially fine‑tuning a model on multiple datasets or objectives over time. There are several common patterns and concerns:

Sequential task transfer. You might first SFT on an instruction dataset and then perform RLHF, or fine‑tune on a general instruction corpus and later fine‑tune on a domain‑specific dataset (e.g., medical instructions). Sequential transfer can improve domain performance, but it can also cause catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses competence on earlier tasks or general language understanding.

Mixing and replay. To mitigate forgetting, a common strategy is to mix examples from earlier tasks with new task data during fine‑tuning (rehearsal), or to interleave training on multiple tasks. Alternatively, you can use distillation: keep performance on old distributions by distilling the old model’s logits or responses while training on new data. Regularization techniques such as elastic weight consolidation (EWC) or L2 penalties toward the original initialization are also used to preserve important weights.

Checkpointing and progressive rollout. Practically, teams maintain checkpoints at multiple stages: a base pretrained model, an instruction‑tuned checkpoint, a domain‑adapted checkpoint, and perhaps models post‑RLHF. This lets you compare and revert if a later fine‑tuning step harms desired behaviors. Progressive rollout and A/B testing are important when deploying models that were continually fine‑tuned.

Choosing the right initialization and continual strategy depends on resources, the number of tasks or domains you must support, and the importance of preserving broad capabilities. If you need a single model that excels across many unseen instructions, starting from a strong pretrained checkpoint and performing broad instruction tuning (possibly with PEFT to maintain flexibility) is a reasonable pattern. If you need many specialized models, PEFT methods allow cheap specialization without duplicating massive base models.

# Evaluation: generalization to unseen instructions

Evaluating how well a fine‑tuned or instruction‑tuned model generalizes to unseen instructions is more subtle than measuring accuracy on a held‑out split of the same dataset. The core question is whether the model has learned a robust mapping from instruction semantics and inputs to the desired behavior, rather than memorizing training examples or superficial cues. Robust evaluation should therefore include several complementary strategies.

Held‑out instruction splits. One direct approach is to hold out a set of instruction types during training and include them only in the test set. For example, if your dataset covers tasks A, B, C, and D, you can train on A, B, C and evaluate on D. This tests cross‑task generalization. Creating meaningful held‑out splits requires careful taxonomy of tasks so that the held‑out group is genuinely distinct.

Compositional generalization tests. Good instruction following often requires composing known operations. You can design evaluations where tasks are compositions of subtasks seen during training but combined in a novel way, such as "translate then summarize" when only "translate" and "summarize" were in the training set separately. Models that truly learn modular behaviors will handle compositions better.

Prompt variation and paraphrase robustness. To test whether a model is overfit to surface phrasing, evaluate it on paraphrases and structurally different prompts that convey the same intent. If performance drops significantly under paraphrase, the model is relying on lexical cues rather than semantic understanding.

Metric selection. Depending on the task, different automatic metrics apply: BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR for translation or summarization variants; exact match and F1 for extractive QA; classification accuracy for labeled tasks. However, instruction following often admits many valid responses that automatic metrics score poorly. For such cases, human evaluation or learned evaluators (e.g., reward models trained to predict human preference) are necessary. When using human raters, define clear rubric categories: correctness, helpfulness, factuality, safety, and style matching.

Out‑of‑distribution (OOD) evaluation. Test the model on data drawn from distributions different from the training data: different domains, different demographic language styles, technical vs. lay audiences, or adversarial instructions. OOD evaluation reveals brittleness that in‑distribution validation hides.

Calibration and confidence. A model’s probability estimates or response style can mislead users if it expresses high confidence in wrong answers. Evaluate calibration — the alignment between model confidence (if available) and accuracy — and consider mechanisms for uncertainty quantification such as generating multiple samples, using an ensemble, or having a verifier model.

A/B testing in deployment. Ultimately, real‑world generalization is best measured with live user interaction. A/B tests comparing the instruction‑tuned model with previous models or baselines, combined with metrics like task completion rate, user satisfaction, and rate of harmful responses, give practical evidence of improvement or regression.

Combining these evaluations provides a rounded picture: automatic metrics for scalability, human judgment for subjective qualities, and structured OOD tests for robustness. Pay attention to distributional shifts between training and evaluation data: good performance on held‑out instructions drawn from the same data generation process is necessary but not sufficient for deployment robustness.

# Pitfalls: overfitting to format, distribution shift

Fine‑tuning and instruction tuning present several common pitfalls that can compromise a model’s utility. Understanding these failure modes and how to mitigate them is crucial for building robust systems.

Overfitting to format and superficial cues. If your dataset uses a narrow set of templates or consistent markers (e.g., always starting with "Instruction:" and ending with "Answer:"), the model may learn to map these surface features to appropriate responses without internally modeling the instruction semantics. The result is poor robustness to paraphrases or different interface formats. To mitigate this, diversify prompt phrasing and template structures during training, or randomly vary placeholder tokens and delimiters. Data augmentation with paraphrased instructions is effective. PEFT methods sometimes exacerbate format overfitting if they reduce representational flexibility; balancing frozen and trainable parameters helps.

Label leakage and target memorization. If the training data contains explicit answers to prompts that also appear verbatim in evaluation or deployment settings, or if the same input appears in both sets, the model may memorize rather than generalize. This is particularly risky when using synthetic data generated from existing models: you can propagate idiosyncrasies and duplication. Strict deduplication between training and evaluation sets and careful data provenance tracking help prevent leakage.

Distribution shift. The language, style, or types of tasks encountered during deployment often differ from the fine‑tuning corpus. Even if a model performs well on a held‑out test drawn from the same source, it can fail catastrophically when users issue instructions in different languages, use slang, demand longer multi‑step chains of reasoning, or request outputs in a different format (tabular vs. free text). To mitigate distribution shift, collect representative data from expected user populations and apply domain adaptation techniques such as continued fine‑tuning on in‑domain examples, domain‑adaptive pretraining, or importance‑weighted sampling.

Spurious correlations and shortcut learning. Fine‑tuned models may exploit correlations that are predictive in the training data but unrelated to the intended reasoning. For example, if all examples of a certain class contain a particular token or phrase, the model will use that token rather than learning the underlying rule. Addressing spurious correlations requires careful dataset construction, adversarial examples that break shortcuts, and explicit regularization or counterfactual augmentation.

Catastrophic forgetting. When continually fine‑tuning on a narrow dataset, models can forget skills and knowledge gained during pretraining. This is problematic when a model is expected to handle both general language and specialized instructions. Strategies like rehearsal (mixing earlier data), distillation from previous checkpoints, and parameter regularization help preserve earlier competencies.

Human preference mismatch. SFT optimizes for likelihood conditioned on a specific target; this does not ensure outputs are preferred by users. A model that faithfully reproduces training targets might produce verbose or bland text that users dislike. To align models with human preferences, a common pipeline is SFT → collect preference data → train a reward model → RLHF. However, RLHF introduces its own complexities, such as reward hacking, instability, and reliance on the quality of preference data.

Evaluation blind spots. Automatic metrics may miss important aspects like factuality, safety, and hallucination. Models can score well on BLEU or ROUGE while producing factually incorrect or unsafe content. Complement automatic evaluation with targeted tests for hallucination (e.g., asking for verifiable facts or citations), adversarial instruction tests (e.g., jailbreak attempts), and human evaluation for subjective quality.

Computational and resource constraints. Large models are expensive to fine‑tune fully. Using PEFT methods trades storage and compute for possible decreases in performance or increased sensitivity to hyperparameters. Budget constraints may push teams to train on smaller, noisier datasets, increasing the risk of overfitting and poor generalization. Careful hyperparameter tuning, using appropriate batch sizes, learning rates, and stopping criteria (early stopping on a relevant validation set) is essential.

In practice, many of these pitfalls interact. For example, overfitting to format can be exacerbated by distribution shift, and catastrophic forgetting can amplify spurious correlations when training on limited new data. A sound development cycle involves iterating on dataset composition, prompt diversity, validation tasks that reflect intended uses, and deploying in controlled A/B tests with rollback capacity.

Conclusion

Supervised fine‑tuning and instruction tuning are powerful tools for adapting pretrained LMs to perform useful, user‑oriented behaviors. The MLE objective remains the workhorse for SFT, while instruction tuning extends this paradigm to teach models to follow natural‑language directions. Success depends critically on dataset quality, prompt diversity, and thoughtful initialization and continual fine‑tuning strategies. Robust evaluation—covering held‑out instructions, compositionality, paraphrase robustness, and real‑world A/B testing—is necessary to establish true generalization. Finally, watch for pitfalls including overfitting to format, distribution shift, spurious correlations, and catastrophic forgetting; these issues can often be mitigated by careful dataset design, PEFT or rehearsal strategies, and thorough validation. With these principles in hand, you will be better equipped to design, train, and assess fine‑tuned instruction models and to critically read research papers reporting advances in this area.
