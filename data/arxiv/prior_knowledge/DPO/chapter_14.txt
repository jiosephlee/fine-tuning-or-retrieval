Introduction

Aligning large language models (LMs) to human preferences is a central problem in modern natural language processing. The dominant pipeline in recent years has been reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): collect human judgments, fit a reward model, then run a policy optimization algorithm such as proximal policy optimization (PPO) to produce a policy that maximizes the learned reward. RLHF can work well, but it is complex, computationally expensive, and can be unstable. Consequently, many alternative training objectives and practical strategies have been developed that aim to capture preference signals more directly, more cheaply, or more stably. This chapter surveys several of those alternatives: supervised fine‑tuning on preferred completions (sometimes called behavior cloning), unlikelihood training that penalizes undesired tokens or sequences, pairwise cross‑entropy style losses for comparisons, and best‑of‑N selection and test‑time selection strategies. We examine the mathematical forms of these approaches, their intuition, algorithmic implementations, empirical tradeoffs, and limitations so that you can read research papers that use or compare these methods and understand the design decisions.

# Supervised fine‑tuning on preferred completions (behavior cloning)

Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) — also called behavior cloning when the goal is to imitate a preferred policy — is the most straightforward way to use preference data. The idea is simple: collect examples where, for each prompt, a human (or some other process) indicates a preferred completion. Then treat those preferred completions as ground‑truth targets and fine‑tune the language model with maximum likelihood on those tokens.

Formally, given a dataset of prompts \(x\) and preferred completions \(y^\star\), SFT minimizes the negative log‑likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)} \log p_\theta(y^\star \mid x).
\]
Because language models are usually autoregressive, this decomposes into token‑wise cross‑entropy:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y^\star_t \mid y^\star_{<t}, x).
\]

Intuition and strengths. SFT directly engineers the model to produce the preferred outputs. It is simple to implement with standard training tooling, is stable (gradient estimates are low variance), and often produces substantial improvements when the preference labels are of high quality and diverse. When human raters provide labels that correspond to high utility, SFT can transfer well because the network simply learns to place high probability on those kinds of completions.

Limitations and mismatch issues. There are two important caveats. First, SFT learns to imitate the distribution of examples in the supervised dataset. If the labels are a small set of curated, high‑quality completions that are not representative of what the model would have sampled, SFT may lead to overfitting or poor generalization to new prompts. For example, if annotators are told to produce a single “best” completion, the model will learn to produce that type of answer but may not learn finer-grained ranking information among alternatives.

Second, SFT ignores the underlying preference structure when the data consists of pairwise or ordinal judgments. Suppose you have many comparisons "A preferred to B" but no absolute labels. One can convert these into preferred completions by taking the better completion as the positive example, but valuable information—such as degree of preference or relative likelihood—might be lost. Lastly, supervised learning does not explicitly control for distributional shift between the training prompts and the prompts the model sees at inference time; repeated sampling feedback loops can induce degenerate behaviors.

Practical considerations. The quality and diversity of the SFT dataset are crucial. Data augmentation methods, mixing the SFT loss with a base LM loss, or temperature mixing during sampling can help preserve generality. People often fine‑tune from a pretrained policy using a relatively small learning rate and sometimes perform early stopping or use a held‑out preference validation set. In production pipelines, SFT is frequently used as an initial step before applying a preference‑aware objective (e.g., PPO) because it rapidly incorporates human judgments with low compute and stabilizes subsequent optimization.

# Unlikelihood training: penalizing undesired tokens or sequences

Unlikelihood training introduces loss terms designed to reduce the probability of particular tokens or sequences that are considered undesirable. The general rationale is that standard maximum likelihood training encourages the model to place mass on observed or preferred tokens, but it does not actively discourage tokens that are known to be bad. Unlikelihood losses provide a direct mechanism for negative training signals.

The token‑level unlikelihood loss for a set of undesired tokens \(U\) (or undesired token indices at a position) is typically written as
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{UL}}(\theta) = -\sum_{t} \sum_{w \in U_t} \log \bigl(1 - p_\theta(w \mid y_{<t}, x)\bigr),
\]
where \(U_t\) is the set of tokens to be penalized at position \(t\). The term \(\log(1 - p_\theta(w))\) grows large and negative as the model assigns higher probability to \(w\), thereby providing a gradient that pushes probability mass away from those tokens.

For sequence‑level unlikelihood, suppose we have an undesired sequence \(z\). We can design a loss that reduces the model’s probability of producing \(z\) as a whole. One approach is to apply unlikelihood to each token in \(z\) conditioned on its prefix; another is to penalize the joint probability directly using the autoregressive factorization:
\[
p_\theta(z \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|z|} p_\theta(z_t \mid z_{<t}, x),
\]
and craft a loss that pushes each conditional probability down.

Intuition and applications. Unlikelihood is especially useful when you can identify clear undesirable behaviors or tokens: hallucinated facts of a specific form, repeated tokens leading to degeneracy, toxic words, or known failure modes. Because the loss explicitly pushes mass away from these tokens, it complements maximum likelihood which by itself only pulls mass toward observed good tokens. This has been successfully used to mitigate repetition (repetition penalty), to reduce toxicity, and to prevent models from outputting personally identifiable information in certain contexts.

Design choices and caveats. One must choose which tokens or sequences to penalize and how strongly to penalize them. If the penalty is too weak, the model may not change. If it is too strong, the model can become unstable or develop other degenerate behaviors to avoid the penalized tokens. Additionally, unlikelihood is only as good as the negative set: if negatives are overly broad, you risk suppressing legitimate tokens that should be allowed in some contexts. It can also conflict with the likelihood objective, so many implementations mix unlikelihood losses with standard cross‑entropy and tune the relative weight.

Optimization and practicalities. In practice, computing \(\log(1 - p)\) requires numerical care when \(p\) is close to 1. Implementations often clamp probabilities or use safe approximations. For sequence‑level undesirables, applying per‑token unlikelihood across a long sequence can create many negative gradient terms; therefore batching and careful curriculum choices (e.g., start with token‑level then move to sequence‑level penalties) are common. Finally, unlikelihood can be combined with reward models: the reward model flags completions as bad and the unlikelihood loss reduces their likelihood, thereby providing a direct supervised analog to negative reward updates.

# Pairwise cross‑entropy losses for preferences

When preference data are collected as pairwise comparisons — for a prompt \(x\), the judge prefers completion \(y_+\) over completion \(y_-\) — we can train models to reflect these comparisons directly. One common approach is a pairwise cross‑entropy or Bradley–Terry style loss that encourages the model to assign higher probability (or higher score) to the preferred completion than to the inferior one. There are different ways to formulate this idea depending on whether the model outputs probabilities over sequences or scalar preference scores.

If we treat the language model as defining a probability \(p_\theta(y \mid x)\) over completions, a straightforward pairwise loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_+,y_-)} \log \frac{p_\theta(y_+ \mid x)}{p_\theta(y_+ \mid x) + p_\theta(y_- \mid x)}.
\]
This is equivalent to using the binary cross‑entropy loss on the two options, where the model outputs normalized probabilities over the two completions. Expanding the log yields
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E} \left[\log p_\theta(y_+ \mid x) - \log\bigl(p_\theta(y_+ \mid x) + p_\theta(y_- \mid x)\bigr)\right].
\]
Because \(p_\theta(y)\) typically factorizes over tokens, calculating this requires evaluating sequence probabilities for both completions.

An alternative is to train a scalar scoring function \(s_\theta(x,y)\) (not constrained to be a normalized probability) and apply a logistic loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_+,y_-)} \log\left(1 + \exp\bigl(-\bigl(s_\theta(x,y_+) - s_\theta(x,y_-)\bigr)\bigr)\right).
\]
This formulation is widely used when a separate reward or preference model is learned; here \(s_\theta\) is exactly that model. It directly models the preference margin and avoids having to compute normalized sequence probabilities. When the language model itself is used as the scorer, \(s_\theta\) might be the log‑probability \(\log p_\theta(y \mid x)\) aggregated over tokens, or it might be a learned head on top of the LM representations.

Intuition and strengths. Pairwise losses leverage comparison data more fully than simple SFT on winners because they explicitly encode relative judgments: given many pairs, the model can infer a ranking over the space of completions rather than being forced to imitate single winners. This is statistically more efficient when many comparisons are available. Pairwise losses are also closer to the objective in RLHF where a reward model is trained on comparisons.

Limitations and complexities. Computing pairwise probabilities can be expensive because each comparison requires evaluating two full sequence likelihoods. If the model uses normalized sequence probabilities \(p_\theta(y)\), these probabilities can be very small and numerically challenging to compare across different lengths or tokenizations. Using an unnormalized score \(s_\theta\) helps but requires care to ensure the score correlates well with human preference.

Another important issue is calibration and consistency: pairwise losses enforce that \(s_\theta\) rank pairs correctly but do not specify absolute calibration of scores. This can lead to problems when combining scores across prompts or when using scores for sampling decisions. There is also the problem of transitivity violations in empirical preference data; pairwise training can overfit noisy or inconsistent comparisons. Regularization, margin terms, or probabilistic models like Plackett–Luce can help address these concerns.

Extensions. Pairwise losses can be extended to listwise losses for ranking multiple candidates and to approaches that model the probability of a particular ordering using the Plackett–Luce or Mallows models. These extensions are more computationally intensive but can incorporate richer preference structure when multiple alternatives are compared per prompt.

# Best‑of‑N selection and test‑time selection strategies

Best‑of‑N (BoN) selection is a practical strategy that separates generation from selection: at inference time, the model samples N candidate completions for a prompt, then a selection function (which may be the LM's own probability, a reward model, or a heuristic) chooses the best candidate. This approach is appealing because it leverages the diversity of sampling and can yield better outputs without changing the model weights.

The simplest variant uses the model's own likelihood to pick the most probable candidate:
1. Sample \(N\) completions \(\{y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N\) using a stochastic decoding strategy (temperature sampling, top‑k, top‑p).
2. Compute the model log probability \(\log p_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x)\) for each candidate.
3. Select the candidate with the highest log probability (argmax).

This method effectively approximates greedy decoding within the stochastic sampling pool and can overcome some issues with direct greedy decoding by exploring less probable but potentially better candidates.

A more powerful variant uses an external scorer or reranker, such as a learned reward model \(r_\phi(x,y)\) or a human evaluator, to select among the \(N\) samples. Here the selected output is
\[
\hat{y} = \arg\max_{i \in \{1,\dots,N\}} r_\phi(x,y^{(i)}).
\]
Using a reward model trained on human preferences allows BoN to harness preference labels without modifying the generative model. This is commonly combined with supervised fine‑tuning or unlikelihood training for additional gains.

Intuition and benefits. BoN is attractive because it is simple to implement and requires no policy optimization. It can dramatically improve output quality even for moderate \(N\), because sampling explores modes of the generative distribution that greedy decoding might miss. When combined with a good scorer, BoN effectively approximates a search over possible completions guided by the scorer. It is especially useful in settings where scoring is cheap relative to generation (e.g., cheap reward model or log‑prob computation) and where parallel generation is possible.

Limitations and costs. The principal cost of BoN is compute at inference time: generating \(N\) samples multiplies generation cost roughly by \(N\). For large models and long outputs, BoN can be prohibitively expensive in latency‑sensitive applications. There are strategies to mitigate this: use smaller cheap generators with a stronger reranker, employ sequential sampling schemes (generate few candidates, rerank, then refine), or cache frequently used replies.

Another concern is selection bias: if the scorer is imperfect, BoN can promote adversarial or degenerate candidates that score well under the surrogate but poorly under true human judgment. There is also the problem of diversity: naive BoN with the model’s own probability as scorer tends to pick high‑probability candidates, which may reduce diversity; conversely, sampling with high temperature can increase diversity but degrade average quality.

Connections to training. BoN can be folded into training by using it to generate candidates for pairwise training or unlikelihood signals: sample multiple completions, use human comparisons or a reward model to label them, then train the model with pairwise losses or SFT on the winners. This hybrid approach can be an economical way to generate labeled preference data and bootstrap alignment without full RL.

# Practical tradeoffs: compute, sample efficiency, stability

When choosing among SFT, unlikelihood, pairwise losses, BoN, and RL methods, practitioners must balance compute requirements, sample efficiency (how many human labels are needed), and optimization stability.

Compute. Supervised fine‑tuning is computationally cheap relative to reinforcement learning because it requires only forward and backward passes for labeled examples. Pairwise training is more expensive per labeled comparison because it typically requires evaluating two sequences per example; if sequence lengths are long and models are large, this doubles the forward cost compared to SFT. Unlikelihood adds some per‑token cost but is similar in magnitude to cross‑entropy in practice. Best‑of‑N is inference‑time expensive: generating \(N\) candidates multiplies generation compute by \(N\), though the generation can often be parallelized on multiple GPUs or using batched inference. RL methods like PPO are much more expensive because they require repeated environment rollouts, reward evaluation, and policy optimization with many gradient steps, sometimes on-policy, leading to very high compute requirements.

Sample efficiency. In terms of human label efficiency, pairwise losses and preference‑model training can be very sample efficient because each comparison conveys relative information about the space of completions. SFT on winners is less efficient per human label: one labeled winner loses information about losers unless the losers are also used as negative examples. Unlikelihood can be sample efficient if negative examples are abundant or automatically generated (for example, repetition detection or toxicity heuristics), but is limited when negatives must be labeled by humans. BoN can be sample efficient in the sense that many candidate outputs can be scored cheaply by a learned reward model, enabling reuse of scoring labels across many generations. However, collecting high‑quality human labels for the reward model remains a bottleneck.

Stability. From an optimization perspective, SFT is the most stable; it is simply maximum likelihood training with low‑variance gradients. Pairwise losses are also stable but can be more sensitive to noise in the pairwise labels and require careful batching and learning‑rate choices. Unlikelihood can introduce instability when negative sets are large or when penalties are strong; the model may respond by collapsing distribution mass in unintended ways. RL techniques are notoriously unstable: policy gradient variance, drift in the reward model, and sensitivity to hyperparameters lead to brittle behavior and can require elaborate safeguards (clipping, trust regions, KL penalties) to stabilize.

Failure modes and reward hacking. Many of these methods are susceptible to misalignment between the proxy objective and true human preferences. For instance, if the reward model exploited for selection or training is misspecified, both BoN and pairwise training can amplify the reward model’s errors, a phenomenon referred to as reward hacking. Unlikelihood, when applied blindly, can suppress legitimate content or create unnatural phrasing to avoid penalized tokens. SFT can overfit to the style of annotators and underperform on out‑of‑distribution prompts.

Engineering mitigations. To mitigate these tradeoffs, practitioners commonly use a mixture of approaches: start with SFT to get a reasonable baseline, train a reward model on pairwise comparisons, use the reward model to annotate many candidate outputs cheaply, and then either do BoN with the reward model at inference or fine‑tune the generator with pairwise or unlikelihood losses derived from the annotated candidates. Regularization techniques such as KL penalties to the pretrained policy, early stopping, and conservatively weighted unlikelihood components help reduce catastrophic drift. For compute‑limited settings, one can use smaller proxy models for generation and a larger reranker, which reduces end‑to‑end latency while keeping quality high.

Empirical evaluation. Quantitative comparisons between these methods depend heavily on task, dataset, and labeling quality. Papers typically report that SFT provides large initial gains with small data, pairwise training improves ranking and reward alignment, unlikelihood reduces obvious failure modes (e.g., repetition or toxicity), BoN improves outputs with increased inference compute, and RLHF yields the strongest measured alignment when large amounts of compute and careful engineering are available. However, RLHF’s marginal gains relative to carefully tuned SFT + reward‑model reranking are often smaller than one might expect given the engineering cost, and many recent works advocate for simpler supervised or contrastive approaches in resource‑constrained environments.

Conclusion

There is no single universally best approach for training language models from preferences. Supervised fine‑tuning is simple, stable, and often effective as an initial step. Unlikelihood losses provide targeted negative training to correct known problems. Pairwise losses and ranking formulations make efficient use of comparative judgments and are conceptually close to reward modeling. Best‑of‑N selection is a practical, inference‑time method that leverages sampling and scoring without altering weights. The right choice depends on the availability of labeled data, compute and latency constraints, and tolerance for optimization complexity and instability. In practice, hybrid pipelines that combine SFT, reward modeling, unlikelihood penalties, and BoN or reranking often yield the best tradeoffs between cost and alignment performance. Understanding the mathematical forms, intuitions, and failure modes presented in this chapter will equip you to read contemporary research papers and to design alignment pipelines suited to your constraints.
