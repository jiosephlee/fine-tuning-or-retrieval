Introduction

Aligning language models (LMs) to human preferences or task-specific objectives commonly requires combining a reward or preference signal with a prior model that represents safe or natural language behavior. A common technique is to constrain the fine-tuned policy so it does not diverge too far from a reference model, often by applying a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty or similar regularizer. This chapter explains how divergences are computed, interpreted, and controlled during LM fine-tuning. We begin with the distinction between sequence-level and token-level KL computations, develop how per-token log-probabilities are accumulated, and cover how design choices such as KL targets, temperature scaling, and length normalization affect training. We also show how probability ratios and log-odds appear in practical objectives, how to trade off reward and KL, and how to measure distributional drift from the reference. The aim is to provide conceptual intuition together with equations and practical guidance so that you can read and implement research papers that use these techniques.

Sequence-level vs. token-level KL computations

A central design choice when adding a divergence penalty between a policy \( \pi \) (current model) and a reference \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) is whether the divergence is computed over whole sequences or at each token step. Both formulations are related but lead to different computational and optimization behaviors.

Sequence-level KL. Consider a sequence of tokens \( x_{1:T} \). The sequence-level KL between two distributions over full sequences is
\[
\operatorname{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid c) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid c)\big)
= \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid c)}\left[\log\frac{\pi(x_{1:T}\mid c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_{1:T}\mid c)}\right],
\]
where \( c \) denotes the conditioning context (prompt). Using autoregressivity, the sequence probabilities factorize:
\[
\pi(x_{1:T}\mid c) = \prod_{t=1}^T \pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c),
\]
so the logarithm becomes a sum of per-token log-probabilities:
\[
\log\frac{\pi(x_{1:T}\mid c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_{1:T}\mid c)}
= \sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{\pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}.
\]
Thus, sequence-level KL is the expected sum of token-wise log-ratios under the policy.

Token-level (stepwise) KL. Instead of summing and then taking expectation, one can impose a stepwise KL at each time step:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x_{<t}\sim\pi}\left[\operatorname{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x_{<t}, c)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t}, c)\big)\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T}\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{\pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}\right],
\]
which equals the sequence-level KL because of linearity of expectation. In other words, the total divergence between sequence distributions equals the expected sum of per-step divergences. However, the distinction matters practically because how you implement and scale the penalty during optimization changes behavior.

Practical differences. Computing a single scalar sequence-level KL and enforcing a global budget or target can be implemented by calculating per-sequence log-ratio sums and treating them as example-level penalties. Token-level KLs allow stepwise control: one can cap or scale the KL at each step, apply different weights at different timesteps (e.g., pay more attention near the start), or use per-step baselines. In reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), stepwise KLs are often implemented naturally because the policy gradient decomposes into per-action log-probability gradients.

Per-token log-probabilities and accumulation

Understanding how per-token log-probabilities accumulate is essential both for computing KL and for evaluating objectives that use probability ratios. For an autoregressive model, the log-probability of a sequence \( x_{1:T} \) given context \( c \) is
\[
\log \pi(x_{1:T}\mid c) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c).
\]
When comparing to a reference model \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \), the sequence log-ratio is the sum of token-wise log-odds:
\[
\log\frac{\pi(x_{1:T}\mid c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_{1:T}\mid c)} = \sum_{t=1}^T \left[ \log \pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)\right].
\]
Implementation notes. For numerical stability and efficiency, log-probabilities are computed in float32 or mixed precision. When operating on batches of sequences of varying length, align token positions with padding and mask out padding tokens in the KL accumulation. In RL-based fine-tuning, the per-step gradient contribution to the policy gradient is proportional to the per-step log-probability; accumulating correctly ensures that any KL penalty or baseline at the sequence level translates to proper per-step gradients.

Baselines and variance reduction. When using reward-to-go estimators, a common technique is to subtract a baseline \( b(x_{<t}, c) \) from the return to reduce gradient variance. Because the KL penalty is additive across tokens, it can be included in the baseline or treated as an additional immediate cost shaping the return at each step. Care is needed to ensure correct credit assignment between reward and KL cost.

Choosing and interpreting KL targets

A KL target is a user-chosen value that expresses how far the fine-tuned policy is allowed to drift from the reference. There are multiple ways to impose a target.

Soft penalty. Add a penalty term \( \beta \operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) \) to the objective, where \( \beta>0 \) controls the strength. This does not enforce a hard bound but encourages the policy toward the reference. Choosing \( \beta \) is often empirical: larger \( \beta \) yields conservatism, smaller \( \beta \) allows more deviation to chase reward.

Hard constraint (KL threshold). Enforce \( \operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) \leq \tau \) for some threshold \( \tau \). In practice, methods like PPO or constrained policy optimization use adaptive penalties or Lagrange multipliers to approximately enforce such constraints. For example, a dual variable \( \lambda \) can be learned to penalize KL only as needed to keep it near \( \tau \).

Interpreting per-sequence targets. Because KL accumulates over tokens, a sequence-level target implicitly scales with expected length. If you want a per-token budget, set the target proportional to average sequence length \( \mathbb{E}[T] \): a per-token bound \( \tau_{\text{token}} \) corresponds to a sequence bound \( \tau = \tau_{\text{token}} \, \mathbb{E}[T] \). Failing to account for length means short sequences will be relatively less constrained than long ones.

Practical guidance. Typical settings in RLHF: pick \( \beta \) or \( \tau \) so that the resulting policy's behavior remains aligned with the style and safety of the reference while still improving on reward. Start conservatively and inspect sample outputs and measured divergence. Use a held-out validation of safety-sensitive metrics to guide tuning.

Temperature scaling and calibration

Temperature scaling modifies the sharpness of a softmax distribution and is a simple but powerful calibration technique. For logits \( z(x) \) from a model, the temperature-\( T \) distribution is
\[
\pi_T(x) = \frac{\exp(z(x) / T)}{\sum_{x'} \exp(z(x') / T)}.
\]
When \( T=1 \) we get the original model; \( T>1 \) makes the distribution flatter (higher entropy); \( 0<T<1 \) sharpens it.

Effect on KL and probability ratios. Applying temperature to the reference or to the policy changes the KL. If you scale the reference logits by \( T_{\text{ref}} \), the reference probabilities change, which affects the log-odds and therefore the KL penalty. Similarly, applying temperature to the policy is equivalent to modifying its entropy: lower temperature reduces entropy and can increase KL to the reference.

Calibration vs. exploration. Temperature is commonly used at generation time for sampling to modulate creativity. In training, implicit changes to entropy (e.g., via entropy regularization) have similar effects. Be careful: temperature changes are not equivalent to model retraining and can produce mismatches between the training-time distribution and generation-time sampling, which complicates KL control.

Calibrating logits before KL computation. If the reference and policy have different calibration (e.g., one is overconfident), KL will penalize what may be purely a calibration mismatch. A common practical step is to use temperature scaling or isotonic regression calibrators to align confidences before computing or interpreting KL. However, calibration-based transformations should be applied consistently to both models or considered part of the reference policy.

Probability ratios and log-odds in objectives

Many RL and fine-tuning objectives use probability ratios directly. Consider a PPO-like clipped objective where the per-step surrogate objective involves the ratio
\[
r_t = \frac{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)},
\]
or in language modeling notation,
\[
r_t = \frac{\pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}.
\]
Algorithms use \( r_t \) to scale advantages or rewards. The log-ratio \( \log r_t \) appears in KL penalties and in maximum likelihood-based policy gradients.

Log-odds additive property. Because log-probabilities add across timesteps, sequence log-odds equal sum of per-token log-odds, as shown earlier. This property is convenient: multiplicative probability ratios over sequences become additive in log-space, which is numerically stable and aligns with gradient computations.

Clipping and stabilization. Large probability ratios can cause instability. PPO addresses this by clipping the ratio in the surrogate objective:
\[
\mathcal{L}_t^{\text{PPO}} = \min\big(r_t \, A_t, \, \operatorname{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, A_t\big),
\]
where \( A_t \) is the advantage. In practice, when using KL penalties, a similar rationale applies: cap the effective contribution of very improbable or very probable tokens to avoid exploding gradients or extreme updates.

Combining with KL. When the objective includes reward \( R \) and a KL penalty with coefficient \( \beta \), the effective per-sequence target is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}[R(x) - \beta \log\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}].
\]
Equivalently, one can view maximizing \( R(x) - \beta \log (\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}) \) as maximizing a reward under an augmented or soft-constrained prior, since the KL term can be moved into the reward shaping.

Length penalties and normalization

Because KL and log-probability accumulate over sequence length, length effects must be considered. Longer sequences typically have larger absolute KL divergence simply because more tokens contribute. If not handled, objectives will bias the policy toward shortening or lengthening sequences depending on the sign of the penalty and reward.

Length penalties. To avoid a length-induced bias, one can divide per-sequence KL by length \( T \) to get a per-token KL:
\[
\frac{1}{T}\operatorname{KL}(\pi(\cdot\mid c)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid c))
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{\pi(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_t\mid x_{<t}, c)}\right].
\]
Alternatively, normalize the reward by sequence length or include an explicit length penalty term in the reward to counter undesired length effects.

Effective per-token weighting. If you want to control divergence per token uniformly, add the KL penalty as a per-step cost. This yields gradients that scale in proportion to sequence length uniformly across examples, avoiding length-induced variance.

Practical length normalization choices. Which normalization to use depends on task. For tasks where shorter outputs are preferred, an absolute KL penalty that increases with length will discourage verbosity; for open-ended generation where content richness matters, per-token normalization is more appropriate. When using baselines or advantage estimators that rely on returns, ensure that returns are normalized in a way consistent with how KL is measured.

Trade-offs between reward and KL

A critical practical question is how to choose the trade-off between maximization of the reward signal and penalization for diverging from the reference. The general objective can be written as
\[
\mathcal{J}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}[R(x)] - \beta \operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}).
\]
Adjusting \( \beta \) controls the trade-off. A small \( \beta \) allows more aggressive pursuit of reward, possibly sacrificing style, safety, or fluency encapsulated by the reference. A large \( \beta \) keeps the policy conservative.

Interpreting Lagrange multipliers. Viewing the constraint \( \operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) \leq \tau \) with Lagrange multiplier \( \lambda \) gives a principled way to choose \( \beta \): it is the multiplier corresponding to the KL constraint. Adaptive algorithms update \( \lambda \) to meet a target KL on average. This is useful when you have a tolerable divergence budget rather than a desired fixed penalty strength.

Reward scaling matters. Because the magnitude and scale of \( R(x) \) directly affect the balance, it is important to scale rewards to be commensurate with KL penalty magnitudes. For example, if typical advantages \( A_t \) are on the order of 1 but per-sequence KL sums to tens, the policy will prioritize staying close to the reference. Standardizing rewards, clipping outliers, or using normalized advantages helps create stable and interpretable trade-offs.

Pareto frontier viewpoint. The space of achievable pairs \( (\mathbb{E}[R], \operatorname{KL}) \) forms a Pareto frontier: as you increase allowed KL, achievable reward typically increases. Understanding this frontier (e.g., by sweeping \( \beta \)) helps choose an operating point that yields acceptable reward improvements for a tolerable increase in divergence.

Measuring distributional drift from reference

Beyond scalar KL, practitioners often want to measure how the distribution of generated content changes relative to the reference in ways that affect safety, style, or factuality.

Empirical KL estimates. Estimate sequence-level or per-token KL empirically by sampling \( x\sim\pi \) and computing
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{KL}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log\frac{\pi(x^{(i)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x^{(i)})}.
\]
Use importance sampling cautiously: if \( \pi \) assigns negligible mass where \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) has support (or vice versa), variance can be high.

Per-feature drift. Compute divergences on features of interest rather than raw tokens. For example, measure changes in toxicity rates, usage of specific phrases, or other classifiers. These supervised measures can be more interpretable and aligned with safety concerns than raw KL.

Token-frequency shifts. Compare marginal token frequencies or n-gram distributions between \( \pi \) and \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). This highlights stylistic shifts, repetition changes, or new modes introduced by fine-tuning.

Embedding and metric-based distances. Compute distances between distributions in embedding spaces (e.g., mean semantic embedding of outputs) using maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) or Earth Mover's Distance (EMD). These capture semantic drift that token-level KL might not reflect.

Calibration and confidence drift. Examine the change in predicted confidences for truth-evaluable statements or calibrate on held-out datasets. A policy might remain close in KL but become overconfident, which can be dangerous depending on deployment.

Practical recipes for measuring drift. Combine multiple measures: report the average sequence-level KL, per-token KL, changes in key feature frequencies, and human-evaluated samples. Monitor these throughout training and especially when sweeping \( \beta \) or KL targets to visualize the Pareto frontier in interpretable metrics.

Putting it together: an example objective and implementation notes

A commonly used RLHF-style objective is to maximize reward under a KL penalty:
\[
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi}\left[ R(x) - \beta \log\frac{\pi(x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)} \right].
\]
In practice, you implement this by collecting samples \( \{x^{(i)}\} \) from the current policy, estimating sequence rewards \( R(x^{(i)}) \), computing per-token log-probabilities from both policy and reference, summing to get sequence log-ratios, and computing gradients of the sample objective. Using PPO, you instead use per-token ratios \( r_t \) and advantages \( A_t \) derived from reward-to-go minus baseline, with an additional KL penalty that can be implemented as a separate term in the loss:
\[
\mathcal{L} = -\mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\big(r_t A_t, \operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\big)\right] + \beta \, \widehat{\operatorname{KL}},
\]
where \( \widehat{\operatorname{KL}} \) is estimated per-batch. Alternatively, update a dual variable \( \lambda \) to satisfy a target \( \tau \) on \( \widehat{\operatorname{KL}} \).

Implementation tips. Compute policy and reference logits with the same tokenization and temperature handling. Use masks to ignore padding tokens. Keep numerical stability by working in log-space for ratios and sums. Monitor per-token and per-sequence KL, average sequence length, reward statistics, and qualitative samples.

Conclusion

Controlling divergence between a fine-tuned language model and a reference model is both mathematically straightforward and practically nuanced. Sequence-level and token-level KLs are equivalent in expectation but lead to different implementation choices and behavior when used as penalties, constraints, or per-step costs. Per-token log-probabilities sum to sequence log-probabilities, making log-odds a natural building block for penalties and ratio-based objectives. Choosing KL targets requires careful consideration of sequence length, reward scaling, and whether you prefer hard constraints or soft penalties. Temperature and calibration impact the computed KL and should be handled consistently. Probability ratios and clipping stabilize policy updates, and length normalization is crucial to avoid bias toward particular output lengths. Finally, measuring distributional drift requires multiple complementary metrics: KL provides a general scalar, but feature-based, frequency-based, and embedding-based measures deliver interpretable insights into how behavior has changed. Together, these tools allow researchers and practitioners to align language models to preferences while retaining control over unwanted drift.
