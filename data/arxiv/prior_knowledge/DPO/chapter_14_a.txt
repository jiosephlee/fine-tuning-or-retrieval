Introduction

When fine-tuning large language models (LMs) to align them with human preferences, safety constraints, or downstream objectives, one often balances two competing goals: improve task-specific reward (or preference metrics) while keeping the fine-tuned model close to a reference model so it does not degrade in unexpected ways. Divergences and regularization techniques are the formal tools used to measure and control how much the tuned model drifts from the reference distribution. This chapter explains how divergences are computed at both sequence and token granularity, how per-token log-probabilities accumulate, how to choose and interpret KL targets, how temperature affects model calibration, how probability ratios and log-odds appear inside optimization objectives, how length needs to be addressed, and how to measure distributional drift. Throughout we emphasize intuition, practical computation, and how these ideas are used in common algorithms like KL-regularized policy optimization and supervised fine-tuning.


# Sequence-level vs. token-level KL computations

Divergence between two autoregressive language models can be expressed at different granularities. Let $q(y\mid x)$ denote the reference model probability of output sequence $y$ given prompt $x$, and let $p_\theta(y\mid x)$ denote the tuned model with parameters $\theta$. The sequence-level Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence for a given prompt distribution $\mathcal{D}(x)$ is
\[
\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[ \operatorname{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{y} p_\theta(y\mid x)\,\log\frac{p_\theta(y\mid x)}{q(y\mid x)}\right].
\]
This sequence-level KL measures the expected divergence between whole-sequence distributions. It is the natural quantity when your objective or constraint is concerned with the entire sequence distribution — for instance, when using a KL penalty in reward-weighted policy optimization or when constructing Bayesian posterior objectives.

However, computing or estimating sequence-level KL exactly is generally intractable because the sum over all possible sequences $y$ is enormous. In autoregressive models, we factor sequence probabilities as a product over tokens:
\[
p_\theta(y\mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x).
\]
That factorization suggests a token-level decomposition of the sequence-level KL. Using the chain rule for KL, we can express the sequence-level KL as the sum of expected per-time-step KLs under the tuned model:
\[
\operatorname{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x)\big)
= \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(y_{<t}\mid x)}\left[ \operatorname{KL}\big( p_\theta(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\,\|\, q(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\big) \right].
\]
This identity is exact for finite-length sequences and is extremely useful in practice: it tells us that the sequence divergence decomposes into a sum of per-token divergences, each weighted by the tuned model’s marginal distribution over prefixes. Note though the expectations are taken under $p_\theta$, not $q$, which matters for estimation and optimization.

Practically, it is common to approximate sequence-level KL by averaging or summing per-token KLs computed on sampled trajectories under $p_\theta$ (or sometimes under an importance sampling distribution). This is how most RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) implementations compute a KL penalty: sample sequences from $p_\theta$, compute per-token KLs between the conditional distributions of $p_\theta$ and $q$, and average them to obtain an empirical sequence KL. Because the marginal prefix distribution is a function of $p_\theta$, the decomposition also shows why token-level regularization can prevent pathological drift early in sequences where the model will condition many future tokens on a changed prefix.


# Per-token log-probabilities and accumulation

The basic quantity computed efficiently by autoregressive LMs is the per-token log-probability. For a sequence $y_{1:T}$,
\[
\log p_\theta(y_{1:T}\mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x).
\]
This additive form is both conceptually and computationally convenient. At training and evaluation time, one typically computes the per-token log-probability vector (the logits followed by a softmax) and indexes the log-probability for the observed token at each time step. Summing those per-token log-probabilities yields the sequence log-probability or the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss.

When using KL penalties or importance sampling, per-token log-probabilities provide the building blocks for sequence-level ratios and divergences. For instance, the log probability ratio between tuned and reference models can be written as the sum of per-token log-ratios:
\[
\log\frac{p_\theta(y\mid x)}{q(y\mid x)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log\frac{p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)}{q(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)}.
\]
This decomposition is numerically stable and convenient: in practice we compute per-token log-probabilities from the networks and accumulate them to get the total log-ratio. When computing expected quantities like KL or importance weights, sums of per-token contributions allow incremental computation and online control (for example, preemptively stopping when the accumulated KL exceeds a threshold).

It is important to be careful about padding, end-of-sequence tokens, and variable-length outputs. Some implementations compute averages of per-token log-probabilities (per-token NLL) by dividing by sequence length, while others use raw sums. Which one to use depends on whether you want length-invariant measures or total sequence-based measures. Also note that when using autoregressive models, the per-token conditionals change as the prefix changes; thus, any statistic that involves per-token quantities must account for prefixes drawn under the tuned model if the sequence-level target involves $p_\theta$ expectations.


# Choosing and interpreting KL targets

When regularizing fine-tuning with a KL penalty, you typically specify a target that constrains how much the tuned policy can deviate from the reference. In simplest form, you minimize an objective like
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[\text{reward}(y)] + \beta\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x))\big],
\]
where $\beta$ is a hyperparameter controlling the trade-off. Interpreting and choosing $\beta$ requires thought: a small $\beta$ allows larger divergence (more freedom to chase reward), while a large $\beta$ keeps the model close to the reference (more conservative). But $\beta$ does not straightforwardly map to an absolute KL value — its effect depends on reward scale, data distribution, and model capacity.

An alternative is to use an explicit KL target $C$ and enforce it via a constrained optimization or an adaptive multiplier. For example, one can solve
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[\text{reward}(y)] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbb{E}_x\big[\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x))\big] \le C.
\]
This is commonly handled with a Lagrangian: maximize reward minus $\lambda$ times KL, but adaptively update $\lambda$ (the multiplier) to enforce the constraint. Choosing $C$ can be intuitive in some settings: it might reflect a maximum acceptable average per-token KL (e.g., 0.1 nats per token) or a maximum sequence KL. Translating between per-token and per-sequence targets matters: a target of 0.1 nats per token over a 100-token average sequence corresponds to an average sequence KL around 10 nats.

Interpretation matters: KL is measured in nats (if using natural logarithm) or bits (if base 2 logs). Small numbers like 0.01 are actually very small divergences; a KL of 0.1 nats per token already implies substantial shift in token distributions in aggregate. Moreover, KL is asymmetric: $\operatorname{KL}(p\|q)$ penalizes when $p$ assigns mass to tokens that $q$ would not (mode-seeking mismatch), while $\operatorname{KL}(q\|p)$ penalizes when $q$ assigns mass where $p$ does not. In RLHF we typically use $\operatorname{KL}(p_\theta\|q)$ because we want to penalize the tuned model for producing token sequences that the reference model would consider unlikely.

In practice, choose targets by: (1) inspecting immediate behavior by sampling models with different $\beta$ on representative prompts, (2) measuring per-token KLs and sequence KL distributions, and (3) aligning the units — decide whether you specify per-token KL, average sequence KL, or a percentile (e.g., 95th percentile sequence KL) that must be respected. Use adaptive multipliers that aim for a target KL rather than a fixed penalty coefficient when the reward scale is uncertain.


# Temperature scaling and calibration

Temperature scaling is a simple way to change the sharpness of a model’s conditional distribution without changing the ranking of token logits. Given logits $z_i$ for token $i$, temperature $T>0$ produces probabilities
\[
\pi_T(i) = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}.
\]
Lower temperatures $T<1$ make the distribution sharper (more peaked around high-logit tokens), while $T>1$ makes it flatter. Temperature scaling is often used in sampling to control creativity and also as a small calibration trick in evaluation.

Calibration refers to the alignment between predicted probabilities and empirical frequencies. A well-calibrated model assigns probability close to the actual frequency of events. Temperature scaling by a single $T$ is a post-hoc calibration method: one can fit $T$ on held-out data to minimize negative log-likelihood or expected calibration error (ECE). Because temperature rescales all logits uniformly, it fixes over- or under-confidence while preserving relative rankings of tokens.

In the context of divergence and KL control, temperature interacts with regularization in important ways. If the reference model $q$ is tempered (e.g., sampling from $q_T$ rather than $q$), then KL between $p_\theta$ and $q_T$ will differ substantially from KL between $p_\theta$ and $q$. Temperature affects the entropy of the reference distribution: higher temperature increases entropy and tends to reduce KL penalties because $q_T$ is closer to uniform. Therefore, if you aim to constrain divergence relative to a reference, you must decide whether the reference distribution includes temperature scaling. Often, practitioners fix the reference at its original temperature (e.g., $T=1$ logits) and consider temperature only at sampling time; but in some RL procedures people use temperature-adjusted reference to modulate the strength of KL.

Calibration and temperature also matter for interpreting probability ratios and importance weights: if $q$ is overconfident but incorrect, enforcing small KL to $q$ might preserve its mistakes. Thus calibration diagnostics (reliability diagrams, ECE, and Brier score) should be applied to both reference and tuned models to understand what a KL target will preserve.
