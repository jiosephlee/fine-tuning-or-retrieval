Introduction

Preference models are statistical models that map unobserved or latent utilities (also called scores or rewards) of items to observable preferences such as pairwise comparisons or full/partial rankings. Two of the most influential and widely used models in this area are the Bradley–Terry model for pairwise comparisons and the Plackett–Luce model for rankings. Both are grounded in Luce’s choice axiom, which ties the probability of choosing an item from a set to its relative strength among the set. These models are simple, interpretable, and amenable to classical estimation theory, yet they also admit many useful generalizations and computational techniques. This chapter develops the mathematical form of these models, explains how to estimate parameters from data, addresses identifiability issues and regularization, discusses practical complications such as ties and indifference, and outlines consistency and computational strategies used in research and applications.


# Bradley–Terry pairwise preference model

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is a canonical probabilistic model for pairwise comparisons. Suppose we have a finite set of $n$ items indexed by $i=1,\dots,n$. Associate with each item $i$ a latent positive skill or strength parameter $w_i>0$. In one common parametrization we write $w_i=\exp(\theta_i)$ where $\theta_i\in\mathbb{R}$ is the latent score (log-strength). For a pair of items $i$ and $j$, the BT model specifies the probability that $i$ is preferred to $j$ (or "beats" $j$) as
\[
\mathbb{P}(i \succ j) = \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j} = \frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)}.
\]
Equivalently, in terms of $\theta$,
\[
\mathbb{P}(i \succ j) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(\theta_j - \theta_i)}.
\]
This logistic form shows the BT model is essentially a logistic regression for pairwise outcomes where the difference in scores $\theta_i - \theta_j$ drives the probability.

Intuition: if $w_i$ is much larger than $w_j$, then $i$ almost always wins; if $w_i$ equals $w_j$, the outcome is a coin flip; if $w_i$ is small relative to $w_j$, $i$ rarely wins. The BT model respects a sort of multiplicative scaling invariance: only the ratios $w_i/w_j$ matter, or equivalently the differences $\theta_i-\theta_j$.

Variants and interpretations: The BT model can be derived from latent utilities with Gumbel noise. If each item $k$ has utility $u_k=\theta_k + \varepsilon_k$ with $\varepsilon_k$ independent and distributed as standard Gumbel, then the probability that $i$ has higher utility than $j$ equals the BT form. This perspective links BT to choice models and extreme-value theory. Another viewpoint is to view a comparison outcome as a noisy measurement of the sign of $\theta_i-\theta_j$.

Data format and likelihood: If comparisons are observed as counts $n_{ij}$ of times $i$ beat $j$ (or as Bernoulli outcomes), the log-likelihood for $\theta$ given pairwise data is
\[
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} \left[ n_{ij} \log \frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)} + n_{ji} \log \frac{\exp(\theta_j)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)} \right].
\]
Simplifying,
\[
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} \big( n_{ij}\theta_i + n_{ji}\theta_j - (n_{ij}+n_{ji}) \log(\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)) \big).
\]
This form is the basis for maximum likelihood estimation and inference.


# Plackett–Luce ranking model and generalizations

The Plackett–Luce (PL) model generalizes the BT pairwise specification to ranking or listwise data. Let items have positive weights $w_i>0$ or log-scores $\theta_i$. When a complete ranking of a set $S$ of items is observed, the PL model assumes the ranking was generated by repeated sampling without replacement according to relative weights. Concretely, for a permutation (ranking) $\pi$ of the items in $S$ with top item $\pi_1$, second $\pi_2$, and so on, the PL probability is
\[
\mathbb{P}(\pi) = \prod_{t=1}^{|S|} \frac{w_{\pi_t}}{\sum_{k=t}^{|S|} w_{\pi_k}},
\]
where at step $t$ the chosen item is drawn from the remaining set with probability proportional to its weight. If $S$ is the entire item set, this gives a probability distribution over permutations. For pairwise data, PL reduces to BT: the probability that $i$ precedes $j$ in a ranking containing just $\{i,j\}$ is $w_i/(w_i+w_j)$.

Interpretation and Luce’s axiom: PL is rooted in Luce’s choice axiom, which says that if choices are made from subsets, the relative odds between two alternatives do not depend on the presence of other items. Formally, for any subset $A$ containing $i$ and $j$,
\[
\frac{\mathbb{P}(i \text{ chosen from } A)}{\mathbb{P}(j \text{ chosen from } A)} = \frac{w_i}{w_j}.
\]
The sequential sampling view of PL is consistent with this axiom.

Generalizations: There are several important generalizations. One is the conditional logit or multinomial logit model where weights depend on item features: $w_i=\exp(\beta^\top x_i)$, turning PL into an instance of a conditional choice model and enabling generalization across items. Another is the Mixed Plackett–Luce model, a latent-class mixture where there are several PL components and items are drawn according to a mixture of weights, capturing context-specific preferences. Other generalizations relax Luce’s axiom, for example the Mallows model (distance-based), or the exploded logit extensions that allow ties, top-$k$ rankings, or subset choices. The repeated-choice without replacement viewpoint extends naturally to partial rankings: if only the top-$k$ positions are observed, PL marginalizes over the unobserved tail by using only the first $k$ factors of the product.

Connections to random utility: The PL model can be obtained from a random utility model where each item $i$ has utility $u_i=\theta_i + \varepsilon_i$ with independent Gumbel errors and the ranking is obtained by ordering utilities. The sequential choice representation follows from the memoryless property of the Gumbel maxima.


# Maximum likelihood estimation for preference models

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the standard approach to infer latent scores $\theta$ or weights $w$ from observed comparisons or rankings. For BT and PL models, the log-likelihoods are concave in the log-weights $\theta$ (under appropriate parameterizations), which is crucial for optimization.

For the BT model with pairwise counts $n_{ij}$, the log-likelihood written earlier is differentiable. The gradient components are
\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_i} = \sum_{j\neq i} \big( n_{ij} - (n_{ij}+n_{ji}) \frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)} \big).
\]
Setting these derivatives to zero yields the likelihood equations, which are nonlinear. A convenient reexpression uses pairwise win counts $s_i = \sum_{j} n_{ij}$ and total matches $m_{ij}=n_{ij}+n_{ji}$ to write
\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_i} = s_i - \sum_{j\neq i} m_{ij}\frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)}.
\]
The Hessian is negative semidefinite, guaranteeing concavity over $\theta$ when the graph of comparisons is connected (modulo the identifiability constant discussed below).

For the PL model with observed full rankings or top-$k$ lists, the log-likelihood is a sum over rankings of terms of the form $\log w_{\pi_t} - \log \sum_{k=t}^{|S|} w_{\pi_k}$. Differentiating with respect to $\theta_i=\log w_i$ gives a gradient that balances how often item $i$ was chosen at a given stage against how often it was available and competed with others. The log-likelihood in $\theta$ is concave, which makes numerical maximization straightforward.

Algorithms for MLE: Because the log-likelihoods are concave, standard convex optimization methods apply. For BT, iterative proportional fitting-like algorithms such as the iterative scaling or Minorization–Maximization (MM) procedure of Hunter (2004) provide stable updates. The MM update for BT can be written in terms of expected losses and produces monotone increases in likelihood. Newton–Raphson can converge faster but requires computing and inverting the Hessian, which is expensive for large $n$. For PL, one can use gradient ascent, conjugate gradient, or Newton methods. When data are large and possibly streaming, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on the negative log-likelihood is a practical choice, especially when the model is parameterized with features.

Practical estimation often involves solving for $\theta$ up to an additive constant (see identifiability), which can be enforced by a constraint like $\sum_i \theta_i = 0$ or fixing one $\theta_i=0$.


# Identifiability and equivalence up to additive constants

A fundamental property of BT and PL models is non-identifiability of absolute scale: only relative differences of log-weights matter. If $\theta$ is a vector of log-weights, then for any constant $c\in\mathbb{R}$ the vector $\theta+c$ yields the same probabilities because $\exp(\theta_i+c)=\exp(c)\exp(\theta_i)$ and the multiplicative factor cancels in ratios. Thus parameters are identifiable only up to an additive constant. In the $w$-parametrization, multiplying all $w_i$ by the same positive constant leaves probabilities unchanged.

To obtain a unique solution, one adds a normalization constraint. Common choices are to set one parameter to zero, for example $\theta_1=0$, or to impose a sum constraint $\sum_i \theta_i =0$ or a mean-zero constraint $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \theta_i =0$. In the $w$-scale, people sometimes constrain $\sum_i w_i =1$, which interprets $w$ as a probability mass across items. The choice of constraint does not change relative comparisons or likelihood-based inference for contrasts, but it matters for parameter outputs and priors in Bayesian formulations.

Another identifiability matter concerns disconnected comparison graphs. Consider pairwise data where some items are never compared, or the comparison graph splits into disconnected components. Then the relative strengths across components cannot be learned: the MLE is not uniquely determined even after fixing an additive constant across the whole set. Practically, one must either impose separate normalizations per component or collect bridging comparisons that connect the components. This graph-theoretic viewpoint is central when designing experiments or data collection strategies.


# Regularization and calibration of latent scores

In finite-sample or sparse regimes, MLEs can be unstable: maximum likelihood may push some weights to zero (or log-weights to $-\infty$) if an item loses all comparisons, or may produce high-variance estimates when data are limited. Regularization and Bayesian priors are remedies that stabilize estimation and provide better predictive performance.

A common regularizer is an $L_2$ penalty on $\theta$, corresponding to a Gaussian prior in a Bayesian view:
\[
\text{penalty}(\theta) = -\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \theta_i^2.
\]
Maximizing penalized log-likelihood trades off fit to data with shrinkage toward zero (or toward the chosen center). Tunable parameter $\lambda>0$ controls the strength of shrinkage. This is straightforward to implement: add the penalty to the log-likelihood and optimize. The $L_2$ penalty also handles non-identifiability by pinning the scale implicitly; however one should still impose a centering constraint to avoid degenerate directions in some cases.

An alternative is a Dirichlet prior on $w$ (equivalently a log-Beta-like prior on normalized weights), which is conjugate for certain aggregated data and yields closed-form or efficient updates. A uniform Dirichlet ($\alpha=1$) corresponds to weak smoothing; $\alpha>1$ moves weights away from zero. In logistic parametrization, a Gaussian prior on $\theta$ is convenient computationally.

Calibration: Because these models map latent scores to choice probabilities, model calibration matters when comparing different datasets or when trying to interpret absolute magnitudes. If one wants calibrated probabilities, cross-validation or empirical Bayes can tune regularization parameters to minimize negative log-likelihood on held-out data. Calibration also includes fixing the additive constant so that scores are interpretable relative to some baseline.

Regularization is particularly important when generalizing via feature-based parameterizations ($\theta_i=\beta^\top x_i$). There the number of features can be large relative to data; standard techniques such as $L_1$ (sparsity), $L_2$, or elastic-net penalties and cross-validation remain applicable.
