# Value function learning and reward design

PPO, being an actor-critic method, typically pairs a policy network (the actor) with a value function network (the critic). The critic estimates the expected return from a given state: $V_\phi(s) \approx \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t r_{t} \mid s_0=s]$. Accurate value estimates reduce variance in policy gradients by providing a reliable baseline for advantage computation.

For language models, states correspond to prefixes or contexts and are high-dimensional sequences. The value function can reuse the base LM architecture (e.g., same transformer encoder) with a small head that maps the final hidden state to a scalar value. Parameter-sharing between policy and value networks is common because it reduces memory and can leverage shared representations. However, full parameter sharing can cause interference; often separate value-head parameters or separate small critic networks are used while still sharing the main transformer weights or frozen parts of them.

Value loss: The critic is trained to minimize a regression loss, most commonly the mean-squared error
\[
L_V(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{t}\big[ \big(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{R}_t\big)^2 \big],
\]
where $\hat{R}_t$ is a target return (e.g., the bootstrapped return or a lambda-return used in GAE). Some implementations use Huber loss for robustness or scale regularization to prevent extreme gradients.

Reward design is crucial in RLHF. The reward signal often comes from a learned reward model trained on human preference data, automated metrics, heuristics, or a combination. In preference-based RLHF, human annotators compare generated outputs (or rank them), and a reward model $r_\psi(x)$ is trained to predict these preferences. During PPO, the reward assigned to a rollout is the scalar score from $r_\psi$ (possibly normalized).

Key issues in reward design:

- Sparsity and credit assignment: Many user-level rewards are only available at sequence-level (e.g., human preference for an entire answer). Token-level credit assignment must rely on advantage estimators like GAE and the value function. Sparse rewards make variance large and can slow learning.

- Scaling and normalization: Reward model outputs may have arbitrary scale and distribution. PPO performance is sensitive to reward scale; excessively large magnitudes amplify gradients and may destabilize learning. It is common to normalize rewards (e.g., subtract running mean and divide by running standard deviation) or clip them.

- Shaping vs. true objectives: Designers sometimes add auxiliary rewards or penalties to shape behavior (e.g., length penalties, safety filters). Shaping can speed learning but can also create unintended incentives that encourage circumventing desired behavior.

- KL as implicit reward: To keep the policy close to the base LM, many RLHF pipelines incorporate a KL penalty term into the reward at each time step:
\[
r'_t = r_t - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid s_t)\;\|\;\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\big).
\]
Here $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference base model. This discourages extreme departures, with $\beta$ controlling the tradeoff. Including this penalty inside the reward ensures that the critic learns to predict returns that reflect these costs, maintaining consistent advantage estimates.

- Reward-model drift and misalignment: Learned reward models may have blind spots or be gamed by the policy. Regularly collecting new human data and monitoring outputs are essential to avoid over-optimizing spurious reward model weaknesses.

In summary, the critic and reward model form the backbone of reliable PPO updates. Careful architecture choices, proper target computation for the critic, and robust reward design (including normalization and KL penalties) are necessary for stable, aligned fine-tuning of language models.


# On-policy sampling loops and rollout generation

PPO is fundamentally on-policy: updates require data collected from the current policy. In the RLHF context, on-policy sampling entails generating text rollouts from the current policy in order to evaluate them with the reward model and compute advantages. The sampling loop encompasses data collection, reward evaluation, advantage calculation, and policy optimization.

Rollout generation for language models typically proceeds as follows. Starting from a prompt or context $c$, the policy autoregressively samples tokens according to $\pi_\theta$ until a stopping condition: a maximum length, an end-of-sequence token, or an externally defined termination condition. Because stochasticity in output is essential for exploration, sampling strategies like ancestral sampling or top-k/top-p sampling are used. However, these sampling methods influence the policy distribution and the importance ratios used in PPO: if sampling deviates from the simple $\pi_\theta$ probabilities (e.g., due to rejection sampling or nucleus sampling), corrections are needed to compute valid importance ratios. In practice, many implementations sample directly from the model’s categorical distribution to keep the on-policy condition intact.

Once a set of rollouts is generated, each sequence is scored by the reward model to obtain scalar returns. If rewards are sequence-level, each token in the rollout receives the same terminal reward (or a shaped per-token reward when used). The critic is used to compute bootstrapped values for intermediate states when applying GAE. Then advantages are computed and used in the surrogate loss.

Batching and parallelization: Collecting on-policy rollouts efficiently requires batching many prompts and parallel generation across GPUs/TPUs/accelerators. Because language-model generation is auto-regressive and latency-sensitive, throughput optimizations—such as cached key-value attention states, vectorized sampling for many sequences, and mixed-precision computation—are essential. For long sequences, storing per-token activations for gradient computation can be memory-intensive; PPO training often uses truncated backpropagation or recomputation techniques.

Data reuse and horizon: On-policy means data used to compute gradients should be generated by the policy that produced the behavior distribution. Some implementations perform multiple optimization epochs over the same batch of on-policy data (a few epochs, e.g., 4–8), which is a pragmatic compromise: it reuses the same data to make optimization efficient but risks violating strict on-policy assumptions if updates are large. Clipped objectives mitigate this by limiting the impact of reusing data. Still, measuring the KL between the updated and behavior policy can flag when data has become stale and further optimization may overfit.

Prompts and context management: In RLHF, prompts are often user-provided or sampled from a dataset. One must decide whether to keep prompts fixed across updates or to sample new prompts each rollout. Using a diverse set of prompts helps generalize improvements. Additionally, different prompts can lead to different sequence lengths and reward structures; batching and padding strategies must account for this to compute per-token losses consistently.

Safety and filtering during sampling: Because exploration can produce unsafe or low-quality outputs, it is common to pre-filter rollouts before scoring or to include safety classifiers in the reward model. However, filtering changes the distribution of observed data and must be considered when computing on-policy estimators.


# Hyperparameters and training stability

PPO’s practical success owes much to engineering choices and hyperparameter tuning. Many hyperparameters affect convergence, stability, and sample efficiency. Important ones include the clipping parameter $\epsilon$, number of optimization epochs per batch, batch size (number of episodes or tokens), learning rate(s) for policy and value networks, discount factor $\gamma$, GAE parameter $\lambda$, entropy bonus coefficient, value loss coefficient, reward scaling, and any KL penalty strength.

Clipping parameter $\epsilon$: Common values are in the range $0.05$ to $0.3$. Smaller $\epsilon$ makes updates more conservative but may slow learning; larger $\epsilon$ allows more aggressive updates at a risk of instability. The right choice depends on model size and reward noise.

Number of epochs over each batch: PPO often performs multiple passes (e.g., 3–10) over each collected batch to improve sample efficiency. More epochs increase optimization of the surrogate but risk overfitting to the finite batch. Clipping helps here by limiting how much the policy can exploit the same data.

Batch size and sequence length: Large minibatches, measured in tokens or episodes, reduce gradient variance and improve stability, but increase memory and compute. For language models, batch size measured in tokens is especially relevant because sequences vary in length.

Learning rates and separate optimizers: Typically, the policy (LM) and value networks have separate learning rates. The value network often requires a smaller learning rate to avoid destabilizing advantage estimates, or conversely may need a larger one if critic learning lags. Using Adam with carefully tuned beta parameters and weight decay is common.

Entropy bonus: Adding an entropy bonus $-c_{\text{ent}} \mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta]$ encourages exploration and prevents premature convergence to deterministic policies. In language settings, excessive entropy can degrade quality, so small coefficients are used, or entropy regularization is omitted and exploration comes from stochastic sampling.

Reward normalization and clipping: Normalize rewards to keep gradient scales consistent across updates. Clip extremes to reduce the impact of outliers or mislabelled data.

Value loss coefficient: The relative weighting between policy surrogate loss, value loss, and entropy affects training dynamics. Typical PPO loss is a weighted sum:
\[
L(\theta,\phi) = -L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) + c_V L_V(\phi) - c_{\text{ent}} \mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta].
\]
Choosing $c_V$ too small leads to poor value estimates and noisy advantages; too large can force the optimizer to prioritize value regression over policy improvement.

Stability techniques: Gradient clipping helps prevent exploding gradients. Learning-rate schedules (e.g., warmup and decay) are important. Early stopping based on KL thresholds (stop updates for a batch if average KL exceeds a target) prevents over-aggressive updates. Monitoring metrics such as average reward, average KL to reference, advantage statistics, and validation prompts helps detect divergence.

Hyperparameter sensitivity grows with model size and reward noise. For large LMs, small changes in learning rate or clipping can have outsized effects due to the scale of gradients and the complexity of the function approximator. Automated tuning (grid search or Bayesian optimization) and conservative defaults (smaller learning rates, smaller clip) are common starting points.


# Mode collapse and reward overoptimization risks

When optimizing a generative model using a scalar reward, the policy may discover ways to exploit the reward function that reduce output diversity or produce degenerate but high-scoring outputs. Two major failure modes are mode collapse and reward hacking.

Mode collapse refers to the policy concentrating probability mass on a narrow set of outputs that yield high reward, thereby losing the model’s original diversity. In language models, this might manifest as repeatedly producing the same phrasing that the reward model favors or preferring safe, bland responses that reliably get positive scores. Mode collapse reduces utility in open-ended tasks where diversity and adaptiveness matter.

Reward overoptimization (reward hacking) arises when the policy exploits imperfections or shortcuts in the reward model. For example, if the reward model correlates certain superficial tokens or formats with high human preference, the policy may inject those tokens to boost the score regardless of user intent. Since a learned reward model can never perfectly capture human preferences, continual monitoring is necessary.

Strategies to mitigate these risks include:

- KL penalties or constraints relative to the base model: Penalizing divergence discourages the model from moving too far from its pretrained behavior, which helps retain diversity.

- Entropy regularization: Encouraging higher entropy mitigates collapse. However, too much entropy leads to lower-quality outputs.

- Reward randomization and augmentation: Training with varied reward signals or perturbing reward-model inputs reduces the chance of overfitting to narrow cues.

- Frequent human evaluation and dataset refreshing: Periodically collecting new human preference data on model outputs helps correct reward-model blind spots and prevents drift.

- Conservative learning rates and clipping: Keeping updates modest reduces the chance that the model rapidly converges to a degenerate strategy.

- Penalize repetition explicitly: Include penalties for repeated tokens or n-gram repeats if the reward model fails to do so.

- Ensemble or adversarial reward models: Using ensembles of reward models or adversarially trained critics makes it harder for the policy to exploit any one model’s flaws.

- Calibration of reward scale: Prevent runaway incentives by normalizing and bounding reward magnitudes. If reward model outputs can be arbitrarily large for certain spurious patterns, the policy will amplify those.

Detecting reward hacking early requires monitoring metrics beyond the reward value, such as diversity statistics, distribution shifts in generated tokens, and manually reviewing high-reward samples. For safety-critical or customer-facing systems, conservative deployment with human-in-the-loop checks is necessary.
