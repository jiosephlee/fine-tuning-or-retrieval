Chapter 5: Decoding Strategies and Sampling from LMs

Introduction

Language models (LMs) are trained to assign conditional probabilities to the next token given a context. At generation time we must convert those conditional probability distributions into discrete sequences of tokens that form text. The choice of decoding strategy is central to the quality, diversity, and reliability of generated text. Some strategies are deterministic and aim to maximize probability (greedy decoding, beam search); others are stochastic and trade off fidelity to the model distribution for diversity (sampling with temperature, top‑k, top‑p). This chapter explains the major decoding algorithms, the mathematical and empirical effects of temperature scaling and truncation methods, the tradeoffs between deterministic and stochastic decoding, how decoding hyperparameters interact with sequence‑level metrics, and selection strategies such as best‑of‑N sampling. Throughout, emphasis is placed on intuition, mathematical description, and how practitioners select and tune methods for different tasks.

# Greedy decoding and beam search

Greedy decoding and beam search are deterministic strategies that attempt to find high‑probability sequences according to the model. They operate in the space of token sequences and use the conditional distribution produced by the LM at each time step, which we denote by $P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ where $x_{<t}$ is the prefix and $\theta$ are model parameters.

Greedy decoding is the simplest: at each time step choose the single token with maximum conditional probability,
\[
x_t^\ast = \arg\max_{w} P_\theta(w \mid x_{<t}).
\]
This produces a single sequence quickly and with minimal computation. Greedy decoding is equivalent to performing a locally optimal choice at each step, but it need not be globally optimal in terms of sequence probability because the greedy choice may lead to a prefix from which all completions are poor. Greedy decoding tends to be safe and conservative: it typically produces high‑probability, repetitive, or generic outputs but can miss longer sequences that are globally higher probability.

Beam search generalizes greedy decoding by keeping multiple candidate prefixes at each time step. With beam width $B$, beam search maintains a set of up to $B$ partial hypotheses. At each step $t$, for each candidate prefix $h$ in the beam, the model proposes a distribution over next tokens; the algorithm forms the $B \times V$ possible one‑step extensions (where $V$ is vocabulary size), computes their scores, and retains the top $B$ prefixes by score. If we use log probabilities and define the score of a length‑$T$ sequence $x_{1:T}$ as the sum of log conditional probabilities,
\[
\mathrm{score}(x_{1:T}) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}),
\]
then beam search approximates the search for the sequence maximizing this score. Beam search reduces to greedy decoding when $B=1$.

Beam search has desirable properties relative to greedy decoding: it can recover globally better sequences that require exploring several alternatives before committing. It is widely used in machine translation and other structured generation tasks where maximizing sequence probability correlates with quality. However, beam search has several caveats. First, the raw sequence probability tends to favor shorter outputs because the product (or sum of logs) of probabilities often decreases with length. This is commonly mitigated by applying length normalization, for example dividing the score by $T^\alpha$ or by using an additive length penalty. Second, beam search can produce repetitive or degenerate sequences, especially with large beam widths. This happens because the model assigns high probability to repeating certain phrases, and beam search amplifies this by exploring many similar continuations. Third, maximizing sequence probability under the model is only desirable if the model is well calibrated with respect to human judgments or downstream metrics; otherwise, beam search systematically exploits model biases.

Variants of beam search include diverse beam search (which tries to encourage diversity among beams by penalizing beams that are similar), stochastic beam search (sample next tokens proportionally to adjusted scores), and constrained beam search (enforce constraints like required tokens). Understanding the interplay between beam width, scoring adjustments (length normalization), and post‑processing is essential to obtaining good outputs for a given task.

# Temperature scaling and its effect on entropy

Temperature is a simple and powerful scalar parameter used to modify the sharpness of a probability distribution before sampling. Given a model’s logit outputs $z_w$ for token $w$ (logits being the unnormalized scores), the model probabilities are
\[
P_\theta(w) = \frac{\exp(z_w)}{\sum_{v} \exp(z_v)}.
\]
Temperature $T>0$ rescales logits as $z_w/T$, producing temperature‑adjusted probabilities
\[
P_{\theta,T}(w) = \frac{\exp(z_w / T)}{\sum_{v} \exp(z_v / T)}.
\]
When $T=1$ we recover the original distribution. When $T<1$ the distribution becomes sharper: larger logits get relatively more mass, reducing entropy. In the limit $T \to 0^+$, $P_{\theta,T}$ approaches a point mass at the argmax token (equivalent to greedy decoding if sampled). When $T>1$ the distribution flattens, increasing entropy, and in the limit $T \to \infty$ it approaches the uniform distribution.

To see the effect on entropy quantitatively, recall entropy of the distribution $P$ is $H(P) = -\sum_w P(w)\log P(w)$. Temperature scaling transforms $P$ nonlinearly; differentiating entropy with respect to $T$ is complex in general, but the monotonic behavior is intuitive: lowering $T$ reduces entropy, raising $T$ increases entropy. More formally, if logits are finite and not all equal, entropy is a continuous monotone increasing function of $T$ over $(0,\infty)$: as $T$ increases the distribution becomes more uniform and entropy increases, and vice versa.

Temperature is used to control diversity in sampling. Lower temperatures concentrate sampling on high‑probability tokens, producing more conservative and repetitive outputs. Higher temperatures allow exploration of lower probability tokens, increasing diversity but also the chance of incoherence. In practice $T$ values in the range $0.7$ to $1.2$ are common when sampling from large LMs for open‑ended generation. For tasks requiring faithful reproduction of particular facts (e.g., deterministic question answering), very low $T$ or greedy/beam search is preferred to avoid inventing content.

An important subtlety is that temperature does not change the relative ordering of tokens by probability — it only changes how peaked the distribution is. Thus it cannot make a low‑rank token higher than a high‑rank token; other truncation techniques are required if you want to allow tokens that were originally outside the top set.

# Top‑k and top‑p (nucleus) sampling

Top‑k and top‑p (nucleus) sampling are truncation strategies that restrict sampling to a subset of tokens with the goal of preventing low‑probability tail tokens from being sampled while preserving diversity among reasonably likely tokens.

Top‑k sampling restricts the candidate set to the $k$ tokens with largest probability under the model. Concretely, at each time step compute the original probabilities $P_\theta(w \mid x_{<t})$, then let $V_k$ be the set of $k$ tokens with highest probability. Define the truncated distribution
\[
P^{\mathrm{top\text{-}k}}(w) = \begin{cases}
\frac{P_\theta(w)}{\sum_{v\in V_k} P_\theta(v)} & \text{if } w\in V_k,\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Sampling from $P^{\mathrm{top\text{-}k}}$ ensures that extremely unlikely tokens are never chosen, and $k$ controls the maximum breadth of choices. Small $k$ (e.g., $k=1$) reduces to greedy sampling; larger $k$ increases diversity but may include noisy tokens.

Top‑p or nucleus sampling adapts the truncation size dynamically based on cumulative probability. Sort tokens by probability in descending order and choose the smallest set $V_p$ such that
\[
\sum_{w\in V_p} P_\theta(w) \ge p,
\]
for some threshold $p\in(0,1]$. Then renormalize and sample from that set. The nucleus method has the advantage of adapting to the context: when the model is confident, $V_p$ will be small; when the model is uncertain, $V_p$ will be larger. This often yields better tradeoffs between coherence and diversity than a fixed $k$.

Practically, values like $p=0.9$ or $p=0.95$ are common. Top‑p tends to be more stable across contexts than top‑k since $k$ that is appropriate for one context might be too small or too large for another. In both top‑k and top‑p sampling one may also combine temperature scaling: compute logits, divide by $T$, then apply truncation and renormalize. The order is important: temperature before truncation is standard and intuitive because temperature changes the mass distribution that determines which tokens fall into top sets.

Truncation reduces the probability mass assigned to very unlikely tokens, mitigating glaring errors while preserving some stochasticity. Still, truncation cannot fix systematic model errors — if the high‑probability region contains bad tokens (e.g., factually incorrect but plausible continuations), top‑k or top‑p will still sample them.

# Deterministic vs stochastic decoding tradeoffs

Decoding strategies fall broadly into deterministic (greedy decoding, beam search with small randomness only in tie breaking) and stochastic (sampling with temperature, top‑k, top‑p). Deterministic decoding tends to produce outputs that are high‑probability under the model and reproducible: given the same input and deterministic algorithm, output is identical. This reproducibility is valuable for tasks requiring reliability, such as automated systems, and for evaluation metrics that measure fidelity to a reference. Deterministic methods also often yield high scores on metrics that reward grammaticality and literal correctness when the model is well trained.

However, deterministic decoding can lead to low diversity, repetitive outputs, and degeneration due to over‑optimization of the model’s likelihoods (e.g., repeated phrases or generic templates). Beam search, despite its intent to maximize sequence probability, can exacerbate repetition and produce pathological outputs if the model has biases toward certain constructs.

Stochastic decoding trades reproducibility for diversity. Sampling allows the model’s distribution to shape outputs, producing more varied and sometimes more creative text. For open‑ended tasks, stochastic methods often produce more humanlike and engaging outputs. They also make it easier to generate multiple hypotheses for downstream selection or human review.

The choice between deterministic and stochastic decoding depends on the task. For tasks with a single correct answer or where factual precision is essential (translation, summarization to match a reference, knowledge retrieval), deterministic methods or low‑entropy sampling (low temperature, small top‑p) are often better. For creative writing, dialogue, brainstorming, or data augmentation, stochastic sampling with higher temperature or larger nucleus values yields preferred diversity.

A further tradeoff is between sample quality and controllability. Deterministic methods are more controllable: small changes to model or hyperparameters produce predictable effects. Stochastic outputs can be tuned with temperature and truncation, but they also introduce variance; metrics computed over single samples may be noisy, so multiple samples and aggregation are used.

Finally, a hybrid approach is often beneficial: use stochastic sampling to produce a set of candidate sequences, then apply a deterministic scoring function (possibly using the model's own likelihood or an external evaluator) to choose the best candidate. This is the idea behind best‑of‑N sampling and reranking, discussed below.

# Sequence‑level metrics and decoding hyperparameters

Decoding hyperparameters (temperature, top‑k, top‑p, beam width) interact with sequence‑level metrics, and tuning them requires understanding what the metric rewards. Common sequence‑level metrics include BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR for reference‑based tasks, perplexity and log probability for model fit, and human evaluation for fluency, coherence, factuality, and relevance. Other automated metrics for open‑ended generation include distinct‑n (diversity measures), BERTScore (semantic similarity to a reference), and task‑specific quality measures.

For tasks where a reference exists and fidelity is measured by overlap metrics (e.g., translation with BLEU), maximizing sequence probability under the model (beam search with appropriate normalization) often yields good performance because the training objective typically encourages assigning high probability to the reference. Aggressive stochastic sampling will reduce overlap with the reference and thus lower BLEU/ROUGE scores even if the text is fluent.

For open‑ended tasks without a single reference, metrics that measure diversity benefit from sampling strategies with higher temperature and larger nucleus. However, increasing diversity may degrade coherence and factuality. Distinct‑n measures (the fraction of unique n‑grams) increase with more stochasticity, but these metrics do not capture correctness.

Tuning hyperparameters typically requires specifying the downstream metric of interest and performing validation. For reference‑based tasks, one can grid search over beam widths and length penalties to maximize BLEU/ROUGE on a held‑out validation set. For open‑ended or human‑centric tasks, tune temperature and top‑p based on human judgments or proxy automatic metrics that correlate with human preference (e.g., BERTScore, learned evaluators). It is important to tune on data that reflect the deployment distribution: parameter choices that work for short prompts may not generalize to long prompts.

Additionally, sequence‑level metrics often encourage post‑processing techniques such as reranking. For example, generate $N$ stochastic candidates and rerank them by the model log probability or by an external scoring model trained to predict human preference. This can significantly improve sequence‑level metrics by combining the diversity of sampling with the selectivity of deterministic scoring.

Be aware of the “beam search curse”: increasing beam width beyond some point can worsen performance on human metrics because the model’s scoring function does not align perfectly with human judgments. Thus beam width and other hyperparameters must be validated empirically.

# Best‑of‑N sampling as a selection strategy

Best‑of‑N (also called sample‑and‑rank) is a hybrid approach that uses stochastic generation to produce a set of candidate sequences, then selects the best candidate according to a scoring function. The procedure is: sample $N$ completions independently using a sampling strategy (temperature, top‑p, etc.), compute a score for each candidate (which can be the model log probability, a sequence‑level metric, or an external evaluator), and return the candidate with highest score.

Best‑of‑N leverages the diversity of sampling to explore multiple modes of the model’s distribution, while using the scoring function to pick a high‑quality—or higher probability—output. If the model’s log probability aligns well with quality, selecting the highest log‑probability sample from the $N$ will often yield better outputs than a single sample or greedy decoding. This is because sampling can discover high‑probability sequences that beam search might miss (due to search limitations or length normalization issues), and reranking mitigates the randomness by choosing the best among many.

There are several design choices. First, how to sample the $N$ candidates: sampling can be done with temperature and truncation tuned to encourage diversity but not produce outright gibberish. Second, which scoring function to use: the model’s own log probability is cheap and often effective, but it may prefer shorter or formulaic sequences; external scorers trained on human preference can yield better selections when available. Third, whether to ensure candidate diversity explicitly (e.g., forcing minimum pairwise edit distance) or rely on stochasticity.

Best‑of‑N raises computational and evaluation considerations. Sampling $N$ candidates and scoring them increases inference cost linearly in $N$. The marginal gain often diminishes with larger $N$, so practical choices balance cost and benefit: $N$ in the tens is common for research experiments; production systems may use smaller $N$ with a fast reranker. Another subtlety is selection bias: if the scorer is the same model that generated candidates, we may simply be selecting the candidate the model already believes is best; however, sampling can expose diverse near‑optimal candidates that were not reached by greedy search.

From a theoretical perspective, best‑of‑N approximates sampling from the top portion of the model’s distribution and then performing an implicit maximization within that subset. If the scorer is the true utility function one cares about, best‑of‑N can be seen as Monte Carlo optimization. If the scorer is model log probability, best‑of‑N is a way to amplify the chance of finding high‑probability modes with limited search.

Conclusion

Decoding is where the probabilities learned by a language model are turned into text, and the choices made here strongly affect model behavior. Deterministic methods like greedy decoding and beam search favor high‑probability sequences but can be repetitive or exploit model biases. Temperature scaling controls distribution entropy and is a simple knob to adjust conservatism versus diversity. Top‑k and top‑p sampling provide structured ways to truncate the tail and maintain sensible stochasticity, with top‑p adapting to context. Tradeoffs between deterministic and stochastic decoding revolve around reproducibility, diversity, and task alignment. Sequence‑level metrics guide hyperparameter selection and often motivate hybrid methods: best‑of‑N sampling combines sampling diversity with reranking to select higher‑quality outputs. In practice, decoding strategy should be chosen and tuned with the target task, metric, computational budget, and human preferences in mind. Understanding these methods and their limitations is essential to interpret and improve the outputs of language models in research and applications.
