Introduction

Pre-trained language models (LMs) provide a powerful foundation for many downstream NLP tasks. However, out-of-the-box pre-trained models do not reliably perform specific tasks when prompted; they require adaptation. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and instruction tuning are two closely related strategies to bridge this gap. In SFT, a model is trained on labeled input–output pairs so that it learns a mapping from task descriptions or inputs to desired outputs. Instruction tuning extends this idea by training on a diverse mixture of tasks phrased as natural-language instructions, encouraging the model to follow novel instructions at inference time. This chapter examines how instruction and demonstration datasets are curated, the objectives and common failure modes of SFT, how instruction following promotes task generalization, issues of data quality and contamination, methods to prevent overfitting, the relationship between demonstrations and preference-based alignment, how SFT can establish reference policies used later in preference learning, and how to evaluate SFT models robustly.

Curating Instruction and Demonstration Datasets

The starting point for SFT and instruction tuning is a dataset of examples that pair inputs (often with an instruction) with desired outputs. The dataset may contain single-task data (e.g., classification labels), multi-task collections, or explicit instruction–response pairs. Effective curation balances scale, quality, diversity, and safety.

Data sources can be grouped into several categories. Human-written demonstrations—where human annotators produce both instructions and ideal responses—are high quality but expensive. Synthetic data can be generated by models (self-instruct strategies) or programmatically transformed from existing corpora. Task-specific datasets (e.g., SQuAD, GLUE) provide labeled examples for particular tasks and are often repurposed into instruction–response format by wrapping inputs with natural-language prompts. Public multi-task mixes, such as FLAN or SuperNI, collect many tasks phrased as instructions. Lastly, scraped web data can be cleaned and heuristically paired with instructions.

Curation decisions include choosing which tasks to include, how many demonstrations per task, and how to format instructions. A key objective is to produce instruction–response pairs that are consistent and informative. For demonstration-style datasets (few-shot style), curators must decide whether to show multiple example input–output pairs within a single prompt at training time, and whether to randomize or canonicalize the demonstration order.

Diversity is crucial: exposing the model to many task formulations, synonyms, and varying contexts helps generalization to novel instructions. However, uncontrolled diversity can introduce noise. Data should be annotated or filtered for correctness, relevance, and safety. For example, some tasks require grounding or factual verification; including incorrect demonstrations will teach the model incorrect mappings.

Finally, data governance must prevent contamination: examples derived from test sets used for evaluation, or from the model’s own pre-training corpus, can produce overestimates of capability. Curators should track provenance and apply deduplication methods (e.g., fuzzy hashing, n-gram overlap checks) to avoid leaking evaluation instances into training.

Supervised Fine-Tuning Objectives and Pitfalls

The canonical objective for SFT is to minimize the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the target outputs given the inputs (and optionally instructions). For an autoregressive decoder model parameterized by $\theta$, and dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}$, the typical loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}} \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x),
\]
where $y_{<t}$ are previous tokens of the response. For encoder–decoder models, the loss is analogous with an encoder representation of $x$. Minimizing this objective trains the model to put high probability mass onto the reference outputs.

Although straightforward, this objective has practical pitfalls. First, it treats each reference as the single authoritative response, even when multiple correct outputs exist. Penalizing alternative valid outputs can reduce diversity and lead the model to prefer common or short responses ("mode collapse"). Second, if the dataset contains noisy or inconsistent annotations, SFT will reproduce that noise. Third, the NLL objective is indifferent to downstream utility beyond surface match; it does not directly reward attributes like helpfulness, factuality, or safety.

Another pitfall is exposure bias: during training the model conditions on ground-truth prefixes, while at inference it conditions on its own previous tokens. This mismatch can cause drift. Teacher forcing used in standard NLL training reduces sequence-level robustness. Scheduled sampling and sequence-level objectives are partial mitigations but introduce complexity and are not universally adopted in instruction tuning.

Instruction Following and Task Generalization

Instruction tuning aims to teach LMs to interpret and follow natural-language instructions broadly, enabling zero-shot or few-shot generalization to unseen tasks. Conceptually, instruction tuning frames many tasks in a unified format so the model learns a mapping from instruction semantics to behavior. Two major mechanisms support generalization.

First, structural generalization: by training on many tasks that share structure (e.g., "translate X", "summarize Y", "extract entities from Z"), the model learns abstract operators that can be composed. When presented with a novel instruction that composes familiar primitives, the model can generalize. This relies on the dataset containing sufficient compositional examples and diversity in phrasing.

Second, paraphrase generalization: training on multiple linguistic formulations of the same task helps the model associate different surface forms with the same underlying operation. This reduces brittleness to prompt wording. In practice, explicit paraphrase augmentation and talk-through explanations help.

However, instruction following is not perfect. Models trained by SFT can overfit to superficial cues, such as instruction templates or frequent answer styles, rather than genuinely understanding the requested transformation. They may also rely on spurious correlations present in training tasks. To encourage robust instruction following, curation should include adversarially paraphrased instructions, diverse examples, and tasks that disambiguate based on content rather than wording.

Data Quality, Diversity, and Contamination

High-quality training data is the single most important factor in successful SFT. Quality means correctness, clarity, and appropriateness. Diversity refers to the breadth of tasks, domains, and linguistic expressions. Both interact: a diverse dataset with low quality yields unpredictable behavior, while high-quality but narrow data limits generalization.

Methods for ensuring quality include human review, multi-annotator agreement, gold-standard validation sets, and automatic checks (e.g., consistency tests, answer format validation). For tasks requiring factual accuracy, cross-checking outputs against trusted sources is important. For safety-critical domains, specialized reviewers and safety filters are necessary.

Diversity should be intentional. It is beneficial to include varying instruction styles (imperative, interrogative), differing input lengths, and different domains (technical, conversational, creative). Including tasks with multiple acceptable outputs and varying evaluation metrics (e.g., exact match, BLEU, human preference) teaches the model to handle ambiguity.

Contamination is a persistent issue. If test or benchmark examples appear in the SFT data, evaluation will be biased. Contamination arises both from explicit copying and subtle paraphrase overlap. Common defenses are strict data provenance tracking, approximate nearest-neighbor checks between training examples and benchmark sets, and content-based deduplication pipelines. When synthetic data is generated from models, care must be taken that the seed examples used for generation are not from evaluation pools.

Overfitting and Regularization During SFT

Overfitting in SFT emerges when the model memorizes dataset patterns that do not generalize, leading to poor performance on new instructions. Symptoms include high training likelihoods but degraded zero-shot or few-shot generalization, brittle responses to paraphrases, and inappropriate confidence. Several strategies mitigate overfitting.

Data-centric regularization is often the first line of defense. Increasing dataset size, adding diverse tasks, and including noisy or contrastive examples make memorization harder. Data augmentation (paraphrasing instructions, perturbing examples) helps the model become robust to superficial variations.

Model-centric regularization includes weight decay, dropout (when applicable), and early stopping based on validation tasks that measure instruction-following generalization rather than only in-distribution loss. Learning-rate schedules and smaller batch sizes can also shape generalization. Techniques like label smoothing are less common in language modeling but sometimes used to prevent overconfident outputs.

Sequence-level regularization can mitigate exposure bias. Scheduled sampling gradually replaces ground-truth prefixes with model-generated ones during training, reducing dependence on teacher forcing. Reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning with sequence-level rewards can explicitly optimize for downstream metrics, but requires careful reward design and can introduce instability.

Another useful technique is multi-objective training: combine the NLL loss with auxiliary objectives that encourage desired properties, such as factuality checks, consistency penalties across paraphrases, or instruction–response alignment losses (e.g., contrastive objectives that push correct responses closer to the instruction embedding than incorrect ones).

Alignment via Demonstrations vs. Preferences

Alignment refers to making model outputs consistent with human values, safety constraints, or broader desiderata like helpfulness and truthfulness. Two complementary approaches to alignment are demonstrations (SFT) and preference learning (RL from human feedback, RLHF).

Demonstration-based alignment uses curated examples to show desirable behavior. It is effective at teaching explicit mappings and safe-answer patterns. Demonstrations are straightforward to collect for many tasks and can encode complex behaviors as sequences of tokens. However, demonstrations can be costly and may not capture nuanced human preferences across many contexts. Moreover, demonstrations implicitly define a policy but do not provide a scalar judgment of relative preference between outputs.

Preference-based alignment elicits scalar comparisons (which of two outputs is better) and uses these to train a reward model that captures human preferences beyond what demonstrations encode. Reinforcement learning can then optimize the model under this learned reward. Preference learning excels when preferences are hard to specify as explicit outputs, such as subtleties in style, tone, or safety. It can also sometimes correct biases learned from demonstrations.

The two approaches are complementary. Demonstrations supply a dense, informative signal that can bootstrap a reasonable policy and be used to construct a reference policy for subsequent preference learning. Preference learning refines behavior where demonstrations are insufficient—e.g., balancing competing objectives or capturing fine-grained trade-offs. A practical pipeline often employs SFT to produce a capable starting policy, then collects preferences over model outputs to train a reward model and refine the policy via RLHF.

Reference Policy Construction from SFT

A reference policy is a baseline model that generates candidate outputs used during preference collection and RLHF. SFT models often serve as the reference policy for several reasons. First, SFT produces reliably coherent responses that are more likely than pre-trained-only models to follow instructions, leading to higher-quality comparisons. Second, sampling from an SFT model yields diverse yet plausible outputs, improving the informativeness of preference labels.

Building a reference policy involves choices about temperature, decoding strategy, and whether to include demonstrations in the prompt. For preference collection, it is useful to generate multiple diverse responses per prompt to expose annotators to a spectrum of behaviors. Diversity can be obtained through nucleus sampling, varying temperatures, or generating with different model checkpoints. Care must be taken: if a reference policy is too narrow (e.g., deterministic greedy decoding), preferences will be less informative because options will be similar; if it is too random, annotators may be forced to compare many poor outputs, reducing label quality.

There is also the option to combine multiple SFT models or ensembles to act as a richer reference policy. However, ensemble outputs need normalization and consistent formatting for preference labeling. When the SFT model contains biases or unsafe tendencies, the reference-policy samples can propagate these into the reward model; collecting preferences that explicitly address those issues mitigates this risk.

Evaluation of SFT Models

Evaluating SFT models requires multiple complementary methods because single metrics can be misleading. Evaluation dimensions include task accuracy, instruction-following generalization, robustness, and alignment attributes like helpfulness and safety.

Task-specific metrics remain useful for narrow tasks: accuracy, F1, BLEU, ROUGE, exact match. For instruction following and generalization, zero-shot and few-shot evaluation on held-out tasks is essential. Benchmarks such as BIG-bench, SuperGLUE variants, and instruction-centric suites (e.g., MMLU, HumanEval for code) test breadth. Importantly, evaluation sets must be strictly held out and decontaminated from training data.

Human evaluations play a central role for alignment-relevant qualities. Pairwise preference judgments are common: annotators compare two model outputs for a prompt with respect to helpfulness, safety, or other axes. Ratings scales and post-hoc error analysis reveal qualitative failure modes. Because human labeling is expensive, active selection of evaluation prompts (including adversarial or edge cases) maximizes insight.

Automatic proxy metrics can help scale evaluation. Likelihood-based measures, calibration assessments, and model-based critique systems (e.g., chain-of-thought critics) can identify factual errors or inconsistencies. However, proxies should be treated with caution and validated against human judgments.

Robustness testing includes prompt paraphrases, input perturbations, and adversarial examples that exploit instruction ambiguities. Distribution-shift evaluations that place models in different domains reveal generalization limits. Safety evaluation should assess toxic generation, policy-influenced behavior, and susceptibility to jailbreaks; red-team exercises and scenario-based tests are useful here.

Finally, when SFT is part of a larger pipeline leading to RLHF, the SFT model is often evaluated as a component: how well does it serve as a reference policy, and does subsequent preference learning improve or degrade properties such as factuality and diversity. Ablation studies that vary dataset composition, instruction style, and SFT hyperparameters help diagnose what drives performance.

Conclusion

Supervised fine-tuning and instruction tuning are foundational techniques for adapting pre-trained language models to perform specific tasks and to follow natural-language instructions. Careful dataset curation—balancing quality, diversity, and contamination controls—underpins successful SFT. The maximum-likelihood training objective is simple and effective but has pitfalls including exposure bias, overfitting, and sensitivity to noise. Instruction tuning promotes broad generalization through exposure to diverse tasks and phrasings, yet models can still latch onto spurious cues if data is not well designed. Regularization strategies span data augmentation, model regularizers, and sequence-level methods. For alignment, demonstrations and preference learning play complementary roles: SFT provides a practical baseline policy and can be used to generate reference outputs, while preference-based methods refine behaviors that are hard to encode directly. Evaluation must be multifaceted, combining task metrics, zero-shot generalization tests, human preferences, and robustness and safety assessments. When designed and executed well, SFT and instruction tuning produce models that are both more useful and better foundations for downstream preference-based alignment.
