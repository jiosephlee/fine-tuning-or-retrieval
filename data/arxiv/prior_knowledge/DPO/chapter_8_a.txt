Introduction

Reward modeling from preferences is a central technique in modern alignment and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Instead of asking humans to write explicit reward functions or to label every possible action, we collect preferences: comparisons of two outputs, trajectories, or behaviors, and use them to learn a scalar reward function that predicts which of two items a human prefers. This chapter explains how reward modeling is formalized as learning a latent score function, how we convert preference labels into losses, practical normalization and calibration tricks, architectural choices, training dynamics, robustness to label noise, how to evaluate reward models, and the fundamental limitations that arise from under-specification. The goal is to give you the mathematical intuition, the training recipes used in practice, and a clear view of the failure modes so you can read current papers and design experiments.


# Formulating reward as latent score functions

The basic conceptual model is that there exists an underlying scalar function $r(\cdot)$ that assigns a latent "preference score" to each item (an item can be a trajectory, a candidate response, or any object being compared). When a human is asked to compare two items $x_A$ and $x_B$, they probabilistically prefer the one with the higher latent score. Formally, we posit a latent score function $r_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}$ parameterized by $\theta$. The simplest probabilistic model for pairwise comparisons is to assume that the probability a human prefers $x_A$ over $x_B$ depends only on the difference $r_\theta(x_A) - r_\theta(x_B)$.

There are several classical statistical models in the psychometric and ranking literature that implement this idea. The Bradley–Terry model uses a logistic link: the probability $P(A \succ B)$ that $A$ is preferred to $B$ is
$$
P(A \succ B) = \sigma\big(r_\theta(x_A) - r_\theta(x_B)\big) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_\theta(x_A) - r_\theta(x_B))}},
$$
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function. The Thurstone-Mosteller model uses a Gaussian cumulative distribution function: $P(A \succ B) = \Phi\big((r_\theta(x_A) - r_\theta(x_B))/\sqrt{2}\big)$. These differ only in the noise assumption (logistic vs Gaussian), but they share the latent-score core: comparisons reveal relative differences, not absolute values.

Modeling preferences with a latent score has three important consequences. First, comparisons are invariant to addition of a constant: if you replace $r_\theta(x)$ by $r_\theta(x) + c$, all pairwise probabilities remain unchanged. Second, scaling of $r_\theta$ affects the sharpness of predicted preferences (a larger scale produces more confident predictions); this motivates normalization and temperature parameters discussed later. Third, pairwise data provides only relative information: often many reward functions are consistent with the same set of comparisons—an under-specification problem that can lead to misgeneralization.


# Binary cross-entropy over score differences

Given the latent-score model with a logistic link, a natural training objective is binary cross-entropy (BCE) applied to pairwise labels. Suppose we collect a dataset of preference labels $\{(x_A^{(i)}, x_B^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, where $y^{(i)} = 1$ if the human preferred $x_A^{(i)}$ and $y^{(i)} = 0$ if they preferred $x_B^{(i)}$. The BCE loss for a single pair is
$$
\ell_\text{BCE}^{(i)}(\theta) = -y^{(i)}\log \sigma\big(\Delta r_\theta^{(i)}\big) - (1-y^{(i)})\log\big(1 - \sigma\big(\Delta r_\theta^{(i)}\big)\big),
$$
where $\Delta r_\theta^{(i)} = r_\theta(x_A^{(i)}) - r_\theta(x_B^{(i)})$. Summing (or averaging) over the dataset gives the training objective. Minimizing this loss corresponds to maximum likelihood under the logistic noise model.

BCE over score differences has several intuitive properties. If a pair is labeled $y=1$, the loss encourages raising $r_\theta(x_A)$ relative to $r_\theta(x_B)$; the gradient w.r.t. the difference is $\sigma(\Delta r) - y$, which pushes the difference toward positive values when the human chose $A$. The magnitude of gradients is largest when the model is most uncertain ($\Delta r \approx 0$) and vanishes as the model becomes very confident. This property helps training focus on borderline pairs.

Alternatives to BCE include margin or ranking losses (e.g., hinge loss: $\max(0, m - \Delta r)$ for $y=1$), pairwise cross-entropy with temperature, and listwise losses for ranking multiple items. Hinge losses can be more robust to label noise in some cases because they do not push differences beyond the margin. Choosing the loss should reflect the noise assumptions and the downstream use: BCE fits a probabilistic model which is helpful for calibration, while margin losses focus purely on ordering.


# Normalization strategies (e.g., zero-mean per context)

Because preference labels depend only on score differences, the absolute scale and offset of $r_\theta$ are underdetermined. Nevertheless, the scale and offset matter during training and when interfacing the reward model with other systems like policy optimization. Several normalization strategies are used in practice.

A common strategy is to center the scores per context. In many settings items are responses conditioned on the same prompt or context $c$. For a set of candidate responses $\{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$ to context $c$, enforcing a zero-mean constraint
$$
\sum_{j=1}^{k} r_\theta(x_j \mid c) = 0
$$
or subtracting the empirical mean before computing losses can stabilize training by preventing drift in the reward baseline. This is especially useful when training with fresh data batches that mix contexts: without centering, parameters can drift to push all responses up or down, and downstream RL algorithms relying on reward magnitudes can suffer.

Temperature and variance normalization address scale. Because the logistic model uses $r$ differences in the exponent, multiplying $r$ by a constant $T^{-1}$ produces sharper or softer preference probabilities:
$$
\sigma\left(\frac{r(x_A) - r(x_B)}{T}\right).
$$
Learning or tuning a temperature $T$ can control calibration and gradient magnitudes. Empirically, fixing a sensible temperature or adding a learned scalar temperature parameter helps prevent the model from trivially inflating score magnitudes to drive confident predictions.

Another practical trick is normalizing scores globally to zero mean and unit variance on a held-out validation set. For downstream RL, subtracting a running baseline and dividing by the running standard deviation of predicted rewards yields more stable policy updates. Finally, regularizing the norm of scores (e.g., L2 penalties on the last-layer outputs) discourages extreme magnitudes.


# Overfitting, generalization, and calibration

Reward models are susceptible to overfitting because the label signal is relatively weak: each pairwise label only constrains one inequality in a high-dimensional input space. Overfitting can take the form of memorizing training pairs, latch onto spurious correlations (e.g., preferring longer texts because annotators correlated longer responses with quality), or becoming overconfident on out-of-distribution inputs.

Generalization requires both sufficient data diversity and inductive bias in the model. Data diversity means collecting comparisons across varied contexts, prompt distributions, and candidate qualities. Inductive bias can come from architecture (e.g., transformer pretraining imbues language structure) and regularization (weight decay, dropout, early stopping). Cross-validation and held-out pairwise tests are essential. It is also helpful to evaluate generalization on targeted distributions you expect during deployment (e.g., model-generated outputs rather than human-written ones).

Calibration is the degree to which predicted preference probabilities match empirical frequencies. If a reward model predicts $\sigma(\Delta r)=0.8$ for many pairs, calibration demands that humans indeed choose the higher-scored item about 80% of the time. BCE training under the logistic link encourages calibration under model assumptions, but mismatches (label noise, unmodeled annotator variability) degrade it. Typical calibration tools include Platt scaling (training a scalar temperature post-hoc), isotonic regression, or reliability diagrams to visualize miscalibration.

A subtle issue is that a model can have good ranking performance (e.g., ordering items correctly) but poor calibration of magnitudes, or vice versa. For RL, magnitude and calibration matter because they influence policy updates and the balance between exploration and exploitation. As a result, practitioners often monitor both ranking metrics (AUC, pairwise accuracy) and calibration metrics (Brier score, expected calibration error).


# Architectures for reward models (LM head vs. lightweight heads)

Architectural choice for the reward model balances representational power, compute cost, and alignment with pretrained weights. There are two common patterns when building reward models on top of pretrained language models (LMs): using the LM head (i.e., the language-modeling logits) or adding a lightweight reward head.

The LM-head approach leverages the pretrained distributional knowledge: for a piece of text $x$, the reward is derived from the log-probability the LM assigns to $x$ given the context, or a learned linear projection of the final hidden state(s) that mirrors the LM head. One way is to learn a linear combination of token log-probabilities or the sum of token log-probs (possibly normalized by length) as a proxy for reward. The advantage is strong inductive bias from pretraining: the LM head already captures fluency and plausibility. The downside is that language-modeling probabilities reflect many properties (commonness, predictability) not necessarily human preference for correctness or helpfulness. Adapting the LM head may also be compute-heavy if it requires full decoding for many candidate responses.

Lightweight heads add a small MLP or linear layer on top of a pooled representation from the transformer (e.g., the CLS token or mean-pooled token embeddings) to produce a scalar $r_\theta(x)$. These heads are parameter-efficient, fast to compute for many candidates (especially when the transformer outputs are reused), and flexible: they can be trained to focus on reward aspects rather than reproducing token-level probabilities. Lightweight heads can be trained with a small number of additional parameters and are easier to regularize.

Hybrid approaches exist: freeze most of the LM and fine-tune only a small head, fine-tune the last few transformer layers, or use adapters. Which approach works best depends on the dataset size, compute budget, and whether you want the reward model to inherit LM priors. In practice, large-scale systems often use a frozen pretrained transformer with a small reward head for efficiency and stability, occasionally fine-tuning the whole model if ample supervised preference data is available.


# Training dynamics and label noise robustness

Training dynamics of reward models reflect interactions between the loss, data sampling, and model capacity. Because BCE gradients are concentrated near zero differences, early in training the model learns coarse ordering signals; later it refines borderline cases. A frequent observation is that reward models quickly learn to rank obviously good versus bad outputs, then slowly fit nuanced human judgments. This implies diminishing returns from more training epochs on the same data—collecting more diverse comparisons is often more valuable.

Label noise arises from human mistakes, ambiguity in prompts, inconsistent annotators, and presentation bias. Robustness to label noise is crucial because even small amounts of noise can mislead the model when data is limited. Several techniques improve robustness. Label smoothing replaces hard labels with soft targets (e.g., 0.9/0.1 instead of 1/0), which reduces overconfidence and can prevent the model from overfitting noisy labels. Loss functions that cap gradient magnitude (e.g., hinge loss with margin) or use robust logistic-like functions reduce sensitivity to outliers. Bootstrapping methods combine the model's own predictions with labels to downweight suspected noisy examples. Curriculum learning—training first on high-agreement labels—focuses learning on reliable signal before handling ambiguous pairs.

Ensembles and annotator models help as well. If multiple annotators rate the same pairs, modeling annotator reliability (e.g., with per-annotator bias and competence parameters) improves likelihood estimation and reduces noise impact. Ensembling multiple reward models and averaging predictions often yields smoother, more robust scores. Active learning can prioritize comparisons that the current model is uncertain about, which improves data efficiency and reduces noise amplification.

Another dynamic risk is reward hacking in downstream use: during RL, policies may discover inputs (or degenerate outputs) that exploit spurious features the reward model learned. To mitigate this, iterative data collection where the preference dataset includes model-generated outputs is essential; the reward model must be retrained on such data to stay robust to distributional shift. Early stopping and validation on held-out comparisons prevent catastrophic overfitting to spurious cues during training.
