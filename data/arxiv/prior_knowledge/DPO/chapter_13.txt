Introduction

Many learning problems require agents to make decisions in environments where absolute, scalar rewards are not available. Instead, feedback often comes as relative comparisons or preferences: which action, arm, or trajectory is better than another. Preference-based approaches have grown in importance in recommendation systems, human-in-the-loop training, and reinforcement learning for complex objectives where specifying a numeric reward is difficult. This chapter unifies several strands of work that replace absolute rewards with comparisons: contextual bandits with noisy labels, dueling bandits that receive pairwise outcomes, tournament and game-theoretic solution concepts such as the von Neumann winner, and preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) that learns from trajectory comparisons. We aim to give the conceptual intuition, common mathematical formulations, algorithms, and sample-efficiency considerations so the reader can understand and evaluate research in this space.

We begin with contextual bandits and offline learning, then introduce the dueling bandit model and pairwise-comparison frameworks. This leads naturally to tournament solutions and the von Neumann winner as a robust target in cyclic preference settings. We show how latent scoring functions can be estimated from comparisons using parametric and nonparametric models, and how those estimators are used in online and offline regimes. We then expand to preference-based RL formulations, discuss exploration and sample complexity issues, and connect these ideas to classical ranking and choice theory.

Contextual bandits and offline learning

The contextual bandit is a minimal sequential decision framework that augments the multi-armed bandit with context information. At each round \(t\), the environment reveals a context \(x_t \in \mathcal{X}\). The learner picks an action \(a_t \in \mathcal{A}\) and observes feedback. In the classical contextual bandit the feedback is a scalar reward \(r_t\) for the chosen action. Here we are interested in settings where feedback is relative or partial: for example, the learner only observes whether the chosen action is preferred to some baseline, or receives a binary comparison between two actions drawn in the same context.

Formally, an offline contextual bandit dataset consists of tuples \((x_i, a_i, y_i)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\), where \(y_i\) is the (possibly partial) feedback associated with action \(a_i\) in context \(x_i\). Offline learning aims to learn a policy \(\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}\) that maximizes expected performance on the deployment distribution of contexts. When only comparisons are available, offline estimation requires modeling how comparisons arise. A common approach is to posit a latent scoring function \(f(x,a)\) such that, for two actions \(a,b\) in context \(x\), the probability the user prefers \(a\) to \(b\) is a function of the score difference:
\[
\Pr(a \succ b \mid x) = \Phi\big(f(x,a) - f(x,b)\big),
\]
where \(\Phi\) is a link function (e.g., logistic or Gaussian CDF). Learning \(f\) from logged pairwise comparisons is then a supervised learning problem (pairwise preference learning). Offline inverse propensity scoring (IPS) and doubly robust estimators can be adapted to the comparison setting to correct for biased logging policies, but they require modeling or estimating the probability that a given pair was compared. In practice, if only one action per context was shown and a binary signal recorded (clicked vs not), the pairwise perspective is recovered by treating comparisons between shown and unshown actions as implicit.

Offline preference data is often abundant (e.g., click logs), but biased: the set of observed comparisons is influenced by past policies, signage may be censored, and counterfactual evaluation is challenging. Careful counterfactual estimators and assumptions about the logging mechanism are required to avoid confounding.

Dueling bandits and pairwise comparisons

The dueling bandit model frames learning as repeated noisy pairwise comparisons between arms. At each round \(t\), the learner selects two arms, \(i_t\) and \(j_t\), and receives a stochastic outcome indicating which arm “wins.” Let \(P_{ij} = \Pr(i \succ j)\) denote the pairwise preference probability. Typically \(P_{ij} + P_{ji} = 1\) and \(P_{ii} = 1/2\). The learner's goal varies: identify a best arm according to a target (Condorcet, Borda, Copeland, or von Neumann), minimize cumulative regret defined in terms of pairwise wins, or find a near-optimal arm with few comparisons.

Dueling bandits differ from standard bandits in two ways. First, feedback is comparative between two chosen arms rather than scalar payoffs for a single arm. Second, pairwise preference matrices \(P\) can be non-transitive: \(P_{ab} > 1/2\), \(P_{bc} > 1/2\), but \(P_{ca} > 1/2\) create preference cycles. This makes the notion of a single best arm ambiguous; algorithms and targets must be chosen carefully.

A canonical performance metric is regret aggregated over rounds: if a ground-truth winner \(i^*\) is defined, regret can count the fraction of comparisons not involving \(i^*\) winning. But when no Condorcet winner exists (no arm beating all others with probability \(>1/2\)), alternatives such as Copeland score, Borda score, or a randomized policy (von Neumann) are used.

Algorithms for dueling bandits include adaptations of UCB to pairwise settings (RUCB, Sparring algorithms that run two independent bandit learners and pit them against each other), elimination-based methods (Beat-the-Mean), and algorithms that directly estimate \(P_{ij}\) with confidence intervals to focus comparisons. Analysis often yields sample complexity or regret bounds scaling with the pairwise gaps \(\Delta_{ij} = |P_{ij} - 1/2|\) and the number of arms \(K\). For example, to identify a Condorcet winner (if it exists) with probability \(1-\delta\), sample complexity can be on the order of \(\sum_{j\ne i^*} \Delta_{i^*j}^{-2} \log(K/\delta)\). When targets are more complex (e.g., von Neumann), different rates appear.

Von Neumann winner and tournament solutions

When pairwise comparisons produce cycles, a single deterministic Condorcet winner may not exist. Game-theoretic and social choice concepts provide alternative solution notions. A tournament is a complete directed graph over items where each pair has a winner; the pairwise probabilities generalize this to weighted tournaments. Two classical ideas are Copeland/Borda scores and the von Neumann winner.

The Copeland score of arm \(i\) is the number (or fraction) of other arms it beats with probability \(>1/2\):
\[
\text{Copeland}(i) = \sum_{j\ne i} \mathbb{I}\{P_{ij} > 1/2\}.
\]
The Borda score aggregates the margin of victory:
\[
\text{Borda}(i) = \sum_{j\ne i} P_{ij}.
\]
Both give deterministic rankings and can be targets for identification. However, these ignore mixed strategies.

The von Neumann winner arises from zero-sum game theory. Treat the pairwise matrix \(M\) with entries \(M_{ij} = 2P_{ij} - 1\), so \(M_{ij} \in [-1,1]\) and \(M_{ij} = -M_{ji}\). Consider a two-player zero-sum game where the row player selects a mixed strategy \(p\) (probability distribution over arms) and the column player selects \(q\). The expected payoff to the row player is \(p^\top M q\). von Neumann's minimax theorem says there exists an equilibrium \((p^*, q^*)\) with equal value \(v^*\):
\[
v^* = \max_{p\in\Delta_K} \min_{q\in\Delta_K} p^\top M q = \min_{q\in\Delta_K} \max_{p\in\Delta_K} p^\top M q.
\]
When imagined as selecting an arm to “play,” the row player's maximin strategy \(p^*\) guarantees that against any opponent mix the expected pairwise advantage is at least \(v^*\). The von Neumann winner is the mixed strategy \(p^*\); when \(v^* \ge 0\) it corresponds to a robust randomized policy that does at least as well as a fair coin on expectation.

Von Neumann winners are attractive in cyclic preference settings because they are always well-defined and can be computed by linear programming:
\[
\max_{p\in\Delta_K, v} \quad v \quad\text{s.t.}\quad p^\top M e_j \ge v \quad\forall j,
\]
where \(e_j\) is the unit vector. In the dueling bandit context, some algorithms aim to learn a distribution approximating \(p^*\) using comparisons and exploit the minimax guarantees for low regret.

Preference-based RL (PbRL) formulations

Extending preference feedback to reinforcement learning, the basic object of interest becomes policies and trajectories instead of arms. In preference-based RL we assume that between two trajectories (or segments, or rolled-out policies) a teacher or reward oracle can provide a noisy comparison. Formally, let \(\tau\) denote a trajectory (state-action sequence), and suppose a latent utility or return function \(R(\tau)\) exists but is not directly observed. Instead, we observe comparisons where for trajectories \(\tau_1, \tau_2\),
\[
\Pr(\tau_1 \succ \tau_2) = \Phi\big(R(\tau_1) - R(\tau_2)\big).
\]
The goal is to find a policy \(\pi\) that maximizes expected utility according to \(R\), using only comparisons collected by running policies and querying the oracle about pairs of trajectories.

PbRL includes several problem formulations:

- Preference-based policy optimization: Use comparisons to estimate gradients or surrogate objectives to optimize policy parameters (e.g., preference-based policy gradient methods).
- Inverse preference learning: Recover a latent reward model \(r(s,a)\) (up to identifiability) consistent with trajectory comparisons, then run RL with the learned reward.
- Direct policy search with pairwise feedback: Treat each policy as an arm and run a dueling bandit-like search in policy space.

Key challenges in PbRL include (1) trajectories are high-dimensional structured objects, so comparing them directly can be noisy and costly; (2) queries are expensive (human feedback), so sample efficiency is paramount; (3) preferences may be inconsistent or context-dependent; (4) credit assignment: a preference over trajectories provides sparse information about per-step rewards.

Estimating latent scoring functions from comparisons

To use comparisons effectively one typically posits a model relating comparisons to latent scores and fits that model from data. Parametric models commonly used are Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) and Thurstone models, both instances of random utility models (RUMs). Under BTL, each item \(i\) has a positive score \(\theta_i > 0\) and
\[
\Pr(i \succ j) = \frac{\theta_i}{\theta_i + \theta_j}.
\]
Equivalently, with log-scores \(s_i = \log \theta_i\), the model becomes logistic:
\[
\Pr(i \succ j) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(s_i - s_j))}.
\]
Thurstone's model assumes Gaussian noise on latent utilities, yielding probabilities via the Gaussian CDF:
\[
\Pr(i \succ j) = \Phi\big((\mu_i - \mu_j)/\sigma\big).
\]
These parametric models enable maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) of scores from observed pairwise outcomes. In contextual settings one generalizes to a parametric scoring function \(f_\beta(x,a)\), e.g., linear \(f_\beta(x,a) = \beta^\top \phi(x,a)\), and fits \(\beta\) by maximizing the log-likelihood of observed comparisons:
\[
\hat\beta = \arg\max_\beta \sum_{(x,a,b,y)} \log \Pr_\beta(a \succ b \mid x),
\]
where the sum runs over observed comparisons and \(y\) indicates the outcome.

Nonparametric and semi-parametric methods include kernelized preference learning, RankSVM (pairwise ranking via hinge loss), and isotonic or monotone MLE under structural assumptions. Regularization and identifiability constraints are essential: additive constants to scores are unidentifiable, so one typically fix \(\sum_i s_i = 0\) or set a reference item's score to zero.

For trajectories in RL, one may model the latent return as an additive function of per-step features: \(R(\tau) = \sum_{t} r_\theta(s_t,a_t)\) and fit \(\theta\) from pairwise trajectory comparisons by maximizing the corresponding likelihood or by employing Bayesian inference. Bayesian approaches provide uncertainty estimates useful for active query selection.

Online vs. offline preference feedback

Offline preference learning uses pre-collected comparisons, where the chosen pairs and contexts may be biased by historical selection. Offline estimation can be efficient if the data covers relevant comparisons, but the logged comparison distribution must allow reliable counterfactual evaluation. Techniques such as inverse propensity scoring (IPS), self-normalized IPS, and doubly robust estimators adapt to pairwise feedback by weighting each observed comparison by the inverse probability it was presented. For pairwise data, estimating or logging the probability that a given pair was compared is often more complex than single-action logging.

Online preference learning allows the learner to adaptively select which pairs to compare, enabling active querying and targeted exploration. Active strategies can dramatically improve sample efficiency by focusing queries where the model is uncertain or where comparisons are most informative for the chosen objective (e.g., discriminating top candidates or learning a von Neumann mixture). In RL, online interaction also allows collecting trajectories under policies designed to produce informative comparisons.

Active learning strategies include uncertainty sampling, expected information gain (choosing pairs that maximize expected reduction in posterior entropy over scores), and contextual bandit-inspired exploration strategies. Confidence-bound-based algorithms extend UCB to preference settings by maintaining confidence intervals on \(P_{ij}\) or on score differences and selecting pairs that trade off exploration and exploitation.

Sample efficiency and exploration

Sample efficiency in preference-based problems depends on the noise model, the complexity of the scoring function class, and the target objective (identify top arm vs approximate von Neumann strategy vs learn full scores). For parametric scoring models with dimension \(d\), sample complexity to estimate parameters to error \(\epsilon\) typically scales as \(O(d/\epsilon^2)\) under iid comparisons; for nonparametric models the rates are slower and depend on smoothness.

In dueling bandits, instance-dependent regret bounds reflect gaps \(\Delta_{ij}\). For identifying a Condorcet winner, sample complexity often scales with \(\sum_{j\ne i^*} \Delta_{i^*j}^{-2}\). For randomized targets like von Neumann mixtures, complexity can depend on spectral properties of \(M\), and algorithms often estimate a distribution over arms directly, which can be more sample-efficient when many arms have small pairwise gaps.

Exploration is crucial because comparisons provide only relative information; naive exploitation of apparent winners can get stuck on suboptimal arms due to noise. Strategies include tournament elimination (randomized comparisons to confirm winners), global estimation methods (estimate all pairwise probabilities with confidence intervals), and posterior-sampling approaches (Thompson sampling analogues for pairwise outcomes, sampling latent scores from a posterior and choosing comparisons accordingly). In RL, exploration must also resolve the confounding of exploration for dynamics (state visitation) and exploration for preference information. Balancing the cost of obtaining comparisons (e.g., human time) with the benefit for policy improvement is a practical concern.

Connections to ranking and choice theory

Preference learning intersects deeply with classical ranking and choice theory. Random utility models (BTL, Plackett-Luce, Thurstone) originated in economics and psychometrics, modeling how agents make choices among sets. Plackett-Luce generalizes pairwise models to full rankings: the probability of observing a ranking \(\pi\) over items under scores \(\{\theta_i\}\) is
\[
\Pr(\pi) = \prod_{t=1}^K \frac{\theta_{\pi(t)}}{\sum_{u\ge t}\theta_{\pi(u)}}.
\]
Learning from full rankings yields stronger identifiability and efficiency compared to pairwise data.

Social choice concepts (Condorcet consistency, Borda count, Kemeny optimality) clarify objective selection when aggregating preferences. Kemeny aggregation seeks a ranking minimizing the total number of pairwise disagreements with observed votes; computing Kemeny-optimal rankings is NP-hard in general. In dueling bandits, Kemeny and Copeland objectives yield different notions of “best” and algorithms must match the chosen normative criterion.

The choice literature also studies context effects and violations of regularity (e.g., attraction effect), reminding practitioners that simple RUM assumptions may fail for human preferences. For PbRL with human feedback, models that account for context-dependent and inconsistent preferences, bounded rationality, or query-dependent noise can be important for reliable learning.

Conclusion

Preference-based learning unifies bandits, dueling comparisons, game-theoretic tournament solutions, and reinforcement learning under the shared theme of learning from relative feedback. Contextual bandits and offline preference datasets motivate parametric and nonparametric score estimation; dueling bandits formalize online pairwise exploration with specific solution concepts such as Condorcet, Copeland, Borda, and the von Neumann winner. Preference-based RL raises additional challenges of trajectory complexity and credit assignment, demanding models that infer per-step rewards or directly optimize policies from comparisons. Sample efficiency hinges on the model class, noise structure, and whether queries can be chosen adaptively. Finally, links to ranking and choice theory provide principled probabilistic models (BTL, Thurstone, Plackett-Luce) and a reminder that human preference behavior may violate simplifying assumptions.

Understanding these connections equips the reader to interpret algorithms and results across papers that use comparisons instead of absolute rewards. When reading the literature, key questions to ask include: What target objective is being optimized (Condorcet, Borda, von Neumann)? What model of noise or random utility is assumed? Is feedback collected online or offline, and how are logging biases handled? How is exploration conducted, and what sample-complexity guarantees are provided? Addressing these clarifies the applicability and limitations of preference-based approaches in practice.
