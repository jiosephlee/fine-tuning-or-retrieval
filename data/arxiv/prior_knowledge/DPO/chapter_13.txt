Chapter 13: Statistical Learning Theory for Policies and Offline Evaluation

Introduction

In many applications we do not merely want to predict outcomes; we want to choose actions or recommend decisions that will influence future outcomes. This broad class of problems is captured by policy learning: we seek a mapping from contexts (or states) to actions that maximizes some expected utility (reward) according to the environment. In practice, we frequently must learn such policies from logged, offline data that were collected under some behavior policy or sampling process. This setting raises fundamental statistical challenges: how well will a policy learned from a finite offline dataset perform when deployed? How can we estimate the performance of a candidate policy using only offline data? What happens when the data distribution differs from the one encountered at deployment time? How do preference-based or relative-feedback estimators behave, and when are they consistent? Finally, how should we trade off bias and variance when performing offline policy optimization?

This chapter develops the statistical learning theory necessary to answer these questions. We begin by formalizing policy learning and discussing generalization error and complexity measures that govern how well a learned policy will perform on fresh data. We then study off-policy evaluation techniques, focusing on importance sampling and its variants for unbiased estimation of policy value. Next we cover distribution shift between training and deployment distributions, its consequences, and ways to mitigate it. We analyze consistency of preference-based estimators, which often use comparative feedback rather than absolute rewards. Finally, we examine the bias–variance tradeoffs that arise in offline policy optimization and how to balance them in practice.

# Generalization error for policy learning and complexity measures

To reason about how a policy learned from finite data will behave at deployment, we must quantify generalization error: the gap between the estimated performance on training data and the true expected performance under the environment. Let the context space be $\mathcal{X}$, the action space $\mathcal{A}$ (finite or continuous), and suppose the environment produces context–reward pairs according to a distribution $P$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{R}$, where $\mathcal{R}$ is the set of reward signals. A deterministic policy $\pi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{A}$ maps contexts to actions; randomized policies map contexts to distributions over actions.

Define the value (expected reward) of a policy $\pi$ as
\[
V(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,r) \sim P}\left[ r\big(x,\pi(x)\big)\right],
\]
where $r(x,a)$ is the reward observed if action $a$ is taken in context $x$. We typically only have an offline dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i,a_i, r_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ collected under some behavior policy (or sampling mechanism) $\mu$, where each $a_i$ is the action chosen under $\mu$ when context $x_i$ was observed and $r_i$ is the resulting reward. The empirical estimate of a policy's value depends on the estimator, but for conceptual discussion we can consider a simple on-policy empirical average when we have data collected under the same policy: if $\pi = \mu$, then
\[
\widehat{V}_n(\mu) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n r_i.
\]
In the off-policy case, where $\pi \neq \mu$, more complex estimators are needed; these will be discussed in the next section.

The generalization error of a learned policy $\widehat{\pi}$ can be framed as the difference between the true value and the value of the best policy within the class (estimation error) plus the approximation error, if the policy class does not contain the true optimum. If we consider a policy class $\Pi$ (a set of candidate policies that our algorithm searches over), define the population best policy in $\Pi$ as $\pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi} V(\pi)$ and the empirical best policy as $\widehat{\pi} = \arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi} \widehat{V}_n(\pi)$, where $\widehat{V}_n(\pi)$ is the empirical estimate of $V(\pi)$ from the data. The regret of the learned policy compared to the best-in-class is
\[
V(\pi^*) - V(\widehat{\pi}).
\]
A standard decomposition of this regret uses uniform convergence: if with high probability we have uniform bounds of the form
\[
\sup_{\pi \in \Pi} | \widehat{V}_n(\pi) - V(\pi)| \le \varepsilon_n,
\]
then
\[
V(\pi^*) - V(\widehat{\pi}) \le 2\varepsilon_n.
\]
This follows because the difference between empirical maximizers and population maximizers is controlled by how well empirical values approximate true values uniformly over $\Pi$.

Thus the central statistical question becomes: how large is $\varepsilon_n$ for a given policy class $\Pi$ and estimator $\widehat{V}_n$? Classical statistical learning theory offers several complexity measures that control uniform deviations: VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, covering numbers, and metric entropy. For policy learning these measures can be adapted in two principal ways.

First, when policies are binary-valued classifiers (e.g., two actions), the VC dimension of the induced decision boundary or the policy class directly controls uniform convergence rates in the usual way. When actions are more than two or continuous, equivalent complexity measures are used—e.g., combinatorial dimensions or pseudo-dimensions for real-valued function classes.

Second, when the estimator for value is more complicated (such as importance-weighted estimators), the concentration analysis must account for the variance or range of the estimator. For example, consider importance sampling (IS) estimation of policy value under behavior policy $\mu$, which uses importance weights $w(x,a) = \frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\mu(a\mid x)}$. Then an IS estimator for $V(\pi)$ is
\[
\widehat{V}_\text{IS}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n w(x_i,a_i) r_i.
\]
Even if $\Pi$ is simple, the potential for large importance weights inflates variance and degrades the finite-sample uniform convergence bounds. Thus complexity for policy classes in the off-policy setting depends on both the function class complexity (how many distinct weight functions appear when varying $\pi$) and on the distribution of importance weights.

A useful approach combines Rademacher complexity with Lipschitz properties of the estimator mapping. Suppose we can write $\widehat{V}_\text{IS}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f_\pi(z_i)$ where $z_i=(x_i,a_i,r_i)$ and $f_\pi(z) = w(x,a) r$. If the function class $\mathcal{F} = \{f_\pi: \pi\in\Pi\}$ has empirical Rademacher complexity $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_n(\mathcal{F})$, then standard symmetrization gives that with high probability
\[
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi} |\widehat{V}_n(\pi) - V(\pi)| \le 2 \widehat{\mathcal{R}}_n(\mathcal{F}) + O\!\left(B\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right),
\]
where $B$ bounds the magnitude of the functions in $\mathcal{F}$. If importance weights are unbounded, no finite $B$ exists and the bound degenerates. In practice one often truncates or smooths weights to control the effective complexity.

An alternative route for deriving tighter bounds is to exploit structure in the policy class to get faster rates. For instance, if rewards are smooth and the policy class is parametric of finite dimension $d$, under additional margin or noise conditions one can achieve $O(d/n)$ rates in favorable cases rather than the slower $O(1/\sqrt{n})$. Margin conditions express how decisively one action is preferred over others near the decision boundary; when margins are large, small errors in estimation do not change the chosen action. These margin-based analyses mirror those in classification but must be adapted to take into account reward magnitudes and importance-weighting in off-policy settings.

In summary, generalization error for policy learning depends on: (1) the complexity of the policy class under the particular estimation method used; (2) the stochastic properties of the estimator (especially variance and tail behavior when using importance weights); and (3) structural properties of the problem (parametric vs nonparametric, margin/noise conditions) that can yield faster rates. Understanding these interacting factors is critical for both theoretical guarantees and practical algorithm design.

# Off-policy evaluation and importance sampling

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is the problem of estimating the value $V(\pi)$ of a target policy $\pi$ using data collected by a different behavior policy $\mu$. OPE is central in offline policy learning because it lets us compare candidate policies without deploying them. The canonical method in OPE is importance sampling, which reweights logged outcomes to account for the mismatch between the behavior and target policies.

Consider again data $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i,a_i,r_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ sampled i.i.d. from distribution induced by contexts $x\sim P_X$, actions $a\sim\mu(\cdot\mid x)$, and rewards $r$ conditional on $(x,a)$. For a deterministic reward function $r(x,a)$ (or conditional expectation), the target value can be written as
\[
V(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_X}\left[ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mu(\cdot\mid x)}\left[ \frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\mu(a\mid x)} r(x,a) \right]\right] = \mathbb{E}_{(x,a,r)\sim \mu}\left[ \frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\mu(a\mid x)} r \right].
\]
From this identity the importance sampling (IS) estimator is obtained:
\[
\widehat{V}_\text{IS}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)} r_i.
\]
IS is unbiased when the support condition holds: $\mu(a\mid x) > 0$ whenever $\pi(a\mid x) > 0$ (absolute continuity of $\pi$ w.r.t. $\mu$). The unbiasedness is immediate from the identity above. However, IS can suffer from very high variance when importance weights $w_i=\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}$ are large or heavy-tailed. This is particularly acute when $\mu$ is deterministic or concentrates on a small set of actions; then many target actions are rarely or never observed, producing extreme weights or undefined values.

Several standard variance reduction and estimator-improvement techniques are used in OPE:

1. Clipped (or truncated) importance sampling. Replace $w_i$ by $\min(w_i, M)$ for some clipping threshold $M$. This yields a biased estimator but often with considerably lower variance. The bias decreases as $M\to\infty$, and $M$ can be chosen to trade bias and variance.

2. Weighted importance sampling (WIS). Normalize weights by their sum:
\[
\widehat{V}_\text{WIS}(\pi) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i r_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i}.
\]
This estimator is biased but often has lower mean squared error (MSE) than plain IS because the normalization reduces sensitivity to a few large weights. WIS is consistent under mild conditions, and in many practical situations leads to substantially lower MSE.

3. Doubly robust (DR) estimators. Combine a regression model of rewards with importance weighting. Suppose we estimate the conditional mean reward $\hat{q}(x,a) \approx \mathbb{E}[r\mid x,a]$. The doubly robust estimator is
\[
\widehat{V}_\text{DR}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \hat{q}(x_i,\pi(x_i)) + \frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid x_i)}\big(r_i - \hat{q}(x_i,a_i)\big) \right],
\]
where for deterministic target policies we interpret $\hat{q}(x,\pi(x))$ as the estimated reward at the action chosen by $\pi$. Intuitively, the model $\hat{q}$ reduces variance by providing a baseline and correcting it with an importance-weighted residual. DR estimators are "doubly robust" in that they remain consistent if either the regression model is consistent or the behavior policy model (for weights) is correct.

4. Self-normalized and control variate methods. Self-normalization (as in WIS) is a form of control variate. More general control variates subtract a known or estimated quantity with zero mean to reduce variance.

5. Per-decision importance sampling for sequential decision-making and temporal-difference style methods. For sequential settings like finite-horizon bandits or MDPs, importance sampling can be applied multiplicatively across time steps; variance explodes exponentially with horizon unless structure (e.g., near-deterministic policies or good models) is exploited.

Analyzing the MSE of these estimators requires understanding both the variability of rewards and the distribution of weights. For IS, Var$(\widehat{V}_\text{IS}) = \mathrm{Var}_\mu\left(w r\right)/n$, which can be enormous if $w$ and $r$ have heavy tails or are unbounded. For WIS or DR, bias terms may appear but the MSE can be lower, especially in finite samples.

Another important notion is effective sample size (ESS), which quantifies how informative the weighted sample is relative to an unweighted sample. A common definition is
\[
\text{ESS} = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2}.
\]
ESS ranges between 1 and $n$; small ESS indicates that a few samples dominate the weighted sum and that estimation will be noisy.

Practical OPE recommends first assessing overlap: if for many contexts the behavior policy places negligible mass on actions favored by the target, OPE will be unreliable. When overlap is poor, one may attempt to restrict the target policy class to those with sufficient support under $\mu$, or collect additional interventional data if possible. When overlap is adequate, DR estimators with good reward models tend to perform well.

Finally, in theoretical analyses it is useful to derive uniform concentration bounds over a class of policies for these estimators. This calls back to the previous section: uniform convergence rates for function classes $\mathcal{F}=\{f_\pi\}$ where $f_\pi(z)=w(x,a)r$ (or the DR influence functions) determine how many samples are needed to select a near-optimal policy in $\Pi$ with high probability. Variance and tail behavior of the influence functions are key to these bounds.

# Distribution shift between training (e.g., SFT samples) and deployment

Distribution shift occurs when the distribution of inputs (contexts, actions, or rewards) during training differs from that at deployment. In offline policy learning, distribution shift can arise in several ways: the context distribution may change over time, the behavior policy generating logged actions may change, or the environment’s reward function may evolve. A particularly important practical case is when a dataset contains supervised fine-tuning (SFT) samples or other expert demonstrations that do not reflect the same policy or context distribution as deployment.

Formally, let $P_\text{train}$ denote the joint distribution of $(x,a,r)$ in the offline data and let $P_\text{deploy}$ denote the distribution encountered when the learned policy is deployed. Even if we had an unbiased estimator of $V(\pi)$ under $P_\text{train}$, what we care about is the value under $P_\text{deploy}$:
\[
V_\text{deploy}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,a,r)\sim P_\text{deploy}}\left[ r(x,\pi(x))\right].
\]
The shift might be in contexts $P_X$, in conditional reward distributions $P(r\mid x,a)$, or in action availability and logging policies. Any of these shifts invalidate off-policy estimates based on $P_\text{train}$ unless corrected.

One standard strategy for covariate shift (shift in context distribution only) is importance weighting by the ratio of context distributions. If $P_\text{deploy}(x) \neq P_\text{train}(x)$ but the conditional reward distribution and behavior policy remain the same conditional on $x$, one can reweight samples by $w(x)=P_\text{deploy}(x)/P_\text{train}(x)$. This yields unbiased estimates under $P_\text{deploy}$ provided $P_\text{deploy}$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $P_\text{train}$. However, as with action importance weights, these context importance weights can have large variance when support mismatch is significant.

When the logging policy itself changes across parts of the dataset (for example, some SFT samples were collected under an "expert" policy and other parts under a different policy), the combined dataset is a mixture of different behavior policies. In that case, OPE must account for the mixture by either stratifying by source or estimating the behavior policy conditional on context and source indicator. Failure to do so can create serious bias in value estimates.

If the reward mechanism changes (a form of concept shift, where $P(r\mid x,a)$ shifts), offline correction becomes much harder and often impossible without further assumptions or new data. One approach is to model the nonstationarity parametrically and try to transfer or adapt using a small amount of follow-up data. Another is to design policies that are robust to certain classes of shifts—for example, policies optimizing worst-case regret over an uncertainty set of models (robust optimization).

A special case that arises in modern systems is selection bias in which the logging mechanism preferentially records examples with higher utility (e.g., when humans select examples to save or when data is filtered). Such selection on the basis of potential outcomes creates missing-not-at-random biases. Correcting selection bias typically requires modeling the selection mechanism or using instrumental-variable-style techniques if suitable instruments exist.

From a statistical learning perspective, distribution shift breaks the usual guarantees that rely on training and testing being i.i.d. from the same distribution. To reason theoretically under shift, one often assumes a specific shift model (e.g., covariate shift only) and then derives bounds that depend on divergence measures between training and deployment distributions, such as total variation distance, KL divergence, or density-ratio bounds. For example, if the time of deployment produces contexts whose density ratio is bounded by $\alpha$, i.e., $P_\text{deploy}(x) \le \alpha P_\text{train}(x)$ for all $x$, then a uniform convergence bound under $P_\text{train}$ can be translated into a bound under $P_\text{deploy}$ that scales by $\alpha$. But large $\alpha$ can make the bounds vacuous.

Practically, detecting and quantifying distribution shift requires held-out validation sets that mimic deployment or careful logging of the data-generating process. One may also use importance-weighted validation or domain adaptation techniques to reweight or adapt models. Robust policy optimization methods explicitly incorporate uncertainty about the deployment distribution, optimizing policies for minimax performance over an ambiguity set. This leads to more conservative policies but can protect against worst-case shifts.

In short, distribution shift complicates both evaluation and optimization. The nature of the shift determines the possible remedies: covariate shift can sometimes be corrected with density-ratio weighting; changes in rewards are much harder and typically require new data or modeling assumptions; mixture-of-behavior policies needs explicit accounting; and selection bias may require modeling the selection mechanism. Any offline policy-learning deployment plan should explicitly consider potential distribution shifts and attempt to bound or test their impact.

# Consistency of preference-based estimators

Preference-based learning refers to situations where the available feedback is comparative or ordinal rather than cardinal reward values. Examples include pairwise comparisons ("A is preferred to B"), rankings, or click-through signals that only indicate relative preference. In some applications, absolute reward values are difficult to elicit, but preferences are easier and more reliable. The statistical question is: can we consistently estimate policy values, or even learn optimal policies, using only preference data? What estimators are consistent under what conditions?

There are several preference-feedback scenarios. In the simplest model, we receive noisy binary comparisons between two actions in a given context: for a context $x$ and two actions $a$ and $a'$, we observe a random indicator $Y$ such that $Y=1$ implies that $a$ is preferred to $a'$ and $Y=0$ otherwise. A common probabilistic model is the Thurstone or Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model, where each $(x,a)$ is associated with a latent utility $u(x,a)$ and the probability that $a$ is chosen over $a'$ is a function of the utility difference, e.g.,
\[
\mathbb{P}(a \succ a' \mid x) = \Phi\big( u(x,a) - u(x,a')\big)
\]
for a link $\Phi$ (e.g., logistic or Gaussian CDF). When such a parametric model is correctly specified and identifiable, maximum likelihood estimation of $u$ is consistent and one can plug-in estimated utilities to obtain policies.

More generally, consider pairwise comparisons sampled according to some design: at each trial a context $x$ is drawn, a pair of actions $(a,a')$ is selected (possibly according to an experimental design or behavior policy), and a binary comparison outcome is observed. If comparisons are noiseless and consistent with an underlying utility ordering, then preference data suffice to rank actions and identify the best action per context. With noise, identifiability requires that the comparison noise model be well-behaved (e.g., nondegenerate and obeying some monotonicity). Under mild conditions, one can consistently estimate the conditional preference probabilities
\[
p_{a,a'}(x) = \mathbb{P}(a \succ a' \mid x),
\]
and from those reconstruct utilities up to additive constants or monotone transformations.

However, there are a number of caveats to consistency in preference-based estimators for policy evaluation and learning:

1. Support and design: If comparisons never cover certain actions or action pairs under particular contexts, those actions’ utilities cannot be estimated consistently. Hence some exploration or coverage is necessary. For example, if the design always compares action A to B but never compares C with either, we cannot learn about C.

2. Link and identifiability: If one only observes ordinal preferences without knowing the link function that maps utility differences to probabilities, it may be impossible to recover cardinal utilities. In many decision problems the policy objective depends on expected utility values, so ordinal information alone may be insufficient. But if policies are treated only up to preference order (i.e., any action that is top-ranked in expectation suffices), ordinal data might be enough.

3. Noise structure: The consistency of estimators depends on the noise being independent of unobserved variables that also affect outcomes, and on sufficient signal-to-noise ratio. If noise is heteroskedastic or adversarially dependent on context, estimation may fail.

4. From preferences to policy value: Even if we can estimate utilities up to monotone transforms, converting this into accurate estimates of expected reward requires assumptions. If utilities are linear scalings of true reward, then rankings suffice. If preferences only inform about a ranking but not magnitudes, then the estimated policy’s true expected reward may differ unpredictably.

An important class of estimators in preference learning is the "dueling bandits" or "pairwise preference" estimators, where algorithms focus on identifying the best action while minimizing regret in online settings. The offline analog is to use logged pairwise comparison data and construct an empirical preference kernel; then, for each context, pick the action with the highest estimated expected preference against others. Consistency here requires that the logging covers enough pairwise comparisons per context and that comparisons are unbiased estimates of true underlying preferences.

A more statistically sophisticated approach uses inverse probability weighting for pairwise comparisons. Suppose comparisons are drawn under a design policy $\nu$ that chooses pairs $(a,a')$ with known probability. Then a doubly robust estimation strategy similar to DR for scalar rewards can be constructed: estimate the pairwise win probabilities and combine with models of latent utilities to get low-variance, consistent estimates of the expected rank or tournament score of an action under the target policy.

Finally, consider interventional preferences like "which action would you prefer the agent to take?" provided by humans during SFT. These preferences often reflect complex latent objectives and may be inconsistent across annotators or contexts. Statistical modeling of annotator heterogeneity and calibration is necessary to obtain consistent estimators of aggregate preferences. When aggregating human preferences, one may also use item-response-style models that account for annotator accuracy and bias, enabling consistent recovery under identifiability conditions.

Summing up, preference-based estimators can be consistent for policy learning provided sufficient coverage, identifiability (or appropriate link assumptions), and appropriate modeling of noise and annotator behavior. In many practical cases, combining preference data with occasional absolute reward data or utilizing models that map preferences to cardinal utilities (with regularization) yields the most robust results.

# Bias/variance tradeoffs in offline policy optimization

Offline policy optimization must balance bias and variance in both estimation of policy values and the policy search process. The bias–variance tradeoff appears in multiple places: in choosing estimators for policy value (IS vs clipped vs DR), in restricting policy classes (complex vs simple policies), and in exploration strategies implicit in the logged data.

From the value-estimation perspective, consider IS (unbiased but high variance) and WIS or clipped IS (biased but lower variance). The mean squared error (MSE) of an estimator decomposes as
\[
\text{MSE} = (\text{Bias})^2 + \text{Var}.
\]
An unbiased estimator can have enormous variance that makes it practically useless in finite samples, while a biased estimator with smaller variance may have lower MSE and yield better downstream policy selection. In offline policy optimization, this tradeoff affects which candidate policies appear best when using finite data: a high-variance estimator may mistakenly favor policies with spurious high IS estimates due to a few lucky large weights, whereas a slightly biased estimator more stably ranks policies.

Beyond estimator choice, restricting policy complexity reduces variance in uniform convergence bounds but introduces approximation bias. Choosing too small a policy class $\Pi$ may preclude finding high-value policies (approximation bias), while choosing too large a class increases estimation error and the risk of overfitting the offline data to spurious correlations or to logged noise. The classical structural risk minimization principle applies: choose the policy class to balance approximation and estimation errors, possibly using cross-validation-like procedures adapted to offline OPE.

Regularization in parametric policy classes plays a similar role: stronger regularization reduces variance at the cost of bias. In addition, regularization can help with support or overlap issues: restricting policies to stay near the logging policy (policy constraint) limits importance weights and helps control variance. Constrained optimization formulations, such as adding a KL-divergence penalty with respect to the behavior policy,
\[
\max_\pi \widehat{V}_n(\pi) - \lambda \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\widehat{P}_X}\left[ \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,||\,\mu(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right],
\]
explicitly trade exploration (moving away from logged actions) against the risk of high-variance evaluation. Such conservative policy update rules are common in batch reinforcement learning and policy-gradient methods for safe off-policy updates.

Another aspect of the bias–variance tradeoff arises in model-based vs model-free approaches. A model-based estimator uses an estimated model \(\hat{P}(r\mid x,a)\) to compute policy value by plugging in predicted rewards:
\[
\widehat{V}_\text{model}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{q}(x_i,\pi(x_i)).
\]
Model-based methods can have low variance but suffer from model bias if \(\hat{q}\) is misspecified. Model-free methods like IS are unbiased but high-variance. Doubly robust estimators bridge these extremes, capitalizing on accurate models when present while retaining unbiasedness properties if models fail.

In offline policy optimization, the downstream objective is not just to estimate value accurately but to find a policy that performs well when deployed. Therefore, MSE of value estimates is only an indirect proxy. What matters is whether the estimator yields correct policy ordering or at least sufficient information to choose a good policy. This suggests focusing on ranking-aware criteria or using surrogate losses that penalize errors likely to change the optimal action for contexts with large impact on performance. For example, focusing estimation precision on high-probability contexts (under deployment) or on actions with large reward variance may be more useful than minimizing global MSE.

Practical strategies to manage bias–variance tradeoffs include cross-validation with OPE-aware criteria, choosing regularization parameters according to held-out or importance-weighted validation, using doubly robust estimators with careful model selection, and constraining policy updates to stay in regions of good overlap. When logging policy probabilities are unknown, one must be cautious because misestimation of $\mu$ affects both bias and variance of IS-based methods.

A final consideration is that offline policy optimization often operates in regimes where small improvements in estimated value might be statistically insignificant but operationally impactful. Therefore, it is prudent to quantify uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals for OPE estimates) and to incorporate risk-awareness into optimization objectives (e.g., lower confidence bounds, or robust optimization). Methods that optimize a lower bound on the estimated value, accounting for estimation uncertainty, tend to produce more conservative but safer policies.

Conclusion

Learning and evaluating policies from offline data touches on many deep statistical issues. Uniform convergence and complexity measures determine how many samples are needed to control generalization error for policy classes. Off-policy evaluation, especially importance sampling and its variants, offers ways to estimate policy value but must contend with high variance and support mismatch. Distribution shift between training and deployment can invalidate offline estimates unless corrected, and preference-based feedback adds layers of identifiability and modeling concerns. Throughout, bias–variance tradeoffs guide estimator and regularization choices in offline policy optimization. In practice, combining model-based predictions, doubly robust corrections, conservative policy constraints, and uncertainty quantification forms a robust toolkit for learning policies that generalize from finite offline data to reliable deployment.
