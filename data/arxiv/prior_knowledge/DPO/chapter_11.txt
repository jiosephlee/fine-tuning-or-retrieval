# Introduction

In classical reinforcement learning (RL), an agent seeks a policy that maximizes expected cumulative reward. A standard formulation focuses purely on rewards and often leads to deterministic optimal policies in finite MDPs. However, many practical problems benefit from maintaining a degree of stochasticity: for exploration, robustness, or to reflect setting-specific priors over behavior. One principled way to incorporate such considerations is to add a regularizer that penalizes deviation from a reference policy, typically using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. This yields KL‑regularized objectives that can be interpreted as a constrained optimization problem or as probabilistic inference.

This chapter develops the interpretation of KL‑regularized control objectives as probabilistic inference and shows how the optimal policies under such objectives take a "soft" or Gibbs form. We will derive the softmax (Gibbs) policy, examine the role and properties of the partition function (normalizer), introduce soft value functions and their role in actor‑critic architecture, explore the change-of-variables relationship between rewards and optimal policies, and conclude with practical implications for reward shaping and policy parameterization.

# KL‑regularized RL objectives and the constrained reward maximization viewpoint

Begin with an MDP characterized by states $s \in \mathcal{S}$, actions $a \in \mathcal{A}$, transition dynamics $P(s' \mid s,a)$, and (possibly discounted) cumulative reward functional. Let $\pi(a\mid s)$ denote a stochastic policy and let $\pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s)$ be a reference or prior policy. The KL‑regularized objective has the form of maximizing expected reward while penalizing divergence from the reference policy:

\[
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r(s_t,a_t) \right] - \beta \, \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big) \right],
\]

where $\beta > 0$ is a temperature-like coefficient that trades off reward versus divergence from the reference, and $\tau$ denotes a trajectory. For infinite-horizon discounted problems, the sums extend to infinity with discount factor $\gamma \in [0,1)$; for episodic tasks the horizon $T$ is finite or random.

An equivalent viewpoint is to treat the divergence term as a constraint: maximize expected reward subject to a bound on the average KL divergence from the reference policy. More precisely, consider

\[
\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\Big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_{t}\gamma^t D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid s_t))\Big] \le C,
\]

for some budget $C$. Using a Lagrange multiplier $\beta>0$, the constrained problem turns into the penalized form above. This shows that $\beta$ has the interpretation of a Lagrange multiplier controlling how strictly the agent must stay close to $\pi_\text{ref}$. Small $\beta$ forces small divergence (strong regularization), producing policies that remain near $\pi_\text{ref}$; large $\beta$ makes the divergence penalty weak, approaching the standard reward-maximization objective.

Two intuitive interpretations make the KL penalty appealing. The first is regularization: it prevents wild policy updates that drift far from a known safe or exploratory reference. The second is information cost: deviating from a prior policy incurs an informational or control cost proportional to KL divergence, representing limited bandwidth for specifying actions or risk-aversion to policy deviation. Both interpretations motivate the emergence of stochastic optimal policies rather than purely deterministic ones.

# Derivation of Gibbs/softmax policy π(y) ∝ π_ref(y) exp(r(y)/β)

To see how the Gibbs (softmax) form emerges, consider the simplified problem of selecting a single decision $y$ from a discrete set $\mathcal{Y}$ under reward $r(y)$ and prior $\pi_\text{ref}(y)$. The KL‑regularized objective for a single-step decision is

\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot)} \; \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} \pi(y) r(y) - \beta \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} \pi(y) \log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_y \pi(y)=1,\; \pi(y)\ge 0.
\]

This is a concave optimization (linear minus KL is concave in $\pi$) that can be solved with Lagrange multipliers. Form the Lagrangian with multiplier $\lambda$ for the normalization constraint:

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda) = \sum_y \pi(y) r(y) - \beta \sum_y \pi(y) \log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} + \lambda\Big(1-\sum_y \pi(y)\Big).
\]

Take derivative with respect to $\pi(y)$ and set to zero (KKT conditions):

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \pi(y)} = r(y) - \beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} + 1\Big) - \lambda = 0.
\]

Rearrange to isolate $\pi(y)$:

\[
\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} = \frac{r(y)-\lambda}{\beta} - 1,
\]

or, exponentiating and absorbing constants into a normalizer $Z$,

\[
\pi(y) = \pi_\text{ref}(y) \exp\!\big(r(y)/\beta\big) \cdot \exp\!\big(-(\lambda+\beta)/\beta\big).
\]

Since the final scalar factor must make $\pi$ sum to 1, we write

\[
\pi(y) = \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(y) \exp\!\big(r(y)/\beta\big)}{Z}, \qquad Z \equiv \sum_{y'} \pi_\text{ref}(y') \exp\!\big(r(y')/\beta\big).
\]

This is the Gibbs or Boltzmann distribution relative to the base measure $\pi_\text{ref}$. When $\pi_\text{ref}$ is uniform, this reduces to the standard softmax $\pi(y)\propto \exp(r(y)/\beta)$. The derivation extends naturally to state‑conditioned policies $\pi(a\mid s) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s)\exp(Q(s,a)/\beta)$ when instantaneous rewards are replaced by action-value functions.

Two limits are informative. As $\beta\to 0^+$, the exponential becomes sharply peaked on the maximizer(s) of $r(y)$ and $\pi$ tends to concentrate on maximum-reward choices (approaching argmax deterministic policies). As $\beta\to\infty$, the influence of reward vanishes and $\pi\to\pi_\text{ref}$ (policy remains at prior). Thus $\beta$ interpolates between pure exploitation and adherence to the reference.

# Partition function (normalizer) properties and computational challenges

The partition function, denoted $Z$ above, is

\[
Z = \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} \pi_\text{ref}(y) \exp\!\big(r(y)/\beta\big),
\]

and plays several crucial roles. First, $Z$ ensures $\pi$ is a valid probability distribution. Second, its logarithm, the log-partition function

\[
\log Z = \log \left( \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\text{ref}}\big[ \exp(r(y)/\beta) \big] \right),
\]

is the cumulant generating function of the reward under the prior and is convex in $r$. Third, $\log Z$ appears as the optimal objective value: substituting the Gibbs policy back into the original objective gives the maximum equal to $\beta \log Z$ (plus any constants), connecting expected reward and free energy in statistical physics.

Several useful identities follow from $Z$. The gradient of $\log Z$ with respect to perturbations of the reward function gives expectations under the Gibbs policy:

\[
\frac{\partial \log Z}{\partial r(y)} = \frac{1}{\beta} \cdot \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(y)\exp(r(y)/\beta)}{Z} = \frac{1}{\beta}\pi(y).
\]

Thus the Gibbs distribution is the score function of the log-partition. Second derivatives yield covariances: the Hessian of $\log Z$ with respect to $r$ is \(\frac{1}{\beta^2}\) times the covariance matrix under $\pi$.

However, computing $Z$ is often computationally challenging. For discrete finite action sets, $Z$ requires summing the exponentially weighted values over all actions; for continuous actions, $Z$ becomes an integral

\[
Z = \int \pi_\text{ref}(a) \exp\!\big(r(a)/\beta\big)\, da,
\]

which may be intractable analytically. In long-horizon RL problems, the "reward" in the exponent is replaced by a return or Q-function, and the partition function involves sums or integrals over whole sequences or actions at every state; this compound structure can make exact computation infeasible.

Several approximation strategies are common. If the action space is small, one can compute $Z$ exactly. For continuous actions, if $\pi_\text{ref}$ and the exponentiated Q have convenient forms (e.g., Gaussian prior and quadratic Q), closed-form integrals might exist. Otherwise Monte Carlo sampling (importance sampling, with $\pi_\text{ref}$ as proposal) is used to approximate $Z$ or the normalized policy directly. For function approximation in large MDPs, one typically avoids computing $Z$ explicitly and instead works with values or gradients that can be estimated from samples, or leverages reparameterizations that bypass $Z$.

A critical computational property is numerical stability: exponentiating large Q-values produces very large numbers, causing overflow; subtracting a baseline (e.g., the max Q) before exponentiation stabilizes computation, because

\[
\pi(a\mid s) = \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\big((Q(s,a)-C)/\beta\big)}{\sum_{a'} \pi_\text{ref}(a'\mid s) \exp\!\big((Q(s,a')-C)/\beta\big)}
\]

is invariant to constant shifts $C$ in $Q$. Since $Z$ is essentially a weighted log-sum-exp, many numerically stable algorithms use the log-sum-exp trick to evaluate $\log Z$ and its gradients.

# Soft value functions and their role in actor‑critic learning

When KL regularization or entropy regularization is applied in sequential decision-making, it is natural to define "soft" counterparts to the standard Bellman operators and value functions. Consider the infinite-horizon discounted setting with action-value function $Q^\pi(s,a)$ and state-value $V^\pi(s)$. Under the KL-penalized objective with reference $\pi_\text{ref}$ and temperature $\beta$, the soft state-value is defined as the expected soft return, or more conveniently via the log-partition:

\[
V(s) \equiv \beta \log \int \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\big(Q(s,a)/\beta\big)\, da.
\]

Equivalently, in discrete action spaces,

\[
V(s) = \beta \log \sum_a \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\big(Q(s,a)/\beta\big).
\]

The soft Bellman equation relates $Q$ and $V$:

\[
Q(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \, \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)} \big[ V(s') \big].
\]

Combining these two relations yields a fixed-point equation (soft Bellman backup) for $V$:

\[
V(s) = \beta \log \sum_a \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(s,a) + \gamma \, \mathbb{E}_{s'}[V(s')]}{\beta}\Big).
\]

This operator is a smooth, contraction-like mapping under appropriate conditions and admits a unique fixed point. The soft value function reduces to the standard Bellman value function as $\beta\to 0$ (due to log-sum-exp approaching max) and approaches a baseline determined by the prior as $\beta\to\infty$.

The optimal soft policy induced by the soft Q is

\[
\pi^*(a\mid s) = \frac{\pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\big(Q^*(s,a)/\beta\big)}{\sum_{a'} \pi_\text{ref}(a'\mid s) \exp\!\big(Q^*(s,a')/\beta\big)}.
\]

This motivates soft actor-critic algorithms: one can estimate soft Q and V functions (or only Q) from data, and then update the policy towards the Gibbs form implied by current Q estimates. In actor‑critic terms, the critic fits a soft Q (or V) using Bellman backups that include the soft log-sum-exp; the actor is updated to minimize the KL between the parametric policy and the target Gibbs policy (or maximize the soft objective directly). The entropy/KL regularization makes the optimization landscape smoother, often improving stability and exploration.

From a gradient perspective, the gradient of the soft objective with respect to policy parameters can be expressed using a soft advantage: the gradient of $\log Z$ yields $\pi$ so that estimated advantages are tempered by the softmax. In practice, sample-based estimators of soft Bellman errors, along with replay buffers and off-policy corrections, are used in soft actor-critic implementations.

# Change‑of‑variables between rewards and policies under soft optimality

A fascinating property of the soft optimality conditions is that, given a desired policy, one can often find a reward function (up to an additive potential shaping term) for which that policy is optimal under the KL regularization. This establishes a many-to-one mapping between rewards and optimal policies when using soft criteria.

From the one-step derivation, the optimal policy satisfies

\[
\pi(a\mid s) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} Q(s,a)\big).
\]

Taking logs gives

\[
\log \pi(a\mid s) = \log \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) + \tfrac{1}{\beta} Q(s,a) - \log Z(s),
\]

where $\log Z(s)$ is the per-state log-partition. Rearranged,

\[
Q(s,a) = \beta\big( \log \pi(a\mid s) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s) \big) + \beta\log Z(s).
\]

Thus if one sets a reward function so that it matches this implied $Q$ minus the discounted expected next-state value (i.e., ensure $Q=r+\gamma \mathbb{E}[V']$), the policy will be optimal. The additive term $\beta\log Z(s)$ is a per-state offset; since only differences in $Q$ across actions matter for policy selection, this offset is analogous to potential-based shaping. Indeed, potential-based reward shaping preserves optimal policies in standard RL; in the soft context, adding a state-dependent potential shifts $Q$ by a state-dependent constant which cancels out in the exponent when normalizing the Gibbs policy.

Therefore, the mapping from reward to policy is not one-to-one: many reward functions differing by shaped potentials or state-only terms produce the same optimal soft policy. Conversely, given a desired policy $\pi$, a corresponding (soft) $Q$ can be constructed via $Q = \beta \log(\pi/\pi_\text{ref}) + \text{const}(s)$. A practical consequence is that when learning policies by reward design, one must be aware that shaping and constants can substantially change optimization dynamics without changing the final optimal policy.

This change-of-variables also highlights invertibility constraints: if $\pi(a\mid s)$ assigns zero probability to some actions but $\pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s)>0$, then $\log(\pi/\pi_\text{ref})$ is $-\infty$, implying an infinitely negative $Q$ there; realistically, one wants $\pi$ to be absolutely continuous w.r.t. $\pi_\text{ref}$ to avoid singularities. In practice, choosing $\pi_\text{ref}$ with full support (e.g., a small-variance Gaussian or a soft uniform) avoids such issues and ensures well-defined $Q$ values.

# Practical implications: reward shaping vs policy reparameterization

The theoretical relationships above have several practical consequences for RL algorithm design.

First, reward shaping. Potential-based reward shaping is well-known to preserve optimal deterministic policies in classical RL. Under KL-regularized objectives and soft optimality, adding state-only shaping terms corresponds to adding constants inside the log-partition, leaving the optimal Gibbs policy unchanged. However, shaping affects the soft value functions and the landscape that learning algorithms navigate. Properly chosen shaping can accelerate learning by providing informative gradients or reducing variance. Conversely, poorly chosen shaping can mislead the learning dynamics, leading to slower convergence even if the final policy set is unchanged. Because soft policies depend on exponentials of Q, scaling and shifting rewards can dramatically change the effective temperature experienced during learning, so shaping should be applied carefully.

Second, policy reparameterization. Algorithms that directly parameterize the policy (e.g., parametric neural network mapping states to action distributions) and attempt to minimize the KL to the Gibbs target face two main pathways: (1) compute an explicit target Gibbs policy from current Q estimates and project the parametric policy onto it (policy-imitation-style update), or (2) directly optimize the KL-regularized objective via policy gradients where the KL term appears explicitly. The choice affects stability and sample efficiency. Direct policy parameterization can avoid computing the partition function explicitly, because gradients of the KL-regularized objective with respect to policy parameters involve expectations under the current parametric policy, which are estimable by samples. On the other hand, projecting onto the Gibbs policy often requires estimating normalizers or performing importance-weighted updates.

Third, exploration and robustness. The soft/Gibbs policy naturally encourages exploration because policies remain stochastic; temperature $\beta$ controls exploration magnitude. KL to a prior $\pi_\text{ref}$ is a flexible instrument: picking $\pi_\text{ref}$ to bias exploration toward safe or likely actions can encode domain knowledge. Moreover, in transfer or continual learning, using a prior learned from previous tasks enforces retention of prior behaviors through the KL penalty.

Fourth, numerical and estimator issues. In continuous-action settings with complex Q-functions, directly computing the Gibbs policy may be intractable. Practical algorithms typically either assume tractable families (e.g., Gaussian $\pi_\text{ref}$ and quadratic approximations of $Q$), use sampling to approximate normalization integrals, or parameterize the policy and update it by minimizing sample-based estimates of the KL. Off-policy correction is also critical: using samples collected from a behavior policy that differs from $\pi$ requires careful importance weighting to estimate expectations involving $\pi$ or the Gibbs policy.

Finally, interpretability and inverse design. The change-of-variables expression $Q = \beta\log(\pi/\pi_\text{ref}) + \text{const}$ offers a way to design reward signals from desired policies: if we want the agent to behave according to $\pi$, we can set rewards to make $\pi$ the Gibbs-optimal policy. This is useful in inverse RL and imitation learning, where one can recover a reward (up to potential shaping) that rationalizes observed behavior under a soft-optimality assumption.

# Conclusion

Treating control as inference via KL regularization yields a unified and conceptually clean framework: optimal policies are Gibbs distributions that balance reward and divergence from a prior. The partition function encodes the normalization and is intimately linked to soft value functions; although it provides elegant analytic properties, it also introduces computational challenges in large or continuous domains. Soft value functions smooth the Bellman operator and underpin stable actor‑critic algorithms. The mapping between rewards and policies under soft optimality reveals degrees of freedom (e.g., potential-based shaping) and offers mechanisms for reward design and policy imitation. Understanding these relationships equips the practitioner to design algorithms and shaping strategies that exploit the tradeoffs between reward maximization, information constraints, and prior knowledge.
