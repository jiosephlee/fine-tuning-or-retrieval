Introduction

Policy gradient methods form the backbone of many modern approaches to optimizing language models (LMs) for task-specific behavior. When the policy is a neural LM that outputs tokens, gradient-based optimization of expected reward enables direct shaping of model behavior toward desired objectives — from improved helpfulness to safety or alignment with human preferences. However, naïve policy gradient estimators suffer from high variance, poor sample efficiency, and unstable updates. This chapter develops the practical toolkit needed to implement policy gradient training for language models: we describe REINFORCE and its baseline and control-variate extensions, explain value functions and advantage estimation, review reward normalization and credit assignment across tokens, analyze variance–bias trade-offs in gradient estimators, cover stochastic approximation and sample efficiency techniques, present clipping and other stability tricks, and conclude with diagnostics and debugging strategies that practitioners need.

Throughout, let the policy be a parameterized conditional distribution over token sequences. Denote by $\pi_\theta(a_{1:T}\mid s)$ the probability under parameters $\theta$ of generating a token sequence $a_{1:T} = (a_1,\dots,a_T)$ given context or state $s$ (where $s$ may include a prompt, past conversation, or other conditioning). We write rewards as scalar functions $r(a_{1:T}, s)$ (possibly delayed, sparse, or computed via a reward model) and our objective is to maximize expected reward
\[
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D},\,a_{1:T}\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[ r(a_{1:T}, s) \big].
\]
Policy gradients estimate $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ from sampled trajectories. The remainder of the chapter explains how to do this in practice with low variance and robust behavior.

REINFORCE with baselines and control variates

The canonical Monte Carlo policy gradient estimator is the REINFORCE gradient:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a_{1:T}}\big[ r(a_{1:T}, s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{1:T}\mid s) \big].
\]
Using the chain rule for autoregressive policies,
\[
\log \pi_\theta(a_{1:T}\mid s) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid a_{<t}, s),
\]
so the gradient decomposes across timesteps. The empirical estimator draws samples and replaces the expectation with an average. Although unbiased, REINFORCE typically has very high variance because the full-return $r$ multiplies every log-probability term.

A standard variance-reduction technique is to subtract a baseline $b(s)$ from the reward:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[ \big(r - b(s)\big)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{1:T}\mid s) \big].
\]
Any function $b(s)$ independent of the action sequence preserves unbiasedness while potentially lowering variance. Choosing $b(s)$ well is critical. The simplest baseline is the scalar running average of recent returns, but more powerful baselines use conditional information such as the state: $b(s) \approx \mathbb{E}[r\mid s]$.

This idea generalizes to control variates, which are functions $c(s,a_{1:T})$ correlated with the return but with a known expected value. If we can compute or estimate $\mathbb{E}[c]$, we can subtract a scaled control variate $\alpha(c - \mathbb{E}[c])$ from the reward while correcting for bias. In practice, baselines are usually state-dependent and trained to estimate expected returns; when the baseline is a learned value function $V_\phi(s)$, choosing $\alpha=1$ and subtracting $V_\phi$ yields the familiar advantage-based estimator described next.

Value functions and advantage estimation

Value functions provide conditional expected returns and serve as natural baselines. Define the state value
\[
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a_{1:T}\sim\pi}\big[ r(a_{1:T}, s) \mid s \big].
\]
When a parameterized value function $V_\phi(s)$ approximates $V^\pi(s)$, the policy gradient can use the advantage
\[
A^\pi(s, a_{1:T}) = r(a_{1:T}, s) - V^\pi(s)
\]
to center updates:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[ A^\pi(s,a_{1:T})\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_{1:T}\mid s) \big].
\]
Using the advantage reduces variance because it replaces the raw return with a residual capturing how much a sequence outperformed expectation.

In sequence generation, it is often more useful to evaluate advantage at each timestep, assigning credit locally. Introduce action-value functions
\[
Q^\pi(s, a_{1:t}) = \mathbb{E}\big[ r(a_{1:T}, s) \mid s, a_{1:t} \big],
\]
and define the advantage at time $t$ as
\[
A_t = Q^\pi(s,a_{1:t}) - V^\pi(s, a_{<t}),
\]
where the conditioning of the value can include the prefix $a_{<t}$ if desired. Practically, we may use a conditional value network that takes the current prefix (or hidden state) and predicts expected future reward from that point.

Temporal-difference and Monte Carlo approaches both exist for training value functions. Monte Carlo targets use full returns $r$ and are unbiased but noisy; temporal-difference (TD) targets trade bias for lower variance by bootstrapping:
\[
V_\phi(s, a_{<t}) \leftarrow r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s, a_{\le t}),
\]
where $r_t$ may be the immediate reward at time $t$ and $\gamma$ a discount factor. For episodic tasks where the reward is only observed at the end, TD becomes equivalent to bootstrapping from later estimates. The advantage actor-critic (A2C/A3C) family jointly trains the policy and value function; the policy uses the advantage for updates, while the value function is trained to regress to returns or TD targets.

Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) is a widely used method to compute lower-variance advantage estimates while retaining control over bias. Define the TD residuals (one-step advantages)
\[
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s, a_{\le t}) - V_\phi(s, a_{<t}),
\]
and form exponentially-weighted sums:
\[
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},
\]
where $\lambda\in[0,1]$ interpolates between high-variance, low-bias Monte Carlo ($\lambda=1$) and low-variance, high-bias TD ($\lambda=0$). Practitioners tune $\lambda$ to balance bias and variance for their environment, with $\lambda$ often near $0.95$ or $0.97$ in practice.

Reward normalization and standardization

Reward scales and distributions have outsized influence on training dynamics. If rewards are too large, gradient magnitudes explode; if too small, learning stalls. Nonstationary and heavy-tailed reward distributions further complicate training. Standard remedies are reward normalization and standardization.

A simple approach is to maintain running estimates of the reward mean $\mu_r$ and standard deviation $\sigma_r$ and to normalize returns or advantages via
\[
\tilde{r} = \frac{r - \mu_r}{\sigma_r + \epsilon},\qquad \tilde{A} = \frac{A - \mu_A}{\sigma_A + \epsilon}.
\]
Normalizing advantages is especially helpful because the learning rate for policy updates then becomes more stable across training. When using a learned value function baseline, one should be careful to compute advantage normalization after baseline subtraction, using statistics collected across recent minibatches or trajectories.

For episodic tasks or when rewards are sparse and delayed, it is often beneficial to clip returns to a range or apply an inverse-squared-root scaling to reduce heavy tails. Another common trick is to use reward shaping: add auxiliary dense rewards that correlate with the final objective, but ensure that shaping does not change optimal policies (e.g., shaping as potential differences).

Credit assignment across tokens

Language modeling requires credit assignment across long token sequences: a reward given at the end of a sampled response must be attributed to token decisions at each timestep. There are multiple strategies to handle this.

The simplest is to apply the same scalar return to each timestep's log-probability (the vanilla REINFORCE approach), but this spreads credit uniformly and ignores temporal structure. Value-function-based advantage estimates that condition on the prefix provide better token-level credit: the value at timestep $t$ estimates expected future reward given prefix $a_{<t}$, so the difference $Q^\pi(s,a_{1:t}) - V^\pi(s,a_{<t})$ measures how much the actual token chosen at $t$ changed expected return.

Another practical method is to use token-level rewards when available: if certain tokens correspond to identifiable events (e.g., generating a factually correct sentence or mentioning a constrained term), assign rewards at the timestep where the event occurs. These dense signals reduce variance and make learning faster.

For long responses or when rewards are sparse, hierarchical credit assignment can help: learn a higher-level policy that chooses coarse actions (e.g., high-level plan or segments) and a lower-level token policy that fills in detail. Alternatively, use truncated backpropagation-style approaches where gradients are computed over segments and value estimates provide bootstrapped continuation estimates.

Finally, attention to autoregressive conditioning matters: the policy gradient for a token depends on how that token influences subsequent tokens and ultimately the reward. Accurate value estimates that take into account the autoregressive hidden state lead to better token-level advantage estimation.

Gradient estimators and variance–bias trade-offs

A central theme is the trade-off between estimator variance and bias. Monte Carlo estimators such as REINFORCE are unbiased but often high variance. Bootstrapping and critic-based methods introduce bias via value-function approximation and temporal-difference updates, but can substantially reduce variance and speed learning.

More formally, suppose an estimator \(\hat{g}\) of the true gradient \(g\) has mean bias \(b = \mathbb{E}[\hat{g}] - g\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). The mean-squared error decomposes as
\[
\mathbb{E}\|\hat{g} - g\|^2 = \|b\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma).
\]
Reducing variance \(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)\) at the cost of adding a small bias \(\|b\|^2\) can lower MSE and improve practical convergence. Methods that deliberately trade bias for variance include using lower \(\lambda\) in GAE, fitting parametric baselines, using truncated rollouts with bootstrapping, and applying entropy regularization implicitly altering the objective.

Control variates beyond baselines can further reduce variance. For autoregressive policies, one can derive per-token control variates using local expected gradients computed under the current policy, though computing exact expectations is usually infeasible. Importance sampling can be used to reuse off-policy data but introduces variance from importance weights; truncating or clipping weights introduces bias but can stabilize learning (see later on clipping tricks).

Variance reduction techniques are not cost-free: estimating a value function reliably may require substantial additional data and capacity, and mis-specified critics can introduce harmful bias or destabilize learning if their predictions are poor. Cross-validation, early stopping, and conservative update magnitudes mitigate these risks.

Stochastic approximations and sample efficiency

Policy gradient training is a stochastic approximation problem: we use noisy samples to approximate expectations and update parameters incrementally. The Robbins–Monro framework gives conditions for convergence under diminishing step sizes and unbiased gradient estimates, but in practice we use finite learning rates, minibatch sampling, and function approximation.

Sample efficiency is especially important with expensive reward signals (e.g., human preferences or large evaluation models). Several techniques improve sample efficiency:

- Baseline and critic usage: reducing variance lowers the number of samples needed to achieve a given gradient estimator quality.
- Experience replay and importance sampling: reuse past trajectories by reweighting with importance ratios $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$. This re-use improves sample efficiency but requires careful handling of variance and distribution shift.
- Off-policy actor-critic and Q-learning-inspired methods: learn value or action-value functions from off-policy data; methods like Deep Q-Networks do not apply directly to large autoregressive action spaces but hybrid approaches (e.g., off-policy correction with retrace or V-trace) can help.
- Synchronous parallel data collection: collecting multiple trajectories in parallel increases throughput. Make sure to account for parameter lag between collectors and learners by using importance weights or small learning rates.
- Reward modeling and distilled critics: train a cheap surrogate reward model or critic to predict expensive reward signals; use the surrogate for many updates and occasionally correct with true signals.
- Variance-reduced gradient estimators: combine control variates, GAE, and layerwise baselines to reduce per-sample variance, improving effective sample efficiency.

Clipping and other stability tricks

Training with high-variance gradients or rapidly changing policies can cause catastrophic updates. Several practical stability techniques are widely used.

Trust-region or clipping methods limit how far the policy changes each update. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a commonly used technique that clips the probability ratio
\[
r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}
\]
in the surrogate objective:
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}} = \mathbb{E}\big[ \min\big( r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \ \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t \big) \big].
\]
The clip prevents large policy updates in directions that would excessively increase the objective, reducing destructive parameter jumps. PPO is popular because it is simple, effective, and compatible with minibatch SGD on neural networks.

Gradient clipping (by norm or value) prevents exploding gradients from causing instability. Typical settings clip gradient norms to values like 0.5–1.0 depending on model size and learning rate.

Learning rate schedules and adaptive optimizers are crucial: use modest learning rates for policy parameters and often a separate, sometimes smaller, learning rate for value-function parameters. Weight decay and layer normalization help maintain well-conditioned training.

Entropy regularization encourages exploration and prevents premature collapse to deterministic policies. Adding $-\beta\mathbb{E}[\sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid \cdot)]$ to the objective with $\beta>0$ pushes the policy to maintain stochasticity, which can be useful when rewards are noisy or sparse. Annealing $\beta$ over time lets exploration gradually decay.

Batching and averaging updates across multiple sampled trajectories reduce variance. When collecting data asynchronously, ensure that the policy used to generate trajectories is not too stale or correct for it.

Diagnostics and debugging RL training

Policy gradient training for LMs can fail for many reasons. Systematic diagnostics help locate problems and decide remedies. Useful diagnostics include:

- Monitor the mean and variance of returns and advantages. If means are extremely noisy or variances explode, check reward computation, normalization, and reward clipping.
- Track the policy entropy and the KL divergence between the current policy and the data collection policy. If entropy collapses quickly, increase entropy regularization or reduce learning rate. If KL grows too large in one update, use clipping or trust-region constraints.
- Validate value-function quality: plot predicted values versus empirical returns and compute RMSE and bias. Large, systematic value errors indicate mis-specified architecture, insufficient capacity, or optimization problems. If the critic is biased high or low, advantage estimates will be corrupted.
- Check gradient norms and loss components separately for policy and value networks. Sudden spikes in gradients, or one loss dominating, indicate instability or learning-rate mismatch.
- Use ablation studies: turn off reward normalization, baselines, or clipping individually to measure their contribution. Monitor sample efficiency when enabling replay or off-policy corrections.
- Inspect sample diversity and mode collapse: examine generated samples qualitatively; token-level diagnostics (e.g., n-gram repetition statistics) reveal degeneracies.
- Run training with synthetic or oracle rewards. A ground-truth reward function that is deterministic and easy to optimize is a helpful debugging baseline: if training fails even with simple rewards, the issue is likely in optimization rather than reward signal noise.
- Use seed and environment determinism to reproduce failures. Randomness in data collection complicates debugging; deterministic runs help isolate bugs.
- Record off-policy importance weights when reusing data and monitor their distribution. Extremely large weights signal severe distribution mismatch and will cause high variance.
- Visualize learning curves for short and long-run metrics: immediate reward increase can hide long-term degradation (e.g., loss of fluency or safety). Regular human-in-the-loop checks or automated safety checks help.

Conclusion

Policy gradient methods enable direct optimization of language models with respect to scalar reward signals, but practical success depends on careful variance reduction, stable optimization, and thoughtful diagnostics. Baselines and learned value functions provide essential variance reduction; advantage estimation (including GAE) tunes the variance–bias trade-off; reward normalization and token-level credit assignment improve learning dynamics for long sequences; stochastic approximation and sample-reuse techniques boost sample efficiency; and clipping, entropy regularization, and gradient normalization stabilize updates. Finally, robust diagnostics and controlled experiments are indispensable for identifying failure modes and ensuring that improvements on training metrics translate to desired behavior in deployed models. Mastery of these techniques lets practitioners reliably shape LM policies with policy-gradient algorithms while managing the practical challenges of variance, bias, and limited reward signal budgets.
