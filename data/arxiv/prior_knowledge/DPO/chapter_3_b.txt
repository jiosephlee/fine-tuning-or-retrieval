# Exposure bias and mitigation strategies

Exposure bias refers to the mismatch between training and inference conditions caused by teacher forcing. During training, at time $t$ the model conditions on the true prefix $y_{<t}$, whereas during generation it conditions on tokens the model itself produced, which may be erroneous. Small mistakes at early steps can lead the model to prefixes it has never seen during training, where its predictions may be poor, leading to error compounding.

Several strategies have been proposed to reduce exposure bias:

1. Scheduled sampling: Proposed as a curriculum, scheduled sampling mixes teacher-forced tokens and model-sampled tokens during training. At each training step and time $t$, with some probability $\epsilon$ the model is fed its own sampled token from $p_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})$ instead of the ground-truth token. The mixing probability can anneal over training, gradually exposing the model to its own errors. While intuitive, scheduled sampling can introduce bias in learning and may harm convergence unless carefully tuned.

2. Data augmentation / noise injection: Injecting noise into the input prefixes during training (e.g., token dropout, token replacements, shuffling) exposes the model to imperfect contexts similar to those encountered at inference. This can help robustness and reduce compounding errors.

3. Professor forcing and adversarial techniques: These methods train a model to not only make correct next-token predictions under teacher forcing, but also to have similar hidden-state dynamics when generating freely. Professor forcing uses an adversarial discriminator to align the distribution of internal states between teacher-forced runs and free-running runs, encouraging the model to behave similarly under both regimes.

4. Sequence-level training and reinforcement learning: One can train to optimize sequence-level objectives (e.g., BLEU, ROUGE, human preference) using policy gradient methods (REINFORCE) or minimum risk training. These methods directly consider the model's sampling dynamics but can be unstable, have high variance gradients, and require careful reward shaping and baseline techniques.

5. Fine-tuning on model-generated prefixes: Iteratively generating model outputs, pairing them with human corrections or scoring, and fine-tuning on these examples helps the model learn to recover from its own mistakes. Self-training and iterative back-translation are variants in different contexts.

6. Constrained decoding and reranking: At inference, one can constrain or rerank candidate sequences using external models (e.g., a separate discriminator or a scoring function trained on human labels) to reduce undesirable outputs caused by exposure to unseen prefixes.

Each mitigation strategy has trade-offs: many complicate training, increase compute, or require task-specific reward signals. For large-scale LMs, the dominant approach remains teacher forcing with careful regularization and often subsequent task-specific fine-tuning or reranking.


# Per-token vs. sequence-level objectives

MLE with teacher forcing produces a per-token objective: the loss decomposes as a sum of cross-entropies per position. Per-token objectives are convenient because they are decomposable, lead to low-variance gradient estimates, and fully leverage parallel computation across sequence positions. However, they do not directly optimize sequence-level metrics such as BLEU or human preference, and they ignore the way local improvements might affect global sequence quality.

Sequence-level objectives evaluate entire generated sequences and often reflect better the real downstream goals. Examples include maximizing BLEU, ROUGE, or human-evaluated reward functions. Optimizing such objectives typically requires policy-gradient methods from reinforcement learning (e.g., REINFORCE) or minimum risk training, where expected sequence-level loss is minimized by sampling sequences or using approximate search to obtain candidate outputs. The gradient formulas involve weighting sampled sequences by their sequence-level reward, which can suffer from high variance and require baselines or variance-reduction techniques.

There is a middle ground. One can use per-token objectives augmented with sequence-level constraints. For example, minimum risk training may compute expected sequence loss over a small beam of candidate outputs and differentiate through the model probabilities for those candidates; or one can train with a mixed objective combining per-token cross-entropy and a small weight on a policy-gradient term to encourage sequence-level improvements. Another practical approach is to use reranking: generate a set of candidates with the autoregressive model and train a separate reranker (possibly sequence-aware) to choose the best candidate according to sequence-level criteria. This leverages the efficiency of per-token training with the flexibility of sequence-level evaluation at inference time.

In practice, per-token MLE remains the core training method for large-scale LMs because it is reliable and scalable. Sequence-level fine-tuning or reranking is then used as a refinement when the evaluation metric or task requires optimizing holistic properties.


# Computing and caching log-probabilities

Computing per-token probabilities during training is straightforward under teacher forcing: for a training example $(x,y)$ of length $T$, you form the input sequence consisting of context plus the token prefix (e.g., shifted right by one), run the Transformer in parallel to obtain logits $z_t\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}$ for each timestep $t$, and compute the conditional probabilities by softmax:

$p_\theta(v\mid x,y_{<t}) \;=\; \frac{\exp(z_{t,v})}{\sum_{u\in V}\exp(z_{t,u})}.$

The loss is $-\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$, computed efficiently as a batch of matrix operations.

At generation time, computing log-probabilities for candidate sequences needs to be efficient because decoding is sequential: each new token conditions on previously generated tokens. Transformers allow caching of key and value tensors for each attention layer so that when the model generates token $y_t$, it only needs to compute the attention contributions for the new token and reuse cached keys/values for $y_{<t}$. Concretely, in self-attention, the attention scores are computed from queries produced by the new token and keys/values aggregated from all previous tokens; caching avoids recomputing the expensive projection of all previous tokens at each step. This reduces per-step complexity from $O(t)$ to $O(1)$ per cached key/value projection (though attention still considers all keys for the new query).

When computing log-probabilities for entire candidate sequences (e.g., during beam search), two modes are common. First, incremental generation computes probabilities online: at each step the model computes the conditional distribution over the next token for all beam hypotheses (using cached states), expands the beam, and repeats. The cumulative log-prob for a candidate is the sum of the log-probs of the sampled tokens, which is tracked incrementally. Second, when verifying or scoring complete sequences (for reranking or evaluation), one can compute the sequence log-probability in a single forward pass by teacher forcing the model on the full ground-truth sequence shifted appropriately; this parallel computation is efficient but requires the sequence to be known a priori.

Batching and parallelization: During training, teacher forcing allows full batch parallelism across timesteps, which is crucial for efficient GPU/TPU utilization. During decoding, caching enables efficient per-step computation but reduces parallelism across time; however, parallelism across beam elements and across different input prompts is usually exploited to keep throughput high. When computing log-probabilities for many candidate completions (as in reranking), it can be beneficial to group candidates by prefix or use batched teacher-forced passes for each candidate to exploit parallelism.

Numerical stability and underflow: Log-probabilities avoid underflow because probabilities of long sequences can be extremely small. Working in log-space and summing log-probabilities is numerically stable. For normalization across different lengths, care should be taken to use consistent length penalties and to avoid exponentiating large negative numbers.

Practical considerations: When implementing beam search at scale, it is common to maintain for each beam hypothesis the cumulative log-prob and the hidden states or cached keys/values necessary to continue expansion. Memory can be a bottleneck for large beams and long contexts; efficient implementations share cached states for prefixes when possible to avoid duplication. When scoring sequences produced outside the model (e.g., from an external policy), computing the exact log-probability requires feeding the entire sequence into the model in teacher-forced fashion to obtain per-token conditional probabilities and summing their logs.

Conclusion

Autoregressive Transformer LMs provide a flexible and powerful framework for conditional sequence modeling. The probabilistic factorization into token-level conditionals, combined with subword tokenization and maximum likelihood training via teacher forcing, makes large-scale training efficient and effective. However, challenges remain: length biases, exposure bias, alignment with sequence-level metrics, and efficient decoding for both deterministic and stochastic strategies. Understanding these topics — tokenization, autoregressive factorization, MLE training, decoding algorithms, exposure bias and its mitigations, the differences between per-token and sequence-level objectives, and practical computation of log-probabilities — is essential for reading and contributing to current research on language modeling and sequence generation.
