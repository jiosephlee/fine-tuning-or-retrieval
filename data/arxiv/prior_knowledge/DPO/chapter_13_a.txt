Introduction

Many real-world decision problems lack readily available, numerical reward signals. Instead, humans or systems provide preference judgments: "I prefer A to B" or "Between these two options, choose this one." Preference-based feedback is pervasive in recommender systems, human-in-the-loop optimization, and scenarios where absolute scoring is hard or ill-defined. This chapter places preference learning within bandit and reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks. We begin with contextual bandits and offline learning, extend to pairwise-comparison settings known as dueling bandits, and introduce tournament-theoretic concepts such as the Von Neumann winner. We then describe preference-based RL (PbRL) formulations and techniques for estimating latent scoring functions from comparisons. Throughout, we compare online and offline preference feedback modalities, discuss sample efficiency and exploration strategies, and connect these ideas to ranking and choice theory. The goal is to give an intuitive and mathematical understanding sufficient for reading research papers in this area.


# Contextual bandits and offline learning

Contextual bandits are a model for sequential decision-making under partial feedback. At each round $t$, the learner observes a context $x_t \in \mathcal{X}$ describing the current situation, chooses an action or arm $a_t$ from an action set $\mathcal{A}$, and receives a reward $r_t(a_t)$ for only the chosen action. The standard objective is to minimize cumulative regret relative to the best policy in some class. In classical (numerical reward) contextual bandits, the learner uses the triplets $(x_t,a_t,r_t)$ to learn a mapping from contexts and actions to expected rewards $r(x,a)=\mathbb{E}[r_t(a)\mid x_t=x,a_t=a]$.

Offline (or batch) contextual bandit learning operates when the learner is given a logged dataset of interactions collected by some behavior policy, and wishes to learn a good target policy without further interaction. This setting is common in applications where online exploration is costly or risky. A key technical challenge in offline bandits is distributional mismatch: the logged data covers only actions chosen by the behavior policy, so evaluating or improving other actions requires careful counterfactual estimation techniques such as importance sampling or doubly robust estimators. The reliability of offline learning depends critically on how well the behavior policy explored; if certain actions or context-action pairs were never or rarely tried, estimates for those pairs will have high variance or be biased.

When reward signals are not absolute but expressed as preferences, the contextual bandit formulation must be adapted. Instead of observing a scalar reward $r_t(a_t)$ after selecting $a_t$, one might observe a comparison between two actions, or a preference involving the chosen action and some baseline. Offline preference learning in this context means we have a dataset of tuples $(x, a, a', y)$ where $y$ encodes a preference or comparison outcome between $a$ and $a'$ in context $x$. Learning from such offline data requires counterfactual correction analogous to numerical offline bandits, but with binary or ordinal outcomes as observations. Estimators often model pairwise preference probabilities $P(a \succ a' \mid x)$. Challenges include selection bias in which pairs were compared, and the need to combine pairwise data into a policy that selects single actions.

Another offline consideration is policy evaluation: how to estimate the expected performance of a candidate policy from logged preference comparisons. Standard inverse propensity scoring generalizes by reweighting comparisons according to the probability that those comparisons would have been observed under the logging policy and the candidate policy. For example, if the logging policy compares arms according to a certain distribution, importance weighting allows estimating the candidate policy's performance, but variance management becomes even more critical because outcomes are binary and typically noisier than scalar reward observations.


# Dueling bandits and pairwise comparisons

Dueling bandits are a bandit variant explicitly built around pairwise comparisons rather than scalar rewards. At each round $t$, the learner chooses two actions $(a_t, b_t)$ (possibly the same action twice) and receives feedback indicating which of the two was preferred, sometimes with ties or probabilistic outcomes. Formally, for arms $i$ and $j$, a stochastic comparison oracle returns a random variable indicating whether $i$ wins against $j$, with probability $P(i \succ j)$. The goal can be to identify the best arm (in some sense) or to minimize regret measured relative to always playing the best arm against a challenger.

The dueling bandit setting captures problems where pairwise preference information is natural or more reliable than absolute scores. For example, in webpage design, users might be shown two alternatives and their clicks used to infer which layout is preferred, while absolute satisfaction scores are unavailable. The dueling bandits literature has proposed algorithms and regret notions adapted to pairwise feedback, such as interleaved comparisons, Copeland score maximization, and exploration strategies that pick informative pairs.

One central difficulty is that pairwise preferences need not be transitive: it may be that arm $i$ is preferred to $j$, $j$ preferred to $k$, but $k$ preferred to $i$. Non-transitivity complicates the notion of a single globally best arm. Consequently, dueling bandit algorithms depend on the target solution concept (e.g., Condorcet winner, Copeland winner, Borda winner, or Von Neumann winner). Algorithms vary in whether they aim to find a Condorcet winner when one exists, to identify a Copeland winner (arm maximizing the number of pairwise wins), or to minimize regret under different notions of optimality.

A common algorithmic idea is to maintain pairwise estimates $\hat{p}_{ij}$ of $P(i \succ j)$ and use confidence intervals to guide exploration. A naive approach would compare all pairs, but that is expensive for large action sets. More sophisticated approaches select comparisons to quickly reduce uncertainty relevant to identifying the target winner. Another key distinction is whether comparisons are restricted to involving a particular incumbent or whether any two arms can be paired; this affects the exploration design.

Finally, dueling bandit models can incorporate context: contextual dueling bandits consider pairwise preferences conditioned on context $x$, and the learner must choose pairs to compare to learn a policy mapping contexts to single actions. Such contextual dueling bandits combine the high-dimensional modeling challenges of contextual bandits with the pairwise nature of feedback.


# Von Neumann winner and tournament solutions

Tournament theory studies pairwise comparison outcomes among a set of alternatives and provides solution concepts for selecting winners when preferences may be cyclic. In the context of stochastic pairwise outcomes, one often constructs a matrix $P$ where $P_{ij} = P(i \succ j)$ denotes the probability that arm $i$ beats arm $j$. When comparisons are deterministic or majority-rule based, the matrix entries are in $\{0,1\}$, and we obtain a tournament graph. For stochastic feedback, the matrix is real-valued in $[0,1]$.

A classic solution concept is the Condorcet winner: an arm $i$ such that $P_{ij} > 0.5$ for all $j \neq i$. The Condorcet winner, when it exists, is an intuitively satisfying champion. However, Condorcet winners need not exist due to cycles. Other notions include the Copeland winner, which maximizes the number of pairwise victories, and the Borda winner, which ranks arms by their aggregate pairwise margins.

The Von Neumann winner arises from applying concepts from zero-sum games to tournaments. Consider a zero-sum game between a row player and a column player with payoff matrix $M$ where $M_{ij} = P_{ij}-1/2$, i.e., positive entries favor row. If the row player plays a mixed strategy (probability distribution) $u$ over arms and the column player plays distribution $v$, the expected payoff to the row player is $u^\top M v$. Von Neumann's minimax theorem guarantees that there exists a saddle-point pair $(u^*,v^*)$ such that
\[
\max_u \min_v u^\top M v = \min_v \max_u u^\top M v = \lambda^*.
\]
In symmetric tournaments (where the column player's choices correspond to the same alternatives), the equilibrium mixed strategy $u^*$ is often called a Von Neumann winner. Intuitively, $u^*$ is a randomized choice over arms that maximizes the minimum winning probability against any adversarial opponent. Operationally, if the metric of interest is robustness to adversarial matchups, choosing according to $u^*$ is a principled strategy.

The Von Neumann solution has attractive properties: it always exists and is unique for generic matrices under mild conditions for the value $\lambda^*$. It handles non-transitivity by randomization, rather than forcing a single deterministic winner. In dueling bandits and preference aggregation, the Von Neumann approach can yield policies with provable guarantees against worst-case opponents. Computing the Von Neumann winner reduces to solving a linear program: find a distribution $u$ that maximizes $\min_j \sum_i u_i M_{ij}$ under $u \ge 0$ and $\sum_i u_i = 1$. This linear-program viewpoint connects tournament solutions to optimization and learning: online algorithms can approximate $u^*$ by maintaining estimates of $M$ and using online learning techniques (e.g., follow-the-regularized-leader on the rows).

Other tournament solutions—like the top cycle, the uncovered set, or various scoring rules—offer different trade-offs between fairness, robustness, and interpretability. When translating these concepts to stochastic comparison settings, one must consider sample complexity: estimating $P_{ij}$ accurately for all pairs may be expensive, so many algorithms target more parsimonious objectives such as identifying a Copeland or Von Neumann winner while adaptively querying the most informative pairs.


# Preference-based RL (PbRL) formulations

Preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) extends preference feedback to sequential decision-making problems where actions influence future states. In standard RL, an agent interacts with an environment modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP) with states $s \in \mathcal{S}$, actions $a \in \mathcal{A}$, transition kernel $P(s' \mid s,a)$, and reward function $r(s,a)$. PbRL replaces the scalar reward function with preference information, such as a preference between trajectories or between local action choices.

There are multiple PbRL formulations. One common abstraction considers preferences over trajectories (or episodes). The environment produces trajectories $\tau = (s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots,s_T)$ under a policy $\pi$. A human or oracle compares two trajectories $\tau$ and $\tau'$ and reports a preference: either a binary outcome indicating which is better or a graded comparison. The learner's goal is to find a policy that generates trajectories that would be preferred. This trajectory-level formulation is natural when humans can inspect episodes and judge which execution is preferable but cannot provide dense scalar rewards at each timestep.

Another formulation assumes preferences over state-action pairs or local snippets. For example, an oracle might say that at state $s$, action $a$ is preferred to action $a'$. These local preferences can be aggregated to learn a latent reward function or to directly guide policy improvement. A middle ground is to elicit preferences over trajectory segments or summaries, which balances cognitive load on the oracle and the richness of the feedback.

PbRL approaches typically map preferences to a latent utility or reward function. One widely-used pipeline first fits a parametric function $r_\theta(\tau)$ or $r_\theta(s,a)$ that assigns a scalar score to trajectories or state-action pairs so that $r_\theta(\tau) > r_\theta(\tau')$ when $\tau$ is preferred to $\tau'$. This is often done by maximum likelihood or pairwise loss functions inspired by Bradley-Terry or Thurstone models. Once $r_\theta$ is learned, standard RL algorithms (policy gradient, value iteration, actor-critic) can optimize expected estimated reward to derive a policy.

An alternative is direct policy search guided by preferences without explicitly estimating a global reward. Preference comparison outcomes can be used to update a policy distribution via bandit algorithms on the policy parameter space, treating each policy as an arm and comparisons between policies as duels. Bayesian optimization over policy parameters using preference kernels (e.g., Gaussian processes over policy parameters with pairwise likelihoods) is another approach.

A key modeling choice is whether the learned reward is stationary and Markovian. Preferences over trajectories do not uniquely identify a Markov reward function unless additional structure is assumed. If the learned reward is non-Markovian, methods that rely on Bellman equations must be adapted. Some PbRL methods enforce a parametric Markov reward structure to permit integration with model-based or value-based RL, while others accept non-Markovian rewards and optimize policy search directly.

Evaluation and safety considerations are significant. Because the policy is optimized with respect to an inferred reward, misestimation can lead to undesirable behaviors. Proper exploration strategies and conservative updates can mitigate risk. Moreover, preference elicitation is costly; therefore, designing query strategies that maximize information per human comparison is crucial.
