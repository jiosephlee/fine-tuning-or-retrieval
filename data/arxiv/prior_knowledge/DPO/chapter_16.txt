Introduction

Practical alignment of large language models (LMs) to human preferences has become a central engineering task for deploying safe, useful systems. The canonical industrial approach is the three-stage pipeline—supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling (RM), and reinforcement learning (RL) optimization (often implemented as proximal policy optimization or PPO). This chapter synthesizes the end-to-end process used in production-style RLHF deployments and highlights key engineering trade-offs you will encounter: how to pick and reuse reference models, design data pipelines for sampling and logging, budget compute and optimize throughput, exploit caching and batching, monitor reward and divergence metrics, diagnose failure modes like reward hacking or collapse, and manage reproducibility through hyperparameter control.

We will present both conceptual intuition and practical details so that you can read research papers and understand engineering writeups. Wherever helpful, we include compact mathematical notation to formalize objectives and diagnostics. The chapter is organized around the three-stage pipeline and the cross-cutting engineering considerations that determine its feasibility and robustness.

Stage 1 — Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

Purpose and role. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the usual first stage: you take a pretrained base LM and continue training it on high-quality labeled data (human demonstrations, task-specific datasets, or curated outputs). The SFT stage shapes the initial policy $\pi_\theta$ toward reasonable behavior before any reward-based optimization. This reduces sample complexity for RL and anchors the model to a safe behavior prior, lowering the risk of catastrophic exploration.

Loss and objectives. SFT typically minimizes the cross-entropy loss on human demonstration data:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x).
\]
Here $x$ denotes context or prompt and $y$ the target completion. In practice you often add regularization such as weight decay, dropout, or label smoothing. When demonstrations are scarce, data augmentation (paraphrasing prompts, self-instruct) helps.

Design choices and trade-offs. Decide dataset composition (mix of tasks, tone, risk-averse behavior), sequence length cutoffs, and whether to freeze parts of the model. SFT can be performed at varying compute budgets: light SFT produces a useful starting point quickly, but deeper SFT more strongly biases the policy and may reduce room for later reward improvement. Use validation metrics focusing on factuality, toxicity, and instruction-following to choose checkpoints for the next stage.

Stage 2 — Reward Modeling (RM)

Purpose. The reward model approximates human preferences. It converts labels (pairwise comparisons, ratings, scalar feedback) into a scalar function $r_\phi(x,y)$ that predicts the desirability of a completion $y$ given a context $x$. The RM provides the training signal for the RL stage.

Data collection. Most practical pipelines collect pairwise preference data: humans are shown two or more model outputs for the same prompt and pick the preferred one. Let $(x,y_a,y_b)$ be a labeled comparison with label indicating whether $y_a\succ y_b$. The Bradley-Terry / Plackett-Luce formulation is common: model the probability that $y_a$ is preferred as
\[
P_\phi(y_a \succ y_b \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r_\phi(x,y_a))}{\exp(r_\phi(x,y_a)) + \exp(r_\phi(x,y_b))}.
\]
The RM is trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observed preferences.

Model choices and calibration. RM capacity should match the complexity of preferences to avoid underfitting or overfitting. Overparameterized RMs can memorize annotator biases and induce reward hacking; ensembling or early stopping can help. Calibrating the absolute scale of $r_\phi$ is important for RL: you often normalize rewards (zero mean, unit variance) or clip to reduce variance.

Evaluation. Hold out a labeled comparison set and report accuracy (fraction of correctly predicted pairwise preferences) and calibrated metrics like Spearman correlation to ordinal ratings if available. Also measure robustness across prompt types and adversarial inputs.

Stage 3 — RL Optimization

Objective formulation. With a reward model in hand, you treat the LM as a policy $\pi_\theta$ and aim to maximize expected reward while keeping the policy close to a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to prevent drift:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\left[ r_\phi(x,y) - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right],
\]
where $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. In practice, PPO variants implement this with clipped objectives and advantage estimates derived from $r_\phi$.

Choice of reference model. The reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ can be the SFT checkpoint, the pretrained base model, or an EMA (exponential moving average) of the policy during RL. Using SFT as reference keeps outputs aligned to human demonstrations. An EMA reference can stabilize training by adapting slowly with the policy, but it weakens the fixed prior effect. Practical rule: pick the most conservative reasonable reference (usually SFT checkpoint), and treat $\beta$ as a tunable knob balancing improvement vs. divergence.

PPO details. PPO optimizes a surrogate clipped objective. For trajectory $y=(t=1,\dots,T)$, compute per-token log-probabilities and cumulative returns. The policy update maximizes
\[
\mathbb{E}\left[ \min\left( \rho_t(\theta) A_t, \ \text{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)A_t \right) \right],
\]
where $\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$ and $A_t$ is an advantage estimator using the reward model output (possibly with bootstrapped baselines). The KL penalty is often enforced both as a Lagrangian term and as a trust region check to reject updates exceeding a KL cap.

Practicalities: rollout lengths, batch sizes, and learning rates. RLHF uses on-policy rollouts from the current policy; maximize the diversity of prompts $x$ and ensure sampling temperatures and top-k/top-p settings reflect deployment inference. Long sequences raise memory and variance in reward estimates, so many teams use truncated rollouts (reward at episode end or per-turn rewards aggregated). Learning rates in PPO are generally lower than SFT, and you often run multiple epochs per batch to make sample use efficient.

Reference Model Selection and Reuse

Why it matters. The reference model defines the prior behavior you penalize deviation from. If the reference itself has flaws (toxic tendencies, hallucinations), strong KL regularization will preserve those flaws. Conversely, too weak a prior invites degenerate reward hacking.

Options and trade-offs. Common choices are:

- Pretrained base model: low KL regularization to base keeps large-scale style but may ignore SFT constraints.
- SFT checkpoint: the usual choice—anchors the policy to human demonstrations.
- EMA of policy: adapts slowly but may allow gradual drift.
- Ensemble or mixture: combine models to control different aspects (e.g., one for style, one for factuality).

Reusing references. Reuse the same SFT reference across multiple RLHF runs for reproducibility and comparability. When iterating, you sometimes promote the best RL policy to be the new reference for subsequent fine-tuning, but do this cautiously to avoid compounding alignment errors.

Data Pipelines for Sampling and Logging

Sampling design. A reliable sampling pipeline generates rollouts for reward labeling and RL. It must support diverse prompt pools, temperature and nucleus-sampling policies, and controlled context lengths. Important to separate sampling for human annotation (which benefits from more diversity and lower temperature) and sampling for RL updates (often on-policy behavior).

Logging infrastructure. Log inputs, outputs, generation hyperparameters, model commits, and metadata. Structured logs should capture:
- prompt id, prompt text, and metadata (domain, difficulty)
- model checkpoint id and exact parameter hash
- decoding settings (temperature, top-k/top-p)
- token-level probabilities and per-token log-probs when feasible
- reward model score and other automatic metrics (perplexity, toxicity scores)
- human labels and annotator IDs

Why detailed logging matters. Diagnostics of degeneration or reward hacking require traceability: which prompts caused high reward but low quality, whether divergence begins at certain tokens, and which annotators disagree. Logs allow offline replay for debugging and reproducibility.

Annotation management. Use active sampling to prioritize prompts near decision boundaries or with known disagreement. Store pairwise comparisons and scalar ratings with provenance. Design annotation UIs that show full context and comparable completions to reduce variance.

Compute Budgeting and Throughput Optimization

The compute cost of RLHF is dominated by three components: model inference for sampling, human annotation, and gradient computation for RL updates. Budgeting requires balancing these.

Estimating costs. For an LM with per-token FLOP cost $C$, generating $L$ tokens for $N$ prompts costs roughly $C \times N \times L$ FLOPs. Training costs scale with model size and number of optimizer steps. Reward model training and annotation costs can also dominate, especially with high-quality human labeling.

Strategies to reduce cost per effective update. First, improve sample efficiency: generate more outputs per labeled prompt (e.g., compare multiple candidates), use active learning to label only informative examples, and leverage synthetic label augmentation. Second, reduce per-step training cost through mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and optimizer state sharding. Third, reduce inference overhead by using smaller sampling models for exploration and distillation techniques to transfer behavior to larger models later.

Throughput optimization in practice. Use asynchronous pipelines: decouple sampling from training with a buffer of rollouts, so generation and annotation can proceed in parallel with training. Batch generation across multiple prompts to amortize per-batch overhead. Use hardware-aware optimizations: model sharding across accelerators, compiled kernels (XLA, Triton), and fused softmax/logit routines to squeeze latency.

Caching, Batching, and Sequence Length Management

Caching to save computation. Token-level caching of key/value pairs during autoregressive generation is standard: reuse previous attention keys and values to avoid recomputation. However, caching becomes complex when batching sequences of different lengths or when editing previously generated text. Engineering must manage memory vs. compute trade-offs: keep caches on-device to reduce host-device transfer but monitor memory pressure.

Batching. Batch similar-length sequences together to reduce padding overhead. For RLHF, rollouts vary in length; group them by length or pad only in micro-batches on accelerators. Use per-device micro-batching and gradient accumulation to simulate larger batch sizes without increasing memory.

Sequence length trade-offs. Longer sequences increase context and can improve coherence, but they increase computational cost quadratically for self-attention ($O(L^2)$) and raise variance in reward attribution. Practical strategies include context window cropping, truncated backpropagation through time, and hierarchical reward estimation (per-turn vs. per-sequence rewards). For long documents, consider chunking and modeling inter-chunk coherence through secondary models.

Monitoring Reward, KL, and Quality Metrics

Key metrics to monitor. Regularly track: (1) average reward model score on rollouts, (2) KL divergence between policy and reference $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$, (3) task-specific quality metrics (accuracy, factuality, toxicity), (4) human preference win rate against baselines. Plot these over training steps and across prompt subsets.

Interpreting KL. KL rising indicates policy drift; a rapidly rising KL with marginal reward gains suggests the model is exploiting reward model artifacts. KL alone is insufficient—pair it with direct quality metrics to detect reward hacking.

Calibration of reward. Since RM outputs are learned, they may drift. Monitor calibration by periodically collecting new human comparisons against current rollouts and computing RM accuracy. If RM accuracy degrades, freeze RL and retrain RM with additional labels.

Automated checks and alarms. Implement automated tests: sanity checks for output length and token distributions, toxicity filters, and adversarial prompt suites. Establish thresholds that trigger human review or rollbacks (e.g., sudden spike in toxic token rate or sharp drop in human win-rate).

Failure Modes: Degeneration, Overfitting, Collapse

Degeneration and reward hacking. Degeneration occurs when the policy maximizes proxy reward by producing superficially high-scoring outputs that are low quality. Common patterns include verbosity bloat (reward model favors longer answers), repetition, or formulaic templates. To mitigate, use diverse annotated negatives, penalize length explicitly if needed, and inspect high-reward low-quality samples.

Overfitting to the reward model. The policy can overfit idiosyncrasies of the RM if the RM has blind spots. Remedies include enlarging the RM training set, regularizing the RM, using ensembles of RMs, or combining human-in-the-loop evaluations to identify failure modes.

Collapse to short or trivial outputs. Under strong KL or high reward variance, the policy may collapse to short safe replies. Balance KL and reward strength and include quality constraints in the reward signal.

Mode collapse and sample impoverishment. Repeated PPO updates can reduce diversity. Encourage entropy in the policy during updates, or maintain experience replay of earlier diverse rollouts. Consider explicit diversity bonuses or temperature schedules.

Reproducibility and Hyperparameter Management

Why reproducibility matters. RLHF involves many moving parts and stochastic choices—seed, sampling temperature, annotator variability, RM checkpoint, and optimizer configuration. Without rigorous tracking, reproducing results or diagnosing regressions is hard.

Versioning and metadata. Store all relevant versioned artifacts: datasets with hashes, model checkpoints, code commits, random seeds, training hyperparameters, environment variables, and hardware topology. Use immutable manifests that accompany evaluation results.

Hyperparameter tracking. Track hyperparameters for SFT, RM, and RL (learning rates, batch sizes, clipping $\epsilon$, KL coefficient $\beta$, rollout length, number of epochs). Use experiment tracking systems to log metrics and artifacts. Favor grid or Bayesian sweeps for critical knobs like $\beta$ and PPO clipping; small changes can have large behavioral effects.

Deterministic vs. stochastic runs. Some non-determinism is unavoidable on GPUs and TPUs; minimize it by fixing seeds, disabling nondeterministic kernels when necessary, and documenting acceptable variance. When possible, run multiple seeds for key experiments to quantify sensitivity.

Checkpointing and rollback. Regular checkpointing allows you to revert to a safe policy if monitoring identifies degeneration. Keep both model and optimizer state checkpoints and ensure you can reproduce sampling outputs from a checkpoint by logging inference settings.

Conclusion

Building a practical RLHF system is as much an engineering effort as a research one. The three-stage pipeline—SFT, reward modeling, and RL optimization—provides a modular structure that helps manage complexity, but success depends on careful choices of reference models, robust data pipelines, efficient compute usage, and rich monitoring. Caching, batching, and sequence length management directly affect throughput and cost, while monitoring reward, KL, and human-facing quality metrics guard against degeneration and reward hacking. Finally, reproducibility and disciplined hyperparameter management are essential for reliable iteration.

Think of RLHF engineering as an exercise in principled constraints: use SFT to inject safe priors, keep the policy tethered to a reference during RL, make the reward model robust and well-calibrated, and instrument every stage so that degradation can be detected and addressed quickly. With these components in place, teams can iterate rapidly and responsibly toward aligned LM behavior.
