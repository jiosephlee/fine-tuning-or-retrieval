# Probability ratios and log-odds in objectives

Many fine-tuning objectives depend on probability ratios between the tuned model and the reference. The log probability ratio for sequence $y$ is
\[
\log r(y\mid x) = \log\frac{p_\theta(y\mid x)}{q(y\mid x)} = \sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)}{q(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)}.
\]
These ratios appear explicitly in policy-gradient and actor-critic algorithms (e.g., PPO), in importance sampling estimators, and in reward-weighted regression schemes. For example, in a KL-regularized objective of the form
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}\left[\text{reward}(y) - \beta\log\frac{p_\theta(y)}{q(y)}\right],
\]
the log-ratio term penalizes deviation. In PPO-style objectives, clipped probability ratios like
\[
\operatorname{clip}\Big(\frac{p_\theta(y)}{p_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)},\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\Big)
\]
are used to limit step sizes relative to the previous policy. For LMs, the ratio is computed from accumulated per-token probabilities.

Working in log space (log-odds) is numerically preferred because probabilities can be extremely small. The log-odds are additive over tokens and are amenable to gradient computation via backpropagation through the logits. Many implementations compute the per-token logit differences $\Delta z_t = z^\theta_t - z^q_t$ and then compute per-token log ratios via softmax operations or via direct log-softmax outputs. For importance sampling, normalized weights are computed from log-weights using the log-sum-exp trick to avoid overflow:
\[
w_i \propto \exp(\log r(y^{(i)}) + \text{reward}(y^{(i)})).
\]

Be aware that probability ratios can have high variance, especially when the tuned model assigns much higher probability to certain sequences than the reference. Clipping ratios, using trust-region constraints, or tempering importance weights are common strategies to control variance. When performing off-policy estimation with importance sampling, even moderate KL divergence can lead to extremely large importance weights due to exponential effects across sequences; this again motivates per-token or per-step controls.


# Length penalties and normalization

Because sequence-level quantities like log-probabilities and KL scale with sequence length (they are sums over tokens), length effects must be handled deliberately. If you compare two models using raw sequence log-probabilities or sequence KL, longer sequences naturally accumulate larger magnitudes, which can distort objectives and constraints.

There are two common normalizations: per-token averages and length-penalty functions. Per-token average normalizes sums by sequence length:
\[
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t},x)
\]
or for KL,
\[
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \operatorname{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\,\|\, q(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\big).
\]
This makes the metric length-invariant and yields interpretable per-token divergences. The per-token view aligns with the decomposition in the chain rule and is widely used when you want to enforce a consistent per-token constraint.

Length penalties are another way to adjust the objective to avoid favoring shorter or longer sequences. In beam search or decoding, heuristics penalize short sequences that have higher average log-probability but less content. In RLHF, one might add an explicit length penalty in the reward to discourage the tuned model from producing extremely short or long outputs. Formally, a length-penalized reward could be
\[
\text{reward}'(y) = \text{reward}(y) - \gamma\, f(T),
\]
where $f(T)$ could be linear in length, logarithmic, or something task-specific. When balancing KL, remember that sequence KL accumulates, so a model that increases average per-token KL by a small amount will exceed sequence-level KL budgets if sequence lengths are large.

Another practical consideration: many datasets and prompts yield variable-length outputs. If you set a KL constraint in terms of average sequence KL, you must account for the distribution of sequence lengths. If most sequences are long, small per-token KLs might still produce large sequence KLs; conversely, per-token constraints might be too strict if your distribution includes many short sequences.


# Trade-offs between reward and KL

The core trade-off in KL-regularized fine-tuning is between maximizing expected reward and minimizing divergence from the reference. This trade-off can be formalized as a constrained optimization:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[\text{reward}(y)] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \operatorname{KL}(p_\theta\|q) \le C,
\]
or equivalently as an unconstrained Lagrangian:
\[
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[\text{reward}(y) - \beta \log \tfrac{p_\theta(y)}{q(y)}].
\]
Intuitively, increasing $\beta$ favors staying close to $q$, decreasing variance and preserving safety properties learned by the reference, but constrains how much reward you can capture if higher reward modes are far from $q$.

The shape of the reward landscape relative to the reference matters a lot. If high-reward sequences lie in regions where $q$ already has some mass, mild KL penalties suffice. If high reward requires shifting probability mass to regions where $q$ assigns near-zero probability, the KL penalty will strongly oppose such moves. There is no free lunch: you cannot obtain arbitrarily large expected reward while keeping KL vanishingly small.

A useful way to reason about this trade-off is via the optimal solution to the Lagrangian when reward is viewed as a utility function. Under assumptions of finite action spaces and tabular settings, the solution takes the form
\[
p^\star(y\mid x) \propto q(y\mid x)\,\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,\text{reward}(y)\big),
\]
which is a Boltzmann posterior combining respect for the reference with reward-seeking behavior. This form shows that $\beta$ acts as an inverse temperature: small $\beta$ makes the exponential sharper and pushes $p$ toward high-reward modes (possibly far from $q$), while large $\beta$ keeps $p$ close to $q$. In practice, models cannot represent that distribution exactly but training procedures often try to approximate it.

Empirical tuning strategies include sweeping $\beta$ or target KL $C$, evaluating reward vs. KL curves (Pareto frontier), and choosing operating points that satisfy safety and performance requirements. Adaptive methods like dual ascent on $\lambda$ to reach a target KL automatically adjust to reward magnitudes, which is helpful when reward scales are unknown or change during training.


# Measuring distributional drift from reference

Measuring how much a tuned model has drifted from its reference involves selecting suitable divergence measures and reliable estimators. Common choices include KL divergence $\operatorname{KL}(p\|q)$, reverse KL $\operatorname{KL}(q\|p)$, Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence, total variation (TV) distance, and practical proxies like perplexity increase or classification-based detectors.

KL and JS have desirable information-theoretic interpretations and connect directly to objectives. However, estimating sequence-level KL requires samples from $p_\theta$ and computing log-probabilities under $q$. Monte Carlo estimation of
\[
\widehat{\operatorname{KL}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log\frac{p_\theta(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{q(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}
\]
is unbiased for the expectation of the log-ratio under $p_\theta$, but high variance can occur when $p_\theta$ places mass on sequences rare under $q$. Estimating $\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_\theta)$ is harder because it requires sampling from $q$ and computing $\log p_\theta(y)$ for sequences where $p_\theta$ may assign nearly zero probability, which can lead to negative infinities or extreme values.

Practical diagnostics often rely on per-token averaged KL, which is more stable and interpretable. Perplexity relative to the reference is another useful metric:
\[
\text{perplexity}(p_\theta\mid q) = \exp\big(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[-\log q(y)]\big),
\]
which measures how surprised $q$ is about samples from $p_\theta$. Monitoring changes in perplexity on held-out prompts or on generated outputs provides an intuitive sense of drift.

Classifier-based drift detection constructs a binary classifier trying to distinguish samples from $p_\theta$ and $q$; the classifier’s AUC or cross-entropy gives an estimate of divergence (via variational representations of divergences). This method is robust when direct KL estimation is noisy, and it can highlight qualitative differences useful for safety audits.

Finally, distributional drift should be evaluated across relevant prompt subpopulations. A small average KL can mask catastrophic drift on a subset of prompts. Thus report percentiles (median, 90th, 99th) of per-sequence KL and per-token KL, and inspect tail behavior. Use aggregate and per-prompt analyses to ensure constraints are satisfied across important slices of the input space.

Conclusion

Controlling divergence during LM fine-tuning is crucial to preserve useful behavior from a reference model while achieving desired improvements. Sequence-level and token-level KLs are mathematically connected via the autoregressive factorization, and per-token log-probabilities are the computational primitives used to accumulate divergences and ratios. Choosing KL targets and penalty coefficients requires careful calibration and often benefits from adaptive strategies. Temperature scaling affects both sampling behavior and calibration, which in turn influences how divergence regularization behaves. Probability ratios and log-odds are central to objectives and must be handled in log-space for numerical stability and variance control. Length effects are important and call for normalization or explicit penalties. Finally, measure drift with multiple metrics and across prompt slices to get a complete picture of how much the model has changed. Together these tools let researchers and practitioners manage trade-offs between reward and fidelity to a reference model in principled ways.
