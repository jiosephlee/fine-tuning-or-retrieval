Introduction

Evaluating aligned text generation—summarization, dialogue, or other open-ended outputs optimized to match human preferences—requires a careful mix of human judgment, automatic measures, and experimental design. Unlike traditional NLP tasks with clear gold labels, alignment projects aim to optimize subjective properties such as helpfulness, harmlessness, factuality, and adherence to stylistic constraints. This makes evaluation both more important and more difficult: the evaluation must faithfully reflect human values, be robust to distribution shifts, and provide reliable, statistically interpretable comparisons between models and system configurations.

This chapter provides a comprehensive view of evaluation methodologies used in aligned text generation. We begin with human evaluation protocols and win-rate measurement methods that are central to comparing models. We then consider inter-annotator agreement and how to express confidence in judgments. We examine why common automatic metrics like ROUGE are insufficient for alignment tasks and survey alternatives. We present the growing practice of using large language models as judges, covering prompt design, reliability checks, and limitations. Task-specific rubrics—helpfulness, harmlessness, style—are explored in depth so that practitioners can design clear, operationalized criteria. We discuss how sampling temperature and other generation parameters affect evaluation and how to control for them. We explain design and analysis of A/B tests with an emphasis on statistical significance, power, and multiplicity correction. Finally, we treat out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation strategies that probe robustness under realistic shifts. Each section combines intuition, formal definitions where useful, and practical recommendations.

Human evaluation protocols and win-rate measurement

Human evaluation remains the gold standard for assessing alignment because it directly captures human preferences and safety judgments. There are several widely used human-eval protocols: absolute rating, pairwise comparison, and win-rate reporting for online A/B tests.

Absolute rating asks raters to score a single model output along predefined scales (e.g., 1–5 for helpfulness). Absolute scores are easy to aggregate but suffer from calibration differences across raters and from scale compression. Pairwise comparison presents two outputs for the same prompt and asks which is better (or whether they're tied). Pairwise comparisons reduce scale calibration issues and are often more sensitive to small differences.

Win-rate measurement is commonly reported as the probability that a model A's output is preferred to a baseline model B's output on a random prompt. If for \(N\) sampled prompts we have outcomes \(w_i\) where \(w_i = 1\) if A wins, \(w_i = 0\) if B wins, and \(w_i = 0.5\) if tie (if ties are allowed), then the empirical win-rate is
\[
\hat{p} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N w_i.
\]
For binary (no-tie) comparisons, the win-rate corresponds to the sample mean of Bernoulli outcomes. Confidence intervals (discussed below) can be constructed by normal approximation or nonparametric bootstrap. In interactive or online settings with many users, an A/B test typically reports win-rate (or a derived metric such as variance-weighted wins) together with statistical tests to declare significance.

Designing human evaluation protocols also requires careful prompt sampling. Prompts should be sampled from the target distribution of use cases, including rare and adversarial inputs relevant to safety. Stratified sampling across categories (e.g., instruction types, toxicity-prone prompts, factual queries) enables per-stratum analysis and better diagnosis. When measuring win-rate across a mixture of strata, report both aggregated win-rate and per-stratum win-rates to reveal where improvements hold or fail.

Inter-annotator agreement and confidence

When judgments are subjective, measuring agreement across annotators is essential. Simple percentage agreement is intuitive but misleading because it conflates agreement by chance. Common chance-corrected coefficients include Cohen's kappa for two raters and Fleiss' kappa for multiple raters. Cohen's kappa is defined for categorical labels as
\[
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},
\]
where \(p_o\) is the observed proportion of agreement and \(p_e\) is the expected proportion under chance agreement (computed from the marginal label distributions).

However, kappa and its variants have known limitations: they are sensitive to prevalence (class imbalance) and can be low even when absolute agreement is high in skewed tasks. For ordinal or continuous ratings, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is often more appropriate; it models the proportion of variance attributable to between-item vs. within-item differences.

A complementary approach is to compute item-level confidence by modeling annotator reliability. One simple method is to bootstrap: resample annotators (or ratings for each item) and compute the variance of aggregated scores. Another is to use probabilistic models like Dawid–Skene, which estimate both true labels and annotator accuracies when labels are categorical. For pairwise preferences, the Bradley–Terry model treats each item (or model output) as having a latent quality score; these scores and their uncertainty can be estimated via maximum likelihood and yield standard errors for win-rate differences.

When reporting results, always accompany point estimates with uncertainty quantification: 95% confidence intervals for win-rates, agreement coefficients with bootstrap confidence intervals, and p-values or Bayes factors for comparisons. Additionally, report the number of annotators per item and the per-annotator variance to make the reliability of aggregated judgments transparent.

Limitations of automatic metrics (e.g., ROUGE) for alignment

Automatic metrics were developed for reference-based tasks like machine translation and summarization, but many fail to reflect human preferences in alignment settings.

ROUGE, BLEU, and similar n-gram overlap metrics measure lexical overlap with one or more reference texts. They are useful when references capture the desired content and phrasing, but in alignment tasks “correct” outputs are diverse; two outputs can both be high-quality yet share little n-gram overlap. This leads to underestimation of true quality. Moreover, these metrics poorly capture factuality, coherence, or safety. For instance, a fluent but hallucinated summary can attain a high ROUGE score.

Embedding-based metrics such as BERTScore or cosine similarity between sentence embeddings partially address lexical variability by measuring semantic similarity. BERTScore computes token-level similarity with contextual embeddings and aggregates them. While better than ROUGE for semantic similarity, embedding metrics can be insensitive to critical differences, such as hallucinated facts or subtle toxic content, because embeddings focus on overall semantic proximity.

Other automatic directions include model-based factuality checks (QA-based entailment or question-answering consistency), entity-based fact-checkers, and specialized classifiers for safety attributes (toxicity, hate speech, privacy leakage). These can be useful as targeted checks but are only as reliable as their training data and tend to miss novel failure modes.

In short, automatic metrics are useful for fast iteration and filtering, but they cannot replace human evaluation when the goal is alignment with nuanced human preferences. They should be used in combination with human judgment and be clearly framed as proxies rather than ground truth.

LLM-as-judge: prompts, reliability, and caveats

Using large language models as automatic judges of text quality is an increasingly common practice—especially when human labels are expensive. The LLM-as-judge approach poses a model with a prompt that describes evaluation criteria and asks it to compare or score outputs. This can scale evaluations quickly, but it introduces new challenges.

Prompt design is crucial. Effective judge prompts provide clear rubrics, examples, and calibration instructions. For pairwise judgments, prompts typically include the task instructions, the reference prompt, output A, output B, and a rubric that defines scoring categories or a preference choice. Chain-of-thought-style instructions can sometimes improve reliability, but revealing internal reasoning can induce additional noise or unpredictable behavior.

Reliability must be validated. Do LLM judgments correlate with human judgments? Compute correlation metrics (Spearman, Kendall) and confusion matrices between LLM and human labels across a representative dataset. Train/validate on held-out items; avoid evaluating judge performance only on items that influenced its development or prompt tuning. Consider ensemble judgment (averaging over multiple prompts or model seeds) to reduce variance, and use temperature control to stabilize outputs.

Caveats include model bias and overfitting to superficial cues. An LLM judge might pick up on stylistic or lexical markers that correlate with human preference in the training data but are not intrinsically better. Judges may be overconfident or fail under adversarial inputs crafted to exploit the judge's priors. Importantly, if the judged models share training data with the judge, there is a risk of contamination: the judge may prefer outputs similar to its own training distribution, inflating agreement with models from the same family.

Best practices: validate an LLM judge against human labels on a diverse dataset; document correlations and failure modes; use multiple judges (different models, different prompts) and report consensus statistics; and when substituting for human evaluation, reserve a subset of human-labeled examples for ongoing calibration.

Task-specific rubrics (helpfulness, harmlessness, style)

Operationalizing subjective goals into rubrics is the central step for reliable human and LLM-based evaluation. A good rubric is specific, examples-driven, and actionable.

Helpfulness rubrics assess whether the response addresses the user’s intent, provides correct and sufficient information, and is presented clearly. Key dimensions include relevance, completeness, correctness, and clarity. For factual tasks, include sub-checks for verifying claims or citing sources. The rubric should define what counts as a minor omission versus a critical factual error.

Harmlessness rubrics define allowed versus disallowed content. These must map policy-level concepts (e.g., encouraging self-harm, facilitating wrongdoing) to concrete annotation rules. For example, the rubric should say whether mentioning how to procure items is allowed in neutral academic contexts, whether transformation of user-supplied illicit content is permitted, and how to handle ambiguous intent. Include gradations: safe, unsafe but non-actionable, and explicitly disallowed. Give annotators examples and edge cases.

Style rubrics capture tone, verbosity, and persona. For customer-support tasks, style might require concise, polite, and solution-oriented answers under a specified word limit. For creative tasks, style can allow divergent choices but require fidelity to an author's voice. Style judgments often interact with helpfulness: a technically correct answer that violates style constraints may be unacceptable for product deployment.

For each rubric, accompany scalar anchors with illustrative examples. When possible, decompose complex labels into multiple scalar subdimensions; this aids diagnostic analysis and increases inter-annotator reliability. Train annotators with practice items and feedback loops.

Sampling temperature effects on evaluation

Sampling temperature \(T\) controls the randomness of generation in autoregressive LLMs. Lower \(T\) concentrates probability mass on high-likelihood tokens, producing conservative, repetitive, and often more "safe" outputs. Higher \(T\) increases diversity, which can raise helpfulness in open-ended creative tasks but also increases hallucination and violation risk.

Evaluation must fix or systematically vary \(T\) to disentangle model capability from sampling stochasticity. When comparing models, ensure generation settings are matched (same \(T\), same decoding method—sampling vs. nucleus/top-\(k\) vs. greedy or beam search). For pairwise comparisons, generate both outputs under identical sampling seeds and protocols; otherwise differences may reflect randomness, not model quality.

When measuring robustness to stochasticity, report performance across a range of temperatures and descriptions of decoding hyperparameters. For safety-sensitive evaluations, conservative settings (low \(T\), greedy decoding) are recommended to estimate worst-case compliance; for creative tasks, evaluate at several \(T\) values and consider reporting expected utility under the intended sampling policy.

A/B testing and statistical significance

A/B testing in aligned generation measures user-level outcomes or preference rates between two variants. Proper experimental design prevents false discoveries and ensures reproducibility.

Randomization should be at the appropriate unit: user-level randomization prevents contamination by multiple prompts from the same user and reduces dependence. If randomization occurs at the query or session level, account for correlations when computing variances.

Statistical significance tests depend on the outcome's distribution. For binary outcomes (win/loss), compare proportions with a two-proportion z-test or a Fisher exact test when sample sizes are small. For continuous or ordinal scores, use t-tests or nonparametric alternatives like the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data. However, many evaluation outcomes violate test assumptions (non-normality, heteroskedasticity); bootstrap methods provide robust confidence intervals without parametric assumptions.

Power analysis is essential before launching experiments. Given an expected effect size \(d\), desired significance level \(\alpha\), and power \(1-\beta\), compute the required sample size. For binary outcomes, sample size for detecting a difference \(\Delta = p_A - p_B\) with pooled standard error approximation is approximately
\[
N \approx \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_A(1-p_A) + p_B(1-p_B)})^2}{\Delta^2},
\]
where \(\hat{p}\) is the pooled estimate. In practice, simulation using estimated variances gives better estimates for complex metrics.

Correct for multiple comparisons when running many A/B tests or examining multiple metrics (Bonferroni, Holm-Bonferroni, or false discovery rate control). Pre-register primary endpoints to avoid p-hacking. Finally, report effect sizes with confidence intervals—the magnitude of improvement is often more important than binary significance.

Out-of-distribution evaluation

Aligned systems can fail spectacularly on inputs far from their training or development distributions. OOD evaluation probes robustness and reveals brittle failure modes.

Constructing OOD tests can be systematic or adversarial. Systematic approaches include distributional shifts along axes of interest: topic shift, language/register shift, input length, or rare entities. For example, build subsets of prompts about specialized medical or legal topics to test factuality. Adversarial approaches intentionally craft prompts that elicit unsafe or toxic outputs—these can be generated manually, by red-teamers, or via automated adversarial generation methods.

A key principle is to treat OOD evaluation as part of the target distribution for safety: if a user might present an OOD prompt in production, the evaluation should include such prompts. When reporting OOD results, specify how the OOD set was constructed, whether it was adversarial, and how representative it is of anticipated real-world inputs. Use robust metrics: measure the rate of severe failures (e.g., explicit disallowed content), not just average scores, because tail events matter more for harm.

OOD evaluation also includes distributional generalization tests: does a model degrade gracefully as input complexity increases? Techniques like stress-testing with incremental perturbations (paraphrases, obfuscations, or multi-step reasoning chains) can identify thresholds where performance collapses. Finally, combine OOD testing with monitoring: in deployment, use uncertainty estimates or detection models to flag inputs likely to be OOD and route them to safer fallbacks.

Conclusion

Evaluating aligned text generation requires integrating human-centered protocols, carefully validated automatic proxies, principled statistical methods, and adversarial robustness testing. Human evaluation—particularly pairwise comparisons and win-rate reporting—remains central, but it must be accompanied by rigorous inter-annotator agreement analysis and uncertainty quantification. Automatic metrics like ROUGE have limited value for alignment; embedding metrics, factuality checks, and safety classifiers are useful complements but not replacements. Using LLMs as judges can scale evaluations but requires explicit validation against human judgments and awareness of biases. Task-specific rubrics translate high-level goals into operational checks for helpfulness, harmlessness, and style, and these rubrics should be example-rich and decomposed where possible. Sampling temperature and decoding choices materially affect outputs and must be controlled in comparisons. A/B testing demands careful randomization, power analysis, and correction for multiple comparisons. Finally, OOD evaluation and adversarial testing are indispensable for understanding tail risks.

By combining these tools and documenting datasets, prompts, rubrics, and statistical analyses transparently, researchers and practitioners can produce evaluations that are informative, reproducible, and aligned with human values.
