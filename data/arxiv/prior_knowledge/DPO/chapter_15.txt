# Introduction

Evaluating generated text is a central challenge in natural language generation (NLG). As models grow more capable, researchers and practitioners need reliable ways to measure quality, compare systems, and determine whether improvements are real or just noise. This chapter surveys the major tools and protocols used to evaluate generated text. We begin with widely used automatic metrics (ROUGE, BLEU, METEOR), explaining how they work, what they measure, and their limitations. We then turn to human evaluation design, describing common setups (pairwise comparisons, Likert scales), how to craft instructions, and how to measure and interpret inter-annotator agreement. The chapter discusses the emerging practice of using large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑4 as automated evaluators, including issues of prompting and calibration. We cover win‑rate and tournament-style comparisons (Elo, TrueSkill, win-rate graphs), and the statistical tools needed to assess significance, sampling variance, and necessary sample sizes. Finally, we consider practical issues for running evaluations via crowdsourcing or with expert annotators: quality control, bias mitigation, cost trade-offs, and ethical concerns. The goal is to equip you to design and interpret robust evaluations so that reported improvements reflect meaningful gains in text quality.

# Automatic metrics: ROUGE, BLEU, METEOR — strengths and weaknesses

Automatic metrics offer quick, inexpensive, and reproducible ways to compare generated text against references. The most common metrics in NLG are BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR. Each captures different aspects of n-gram overlap, precision and recall, and alignment between candidate and reference outputs. Understanding their formulas and failure modes is crucial to interpreting evaluation results.

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) was developed for machine translation and is based on modified n-gram precision and a brevity penalty. For a candidate sentence and a set of reference sentences, BLEU computes the clipped precision for n-grams up to order $N$ and then aggregates via a geometric mean. Let $p_n$ be the modified precision for n-grams of order $n$. BLEU score with weights $w_n$ typically equal to $1/N$ is
\[
\text{BLEU} = \text{BP} \cdot \exp\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^N w_n \log p_n\right).
\]
The brevity penalty $\text{BP}$ prevents tiny hypotheses from receiving a high score solely by matching short n-grams. If $c$ is the candidate length and $r$ is the effective reference length,
\[
\text{BP} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } c > r,\\
\exp(1 - r/c) & \text{if } c \le r.
\end{cases}
\]
BLEU emphasizes precision — it rewards n-grams in the candidate that match the reference — and the geometric mean penalizes low counts at any n-gram order. Strengths of BLEU include simplicity, interpretability in terms of n-gram overlap, and widespread adoption enabling comparisons across papers. Its weaknesses are important: BLEU correlates poorly with human judgments at the sentence level, is sensitive to tokenization and reference set size, and ignores semantic equivalence when surface forms differ. For tasks with many valid outputs (e.g., dialogue, creative writing), BLEU often underestimates quality.

ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) was designed for summarization and emphasizes recall over precision. The most commonly used variant, ROUGE‑N, computes recall of n-grams between a candidate summary and a reference summary:
\[
\text{ROUGE-}N = \frac{\sum_{\text{n-grams}\in \text{Ref}} \text{Count}_{\text{match}}(\text{n-gram})}{\sum_{\text{n-grams}\in \text{Ref}} \text{Count}(\text{n-gram})}.
\]
There are other ROUGE variants such as ROUGE‑L (longest common subsequence) and ROUGE‑S (skip-bigram). ROUGE's recall focus makes it suitable when the goal is to cover reference content (e.g., extractive summarization). Its strengths include sensitivity to content coverage and widespread use in summarization benchmarks. However, ROUGE can over-reward verbatim overlap and penalize good abstractive summaries that use different wording or structure. Like BLEU, ROUGE struggles when multiple valid outputs exist.

METEOR aims to bridge precision and recall and to account for synonyms and paraphrases. METEOR aligns unigrams between candidate and reference using exact matches, stem matches, synonym matches (from resources like WordNet), and paraphrase tables. It computes precision $P$ and recall $R$ of matched unigrams, then combines them via a harmonic mean with a tunable parameter that weights recall more heavily:
\[
F_\text{mean} = \frac{P \cdot R}{\alpha P + (1-\alpha) R}
\]
for some $\alpha \in [0,1]$. METEOR also includes a fragmentation penalty $p$ based on the number of chunks in the alignment to penalize disordered matches, yielding a final score
\[
\text{METEOR} = (1 - p) \cdot F_\text{mean}.
\]
METEOR typically correlates better with human judgments than BLEU at sentence-level because it accounts for synonyms and word order to some extent. But METEOR relies on external lexical resources and manually tuned parameters and can still miss deeper semantic equivalence or pragmatic appropriateness.

Across these metrics, common strengths are speed, reproducibility, and ease of use. They are invaluable in development (fast iteration) and for large-scale benchmarks where human evaluation is impractical. Their weaknesses are systematic: they chiefly capture surface-form overlap and often miss semantic correctness, factuality, coherence, style, and pragmatic suitability. For example, a generated summary may paraphrase the reference perfectly but get a low BLEU/ROUGE because of different wording; conversely, a text that copies parts of the reference verbatim could score high while omitting crucial content. Therefore, automatic metrics should be interpreted cautiously and usually supplemented by human evaluation, especially for tasks where multiple outputs are acceptable or where meaning, coherence, and factuality matter.

# Human evaluation design: pairwise comparison, Likert scales, inter‑annotator agreement

Human evaluation is the gold standard for assessing text quality because humans can judge semantics, coherence, style, and usefulness in ways automatic metrics cannot. Designing human evaluations requires careful attention to the task, instructions, response formats, and how to measure agreement and reliability.

Two common response formats are pairwise comparisons and Likert scales. In pairwise comparisons, annotators see two candidate outputs (often anonymized and presented in random order) for the same input and are asked which of the two is better, or whether they are tied. Pairwise tests align well with ranking-oriented goals and often reduce annotator cognitive load: it is easier to decide which of two is better than to map a single text to an absolute score. Pairwise data can be aggregated into win rates and used with ranking models (Elo, TrueSkill) to produce global system rankings. However, pairwise setups require more annotation per system pair if one wants comparisons between many systems, and they give less information about absolute quality.

Likert scales ask annotators to rate a single candidate along one or more dimensions (e.g., fluency, coherence, factuality, relevance) using a fixed scale such as 1–5 or 1–7. Likert ratings provide absolute judgments that can be compared across systems and allow multi-dimensional assessment. Care is required in scale design: label anchors should be explicit (e.g., 1 = "completely unintelligible", 5 = "perfectly fluent"), and scales should ideally be symmetric and not too fine-grained for reliable use. Likert scales can introduce interpersonal differences (different annotators use the scale differently), which complicates aggregation. One remedy is to treat ratings as ordinal and use nonparametric statistics, or to normalize annotator responses (z-scoring) before aggregation.

Inter-annotator agreement (IAA) quantifies consistency among annotators and is essential for validating that the annotation task is well-specified. Several agreement coefficients are commonly used depending on the task and data:

Cohen's kappa measures agreement between two annotators correcting for chance agreement. If $p_o$ is the observed proportion of agreement and $p_e$ is the expected agreement by chance (computed from annotator marginal distributions), Cohen's kappa is
\[
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}.
\]
Kappa ranges from $-1$ to $1$, where $1$ indicates perfect agreement and $0$ indicates agreement expected by chance. Kappa can be extended to weighted versions (for ordinal scales) where disagreements are penalized according to their magnitude.

Fleiss' kappa generalizes Cohen's kappa to more than two annotators. It computes agreement relative to chance across multiple annotators and items. Krippendorff's alpha is an even more general measure that supports arbitrary numbers of annotators, missing data, and different measurement levels (nominal, ordinal, interval). Krippendorff's alpha is defined in terms of observed disagreement $D_o$ and expected disagreement $D_e$:
\[
\alpha = 1 - \frac{D_o}{D_e}.
\]
For interval data, the disagreement function is typically the squared difference; for nominal data, it is an indicator of inequality. Krippendorff's alpha is robust and widely recommended when annotator counts vary or when you have missing annotations.

Interpreting agreement coefficients requires context: there are no absolute cutoffs that universally indicate "good" agreement. For example, in subjective tasks like creativity or interestingness, lower kappas are expected than in objective tasks like named-entity annotation. Additionally, kappa can be affected by skew in labels: when most items are in one category, $p_e$ becomes large and kappa can be low even with high observed agreement. Therefore, report both raw agreement and a chance-corrected measure, and discuss likely causes of disagreement.

Beyond numeric agreement, you should pilot the evaluation to refine instructions, provide examples and edge cases, and ensure annotators understand the task. Use qualification tests, gold-standard checks, and iterative feedback during pilots to increase clarity and reliability. For multi-dimensional evaluations, consider having each item rated by multiple annotators and combining ratings via majority vote, median, or weighted aggregation, and measure the effect of varying annotator counts on stability.

# Using LMs (e.g., GPT‑4) as automated evaluators: prompting and calibration

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as automatic evaluators because they can assess semantic and pragmatic aspects beyond n-gram overlap. Models like GPT‑4 can judge fluency, factuality, coherence, and even instruction-following. However, treating LMs as evaluators raises methodological questions about prompting, calibration, bias, and reliability.

Prompting is central. When using an LLM to score a candidate, you must design a prompt that elicits stable, interpretable outputs. A good prompt includes a clear task description, rating scale, and grading rubric with examples. For instance, to evaluate factuality, the prompt should define what "factual" means in the task context and provide examples of true, partially true, and false responses. Prompts that ask for structured outputs (e.g., JSON with fields "score" and "explanation") help downstream aggregation. Use few-shot examples carefully: include varied positive and negative examples and ensure they do not bias the model toward certain surface patterns.

Calibration is necessary because raw model outputs may not be well-calibrated probabilities or scores. For numeric scores, one can calibrate an LLM by mapping its outputs to a desired numeric scale using a calibration set with human annotations. Simple calibration methods include linear rescaling, isotonic regression, or ordinal calibration where the model is asked to choose among discrete labels and the distribution over labels is mapped to scores. Calibration also includes controlling generation randomness: set temperature to near zero or use deterministic decoding methods to reduce variability in evaluations. Additionally, run the same prompt multiple times to measure evaluator consistency.

LLM evaluators can be biased in ways that mirror their training data and objectives. They may favor outputs that match training distribution patterns, exhibit politeness bias, or be sensitive to surface cues like length or lexical overlap. They may also be influenced by annotation artifacts if the prompt examples are similar to system outputs. To mitigate bias, perform adversarial tests (e.g., swap synonyms, paraphrase correct answers) and compare LLM judgments against human annotations on a representative sample to estimate systematic differences.

Reliability and validity should be measured empirically. Compute correlation metrics (Spearman's rho, Kendall's tau for ordinal judgments; Pearson's $r$ for continuous scores) between LLM scores and human scores. Assess calibration via reliability diagrams or expected calibration error. Evaluate LLM consistency by repeating evaluations across seeds and prompt variants. Where LLMs disagree with humans, analyze explanations to see whether the model is detecting errors humans missed or making mistakes.

There are practical considerations: using LLMs is cheaper and faster than human evaluation at scale, but not free. API costs and compute constraints matter. Also be mindful of the potential for over-reliance on LLMs to the point that progress is tuned to what the LLM scores rather than to human preferences. A recommended workflow is to use LLM evaluation for large-scale screening and human evaluation for final reporting and calibration.

# Win‑rate metrics and tournament style comparisons

When comparing multiple systems, especially many variants of a model, pairwise comparisons aggregated into win-rate statistics provide an intuitive measure of relative quality. Tournament-style approaches, inspired by chess ranking systems, transform pairwise wins into global rankings and allow transitive inconsistencies to be smoothed.

Win rate is the fraction of pairwise comparisons in which system A is preferred over system B. If annotators choose among pairs $(A,B)$, the empirical win rate for A versus B is
\[
\hat{w}_{A,B} = \frac{\text{number of times A preferred}}{\text{number of comparisons between A and B}}.
\]
Win rates can be aggregated across opponents to compute an overall score, or used directly to report head-to-head superiority. One simple aggregate is the average pairwise win rate:
\[
\hat{w}_A = \frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{B \ne A} \hat{w}_{A,B}
\]
for $M$ systems. However, such averages treat every opponent equally; in practice, some opponents are more informative than others.

Elo and TrueSkill are rating systems that convert pairwise outcomes into scalar ratings that reflect expected win probabilities. Elo updates ratings based on the observed result versus expected result. If $R_A$ and $R_B$ are Elo ratings, the expected score for A is
\[
E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}.
\]
After a match, A's rating is updated by $K(S_A - E_A)$ where $S_A$ is the actual score (1 win, 0 loss, 0.5 draw) and $K$ is a learning rate. TrueSkill is a Bayesian extension that models each player's skill as a normal distribution and updates both mean and variance after matches, allowing explicit uncertainty estimates. TrueSkill is particularly useful when match coverage is sparse or when you want confidence intervals on ratings.

Tournament-style comparisons help when there are many systems but only a subset of pairwise comparisons are feasible. Randomized match scheduling and use of probabilistic rating updates allow global inference of system strengths. However, transitivity issues can arise: A may beat B, B may beat C, and C may beat A (rock-paper-scissors effect). Rating systems manage such cycles probabilistically but cannot fully eliminate them.

When reporting win rates and tournament results, always show uncertainty (confidence intervals) and the number of comparisons underlying each estimate. Be wary of ties and "no preference" responses: these should be accounted for (e.g., split ties evenly or model them as draws). Also consider stratifying win rates by input difficulty or other covariates (e.g., length, domain) to see whether system superiority holds across conditions.

# Statistical significance, sampling variance, and experiment design

Careful experiment design and appropriate statistical tests are required to determine whether observed differences between systems are likely to reflect real performance differences rather than sampling noise. Key concepts include hypothesis formulation, test selection, confidence intervals, and power analysis.

Start by defining the null and alternative hypotheses. For comparing two systems under a particular metric, the null hypothesis $H_0$ might be that their mean scores are equal. Choose an appropriate test based on data properties (paired vs unpaired, distributional assumptions, ordinal vs continuous).

For continuous, approximately normally distributed paired differences (e.g., differences in Likert scores for the same items), a paired t-test is often used. The paired t-test statistic is
\[
t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d / \sqrt{n}}
\]
where $\bar{d}$ is the mean of the $n$ paired differences and $s_d$ is their sample standard deviation. For non-normal or ordinal data (Likert), nonparametric tests like the Wilcoxon signed-rank test are preferable. For binary outcomes (win/loss in pairwise comparisons), McNemar's test or paired proportion tests are appropriate.

Permutation (randomization) tests provide distribution-free significance testing. Under a null hypothesis that there is no difference between systems, we can permute labels within paired items and compute the test statistic across permutations to obtain an empirical $p$-value. Bootstrap methods can estimate confidence intervals for almost any statistic: repeatedly resample with replacement and compute the statistic to get an empirical distribution, then take appropriate quantiles. Bootstrapping is especially useful for metrics with unknown analytic variance.

When comparing proportions (e.g., win rates), the standard error for a proportion $\hat{p}$ with sample size $n$ is
\[
\text{SE}(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}.
\]
For differences of proportions between two independent samples $\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$, the standard error is
\[
\text{SE}(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}.
\]
For paired proportions (common in paired comparative designs), account for the covariance by considering counts of discordant pairs.

Multiple comparisons are common when evaluating many models or many metrics. Apply corrections (Bonferroni, Holm-Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg for false discovery rate control) when conducting many hypothesis tests to control family-wise error rates or false discovery rates. Alternatively, use hierarchical testing or pre-register a small number of primary comparisons to reduce the multiplicity problem.

Power analysis helps determine the required sample size to detect an effect of interest with desired probability. For a paired t-test with desired power $1-\beta$ and significance level $\alpha$, the needed sample size depends on the expected effect size $\delta$ and the standard deviation of differences $\sigma_d$. The standardized effect size is $d = \delta/\sigma_d$, and one can use tables or software to obtain $n$ given $d$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$. For proportions, approximate formulas exist: to detect a difference $\Delta$ between paired proportions with power $1-\beta$, estimate the required $n$ considering the variance of the paired differences.

Always report uncertainty: confidence intervals for means, win rates, correlations, and rating system scores. When reporting correlations between automatic metrics and human judgments, give both the correlation coefficient (Pearson, Spearman, or Kendall) and confidence intervals, and be transparent about which correlation is appropriate (e.g., Spearman for ordinal human ratings). Finally, perform robustness checks: test whether conclusions hold across annotator subsets, prompt variants, or sample stratifications.

# Practical considerations for crowdsourcing and expert annotation

Choosing between crowdsourced annotators and experts depends on the evaluation complexity, required domain knowledge, cost, and timeline. Each approach has trade-offs that affect data quality, reliability, and interpretability.

Crowdsourcing platforms (e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk, Prolific) are cost-effective and fast for large-scale annotation. However, crowd workers vary in skill and motivation. To obtain reliable results, design robust quality control pipelines. Include qualification tests that assess basic comprehension and task-specific skills. Use attention checks (but avoid trivial or invasive checks that frustrate workers) and hidden gold-standard items interspersed to measure ongoing quality. Provide clear, concise instructions with positive and negative examples and edge cases. Keep per-task time reasonable to avoid fatigue and offer fair compensation that reflects local living wages; underpaying increases noisy responses and ethical concerns.

Batch effects can arise if different systems are evaluated at different times, by different worker pools, or under different interface conditions. Randomize items and systems across workers and time, and where possible evaluate multiple systems in the same HITs to control for worker-specific biases. Use blocking so that each worker sees diverse inputs and candidates, preventing learned artifacts specific to one system.

Expert annotators are necessary when domain knowledge matters (e.g., medical summarization, legal text, code generation). Experts provide higher-quality and more interpretable judgments but are more expensive and slower. For experts, define precise annotation guidelines and provide training sessions and calibration meetings. Consider inter-annotator adjudication meetings to reconcile disagreements and refine guidelines. Experts also facilitate more nuanced annotation types (error categorization, detailed rationales, severity ratings).

Compensation and motivation matter. For crowdsourcing, pay rates should reflect task complexity and expected time; for experts, budget for higher per-item costs and potentially longer timelines. Consider incentives that reward careful work, such as bonus payments for attention or consistency.

Interface design influences annotation quality. Provide a clean UI that displays the input and candidate outputs clearly, allows easy comparison (for pairwise tasks), and prevents accidental submissions. For long texts, provide scrolling with anchored reference points and allow annotators to mark spans or leave comments. Allow annotators to flag problematic items (e.g., unclear prompts, offensive content) for exclusion or further review.

Ethical considerations include informed consent, fair pay, privacy (do not expose sensitive data to crowd workers), and handling harmful content. When using crowd workers from diverse backgrounds, consider linguistic and cultural differences that may affect judgments, and when appropriate, recruit annotators who match the target user population.

Finally, plan for annotation scale and redundancy. For stable estimates, many tasks benefit from multiple annotators per item. Use pilot studies to estimate variance and determine the number of annotators needed. Aggregate ratings carefully (majority vote, median, weighted averages), and analyze annotator behavior to detect spammy or adversarial responses. Document all choices and report them transparently in publications to allow reproducibility.

# Conclusion

Evaluating generated text is both art and science. Automated metrics like BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR offer speed and reproducibility but have known blind spots. Human evaluations remain essential and must be carefully designed with clear instructions, suitable response formats, and measures of inter-annotator agreement. LLMs provide powerful tools for automated evaluation but require careful prompting, calibration, and validation against human judgments. Tournament-style comparisons and win-rate metrics help when comparing many systems, while Elo/TrueSkill provide principled methods for global ranking. Underpinning all evaluation is rigorous statistical thinking: design experiments with sufficient power, report uncertainties, correct for multiple comparisons, and use appropriate tests. Practical implementation—whether via crowdsourcing or expert annotation—requires attention to quality control, ethics, and cost. Combining automatic metrics, human judgments, and principled statistics produces robust conclusions about model improvements and helps the field progress toward systems that genuinely serve human needs.
