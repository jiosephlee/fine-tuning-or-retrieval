Introduction

Autoregressive language models (LMs) form the backbone of most modern generative natural language systems. These models define a conditional probability distribution over a target token sequence $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ given some context $x$ (which may be a prefix, prompt, or additional conditioning information). Contemporary LMs based on the Transformer architecture are trained to approximate this conditional distribution and to efficiently produce high-quality generations at inference time. This chapter explains the probabilistic factorization used by autoregressive models, how tokenization and vocabulary choices affect modeling, how models are trained with maximum likelihood (teacher forcing), how we compute sequence log-probabilities and handle length effects, common decoding algorithms (deterministic and stochastic), the phenomenon of exposure bias and strategies to mitigate it, the distinction between per-token and sequence-level objectives, and practical issues around computing and caching log-probabilities for training and decoding.


# Tokenization and vocabulary (BPE/WordPiece)

Before modeling sequences, text must be converted into discrete tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary. The choice of tokenization affects how the model represents subword structure, handles rare words, and trades off sequence length versus vocabulary size. Two widely used subword algorithms are Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece.

Both BPE and WordPiece start from a base set of characters (or bytes) and iteratively merge frequent adjacent symbol pairs into new tokens. In BPE, the algorithm counts every pair of adjacent symbols across the corpus and repeatedly merges the most frequent pair into a new token until a desired vocabulary size is reached. WordPiece uses a related criterion but typically optimizes a likelihood-like objective (or a similar scoring heuristic) to select merges. The result in both cases is a vocabulary of subword units that include common whole words, frequent prefixes and suffixes, and character-level pieces to handle rare words. Using bytes as the base symbols enables seamless handling of any unicode text without an out-of-vocabulary token.

Why subword tokenization matters: If tokens are characters, sequence length is very long and models must learn many long-range dependencies. If tokens are full words, the vocabulary becomes enormous and rare words are hard to model. Subword units strike a balance: common words appear as single tokens while rare words are split into predictable parts, allowing the model to generalize morphological patterns. Subword tokenization also affects model outputs: because the model generates tokens, not characters, decoding must consider how generated token sequences correspond to surface text, including byte-level details and whitespace handling. At evaluation time, metrics such as BLEU or human-readability are computed on reconstructed text after detokenization.

Vocabulary size is an important hyperparameter. Larger vocabularies reduce sequence length but increase the softmax output dimension and memory/computation per token. Transformer models typically use vocabularies in the range from 30k to 200k subword tokens, with trade-offs driven by training compute and expected domain languages. In addition to vocabulary size, special tokens are introduced for control (e.g., start-of-sequence, end-of-sequence, padding, separator tokens). The model must learn the semantics of these special tokens for conditioning and termination behavior.


# Autoregressive factorization $p(y\mid x)=\prod_t p(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$

Autoregressive models break the joint conditional distribution over a sequence into a product of conditional distributions over each token, conditioned on past tokens (left-to-right factorization). For a target sequence $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ and context $x$, the model represents

$p(y\mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^T p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$,

where $y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$. This factorization is exact and comes from the chain rule of probability. The modeling task reduces to specifying each conditional distribution $p(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$, which is implemented with a neural network (commonly a Transformer decoder or a sequence-to-sequence Transformer where the decoder attends to encoder outputs representing $x$).

Intuitively, the model predicts the next token given the entire previous generated prefix and any external context. The Transformer encodes $x$ (if present) and the prefix $y_{<t}$ into representations that the final linear+softmax layer maps to a distribution over the vocabulary at time $t$. Because attention permits direct access to any prior token representation, the model can learn both local (n-gram-like) and long-range dependencies (topic, coherence, coreference), as long as the architecture and training data support those patterns.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the autoregressive factorization imposes a causal structure: future tokens do not influence the conditional distribution at time $t$. This is key for left-to-right decoding and for efficient caching of past computations. Second, while the factorization is conceptually simple, the combinatorial number of possible sequences means the model must generalize and assign meaningful probabilities across a vast output space; the model's capacity, training objective, and data are what shape this generalization.


# Teacher forcing and maximum likelihood training

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the standard training objective for autoregressive LMs. Given a dataset of paired contexts and targets $\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, MLE seeks parameters $\theta$ that maximize the conditional log-likelihood:

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N \log p_\theta(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)}) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T^{(i)}} \log p_\theta(y^{(i)}_t \mid x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_{<t}).$

Since the product factorization decomposes the sequence log-probability into a sum of per-token log-probabilities, the negative log-likelihood is equivalent to the cross-entropy between the empirical token distribution (one-hot at the ground-truth token) and the model softmax at each timestep. Optimization is usually performed with stochastic gradient descent variants (e.g., Adam) on mini-batches computed in parallel.

Teacher forcing is the operational practice used during training: at each step $t$ the model conditions on the true previous tokens $y_{<t}$ from the dataset, not on tokens sampled from the model's own predictions. This allows full parallelization across the sequence dimension during training, because the entire sequence of inputs (shifted by one position) is known. It also provides stronger supervision since the model sees the correct prefix at each time step; the gradient signal directly pushes the model to increase the probability of the observed next token in the context of the true prefix.

Teacher forcing simplifies and stabilizes training, but it introduces a distributional mismatch between training and inference: at inference, the model conditions on its own past sampled predictions rather than on the true tokens. This mismatch, known as exposure bias (discussed later), can degrade performance at generation time, particularly when errors compound. Despite this, teacher forcing with MLE remains the dominant method because it is simple, scales well, and optimizes a well-defined probabilistic objective whose value can be monitored via perplexity.


# Sequence log-probabilities and length effects

The joint log-probability of a complete generated sequence $y$ under an autoregressive model is the sum of per-token log-probabilities:

$\log p_\theta(y\mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).$

Because these are sums of typically negative numbers (log-probabilities), longer sequences tend to have more negative log-probabilities. This introduces a length bias: if two sequences have similar per-token average probability, the longer sequence will have a lower total log-prob (more negative) simply because it has more tokens. Consequently, choosing a generation by maximizing the raw joint probability will tend to favor shorter sequences, which is undesirable when the natural or correct response is longer.

Several remedies are used in practice. One is to normalize by length, using the average log-probability per token:

$\frac{1}{T}\log p_\theta(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).$

Another is to use a length penalty term during search, typically of the form $LP(y)=\alpha\cdot T$ or $LP(y)=\frac{5+T}{6}^\beta$ (the latter used in some prior works). When comparing candidate sequences during beam search, one compares $\log p_\theta(y\mid x) / T^\gamma$ or $\log p_\theta(y\mid x) + \alpha T$ depending on the heuristic. The goal is to correct for the model's a priori bias against length and to align search criteria with evaluation metrics (e.g., BLEU or human preference) that are not monotonic with sequence length.

Perplexity is a standard intrinsic evaluation measure derived from average negative log-likelihood. For a test set of tokens, perplexity is $\exp\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum \log p_\theta(y_t\mid \cdot)\right)$, representing the geometric mean inverse probability per token. Lower perplexity indicates a model that assigns higher probability mass to the observed data. However, perplexity alone does not fully predict downstream generation quality, because decoding algorithms and exposure bias interact with model calibration and entropy.


# Decoding strategies: greedy, beam, temperature, top-k/p

Decoding is the process of generating a token sequence given the model and a context. Different strategies trade off between quality (finding high-probability sequences) and diversity (sampling varied outputs). We summarize the main strategies.

Greedy decoding selects the token with maximum conditional probability at each step:

$y_t = \arg\max_v p_\theta(v\mid x, y_{<t}).$

Greedy is fast and deterministic but can get trapped into locally optimal but globally suboptimal sequences because it ignores future consequences of a choice.

Beam search generalizes greedy decoding by maintaining $B$ hypotheses (the beam) at each timestep. At each step, each hypothesis is expanded by all candidate tokens (or top-k tokens) and the resulting expansions are scored by their cumulative log-probability (often with length normalization or penalties). The top $B$ expansions are kept as the new beam. Beam search can increase the chance of finding high-likelihood sequences compared to greedy, but larger beams may produce degenerate outputs (repetitive or overly generic text) and come at greater computation. Beam search effectively approximates maximum a posteriori (MAP) decoding under the model.

Sampling-based decoding injects stochasticity to produce diverse outputs. Two important sampling techniques are temperature scaling and top-k/top-p (nucleus) sampling.

Temperature scaling adjusts the sharpness of the predictive distribution by dividing the logits by a temperature parameter $T_{temp}>0$ before softmax:

$p_T(v\mid\cdot) \;\propto\; \exp\left(\frac{z_v}{T_{temp}}\right)$,

where $z_v$ are logits. When $T_{temp}<1$ the distribution becomes sharper (more concentrated on high-probability tokens); when $T_{temp}>1$ it becomes flatter, increasing diversity. Temperature provides a simple knob to control randomness but does not directly guard against sampling extremely low-probability tokens if $T_{temp}$ is large.

Top-k sampling restricts sampling to the $k$ tokens with highest probability at that timestep, renormalizing probabilities over that set and sampling from it. Top-p (nucleus) sampling chooses the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds threshold $p$ (e.g., $p=0.9$) and samples from that set. Nucleus sampling adapts the candidate set size to the distribution's entropy at each step, allowing for both focused and diverse sampling depending on uncertainty.

Other hybrid strategies exist: applying temperature plus top-k or top-p, filtering tokens below a minimum probability, or combining beam search with stochastic perturbations. Each strategy affects the trade-off between determinism, diversity, and faithfulness to the model distribution. Importantly, sampling-based methods do not attempt to maximize joint probability; they sample from a modified distribution that aims to produce fluent and diverse outputs.
