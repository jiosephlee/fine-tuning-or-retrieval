Introduction

Evaluation of aligned text generation—models that produce summaries, dialogue, and other open-ended outputs intended to match human preferences—is both crucial and challenging. Unlike classification tasks with clear right or wrong labels, alignment-oriented generation seeks to satisfy subjective criteria such as helpfulness, safety, factuality, and style. This chapter covers the practical methodologies used to evaluate such systems, focusing on how to measure human preferences, quantify agreement and confidence, understand the limits of automatic metrics, and apply modern tools such as large language models (LLMs) as judges. We will also discuss task-specific rubrics, how sampling temperature changes evaluation outcomes, how to design and analyze A/B tests with statistical rigor, and why out-of-distribution evaluation is necessary for robust assessment.


# Human evaluation protocols and win-rate measurement

Human evaluation remains the gold standard for alignment-focused generation because human raters can judge nuance, context, and values that automatic metrics cannot reliably capture. Several protocols are commonly used.

Pairwise comparison (preference) is widely adopted: a human is shown two outputs for the same prompt (for instance, responses from two systems) and asked which they prefer according to a specified rubric. Pairwise comparison is attractive because it reduces calibration problems that arise with absolute scale ratings (a rater’s notion of "good" can vary). The primary summary statistic for pairwise comparisons is the win-rate. If system A is compared to system B across $N$ independent comparisons and system A is preferred in $k$ of those, the empirical win-rate for A against B is $\hat{p} = k / N$. The win-rate can be reported directly, or it can be transformed into other comparative statistics (for example, converting to an Elo-like score in tournaments).

Absolute rating protocols (Likert scales) ask raters to score outputs on one or more dimensions (e.g., helpfulness from 1 to 5). These scores allow more granular information (mean scores, rating distributions) but require careful rater calibration to avoid inter-annotator scale drift. For alignment tasks with multiple dimensions (helpfulness, style, harmlessness), absolute ratings make it straightforward to compute multi-dimensional trade-offs.

Another useful protocol is binary correctness or pass/fail on a rubric criterion. This is natural for certain tasks like factuality checks ("contains a factual error? yes/no") and can be aggregated as a pass rate.

When evaluating many systems or many dimensions, it is common to pool preferences and convert them into a ranking or rating via statistical models. The Bradley–Terry model is used to convert pairwise win counts into latent scores: assume each system $i$ has a quality parameter $\theta_i$, and the probability that $i$ beats $j$ is $\frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i) + \exp(\theta_j)}$. Fitting these models yields interpretable scores and confidence intervals.

No matter the protocol, precise description of the evaluation setup (annotator instructions, number of annotators per item, whether comparisons are paired or unpaired, whether raters see system identifiers) is essential for reproducibility.


# Inter-annotator agreement and confidence

Human evaluations are noisy because judgments are subjective and annotators differ. Quantifying inter-annotator agreement helps you judge the reliability of collected labels and informs how many annotators you need.

For categorical labels (including pairwise preferences interpreted as categories), Cohen's kappa measures agreement between two annotators beyond chance. For more than two annotators, Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha are commonly used. Krippendorff's alpha is especially versatile: it handles different numbers of annotators per item, missing data, and different measurement levels (nominal, ordinal, interval). The formula for Krippendorff's alpha is
\[
\alpha = 1 - \frac{D_o}{D_e},
\]
where $D_o$ is the observed disagreement and $D_e$ is the expected disagreement by chance.

For continuous ratings (Likert scales treated as interval), the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) quantifies the proportion of variance attributable to true differences among items versus annotator noise. An ICC near 1 indicates high reliability; near 0 indicates ratings are mostly noise.

Interpretation of agreement scores requires context. There is no absolute cutoff, but practical heuristics are: kappa or alpha below 0.2 is poor, 0.2–0.4 fair, 0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 substantial, and above 0.8 almost perfect. For alignment tasks with subjective trade-offs, moderate agreement is common; low agreement signals either ambiguous instructions, inconsistent annotators, or inherently subjective criteria.

Confidence and sample size. When reporting aggregated metrics like win-rate or mean rating, include confidence intervals. For a binomial win-rate $\hat{p}$ with $N$ independent comparisons, an approximate 95% confidence interval is
\[
\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{N}}.
\]
For mean ratings, use the standard error $s/\sqrt{n}$ with $s$ the sample standard deviation and $n$ the number of independent items. Bootstrapping—resampling items with replacement and recomputing the statistic many times—provides nonparametric confidence intervals that are robust to non-normal distributions and complex aggregations (e.g., aggregated per-rater or per-item first, then averaged). When comparisons are paired (same items compared across systems), compute paired confidence intervals accordingly—this usually reduces variance compared to unpaired estimates.

Finally, to increase reliability, use multiple annotators per item and aggregate (majority vote for categorical labels or average for continuous ratings), and perform quality control such as qualification tests, gold-standard checks, and monitoring time-on-task and agreement with anchor items.


# Limitations of automatic metrics (e.g., ROUGE) for alignment

Automatic metrics such as ROUGE, BLEU, METEOR, and newer neural metrics like BERTScore or BLEURT are useful for rapid iteration, but they have important limitations for alignment-focused evaluation.

ROUGE and BLEU are based on $n$-gram overlap between a generated text and a reference. They assume that high lexical overlap implies quality, which is not true for many alignment goals. For summarization, a correct, fluent, and helpful summary may use very different words than a reference, leading to low ROUGE despite good quality. In dialogue and instruction-following, the number of acceptable responses is large and reference-based measures systematically undercount valid variations.

Semantic metrics (BERTScore, BLEURT, MoverScore) use contextual embeddings or learned models to estimate semantic similarity. These are better at capturing paraphrase-level similarity but still have blind spots. They may reward similarity to a particular style or phrasing and can be misled by hallucinated but fluent content. They are also trained on certain datasets and can be biased toward data distributions seen in pretraining or fine-tuning.

Automatic metrics generally do not measure safety, alignment with human values, or pragmatic helpfulness. For example, a toxic but on-topic response may score highly on ROUGE or BERTScore. Metrics that correlate with human preferences on one dataset can fail on others; correlation experiments often show that metric-human correlation drops when evaluating across different model families or out-of-distribution prompts.

In short, automatic metrics are best used for fast iteration and as proxy signals, but any deployment or research claim about alignment should be corroborated with human evaluations specifically designed for the relevant preference or safety criteria.


# LLM-as-judge: prompts, reliability, and caveats

Large language models can be used as automatic judges to rate or compare outputs. The appeal is obvious: LLMs can produce human-like judgments at scale and can be prompted in flexible ways to follow rubrics. However, using LLMs as judges requires caution.

Prompt design. When asking an LLM to judge outputs, you must provide a clear rubric, example judgments (few-shot prompting), and possibly a method to calibrate the model. For instance, give the LLM a description of what "helpful" means, provide two labeled examples of helpful versus unhelpful responses, and then ask it to choose. Chain-of-thought style prompting can sometimes help the model explain its reasoning, which aids interpretability and reveals failure modes. You can also ask the LLM to produce a numeric score with a brief justification.

Reliability and evaluation. LLM judges are only heuristics; you must validate them against human judgments before relying on them. Typical validation involves computing agreement metrics between LLM-generated judgments and human labels on an independent dataset. Report correlation coefficients (Spearman or Pearson for continuous scores) or agreement statistics (accuracy, kappa) for categorical judgments. Expect moderate correspondence that varies by task and metric. LLMs often have systematic biases: they may favor longer or more verbose outputs, be influenced by surface cues, and can be overly conservative or excessively literal.

Caveats and failure modes. LLM judges can be adversarially exploited: small changes to an output that do not affect human perception can flip an LLM’s judgment. They can hallucinate reasoning in chain-of-thought and produce plausible-sounding but incorrect justifications for a decision. They also inherit biases from their training data, which can lead to unfair or culturally skewed evaluations. LLMs might overfit to the rubric wording, focusing on easily measurable features rather than deeper qualities. Finally, when comparing models, avoid prompting the judge to "prefer larger models" or otherwise include cues that leak identities; keep judgments blind to system origin.

Best practices. Treat LLM-as-judge as a scalable proxy, not a replacement for human evaluation. Validate on held-out human labels and report the validation metrics. Use multiple LLM judges and ensemble their judgments where possible. When feasible, use LLM-generated explanations to triage surprising ratings and identify systematic errors.


# Task-specific rubrics (helpfulness, harmlessness, style)

Alignment evaluations must operationalize what "good" means via rubrics—concrete criteria that annotators (human or machine) use. A well-designed rubric reduces ambiguity, improves inter-annotator agreement, and clarifies the exact behaviors you want to optimize.

Helpfulness. For helpfulness, rubrics typically include facets such as relevance to the prompt, completeness (does the answer cover the necessary points?), correctness or factuality, clarity (is the explanation understandable?), and usefulness (does it enable the user to act or learn?). Each facet can be rated separately to expose trade-offs: an answer might be very helpful but contain a minor factual error, or it might be factually correct but poorly organized. The rubric should define examples of each score level and instruct annotators how to treat partial credit.

Harmlessness. Safety or harmlessness rubrics enumerate classes of problematic content: hate speech, harassment, violent instructions, promotion of self-harm, privacy violations, disallowed medical or legal advice, and so on. The rubric should be prescriptive about what constitutes a violation, with edge-case examples. For dialogue and instruction-following systems, harmlessness is often judged both by content (does the response contain disallowed content?) and by latent intent (does the response assist in wrongdoing?). Because safety is high-stakes and often low-prevalence, consider focused adversarial evaluation where prompts are crafted to elicit risky behavior.

Style. Style rubrics capture tone, formality, brevity, persona consistency, and other surface-level attributes. For product-oriented systems, adherence to brand voice or persona may be assessed via specific style checklists. Style is particularly subjective, so provide clear anchors (sample outputs exemplifying each rating) and possibly ask raters to choose which style template the output matches.

Multi-attribute evaluation. Often you will evaluate across multiple rubric dimensions simultaneously. When aggregating, avoid simplistic averaging when dimensions are qualitatively different (e.g., helpfulness vs. safety). A safer approach is to treat certain dimensions as constraints (must pass harmlessness) and optimize others only on safe outputs. Alternatively, multi-objective reporting—showing a Pareto frontier across metrics—helpfully communicates trade-offs.

Rubrics must be piloted and iteratively refined with annotator feedback. Ambiguity in instructions is the main source of low agreement, so include many examples and clarifying rules.


# Sampling temperature effects on evaluation

Sampling temperature (and sampling strategy more generally) controls the randomness of generation and has substantial effects on evaluation outcomes. Temperature $T$ scales the logits before sampling: the probability of token $i$ is proportional to $\exp(\ell_i / T)$ where $\ell_i$ is the logit. Lower $T$ makes the distribution peakier and more deterministic; higher $T$ yields more diverse, stochastic outputs.

Impact on metrics. For deterministic metrics and tasks where a single correct answer exists (e.g., math solutions), low temperature (or greedy decoding) is appropriate. For open-ended generation, higher temperature creates more diverse outputs; this diversity can both help and hurt. In human evaluations of helpfulness, slightly higher temperature might produce more creative or verbose responses that some users prefer, increasing variance in ratings. For safety, increased temperature may lead to more risky or bizarre completions, increasing the probability of harmful outputs even if average helpfulness improves.

Comparability. When comparing models, control the sampling temperature to ensure fair comparisons. A model evaluated at $T=0.2$ will produce more conservative outputs than the same model at $T=1.0$. If you compare systems across temperatures, report results as functions of $T$ or fix $T$ across systems. For some comparisons, evaluating at multiple temperatures and reporting aggregate statistics (e.g., mean rating across sampled seeds) provides a fuller picture.

Sampling strategies. Besides temperature, other choices matter: nucleus sampling ($p$-sampling), top-$k$, greedy decoding, or beam search. Beam search can artificially optimize for n-gram overlap metrics and may produce overly safe or repetitive outputs; it is not always reflective of user-experienced behavior in interactive settings. Select strategies that match intended deployment and document them.

Statistical considerations. Stochastic sampling increases evaluation variance, so collect multiple sampled outputs per prompt and aggregate. When reporting preference win-rates between two stochastic systems, treat each sampled output as an independent item only if it is drawn from independent seeds; better is to sample multiple outputs per prompt and have raters judge pairs sampled jointly to reflect the distributional comparison.
