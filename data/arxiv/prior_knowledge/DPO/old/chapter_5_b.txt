# Reference policy construction from SFT

A reference policy is an explicit policy used as a baseline or prior during later stages of alignment (for instance, as the KL-constraint in RLHF or as a target in distillation). Constructing a good reference policy from SFT is important because it sets the initial behavioral distribution and affects the stability and safety of downstream optimization.

The simplest reference policy is the final SFT model itself: train a model with supervised objectives and freeze it to serve as the reference. This works well when SFT results in reliable outputs and is often used because it reflects the desired basic instruction-following behavior. Using the SFT model as the reference ensures that subsequent policy updates are anchored to reasonable responses.

However, there are nuances. If SFT overfits or incorporates undesirable biases, using it naively as the reference can propagate these problems. Therefore, it's common to construct a reference that is either an ensemble average of several SFT checkpoints (to smooth out idiosyncrasies), a temperature-adjusted version (to increase entropy and avoid overconfidence), or a mixture of the pre-trained base and the SFT model (weighted average) to balance new behaviors with retained prior knowledge.

Another approach is to distill SFT into a smaller or simpler policy specifically designed for use as a reference. Distillation can remove extraneous noise and produce a compact model that is easier to compute KL divergence against. When constructing reference policies for RL steps, computational considerations matter because reference likelihoods must be evaluated frequently during optimization.

When using KL penalties or trust-region methods, the exact form of the reference matters. If the reference is too strong, it will prevent meaningful policy improvement; if too weak, the policy can drift into unsafe behaviors. Thus, hyperparameters that control the strength of anchoring to the reference (e.g., KL coefficient) must be tuned carefully, typically on held-out preference or safety evaluations.


# Evaluation of SFT models

Evaluating supervised fine-tuned models requires multiple complementary strategies because no single metric captures all aspects of instruction following, safety, and generalization.

Automated metrics are the first line. Perplexity and log-likelihood measure how well the model fits the training distribution, but they are poor proxies for instruction following quality. Task-specific metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, or exact-match are useful for narrow tasks like translation or QA, but they do not capture correctness in open-ended tasks. Embedding-based similarity measures and learned evaluation models can better handle paraphrases and diverse valid responses.

Human evaluation remains the gold standard for instruction-following quality, especially on dimensions like helpfulness, harmlessness, factual accuracy, and preference. Pairwise comparisons are effective: humans are presented with two model outputs for the same prompt and indicate their preference. These comparisons are used to train preference models and to estimate win rates between models, which are intuitive aggregate measures.

Robustness and generalization tests are necessary. This includes evaluation on held-out tasks and unseen instruction phrasing, stress tests with adversarial or ambiguous prompts, and zero-shot or few-shot evaluations on tasks not explicitly represented during training. Evaluating on synthetic benchmarks that probe specific capabilities (e.g., algebraic reasoning, commonsense, or multi-step planning) helps isolate strengths and weaknesses.

Safety evaluation is essential. Assessments should include adversarial prompt tests designed to elicit harmful outputs, checks for sensitive content leakage, and audits for bias across demographic axes. Automatic toxic content detectors are useful as a first filter, but they have false positives and negatives; human review is often required for high-stakes assessments.

Calibration and uncertainty estimates should be measured: when should the model admit ignorance? Techniques like selective prediction tests how often the model's confidence correlates with correctness. Evaluating the model's ability to decline inappropriate requests or to ask clarifying questions when prompts are underspecified is important for safe deployment.

Finally, evaluation must be mindful of data contamination: if test prompts or references were part of the training data (pre-training or fine-tuning), performance will be overestimated. Evaluations should therefore include checks for overlap with training corpora and prefer held-out benchmarks with documented provenance.

Conclusion

Supervised fine-tuning and instruction tuning are foundational steps in adapting pre-trained language models to behave in ways that are useful, safe, and aligned with human intent. Careful curation of instruction and demonstration datasets, awareness of the objectives and pitfalls of SFT, and strategies to prevent overfitting are essential. Combining demonstrations with preference learning often yields the best practical alignment outcomes: demonstrations provide a concrete initial policy and teach format and style, while preference-based methods refine behavior along more nuanced axes. Constructing robust reference policies and deploying comprehensive evaluation suites—including human judgments and contamination checks—ensure that SFT produces models that generalize beyond their training examples and are suitable as the basis for further alignment. Understanding these components prepares you to read and contribute to research on instruction-tuned and aligned language models.
