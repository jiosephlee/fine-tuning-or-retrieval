# Monitoring reward, KL, and quality metrics

Training diagnostics are essential in RLHF to detect divergent behavior early. The most used metrics are reward (the RM score), KL divergence to the reference policy, and explicit quality metrics (perplexity, human evaluation, safety checks). Monitoring them together provides a coherent picture.

Reward: Track the average and distribution of reward model scores over time and across prompt types. An increasing average reward indicates the policy is finding ways to satisfy the RM, but this can be misleading if the policy learns to exploit RM weaknesses rather than genuinely improving satisfaction. Therefore, complement average reward with percentile statistics (e.g., median, 90th percentile) and track reward on held-out datasets or prompts that are not used for optimization. Also monitor per-annotator and per-prompt-class rewards to catch regressions in specific domains.

KL: If you are using a KL penalty, monitor the empirical KL divergence between the policy and the reference model. Changes in KL reflect how far the policy has drifted. Typical practice is to enforce a target KL or bound and adjust the penalty coefficient $\beta$ adaptively. However, raw KL numbers are sensitive to batch composition and sequence length, so compute them on standardized prompt sets and log the decomposition (per-token KL, masked KL). Sudden drops or spikes in KL often signal instability: a doubling of KL might show collapse toward a degenerate low-entropy policy or a mis-specified penalty.

Quality metrics: Track conventional language model metrics such as perplexity and negative log-likelihood on held-out supervised data. Track human-evaluation metrics periodically: pairwise comparisons between baseline and current policy, safety labels, factuality checks, and downstream task performance. Automated classifiers for toxicity and hallucination can provide continuous monitoring but should not replace human evaluation as they themselves can be gamed. Also monitor generation-specific artifacts: frequency of repeated tokens or phrases, average length, rates of end-of-sequence tokens, and syntactic coherence measures.

Combine these signals in dashboards that can slice by prompt category, model version, and labeler population. Alerts should be set for when reward rises but quality metrics fall, or when KL exceeds thresholds. When anomaly detectors trigger, the pipeline should be able to roll back to a safe checkpoint and flag examples for human review.


# Failure modes: degeneration, overfitting, collapse

Understanding and anticipating failure modes is essential because RLHF readily produces undesired behavior if components are imperfect.

Degeneration refers to declines in surface-level quality—examples include repetitive outputs ("I don't know" repeated), loss of coherence, or production of terse, generic answers. Degeneration often emerges when the RM rewards short or safe answers, or when a strong KL penalty encourages conservative sampling. To diagnose degeneration, track repetition rates, n-gram uniqueness, and average response length. Mitigation strategies include adjusting RM training to penalize degenerate patterns, adding explicit diversity or information-content terms to the reward, and tuning the KL coefficient.

Overfitting happens at two levels: the reward model overfitting to annotator idiosyncrasies or to narrow prompt types, and the policy overfitting to the RM (reward hacking). If the RM has limited coverage, the policy can discover ways to maximize RM score that humans would not perceive as improvements. This is sometimes called reward gaming. Signs include very high RM scores but poor human evaluation. Remedies include collecting more diverse human comparisons, using regularization for the RM (early stopping, weight decay, ensembling, input perturbations), and penalizing unnatural shortcuts in the policy objective (e.g., adding auxiliary losses for fluency or factuality).

Collapse denotes catastrophic modes where the policy's entropy falls sharply and it emits the same output or a small set of outputs for many prompts. Collapse can be precipitated by an overly large KL penalty (which can push the policy to a low-entropy point) or by unstable RL updates that push probabilities to extremes. Monitoring entropy and per-token probability distributions will flag collapse early. To recover, reduce update sizes, increase entropy regularization, and restore checkpoints. In addition, maintaining a replay buffer of diverse high-quality examples, or mixing in supervised MLE steps periodically, helps maintain diversity.

Reward model mis-specification is another major failure mode. If the RM incorrectly scores unsafe or low-quality responses highly (perhaps because annotation instructions were ambiguous), the policy will optimize toward those undesirable outputs. Frequent sanity checks by humans on high-RM examples, and adversarial annotation where prompt-response pairs are designed to detect such weaknesses, are practical countermeasures. Combining multiple reward heads or ensembling RMs trained on different annotator populations can also increase robustness.


# Reproducibility and hyperparameter management

RLHF pipelines contain many interacting hyperparameters: SFT learning rates and schedules, RM model architecture and regularization, RL optimizer choices, KL coefficient $\beta$, entropy regularization, batch sizes, generation hyperparameters (temperature, top-k), and data selection strategies. Reproducible experiments and reliable production deployments require careful tracking and management.

Use experiment-tracking systems that log hyperparameter configurations, random seeds, code diffs, and dataset versions for every run. Store model checkpoints with semantic versioning and tie each evaluation to the exact checkpoint used. For RL runs, log rollout seeds and the exact sampling parameters so generations are reproducible.

Hyperparameter tuning in RLHF is expensive. Practical approaches include coarse sweeps to find stable ranges, followed by focused grid or Bayesian searches on key knobs such as $\beta$, learning rate, and batch size. Use smaller-scale proxies (e.g., smaller models or reduced datasets) to explore hyperparameter sensitivity before committing to full-scale runs, but validate final settings at production scale because some behaviors do not scale linearly.

For the KL penalty, an adaptive controller can reduce the need for manual tuning: for example, adjust $\beta$ every epoch to track a target KL by increasing it if KL exceeds the target and decreasing if KL is below target. Implement conservative bounds on how quickly $\beta$ can change to avoid oscillation.

Maintain reproducibility across teams by codifying recommended default settings and recording meta-parameters: which prompts are in the evaluation set, how reward labels are aggregated (majority vote, weighted by annotator reliability), and how preference ties are handled. Automate pipeline steps where possible so manual ad-hoc changes do not introduce silent differences.

Finally, perform ablations and sensitivity analyses to identify which hyperparameters materially affect outcomes. Publish or save these analyses: they are valuable when debugging or on-boarding new engineers and researchers.

Conclusion

Engineering an RLHF system requires attention to both ML algorithms and practical systems design. The three-stage pipeline—SFT, reward modeling, and RL optimization—defines the core research choices, while reference model selection, data pipelines, compute budgeting, caching/batching, and monitoring make the pipeline operational. Vigilant monitoring for degeneration, overfitting, and collapse, combined with disciplined reproducibility practices and pragmatic hyperparameter management, separates experiments that succeed in the lab from robust production systems. With the principles described in this chapter, you should be equipped to read, evaluate, and implement RLHF research in realistic settings and reason about the critical engineering trade-offs.
