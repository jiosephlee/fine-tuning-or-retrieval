# Classification losses: BCE, hinge, pairwise ranking losses

Choice of loss function encodes modeling assumptions and affects both optimization dynamics and the behavior of the learned predictor. We cover several widely used losses.

Binary cross-entropy (BCE), also called logistic loss for binary classification, is defined for scalar logits $z(\theta; x)$ with predicted probability $\hat{p} = \sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$. For label $y\in\{0,1\}$, the loss is
\[
\ell_\text{BCE}(\theta; x,y) = -y\log\hat{p} - (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p}) = \log\big(1+e^{-y' z}\big),
\]
where $y' = 2y-1\in\{-1,1\}$ and we used the equivalence with the logistic loss $\log(1+e^{-y' z})$. BCE is convex in $z$ and smooth; it encourages calibrated probabilistic outputs and works well with probabilistic interpretations.

The hinge loss arises from support vector machines and focuses on margin maximization. For a label $y'\in\{-1,1\}$ and score $s$, the hinge loss is
\[
\ell_\text{hinge}(s, y') = \max(0, 1 - y's).
\]
This loss penalizes examples that are either misclassified ($y's < 0$) or too close to the decision boundary ($0 \le y's < 1$). Unlike BCE, hinge loss does not produce probabilistic outputs and is not differentiable at $y's=1$, but it promotes large margins and can be robust. Smooth versions of hinge exist (e.g., squared hinge) that are differentiable.

Pairwise ranking losses appear when the supervision is relative rather than absolute, such as preference data: given two items $a$ and $b$ and a preference label indicating $a$ is preferred to $b$, the objective is to assign higher scores to preferred items. One common formulation is the pairwise logistic loss (also called Bradley–Terry or binary cross-entropy over pairwise differences). If the model gives scores $s_a$ and $s_b$, define the difference $d = s_a - s_b$. The pairwise logistic loss for a preference that $a$ is preferred over $b$ is
\[
\ell_\text{pair}(d) = \log\big(1 + e^{-d}\big).
\]
Minimizing this loss encourages $d$ to be large and positive. This view corresponds to modeling the probability that $a$ is preferred as $\sigma(s_a - s_b)$. More generally, the Plackett–Luce model or Bradley–Terry extension handles ranking over multiple items by modeling the probability of any ordering in terms of exponentiated scores.

Pairwise losses are particularly important in reward modeling from human preferences, because explicit numerical reward labels may be unavailable; instead, humans provide comparisons between sequences. Training a reward model with pairwise losses produces a scalar reward function aligned with preferences. When using pairwise losses, it is important to consider how to sample pairs (uniformly, biased towards near-ties, or to high-uncertainty examples) and how to calibrate the resulting scale of rewards.


# Importance weighting and class imbalance

In many real datasets examples are sampled from a distribution that does not match the distribution of interest, or class frequencies are imbalanced. Importance weighting adjusts losses to account for distributional shifts and to handle class imbalance.

Suppose training samples come from distribution $p_\text{train}(x)$ but we want to minimize expected loss under a target distribution $p_\text{target}(x)$. The importance-weighted objective is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\text{target}}[\ell(\theta; x)] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\text{train}}\left[\frac{p_\text{target}(x)}{p_\text{train}(x)} \ell(\theta; x)\right].
\]
Thus each training example is reweighted by $w(x) = p_\text{target}(x)/p_\text{train}(x)$. In practice, exact densities are unknown; one may estimate weights or subsample to approximate the target distribution.

For class imbalance, a common and practical approach is to weight examples inversely proportional to class frequency. In binary classification with classes $c\in\{0,1\}$ having frequencies $f_c$, use per-example weight $w_c \propto 1/f_c$ (often normalized). This encourages the loss to treat rare classes with equal importance. Alternative approaches include resampling (oversampling minority classes or undersampling majority ones), using focal loss (which emphasizes hard examples by downweighting well-classified ones), or designing class-balanced sampling strategies.

Importance weighting can worsen variance in gradient estimates: if some weights are large, they dominate gradients and increase variance. Techniques to mitigate this include clipping weights, smoothing with a floor, or combining weighting with stratified sampling to ensure stable mini-batch compositions.

When learning from preference data, importance weighting can also adjust for biases in which pairs humans are asked to compare. If annotators are more likely to compare certain types of pairs, weighting can correct the empirical pair distribution to better reflect the intended evaluation distribution.


# Stability and variance considerations in stochastic training

SGD introduces noise into the optimization process because gradients are computed on mini-batches. Controlling variance and ensuring stability of updates is crucial, especially for large models where instability can cause catastrophic divergence.

One key lever is batch size. Larger mini-batches produce lower-variance gradient estimates and smoother training curves, enabling larger learning rates and faster wall-clock training when used in parallel hardware. However, very large batches can harm generalization and reduce the beneficial stochasticity that helps exploration. There is often a sweet spot or a scaling rule (e.g., linear scaling of learning rate with batch size up to a limit). Empirical practice often uses batch sizes that balance hardware efficiency and generalization.

Gradient clipping is widely used to prevent rare but large gradients from blowing up parameter updates and causing numerical instability. For example, gradient norm clipping rescales $g_t$ if $\|g_t\|_2$ exceeds a threshold $C$:
\[
g_t \leftarrow g_t \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{C}{\|g_t\|_2}\right).
\]
Clipping is essential for training recurrent models and large transformer models at high learning rates.

Variance reduction techniques beyond increasing batch size include momentum, which averages gradients over time and reduces effective variance; variance-reduced gradient methods (e.g., SVRG, SAGA) that correct stochastic gradients using occasional full-gradient information; and control variates when using importance weighting. In practice for deep learning, momentum and adaptive optimizers provide most of the benefit.

Numerical precision also impacts stability. Mixed precision training (using float16 for activations and float32 for some accumulators) accelerates training but can introduce underflow or overflow in very large models. Loss scaling and careful implementation of layer normalization and softmax are necessary.

Finally, the initialization of parameters affects early training dynamics. Poor initialization can produce large initial activations and gradients; initialization schemes (e.g., He, Xavier) are designed to keep signal magnitudes stable through depth. Combined with learning rate warmup, good initialization helps prevent early divergence.


# Hyperparameter tuning and validation

Hyperparameters—learning rate, batch size, weight decay, optimizer choice, architecture choices, data augmentation and preprocessing, regularization rates, dropout probabilities—strongly influence final model quality. Tuning hyperparameters systematically is essential.

Validation protocols begin by partitioning data into training, validation (development), and test splits. The validation set is used to select hyperparameters and perform early stopping; the test set is used only for final evaluation. For small datasets, cross-validation (k-fold) provides more robust estimates by averaging performance across multiple splits. For large-scale language model training with massive datasets, cross-validation is often impractical; instead, a fixed held-out validation set (possibly several) is used.

Search strategies include:

- Grid search: evaluate a Cartesian product of hyperparameter values. Exhaustive but exponentially expensive with many hyperparameters.
- Random search: sample hyperparameters uniformly or from log-uniform distributions. Often more efficient than grid search because not all hyperparameters are equally important.
- Bayesian optimization: build a surrogate model of hyperparameter performance (e.g., Gaussian process, Tree-structured Parzen Estimator) and select promising points. More sample-efficient but more complex to implement.
- Population-based training and bandit methods: maintain multiple trials, exploit early results to allocate resources adaptively, and allow hyperparameters to evolve during training.
- Multi-fidelity methods like Hyperband and successive halving: evaluate many configurations with small budgets and allocate more compute to promising runs.

When tuning, use log-scales for learning rates and weight decay because performance is sensitive multiplicatively. Learning rate warmup length, peak learning rate, and decay schedule are typically tuned together. Keep the validation metric aligned with the final objective; if your model will be evaluated on pairwise preference accuracy, use a related validation metric rather than only cross-entropy.

Early stopping uses validation performance to choose when to terminate training. To avoid overfitting the validation set when hyperparameter tuning, you can use nested cross-validation or hold out a separate meta-validation set for hyperparameter selection in critical evaluations.

Practical considerations include computational budget: hyperparameter tuning can be expensive. Use smaller proxies (smaller model, fewer steps, smaller dataset slices) to quickly explore coarse hyperparameter ranges, then refine on larger runs. Keep detailed logs and deterministic seeds where possible to improve reproducibility.


# Putting it together for reward models and preference learning

Reward models trained from human preference comparisons typically use pairwise ranking losses (logistic pairwise loss or listwise losses) and are optimized with SGD or adaptive optimizers. Important practical points include:

- Choice of loss: pairwise logistic loss is a natural choice, modeling preference probabilities as $\sigma(r(x_a) - r(x_b))$. For multiple options, Plackett–Luce or listwise losses model full rankings.
- Sampling pairs: uniform sampling may waste pairs that are trivially ranked; sampling pairs with similar scores (hard negatives) or with high human disagreement can be more informative. Care must be taken to avoid sampling bias—importance weighting may be needed to correct the empirical pair distribution.
- Regularization and calibration: reward models can be miscalibrated in magnitude; temperature scaling or isotonic regression on a validation set can align predicted differences with human probabilities. Weight decay and early stopping reduce overfitting to limited human-labeled pairs.
- Stability: preference data is often noisy and small in scale relative to language model pretraining. Use small learning rates, gradient clipping, and conservative optimizers (AdamW) to avoid catastrophic updates, especially when fine-tuning large base models.
- Evaluation: validation for reward models should use held-out comparisons and involve metrics like pairwise accuracy, AUC, and calibration error. When deployed in policy optimization loops (e.g., RL from human feedback), reward model properties (scale, smoothness) strongly influence downstream policy learning; thus calibration and stability are crucial.

Conclusion

Optimization and loss design are central to successful machine learning. Understanding gradient descent and SGD, the roles of learning rates and adaptivity (Adam/AdamW), regularization strategies, the geometry of convex vs. non-convex landscapes, appropriate loss choices for classification and ranking, the need for importance weighting with imbalanced or shifted data, and how to ensure stable stochastic training are foundational skills. Coupled with principled hyperparameter tuning and validation practices, these tools allow you to train reliable classifiers and reward models and to interpret and reproduce state-of-the-art research results. As you work with real models and datasets, experiment with these techniques, monitor training dynamics carefully, and use validation-driven decisions to guide your choices.
