# On-policy vs. off-policy learning

On-policy and off-policy describe whether the data used to update the policy come from the current policy or from a different behavior policy. In on-policy learning, trajectories are sampled from the policy we are optimizing, $\pi_\theta$. Examples include REINFORCE, actor-critic, and PPO when implemented with fresh rollouts from the current parameters. On-policy methods often produce stable updates because the data distribution matches the optimization target, but they can be sample-inefficient since you must re-sample whenever the policy changes.

Off-policy learning uses data collected by a different policy, which might be older versions of the policy, a separate exploration policy, or logged data collected offline. Off-policy methods can be more sample-efficient because they can reuse past data and incorporate diverse experience, but they require care to correct for distribution mismatch. Importance sampling provides an unbiased correction factor: for a trajectory sampled from behavior policy $\mu$, reweighting by the likelihood ratio $\prod_t \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)}$ recovers expectations under $\pi_\theta$. However, these importance weights can have extremely high variance, especially for long sequences, because they multiply many ratios together. Many off-policy algorithms therefore use truncated importance sampling, Retrace-style corrections, or learn a critic that is trained off-policy.

In language-model fine-tuning, on-policy methods are conceptually simple: sample candidate outputs from the current model, score them, and update the model. This is what early RL for sequence generation used. Off-policy methods are attractive because large datasets of human feedback, demonstrations, or previously generated outputs may be available, allowing reuse of costly reward annotations. For example, offline RL tries to learn a policy from a fixed dataset without further interaction; however, offline RL is challenging because the learned policy may visit regions of state-action space not covered by the data, where value estimates are unreliable. Practical off-policy approaches in NLP often combine importance weighting, behavioral cloning regularization, or conservative value estimation to avoid catastrophic extrapolation.

A middle ground used in practice is to keep a replay buffer of recent trajectories and perform multiple updates per sampled batch (more efficient than pure on-policy), while limiting how far the policy can change between updates (trust-region or KL penalty) to maintain approximate on-policy behavior.


# Episodic vs. continuing tasks in text generation

Episodic and continuing tasks differ in whether interactions naturally terminate and whether returns are finite. In episodic tasks, there is a clear episode boundary: sequences have a start and an end, and rewards are associated with the episode. Text generation is typically modeled as episodic: an episode is the generation of a single utterance, answer, or document, ending when a special end-of-sequence token is emitted or when a maximum length is reached. The episodic formulation simplifies both modeling and evaluation since returns are finite and measured per episode.

Continuing tasks model ongoing interaction without terminal states, often with $\gamma<1$ to ensure finite returns. While continuing formulations are important in many control domains, they are less natural for single-turn sequence generation. They can arise in dialog systems where the agent and user interact indefinitely; dialog may be better modeled as continuing or as a sequence of episodes corresponding to turns or conversations. When using RL for whole conversations, the choice between episodic and continuing has implications for reward design and credit assignment across turns.

In episodic text generation, it is common for rewards to be only available at the episode end (sequence-level reward). This sparsity motivates techniques like reward shaping, reward-to-go, or auxiliary losses to provide denser learning signals. When reward is available at intermediate steps (for example, a per-token quality score or immediate penalties for violating constraints), we have access to denser supervision and can apply standard temporal-difference methods.

Discounting plays a role: even in episodic tasks we often set $\gamma=1$ because the episode is finite and we care about total sequence reward. Setting $\gamma<1$ can be used intentionally to emphasize early tokens or to ensure numerical stability, but it also changes the optimization objective. Practitioners must decide whether they want to treat long-term dependencies explicitly (higher $\gamma$) or prefer to bias toward earlier rewards (lower $\gamma$).


# Variance sources in sequence-level RL

Variance in gradient estimates is the dominant practical challenge in RL for sequence models. Multiple interacting sources contribute:

1. Reward sparsity and scale. When reward is given only at the end of a long sequence, the same return is used at many time steps (unless reward-to-go is used), and small stochastic variations in the trajectory cause large fluctuations in the gradient estimate. If rewards have large magnitude or wide variance, they multiply $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$ and increase gradient variance.

2. Stochastic policies and large action spaces. Language models often have vocabularies of tens of thousands of tokens. Sampling from a high-entropy distribution over many actions introduces variance. The gradient at each step is proportional to $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$, which can be noisy when $a_t$ is sampled from a broad distribution.

3. Long horizons. The gradient sums contributions across many time steps. Even with reward-to-go, credit assignment across long sequences magnifies variance because each decision can be only weakly correlated with final outcomes. Moreover, importance weights for off-policy corrections multiply across steps, causing exponential variance in trajectory length.

4. Model stochasticity and exploration. Exploration strategies (e.g., temperature, top-k sampling) change the distribution of generated sequences, causing variance in the observed rewards. This is necessary to discover better sequences but incurs additional noise in estimates.

5. Estimation of baselines/critics. When the baseline or value function is learned, its estimation error can introduce bias (if improperly used) and variance. Bootstrapped value estimates in actor-critic algorithms can propagate errors that increase the variance or instability of policy updates.

6. Reward noise. If the reward source itself is noisy—human raters with inter-annotator variability or stochastic automatic metrics—this noise appears directly in the learning signal.

Because gradient steps may be very small relative to the scale of stochastic fluctuations, high variance slows learning and can lead to instability where performance oscillates or degrades. Addressing these variance sources is therefore crucial to practical RL for LMs.


# Stability challenges in RL for LMs

Training large language models with RL introduces a collection of stability challenges that go beyond classical RL issues. First, the parameterization is high-dimensional: modern LMs have millions to billions of parameters, making optimization delicate. Small, noisy gradient steps can cause catastrophic forgetting of language fluency learned during supervised pretraining, producing degenerate output distributions (repetitions, incoherence). To avoid this, practitioners often include strong regularization toward the pre-trained model, for example via KL-penalties (constraining the new policy to stay close to the behavior policy) or trust-region steps.

Second, reward hacking and misalignment with human preferences are serious concerns. If the reward function is imperfect or allows trivial exploitation, the model may discover degenerate ways to maximize reward that do not correspond to genuinely better language. For example, when optimizing an n-gram overlap metric, the model might memorize references or generate templates that exploit the metric. Because RL directly optimizes the reward, even subtle mis-specifications can be amplified. Safeguards include careful reward design, human-in-the-loop evaluation, and incorporating constraints or auxiliary objectives.

Third, the credit assignment and high variance issues discussed previously can make learning unstable: large gradient estimates in one batch can push the policy far, changing the data distribution and invalidating previously learned value estimates or baselines. To mitigate this, algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) put implicit trust-region constraints by clipping probability ratios, limiting per-update policy drift. Other approaches use explicit KL penalties in the loss to keep updates near the pre-trained model. In language-model RL, such constraints are commonly used and often essential.

Fourth, off-policy or replay-based methods introduce distributional shift risks. When reusing past data, the model may be updated using trajectories that are no longer consistent with the current policy, producing biased gradients or encouraging the model to generate sequences it can no longer support. Conservative algorithms and careful importance sampling/weighting are required but add engineering complexity.

Fifth, computational cost and sample efficiency are practical stability factors. Obtaining rewards—especially human feedback—is expensive. Small sample sizes yield noisy gradients and overfitting to scarce reward examples. Combining RL with supervised fine-tuning, using mixed objectives (a weighted sum of RL loss and supervised negative log-likelihood), or using offline datasets to pretrain critics are common ways to mitigate sample inefficiency.

Finally, evaluation instability complicates development. Because RL objectives can lead to non-monotonic improvements on human-preference metrics, it is important to use held-out validation, human evaluation, and other diagnostics to detect regressions. Ablation and sanity checks (e.g., ensuring that KL remains bounded, monitoring language quality metrics) are necessary to maintain stable progress.

Taken together, these challenges motivate a pragmatic approach to RL for sequence generation: start from a strong pretrained policy, use conservative update procedures (KL penalties, small learning rates, clipped updates), apply baselines or critics to reduce variance, shape rewards carefully, and validate with robust evaluation. Understanding both the mathematical underpinnings and practical failure modes allows researchers and practitioners to design methods that balance optimization effectiveness with linguistic quality and safety.

These ideas underpin modern approaches such as policy-gradient fine-tuning, actor-critic methods, and sophisticated algorithms (PPO, TRPO, conservative offline RL) used to optimize language models against reward functions like human preference models.
