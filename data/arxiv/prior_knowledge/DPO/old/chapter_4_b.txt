# Fine-tuning vs. adapters/LoRA

After pretraining a base transformer model, we often want to adapt it to downstream tasks. Full parameter fine-tuning adjusts all model weights on task-specific data, which often yields strong performance, but it requires storing a full copy of model weights for each task and can be computationally expensive. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques are designed to retain most base parameters frozen while learning a small number of additional parameters per task, enabling efficient multi-task or multi-tenant deployments.

Adapters are one such technique: small bottleneck feed-forward modules are inserted between existing layers, and during task adaptation only the adapter parameters are trained while the base model stays frozen. An adapter module typically reduces the hidden dimension via a down-projection, applies a nonlinearity, then upsamples back, so it learns a compact transformation that can steer model behavior. Because adapters are small, they require little storage per task and often generalize well when multiple tasks share adapter layers.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is another widely used method. It freezes the original weight matrices and injects trainable low-rank matrices into specific linear transformations, typically the attention projection matrices. For a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, LoRA represents the update as $W' = W + \Delta W$ where $\Delta W = BA$ with $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ and $r \ll \min(d,k)$. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained. This restricts the adaptation to a low-rank subspace which is often sufficient for many tasks. LoRA is attractive because it integrates seamlessly into existing inference pipelines: the low-rank updates can be applied at load time or fused into computation, and they are parameter-efficient.

Prompt tuning and prefix tuning are alternative approaches that optimize input-space vectors that are prepended to the network's activations. In prompt tuning the learned vectors are embeddings seen by the input layer; in prefix tuning the learned vectors act as virtual past key/value pairs that the attention mechanism can attend to. These methods leave the base model frozen and only learn a small set of inputs, which can be very compact and effective for some tasks.

The choice between full fine-tuning and parameter-efficient methods depends on available compute, privacy/isolation requirements, storage constraints, and the degree of desired task specialization. For many practical deployments where many tasks or users need customized behavior, adapters or LoRA are more scalable.


# Mixed precision and distributed training

Training large transformer models requires huge amounts of computation and memory. Mixed-precision training and sophisticated distributed training strategies are two pillars that make large-scale training feasible.

Mixed-precision training uses lower-precision floating point formats (commonly IEEE FP16 or the NVIDIA-specific BF16) for forward and backward passes, while keeping a master copy of weights at higher precision (usually FP32) to maintain update fidelity. FP16 reduces memory bandwidth and storage and can accelerate matrix operations on GPUs and dedicated accelerators. However, FP16 has a narrower dynamic range and smaller exponent which can lead to underflow or overflow. BF16 preserves the same exponent range as FP32 but with reduced mantissa, offering improved stability without as many numerical issues. To maintain stable training in mixed precision, dynamic loss scaling is used: the loss is multiplied by a scale factor before backpropagation and then scaled back when updating, preventing gradients from underflowing to zero.

Distributed training encompasses data parallelism, tensor (model) parallelism, and pipeline parallelism. Data parallelism replicates the full model across multiple devices and splits mini-batches across them, aggregating gradients periodically. This is simple and scales until GPU memory limits are reached. Tensor parallelism splits large matrix multiplications across devices by slicing weight tensors (e.g., column or row parallelism) so that a single matrix multiply is distributed. Pipeline parallelism splits layers across devices and streams microbatches through the pipeline, which is effective at distributing deep models. Hybrid strategies combine these approaches to scale to billions or trillions of parameters. Communication overhead is a critical constraint: collective operations to synchronize gradients or exchange activations must be optimized and overlapped with computation to maintain high utilization.

Checkpointing (activation recomputation) trades compute for memory by recomputing activations during backpropagation rather than storing them, enabling deeper models to be trained within memory budgets. Sharded optimizer states reduce memory by synchronizing only necessary parameters and storing optimizer state across devices. All these engineering elements are crucial to scale large transformers efficiently and reproducibly.


# Inference efficiency and caching

At serving time, inference efficiency is essential, especially for autoregressive generation where tokens are produced sequentially. The naive autoregressive approach recomputes the entire forward pass for all previous tokens at each generation step, leading to an $O(n^2)$ complexity in time. Key/value caching addresses this by storing the keys and values computed for each past token from each attention layer. When generating token $t+1$, the model needs only to compute the query for the new token and attend to the cached keys and values, avoiding recomputing attention projections for the whole prefix. This reduces computation per token from $O(n d^2)$ to $O(n d)$ for the attention components, yielding substantial speedups in practice.

Caching requires memory proportional to the past context length times $d_k$ times the number of heads and layers. For very long contexts this can be expensive; implementations often employ compact storage formats, eviction strategies (sliding windows), or efficient offloading to CPU/NVMe. Efficient memory layout and fused kernels that compute attention with cached keys/values directly on device hardware are important to reduce overhead and latency.

Beam search, sampling strategies (top-k, nucleus sampling), and temperature control are algorithmic choices that affect quality and computational cost of generation. Batch decoding across multiple requests can improve throughput by amortizing per-step overheads, but if request lengths differ, batching introduces padding overhead. Techniques like speculative decoding attempt to use a smaller, faster model to propose token sequences and then verify them with the large model, gaining throughput at modest quality trade-offs.

Quantization reduces memory and compute by representing weights and/or activations with fewer bits (e.g., INT8, INT4). Quantized inference can greatly reduce model size and speed up inference when supported by hardware. However, quantization often requires calibration or fine-tuning to maintain model quality, and some layers are more sensitive to low-precision representations than others.

Finally, architecture-level choices also impact inference efficiency. Smaller head counts with larger head dimension, fused layer implementations, and specialized attention patterns can be chosen to match hardware characteristics and latency constraints. The overall system is an interplay between model architecture, numerical representation, caching, parallelism, and decoding algorithms.


# Conclusion

Transformer architectures and their training and deployment practices form a complex ecosystem. Self-attention and multi-head attention provide flexible mechanisms for modeling interactions, while positional encodings and masking impose sequence structure. The choice between decoder-only and encoder–decoder models drives training objectives and applicability. Stability in training large transformers depends on careful initialization, normalization, and optimizer choices. Scaling introduces trade-offs between performance and compute or memory cost, motivating many efficiency techniques. When adapting pretrained models, parameter-efficient methods like adapters and LoRA offer strong practical benefits. Mixed-precision and distributed training techniques are essential for scaling, and inference efficiency hinges on caching, quantization, and decoding strategies. Mastering these essentials gives you the tools to understand, evaluate, and work with the transformer-based language models that are central to contemporary research and alignment pipelines.
