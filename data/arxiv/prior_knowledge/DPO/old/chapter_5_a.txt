Chapter 5: Supervised Fine-Tuning and Instruction Tuning

Introduction

Large pre-trained language models (LMs) acquire broad linguistic and factual knowledge during unsupervised pre-training on massive corpora. However, to be useful for downstream applications and to reliably follow human instructions, these models need further adaptation. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and instruction tuning are two closely related approaches for adapting pre-trained LMs so they better follow task specifications, produce higher-quality outputs, and behave more predictably. This chapter describes how instruction and demonstration datasets are curated, the objectives used during SFT, common pitfalls and regularization techniques, and how SFT relates to alignment methods that use human preferences. We close by discussing how to construct a reference policy from SFT and how to evaluate SFT models. The emphasis is on the intuition behind each idea and on practical considerations that matter when reading or conducting research in this area.


# Curating instruction and demonstration datasets

Instruction and demonstration datasets are the raw materials for instruction tuning and supervised fine-tuning. The goal is to produce pairs of inputs and desired outputs (often called prompts and responses, or contexts and targets) that teach the model how to perform tasks in a way that aligns with user expectations. Curation involves several steps: selecting tasks, choosing or designing prompt templates, sourcing or generating ground-truth outputs, and partitioning data into training, validation, and test splits.

Task selection should cover a broad spectrum of capabilities you want the LM to handle: question answering, summarization, translation, classification, code generation, instruction-following, and multimodal tasks if supported. The breadth of tasks influences the model's ability to generalize to unseen instructions. If the dataset focuses narrowly on a few tasks, the model will become stronger at those but may overfit to the format or style of those tasks and struggle with new ones. Conversely, a diverse collection encourages learning higher-level instruction-following behaviors.

Prompt engineering or template design determines how tasks are presented. A standard structure includes an instruction (what to do), optional context (e.g., documents to summarize), and examples (demonstrations) that show the desired format. Demonstrations can be zero-shot (none provided), few-shot (a few examples included in the prompt), or chain-of-thought style (explanatory intermediate steps). The formatting choices—how instructions are worded, how examples are separated—impact model learning because the model internalizes both content and style.

Ground-truth outputs can be sourced from human annotators, expert-created solutions, public datasets, or synthetic generation. Human-written responses are often higher quality but expensive. When using synthetic or model-generated outputs, careful filtering and quality checks are essential to avoid teaching undesirable behaviors. For some tasks, it is crucial to capture multiple valid outputs (e.g., many possible summaries or translations), and datasets should represent this variability.

Finally, dataset partitioning and metadata management are important. Holdout sets should include tasks and phrasing variations not present in training to assess generalization. Metadata such as task type, instruction complexity, and whether an example includes chain-of-thought can help analyze model behavior. Good curation practices also track provenance and licensing to avoid legal or ethical issues.


# Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) objectives and pitfalls

Supervised fine-tuning adapts a pre-trained LM by minimizing a loss on a set of input-output pairs. The canonical objective is maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) conditioned on the input: if the dataset contains pairs $(x,y)$ where $x$ is the prompt and $y$ the target text, the loss is the negative log-likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim \mathcal{D}} \log p_\theta(y \mid x),
\]
where $p_\theta$ denotes the LM parameterized by $\theta$ and $y$ is usually tokenized as a sequence. Training minimizes this loss with gradient-based optimizers and typical LM training techniques.

Although MLE is straightforward and effective, there are pitfalls. First, distribution mismatch can arise: the training distribution of instructions and responses might differ significantly from the distribution of real user inputs. Models trained by SFT therefore may give brittle or overconfident outputs for out-of-distribution prompts. Second, label noise and ambiguous targets create problems. For many open-ended tasks, there are multiple acceptable outputs; training on a single reference teaches the model to mimic that particular style and may penalize other legitimate answers. This is particularly problematic when the dataset includes low-quality or biased target texts; the model will internalize those flaws.

Another pitfall is exposure bias. During training, the model conditions on ground-truth prefixes; at inference, it conditions on its own previous tokens, which can compound errors. While techniques like scheduled sampling attempt to bridge this gap, they have limited success for large LMs. Furthermore, SFT can cause the model to unlearn capabilities acquired during pre-training if the fine-tuning dataset is small or narrow. This catastrophic forgetting happens because gradients from SFT can overwrite weights important for the pre-training objectives.

A related issue is calibration. Minimizing MLE alone does not guarantee that the model's probabilities reflect true uncertainty; fine-tuned models can become overconfident. For tasks where uncertainty quantification or rejecting to answer is important, additional objectives or calibration steps may be needed.

To mitigate these pitfalls, practitioners combine careful dataset curation (high-quality, diverse examples), regularization methods (discussed below), and validation on held-out, diverse prompts. Additionally, treating SFT as part of a larger pipeline—e.g., followed by preference learning or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)—can address some limitations of pure supervised losses.


# Instruction following and task generalization

Instruction following refers to a model's ability to interpret natural language instructions and produce outputs that satisfy them. Task generalization refers to performing well on tasks not explicitly seen during training or on novel phrasings of known tasks. These capabilities are central goals of instruction tuning.

There are two complementary routes to improve instruction following. One is to expose the model to many instructions with various wording, contexts, and desired outputs. This teaches the model a mapping from natural language directives to output behaviors, helping it generalize to instructions whose semantics are similar but whose surface form differs. The other route is to include meta-instructions and higher-level tasks, such as "explain step-by-step how to solve X" or "convert the following example into this formal representation." Training on tasks that require reasoning, decomposition, or self-explanation encourages the model to internalize procedures rather than rote mappings.

Task generalization is influenced by the diversity and distribution of training tasks. If the model sees many task families and a variety of input distributions, it is more likely to learn underlying abstractions like 'summarize', 'compare', or 'translate' rather than memorize templates. Prompt format variety helps too; presenting the same task in multiple ways (different phrasings, different instruction tokens, or with/without examples) prevents overfitting to a single template.

A key concept is compositional generalization: the model's ability to recombine known operations in new ways. For instance, if the model has been trained to "translate" and to "summarize," can it perform "summarize then translate" when asked? Achieving compositional generalization remains challenging, but including examples that demonstrate composition and training on intermediate reasoning steps (chain-of-thought) can help.

Evaluation of instruction following and generalization should go beyond test sets that mirror training. Cross-domain and few-shot evaluations, held-out instruction templates, and human assessments are necessary to understand real-world behavior. Researchers sometimes create synthetic stress tests that measure sensitivity to negations, paraphrases, or adversarial instructions to probe robustness.


# Data quality, diversity, and contamination

Data quality and diversity are the primary determinants of how well SFT will teach desired behaviors. High-quality examples: clear, correct, and appropriately detailed responses; accurate labels for classification or structured tasks; and examples that reflect the intended style and safety constraints. Low-quality data—noisy labels, inconsistent or conflicting instructions, biased or unethical content—transmits errors to the model.

Diversity ensures that the model doesn't specialize to a narrow set of inputs. Diversity has many dimensions: task types, linguistic styles, user intent granularity (short vs. verbose instructions), cultural perspectives, and domain coverage. Including diverse examples reduces spurious correlations between surface features of prompts and outputs, improving robustness.

Data contamination is a serious practical concern. When the pre-training corpus or the fine-tuning set contains examples from the test sets used to evaluate the model, performance estimates become overly optimistic. For instruction tuning, contamination can occur if the fine-tuning set includes data that is later used as evaluation prompts, or if pre-training data includes the test references. Preventing contamination requires careful deduplication, provenance tracking, and filtering by similarity. Techniques like near-duplicate detection (using n-gram overlap, embedding similarity, or checksums) help identify potential leaks.

Beyond direct contamination, there is subtle contamination from model-generated data. When synthetic datasets are created by prompting models and that synthetic data is then used to fine-tune the same or similar models, the system can amplify existing biases or errors and obscure the model's true capabilities. If such synthetic data is included in training without rigorous human review, it can degrade performance or create overfitting to model idiosyncrasies.

Finally, ethical and legal considerations are tied to data quality and contamination. Respecting data licenses, removing personally identifiable information, and avoiding proprietary or sensitive sources are essential. Annotator guidelines and quality control measures—such as gold-standard checks, inter-annotator agreement, and iterative feedback loops—help maintain high data standards.


# Overfitting and regularization during SFT

Overfitting is the model's tendency to fit the training examples too closely at the expense of generalization. In SFT, overfitting manifests as rote memorization of training outputs, loss of pre-trained capabilities, or brittle behavior on slight prompt variations. Because modern LMs are overparameterized, they are prone to overfitting when the fine-tuning dataset is small, narrow, or noisy.

Regularization strategies help prevent overfitting. A simple approach is early stopping based on held-out validation loss or, better, task-specific validation metrics that reflect desired behaviors. Weight decay and dropout can be applied, but their benefits vary with model size and optimizer choices. Data augmentation—creating paraphrases of instructions, altering formatting, or adding noisy variants—expands the effective training distribution and promotes robustness.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods are another powerful regularization technique. Instead of updating all model parameters, approaches like adapters, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), or prefix-tuning update a small subset of parameters or additional low-dimensional modules. These methods reduce the capacity available for overfitting and help retain pre-trained behaviors, making catastrophic forgetting less likely. They also enable economical multi-tasking where different adapters handle different tasks.

Another useful regularizer is Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to the pre-trained model or to a reference policy. In other words, add a penalty term of the form
\[
\lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}} \, \text{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big),
\]
where $p_{\text{ref}}$ might be the original pre-trained model. This discourages the fine-tuned model from drifting far from the reference distribution and helps preserve pre-trained knowledge. Careful tuning of $\lambda$ is important: too large and the model cannot learn new behaviors; too small and you risk forgetting.

Finally, ensembling and model selection can mitigate overfitting. Training multiple SFT checkpoints with different random seeds or hyperparameters and selecting models by validation on diverse held-out tasks increases the chance of robust generalization. Monitoring not only loss but also response quality indicators (e.g., factuality, harmfulness) on validation helps detect overfitting that is not captured by likelihood alone.


# Alignment via demonstrations vs. preferences

Alignment refers to the process of aligning model behavior with human values, safety constraints, or desired norms. Two major strategies are alignment via demonstrations (supervised teaching) and alignment via preferences (learning from human comparisons). Understanding their differences and how they complement each other is crucial.

Alignment via demonstrations uses explicit examples of desired behavior. Annotators provide high-quality outputs for prompts; the model is trained to imitate these. This approach is straightforward, interpretable, and efficient when clear examples exist. Demonstrations are excellent at teaching format, style, and concrete task procedures. However, demonstrations have limitations: they can be expensive for complex or subjective tasks, they often reflect annotator-specific biases, and they struggle when there's no single "correct" answer or when trade-offs between competing values are required.

Alignment via preferences, commonly implemented through preference modeling and RLHF, uses pairwise human judgments between model outputs rather than absolute demonstrations. Annotators see two or more outputs generated by models and indicate which they prefer. The preference model learns a reward function $r_\phi$ that predicts human preference and is then used to optimize the LM (e.g., via reinforcement learning or direct policy optimization). Preference-based alignment captures comparative judgments that are easier for humans to state than to exemplify, and it can aggregate nuanced human values, such as balancing helpfulness against safety.

The methods differ in signal type: demonstrations provide a dense, supervised signal mapping prompts to desired texts, while preferences provide relative signals indicating which candidate is better. Demonstrations yield a direct policy to imitate, making them suitable as an initial training step that shapes style and basic behavior. Preferences are powerful for fine-grained alignment—especially when considering trade-offs, avoiding undesired behaviors, or when one wants to steer a model toward a spectrum of acceptable outputs rather than a single canonical answer.

Practically, pipelines often combine both: SFT with demonstrations teaches the model a good initial policy and reduces the space of plausible outputs, then preference learning refines this policy to better match human judgments on subtle aspects such as tone, safety, and factuality. This staged approach leverages the strengths of each method and helps control variance and instability that can arise when optimizing directly with preference-based objectives.
