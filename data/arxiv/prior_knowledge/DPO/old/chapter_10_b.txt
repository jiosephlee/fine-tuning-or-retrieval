# Partition function and log-normalizer interpretation

The normalization constant \(Z(x)\) appearing in the closed-form policy
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)}{Z(x)}
\quad\text{with}\quad
Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)
\]
is often called the partition function in analogy with statistical physics and the normalization constant in probability models. Its logarithm,
\[
\log Z(x) = \log \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta},
\]
plays an important interpretive role: it is the log-normalizer of the policy family and also equals the optimal value of the objective in the inner maximization (up to a multiplicative \(\beta\)). To see this, substitute \(\pi^*\) into the Lagrangian objective. A short calculation yields the maximal objective value
\[
\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Thus \(V(x) := \beta\log Z(x)\) can be interpreted as a soft value function: it captures, in a single scalar, the expected reward one can attain from state \(x\) when allowed to randomize subject to KL regularization with temperature \(\beta\). In the multi-step case, the same log-normalizer arises but with Q-values replacing immediate rewards, so the recursive soft Bellman equation becomes
\[
V(x) = \beta \log \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}Q(x,y)\big),
\]
and the corresponding soft Q-function satisfies
\[
Q(x,y) = r(x,y) + \gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{x'\sim P(\cdot\mid x,y)}[V(x')].
\]
This set of equations replaces the hard max in standard Bellman backups by a smooth log-sum-exp, which is numerically and analytically advantageous.

Because \(\log Z(x)\) equals the cumulant-generating function of the reward under \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) scaled by \(1/\beta\), it inherits convexity properties and well-known bounds. The partition function is intimately linked to free energy in physics and to the (negative) cumulant generating function in probability theory. It determines normalization and also appears in gradients: the gradient of \(\log Z(x)\) with respect to parameters embedded in rewards or the reference policy yields expectations of sufficient statistics under the optimal policy. This is why maximum-likelihood-style or policy-gradient methods that use the soft value function produce stable and informative gradients.


# Energy-based view of policies and rewards

The closed-form policy can be written in the language of energy-based models. Define an energy function
\[
E(x,y) := -\big( \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y) \big).
\]
Then the optimal policy is a Gibbs distribution with energies \(E(x,y)\):
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{e^{-E(x,y)}}{\sum_{y'} e^{-E(x,y')}}.
\]
This perspective highlights that rewards and priors combine additively in the energy: high reward reduces energy and increases probability, while a high prior probability \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) also reduces energy. Viewing policies as energy-based models clarifies connections to probabilistic modeling: the optimal policy is exactly the normalized Boltzmann distribution corresponding to an energy that mixes prior log-probability and scaled negative reward.

Interpreting rewards as negative energies gives a unifying intuition. In classical RL the agent seeks to maximize reward; in the energy-based view, it seeks to minimize energy. Exponentiating a scaled reward corresponds to preferring low-energy configurations. This analogy becomes particularly tight in the control-as-inference framework, where the reward is treated as a log-likelihood of optimality, and the posterior distribution over trajectories conditioned on optimality is a Boltzmann distribution over reward-weighted trajectories.

The energy perspective also makes it natural to import tools from undirected graphical models and thermodynamics. For example, one can study the landscape of \(E(x,y)\), modes of the Gibbs distribution, and partition-function approximations. It also clarifies how combining multiple signals — e.g., an expert prior and a learned reward — simply sums the corresponding energies.


# Role of \(\beta\) in exploration–exploitation trade-offs

The parameter \(\beta\) (temperature) plays a central role in shaping the behavior of the policy by governing how strongly reward differences influence probability mass. At a high level, \(\beta\) controls the exploration–exploitation trade-off: how much the agent favors high-reward actions (exploitation) versus maintaining diversity (exploration).

When \(\beta\) is very small, the term \(\exp(r/\beta)\) magnifies reward differences and the policy concentrates mass on the action(s) with highest reward (or highest Q-value in multi-step problems). This yields behavior that is nearly deterministic and focused on exploitation. As \(\beta \to 0\), the softmax collapses toward a point mass at the argmax of the exponent. On the other hand, when \(\beta\) is large, the exponent flattens and the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) dominates, producing near-random behavior and heavy exploration.

The exact numerical effect of \(\beta\) depends on the scale of rewards. If rewards are large in magnitude relative to \(\beta\), small relative differences are amplified; conversely, rescaling rewards is equivalent to rescaling \(\beta\). This dependence motivates careful tuning or adaptive schemes for \(\beta\). Practical algorithms sometimes treat \(\beta\) as a learnable parameter, tuning it to maintain a target entropy level. For example, in Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an automatic entropy-tuning procedure adjusts a temperature parameter to keep the policy entropy near a desired set point; this trades principled control of exploration for empirical stability.

From a theoretical standpoint, \(\beta\) also controls convergence and value-smoothing properties. Soft Bellman operators with higher \(\beta\) are closer to averaging (smoother), which can lead to smaller variance in estimates but slower concentration on the true optimal policy. Conversely, low \(\beta\) gives operators close to hard maxes, which can be more sample-inefficient and numerically unstable. Thus \(\beta\) must be chosen to balance stability, sample complexity, and the intended degree of stochasticity in the final policy.


# Trust-region interpretations and stability

KL constraints and the associated Lagrangian form yield a principled trust-region interpretation of policy updates and thereby enhance stability. In iterative policy optimization, a common failure mode is that aggressive updates to policy parameters cause large distributional shifts in behavior, rendering value estimates stale and causing performance collapse. By limiting the KL divergence between successive policies, one enforces that the new policy remains close to the old, ensuring that estimates built from previous data remain approximately valid.

Consider an iterative scheme that at iteration \(k\) seeks a new policy \(\pi_{k+1}\) by solving
\[
\max_{\pi} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d_k,\,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d_k}\big[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_k(\cdot\mid x))\big] \le \epsilon,
\]
where \(d_k\) is the state visitation distribution under the current policy. This mirrors trust-region approaches in optimization: restrict the update to a ball (here, a KL ball) around the current iterate. The solution has the same Boltzmann form
\[
\pi_{k+1}(y\mid x) \propto \pi_k(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\big),
\]
so the new policy is explicitly a reweighting of the old. By controlling \(\epsilon\) (or equivalently the dual parameter \(\beta\)), one can ensure that policy change between iterations is limited.

Trust-region policy optimization (TRPO) and proximal policy optimization (PPO) implement this idea with different approximations and practical algorithms. TRPO enforces a hard KL constraint solved approximately via conjugate-gradient-based updates, while PPO uses clipped surrogate losses as a computationally cheaper proxy. Both aim to ensure monotonic improvement or at least to reduce the likelihood of catastrophic changes. In many modern actor-critic methods, adding KL regularization or entropy bonuses stabilizes learning by tempering the magnitude of policy updates and smoothing gradients.

Beyond local stability, KL regularization confers robustness: constraining divergence from a prior or demonstration policy ensures that the agent does not stray too far into unsafe or poorly-sampled regions. In safety-critical settings, a fixed reference policy that encodes baseline safe behavior, together with a small KL budget, provides a formal way to keep the learned policy within acceptable bounds.


# Connections to maximum entropy IRL

Maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning (MaxEnt IRL) frames the problem of recovering a reward function from expert behavior as probabilistic inference: assume the expert acts stochastically according to a maximum-entropy distribution over trajectories weighted by cumulative reward. Concretely, MaxEnt IRL posits that the distribution over trajectories \( \tau = (x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1,\dots) \) produced by the expert is
\[
p(\tau) \propto \exp\!\big( \tfrac{1}{\beta} R(\tau) \big),
\]
where \(R(\tau)\) is the cumulative reward of the trajectory and \(\beta\) is an inverse temperature. The maximum entropy principle ensures that among all trajectory distributions that match observed feature expectations, the chosen distribution has maximal entropy. Learning the reward function is then done by maximizing the likelihood of expert trajectories under this model, which yields gradient updates involving expectations under the model and under the expert.

The entropy-regularized RL framework is tightly connected: the optimal policy for a known reward under maximum-entropy control is exactly the Boltzmann policy described earlier, and the partition function over trajectories plays the role of the normalizer for the expert distribution. From the IRL perspective, policy optimization with entropy or KL regularization corresponds to fitting a distribution over trajectories that both attains high reward and remains broad (high entropy), mirroring the MaxEnt IRL objective. Conversely, MaxEnt IRL can be seen as performing maximum likelihood estimation in a family of entropy-regularized control distributions.

This connection yields practical algorithms and insights. For instance, when performing behavior cloning with a KL prior, or when using demonstrations to guide RL via a reference policy, one can interpret the learning as shaping the energy (negative reward plus prior) so that the posterior over trajectories matches demonstrations. Similarly, the duality between control and inference implies that techniques from probabilistic inference—such as importance sampling, variational approximations, and message passing—can be applied to control problems and to IRL.

The MaxEnt viewpoint also clarifies ambiguity in reward recovery: because the Boltzmann distribution only pins down reward up to additive and scale constants (which can be absorbed into \(\beta\)), IRL problems are inherently ill-posed unless regularization or additional constraints are imposed. Recognizing this helps in designing algorithms that regularize rewards or jointly learn temperature parameters.

Conclusion

Entropy and KL regularization enrich reinforcement learning by producing probabilistic optimal policies with closed-form Gibbs structure, stabilizing learning through trust-region behavior, and connecting control to probabilistic inference. The temperature parameter \( \beta \) governs the exploration–exploitation trade-off and controls how sharply rewards shape the policy. The partition function and its log emerge as soft value functions, and the energy-based view ties rewards and priors into a single energy landscape. Finally, the control-as-inference perspective links these ideas to maximum entropy IRL and opens the door to applying probabilistic inference techniques to control problems. Understanding these connections equips you to read and design algorithms that exploit entropy and KL regularization for robust, stable, and principled RL.
