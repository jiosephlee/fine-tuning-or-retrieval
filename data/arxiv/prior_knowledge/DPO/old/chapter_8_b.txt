# Evaluation of reward models (AUC, correlation with humans)

Evaluating reward models requires complementary metrics because no single number captures ranking quality, calibration, or practical usefulness in RL. The most common metrics include pairwise accuracy (fraction of correctly predicted preferences), area under the ROC curve (AUC), and rank correlation coefficients (Spearman's rho, Kendall's tau) between model scores and human ordinal ratings.

Pairwise accuracy is intuitive: given held-out pairs, compute the fraction where $\sigma(\Delta r) > 0.5$ matches the human label. AUC generalizes this by measuring the probability the model ranks a randomly chosen positive pair above a negative one; it is equivalent to pairwise accuracy under balanced datasets but handles class imbalance gracefully. AUC is useful because it focuses on ordering independent of a specific threshold.

Rank correlation metrics such as Spearman's rho measure monotonic agreement between model scores and human-elicited continuous ratings (for example, when humans assign Likert-scale scores). Pearson correlation can be useful when absolute magnitudes are meaningful, though it is sensitive to scale and outliers. Kendall's tau has an intuitive interpretation as the fraction of concordant pairs minus discordant pairs and is robust to small sample sizes.

Calibration metrics matter if you intend to use predicted probabilities. Brier score measures mean squared error between predicted probabilities and binary outcomes. Expected calibration error (ECE) bins predicted probabilities and compares empirical frequencies; reliability diagrams visualize calibration.

Beyond static metrics, practical evaluation often involves downstream tests: plug the reward model into RL to train a policy and see whether human raters prefer the resulting behavior. This is the ultimate test of utility: a reward model that ranks well may still produce bad policies if it incentivizes undesirable shortcuts. Robustness evaluation includes testing on out-of-distribution model-generated outputs, adversarially constructed inputs, and counterfactuals to reveal failure modes.

Finally, human-model agreement should be measured not only in aggregate but also stratified by context difficulty, annotator agreement, and by classes of failure (e.g., factual mistakes vs. style issues). This analysis reveals where the model generalizes and where more data or different modeling choices are needed.


# Limitations and under-specification

Reward modeling from preferences faces fundamental limitations rooted in under-specification: many reward functions can explain the same preference data. This ambiguity arises because pairwise comparisons only constrain relative orderings among observed items, leaving behavior on unobserved inputs unconstrained. Consequently, learned reward models can extrapolate in undesirable ways, and policies optimized against them can exploit these extrapolations.

One manifestation is reward hacking: the policy discovers inputs or strategies that receive high predicted reward despite being misaligned with human intent. For example, if annotators equate longer responses with higher quality in the dataset, the reward model may prefer verbosity, and the policy will generate long but unhelpful outputs. Another issue is spurious correlates: the model may latch onto superficial cues that correlate with human choices in the dataset (such as punctuation patterns or canned phrases) rather than the intended semantic properties.

Annotator inconsistency compounds these problems. Humans have noisy and sometimes contradictory preferences; aggregated labels hide subtleties and conditional preferences (e.g., preferences that depend on context or task). Modeling per-annotator variability or collecting richer annotations (pairwise with free-text rationales, graded ratings, or conditional preferences) can reduce under-specification but cannot eliminate it.

There are also distributional shift concerns. Reward models trained on human-written examples may poorly evaluate model-generated outputs, which can occupy different regions of input space. Iterated approaches—where the policy generates candidates, humans compare them, and the reward model is retrained—help align the reward model to the policy's distribution. Nevertheless, this requires careful design to avoid feedback loops that amplify bias.

Finally, even a perfectly learned reward model (with zero error on held-out pairs) may still be insufficient: human preferences can be multi-dimensional and incommensurable, and scalar rewards compress complex desiderata into a single axis. Some alignment approaches consider richer feedback (hierarchical preferences, constraints, or multi-objective formulations) to mitigate this compression.

Understanding these limitations is crucial when reading research: claims that "we learn a reward that matches human preferences" must be contextualized by the data, the distribution, and the downstream usage.

Conclusion

Reward modeling from preferences provides a practical route to capture human judgments without hand-engineering reward functions. The formulation as latent score functions, trained with binary cross-entropy or related ranking losses, is mathematically simple yet practically powerful. However, training robust, well-calibrated reward models requires attention to normalization, architecture, noise robustness, and iterative data collection to cover the policy's distribution. Evaluation must consider ranking performance, calibration, and downstream effects. Above all, the under-specification and brittleness of scalar rewards mean that care is needed when using reward models to optimize behavior: continuous human oversight, rich data collection, and awareness of failure modes remain essential.
