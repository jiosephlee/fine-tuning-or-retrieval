Introduction

Probability and information theory form the mathematical backbone for reasoning about modern machine learning systems, including language models and the procedures used to align them to human preferences such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). This chapter develops the core concepts that you will repeatedly encounter in papers about model training, evaluation, divergence minimization, and reward modeling. We begin with the elementary objects—random variables and expectations—and build up to information-theoretic quantities such as entropy, cross-entropy, and Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. Along the way we introduce practical tools and numerical tricks—most importantly the log-sum-exp trick and an explanation of partition functions—that are crucial when implementing algorithms for real-world neural networks. We also present important inequalities, especially Jensen’s inequality, which underlie many proofs and intuitions about convexity and bounds used in optimization and variational inference.


# Random variables, expectations, and log-likelihoods

A random variable is a mathematical object that assigns a real value to every outcome in a probability experiment. We typically denote random variables by uppercase letters such as $X$ or $Y$. If $X$ takes values in a discrete set $\mathcal{X}$, its distribution is described by the probability mass function $p_X(x)=\Pr(X=x)$. If $X$ is continuous, its distribution is described by a probability density function $p_X(x)$ with respect to some base measure (usually Lebesgue measure), and probabilities of events are integrals of the density.

The expectation (or mean) of a function $f(X)$ of a random variable is the average value of $f$ under the distribution of $X$. For a discrete variable,
$\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} f(x) p_X(x)$,
and for a continuous variable,
$\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int f(x) p_X(x)\,dx$.
Expectations are linear: $\mathbb{E}[a f(X) + b g(X)] = a\mathbb{E}[f(X)] + b\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$. Many performance measures in machine learning are written as expectations, because learning problems boil down to minimizing expected loss or maximizing expected reward.

Log-likelihoods arise when we treat observed data as drawn from a parameterized distribution and ask how well different parameters explain the data. Suppose we have data points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ assumed i.i.d. from a model $p_\theta(x)$. The likelihood of the dataset is $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(x_i)$. Working with products is numerically inconvenient and analytically awkward, so we usually use the log-likelihood,
$\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i)$.
Maximizing the log-likelihood is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood. For large models such as language models, the objective used in training is the average negative log-likelihood per token, which is exactly the cross-entropy between the data distribution and the model (this link is covered in the next section). The log-likelihood is also central in gradient calculations because gradients of $\log p_\theta(x)$ are often easier and more numerically stable than gradients of $p_\theta(x)$ directly. In probabilistic inference or variational methods, expectations of log-likelihood terms appear frequently; for example, variational evidence lower bounds (ELBOs) contain terms like $\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]$.

Intuitively, the log-likelihood gives a measure on the log scale of how probable the model considers the observed data. If the model assigns tiny probability to an observed event, that event contributes a large negative amount to the log-likelihood. This is why likelihood-based training naturally penalizes models that place low probability mass on observed data.


# Cross-entropy and binary cross-entropy losses

Cross-entropy is an information-theoretic measure of how well one probability distribution predicts another. Given two discrete distributions $p$ (the true distribution) and $q$ (the model distribution) over the same set $\mathcal{X}$, the cross-entropy of $p$ relative to $q$ is defined as
$H(p, q) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} p(x) \log q(x)$.
This expectation can be read as the expected negative log-probability that $q$ assigns to samples drawn from $p$. Cross-entropy is the objective minimized by maximum likelihood estimation when the training data are sampled from $p$: minimizing $H(p,q)$ with respect to $q$ is equivalent to maximizing the expected log-likelihood $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\log q(x)]$.

Cross-entropy decomposes into the true entropy $H(p) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)$ plus the KL divergence from $p$ to $q$:
$H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$.
Because $H(p)$ is fixed with respect to $q$, minimizing cross-entropy over $q$ is equivalent to minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$.

In supervised classification with $K$ classes, with one-hot labels representing the empirical distribution $p$, the cross-entropy loss on a single example with true class $y$ is $-\log q(y)$, where $q$ are the predicted class probabilities. This generalizes to minibatches and datasets by averaging.

Binary cross-entropy is the special case for binary outcomes. If $Y\in\{0,1\}$ and $p$ denotes the true probability $\Pr(Y=1)$ while $q$ denotes the model's predicted probability for $Y=1$, the cross-entropy becomes
$H(p,q) = -p\log q - (1-p)\log(1-q)$.
When labels are deterministic (either 0 or 1), this reduces to the per-example binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss: for a single example with label $y\in\{0,1\}$,
$\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(q; y) = -y\log q - (1-y)\log(1-q)$.
BCE is widely used for training binary classifiers and is numerically stable when combined with logits and the log-sum-exp trick (covered later). In RLHF, BCE can appear when training reward models or preference predictors that output the probability that one trajectory is preferred over another.

Cross-entropy is not symmetric in its two arguments and depends on whether the empirical distribution or the model distribution sits in the $p$ or $q$ slot; this asymmetry matters when we compare different optimization strategies (for example, forward vs. reverse KL).


# Logistic (sigmoid) and logit functions

The logistic function, or sigmoid, maps real numbers to the interval $(0,1)$ and is the canonical function for turning an unbounded real-valued model output into a probability for binary outcomes. The logistic function $\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)$ is defined by
$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$.
As $z\to\infty$, $\sigma(z)\to 1$, and as $z\to -\infty$, $\sigma(z)\to 0$. The logistic function is smooth and has derivative $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, which peaks at $z=0$ where $\sigma(0)=1/2$.

The logit function is the inverse of the logistic; it maps probabilities in $(0,1)$ back to the real line:
$\operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$.
If a model produces a real-valued score $s$, we often interpret $\sigma(s)$ as the predicted probability of class 1, and $s=\operatorname{logit}(\sigma(s))$ is called the log-odds. Working in logit space is useful numerically because it avoids saturating probabilities and yields gradients that can be simpler to manipulate.

In training, one often works with logit outputs directly and combines the logit with an appropriate loss (e.g., BCE) in a numerically stable way. For instance, the binary cross-entropy loss for label $y\in\{0,1\}$ and logit $s$ can be written as
$\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s;y) = \log\big(1 + e^{-s}\big) + (1-y) s$,
which is algebraically equivalent to $-y\log\sigma(s) - (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(s))$ but avoids explicitly computing $\sigma(s)$ and then taking logs in unstable ranges. Using logits and the alternative algebraic forms prevents catastrophic cancellation and overflow in implementations.

Intuitively, logits represent the model’s raw preference for class 1 relative to class 0. A large positive logit means the model strongly favors class 1; a large negative logit means it strongly favors class 0. Because the logistic function compresses the whole real line into $(0,1)$, finite changes in the logit near zero produce larger changes in probability than equal-sized changes far in the tails. This property influences optimization dynamics; for example, pushing logits further away from zero when they are already large yields diminishing improvements in cross-entropy.


# KL divergence (forward vs. reverse) and properties

The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence measures how one probability distribution diverges from another. For discrete distributions $p$ and $q$ on $\mathcal{X}$, the KL divergence from $p$ to $q$ (often called forward KL) is
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} p(x) \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$.
For continuous distributions, the sum is replaced by an integral. KL divergence is always nonnegative and equals zero if and only if $p=q$ almost everywhere. KL is not symmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ in general. This asymmetry is crucial in practice because minimizing one direction leads to different behaviors than minimizing the other.

Forward KL, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$, penalizes $q$ when it puts low probability mass where $p$ has significant mass. Intuitively, minimizing forward KL encourages $q$ to cover the support of $p$ and to place probability mass wherever $p$ does. This is why forward KL is often described as "mass-covering": if $p$ is multimodal but $q$ is constrained to be unimodal, minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ will push $q$ towards regions that cover many modes to avoid assigning near-zero probability to any region where $p$ places mass.

Reverse KL, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$, behaves differently. It penalizes $q$ when $q$ puts mass where $p$ has little or no mass. Minimizing reverse KL leads $q$ to place mass on modes of $p$ while possibly ignoring other modes—this is often called "mode-seeking" behavior. If $q$ is unimodal and $p$ is multimodal, minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ will typically cause $q$ to collapse onto a single dominant mode of $p$, since putting mass on modes with low $p$-probability is expensive. Reverse KL is commonly used in variational inference because it yields tractable optimization in many cases, but it can underestimate uncertainty by collapsing modes.

Mathematically, the KL divergence is an expectation of the log-density ratio:
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]$.
This form highlights its meaning as the excess code length (in nats if natural logs are used) incurred by encoding samples from $p$ using a code optimized for $q$ rather than a code optimized for $p$.

Some important properties: KL divergence is lower-bounded by zero (Gibbs' inequality), it is additive for independent components (if $p(x,y)=p_x(x)p_y(y)$ and $q$ factorizes similarly, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_x\|q_x)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_y\|q_y)$), and it satisfies a data-processing inequality that loosely states that applying the same stochastic transformation to both distributions cannot increase the divergence. However, KL is not a metric because it is asymmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality.

In the context of alignment and RLHF, which divergence direction to minimize matters. When distilling a model or matching a reward-weighted policy to a prior policy, minimizing forward KL encourages capturing the full distribution induced by human preferences, while minimizing reverse KL may lead to conservative, peaked solutions that focus on the most likely preference mode. Papers will often choose one based on computational tractability and the desired qualitative behavior (coverage vs. mode-seeking).
