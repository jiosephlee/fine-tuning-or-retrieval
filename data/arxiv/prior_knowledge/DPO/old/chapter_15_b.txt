# A/B testing and statistical significance

When deploying or comparing models, A/B testing is the standard experimental method. A/B testing measures user preference or engagement in an online environment by randomizing users (or prompts) to versions A and B and observing outcomes.

Design. Proper randomization and isolation (users should see only one variant to avoid carryover effects) are essential. Choose primary metrics a priori (e.g., click-through rate, session length, user-reported satisfaction) and record secondary metrics. Pre-register analysis plans to avoid p-hacking.

Hypothesis testing and p-values. Suppose the null hypothesis $H_0$ is that the probability of a positive outcome is equal for both variants. For binary outcomes, you can use a two-proportion z-test: with observed positive rates $\hat{p}_A$ and $\hat{p}_B$ and sample sizes $n_A$ and $n_B$, compute the pooled proportion $\hat{p} = \frac{n_A\hat{p}_A + n_B\hat{p}_B}{n_A + n_B}$ and the z-statistic
\[
z = \frac{\hat{p}_A - \hat{p}_B}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)}}.
\]
Convert $z$ to a p-value. For continuous metrics, use t-tests or nonparametric tests like Mann–Whitney U when distributions are skewed.

Power and sample size. Before running an A/B test, compute required sample size to detect a minimum effect size with power $1-\beta$ at significance $\alpha$. For detecting a difference $\delta$ in proportions with baseline $p$, an approximate required sample size per group is
\[
n \approx \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2p(1-p)} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p(1-p) + (p+\delta)(1-p-\delta)})^2}{\delta^2},
\]
where $z_{q}$ is the $q$-quantile of the standard normal. In practice use standard sample size calculators or bootstrap power analysis.

Multiple comparisons. When testing many variants or metrics, adjust for multiple hypothesis testing (Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg) to control family-wise error or false discovery rate. Sequential testing and peeking at results early inflate Type I error; use sequential methods like alpha spending, Bayesian approaches, or pre-specified stopping rules.

Randomization units. Decide whether to randomize by user, by session, or by request. Randomization at the user level avoids cross-contamination but requires more users; randomization by request is cheaper but may introduce correlated observations and violate independence assumptions, necessitating cluster-robust variance estimation.

Interpreting significance in context. Statistical significance does not imply practical significance. Report effect sizes and confidence intervals. For rare but severe harms (low base rate but high cost), classical A/B tests may lack power; targeted safety evaluations or adversarial testing are necessary.


# Out-of-distribution evaluation

Robust alignment demands testing models on prompts and scenarios that differ from their training or development data. Out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation reveals brittleness, failure modes, and unexpected alignment issues that appear in the wild.

Sources of OOD. OOD can arise from novel user intents, adversarial prompts, cultural or linguistic variation, shifts in content domains (e.g., new technologies or current events), or from system compositionality when models are used in pipelines with other tools. Constructing OOD datasets requires understanding realistic deployment distributions and potential adversarial behaviors.

Approaches. One approach is distribution-shift testing: hold out entire domains during training and then evaluate on those domains. Another is adversarial testing: generate prompts specifically designed to elicit unsafe or misaligned responses using human red-teamers or automated prompt generators. Stress tests evaluate edge conditions such as ambiguous instructions, conflicting constraints, or multi-turn interactions.

Evaluation strategies. For OOD tasks, human evaluation is indispensable because automatic metrics often fail in novel domains. Use targeted annotation schemes that focus on critical properties (e.g., whether the model follows a constraint, whether it hallucinates facts, whether it produces disallowed content). Track performance as a function of "distance" from the training distribution (when that can be defined) to identify degradation curves.

Model uncertainty and abstention. In OOD settings, the model should ideally express uncertainty or abstain when it cannot reliably satisfy the prompt. Evaluate the model's calibration and its ability to defer or request clarification. Techniques like Monte Carlo dropout, ensembles, or explicit uncertainty predictors can be incorporated and evaluated for their OOD detection performance (e.g., using AUROC on an in/out classification task).

Generalization versus memorization. OOD evaluation helps separate models that have learned robust, generalizable capabilities from those that memorize patterns. Look for failures that indicate brittle heuristics (e.g., failing to follow negated instructions) and design interventions (data augmentation, adversarial fine-tuning, constraint enforcement) followed by renewed OOD evaluation.

Reporting. Always report where the evaluation data comes from, whether it overlaps with model training data, and the degree to which it represents likely deployment scenarios. Explicit documentation of OOD tests and results is crucial to build trust and understand limitations.

Conclusion

Evaluating aligned text generation is inherently multidimensional and requires a mix of human and automatic methods, clear rubrics, statistical rigor, and an appreciation of deployment realities. Human protocols such as pairwise comparisons and Likert ratings remain central, but must be accompanied by careful inter-annotator agreement analysis and confidence estimation. Automatic metrics and LLM judges are useful tools when validated and used appropriately, but they are no substitute for human judgment—especially in safety-critical or novel situations. Sampling temperature and decoding choices materially affect outputs and thus evaluation outcomes, so these must be controlled and reported. Rigorous A/B testing with attention to power, significance, and multiple comparisons supports product decisions, while out-of-distribution and adversarial evaluations reveal the limits of alignment and guide further development. Together, these methodologies provide a robust framework for measuring how well models align with human preferences in the messy, varied contexts of real use.
