# Handling ties and indifference

Standard BT and PL assume strict preferences (no ties). Real-world data, however, often contain ties or indifference: raters may view two items as equally good, or an observed match may end in a tie. Ignoring ties can bias estimates. There are established ways to extend BT and PL to handle ties.

One approach for pairwise ties is the Davidson model, which augments BT by adding a tie parameter $\nu\ge 0$. Under Davidson, for a pair $(i,j)$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(i \succ j) &= \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j + \nu\sqrt{w_i w_j}},\\
\mathbb{P}(j \succ i) &= \frac{w_j}{w_i + w_j + \nu\sqrt{w_i w_j}},\\
\mathbb{P}(i \sim j) &= \frac{\nu\sqrt{w_i w_j}}{w_i + w_j + \nu\sqrt{w_i w_j}},
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\nu$ controls the propensity of ties. The $\sqrt{w_i w_j}$ term provides symmetry and turns into zero ties when $\nu=0$. This model can be fit by maximum likelihood by adding $\nu$ as an additional parameter; the likelihood remains well behaved and can be optimized numerically.

Another approach treats ties as partial rankings in Plackett–Luce: if a tie occurs among a subset of items, one can treat the tie as a simultaneous selection of those items in a particular stage, modeling the probability of that tied set using combinatorial factors derived from the Luce choice probabilities. Alternatively, one can model ties by introducing a latent threshold or noise distribution that allows differences smaller than some threshold to be declared ties.

Modeling indifference in rankings: For partial orders or weak orders where some items are incomparable or declared equal, extensions of PL that allow for set-valued choices or top-set selection can be used. The Mallows model and more complex random utility formulations that incorporate explicit tie-probability functions are also used.

When ties are infrequent, a pragmatic approach is to break ties randomly (e.g., at random order), but this can introduce bias. Better practice is to use a model that explicitly accounts for ties or to treat ties as missing information and marginalize over underlying strict orders consistent with the tie.


# Consistency and statistical properties

Statistical properties of the MLE for BT and PL are well understood in classical asymptotic regimes. Under standard regularity conditions and when the comparison/choice graph is connected and data are i.i.d. according to the model, the MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal. That is, as the number of independent observations $N\to\infty$,
\[
\hat\theta \xrightarrow{p} \theta^*, \qquad \sqrt{N}(\hat\theta - \theta^*) \overset{d}{\to} \mathcal{N}(0, I^{-1}(\theta^*)),
\]
where $I(\theta^*)$ is the Fisher information matrix. The degeneracy due to the additive constant shows up as a singular Fisher information: one direction has zero information; with a constraint like $\sum_i \theta_i=0$ the information on the constrained subspace is nonsingular.

Rates: For standard parametric asymptotics one gets $\sqrt{N}$ convergence rates. When the number of items $n$ grows with $N$, the behavior becomes more complex. Recent literature studies high-dimensional asymptotics, showing that estimation becomes harder when many items have few comparisons, and that minimax rates depend on the graph structure of comparisons. For instance, when each pair is compared a constant number of times but the number of items grows, consistent ranking of all items becomes impossible without additional structure (e.g., sparsity or feature-based models).

Consistency under misspecification: If the true data-generating process deviates from the BT or PL form, the MLE converges to the parameter that minimizes Kullback–Leibler divergence between the true model and the model family. This "quasi-MLE" remains a meaningful estimator for prediction but its inferential properties must be interpreted with caution.

Efficiency and variance estimation: The asymptotic covariance of the MLE can be estimated by the inverse observed Fisher information (negative Hessian at the MLE). For ranking problems, bootstrap methods are commonly used to assess uncertainty because analytic variance estimation can be complicated by dependence among observations (e.g., overlapping subsets) or by regularization.


# Optimization and computational considerations

While the log-likelihoods are concave in $\theta$, computational issues arise in large-scale settings (many items, many comparisons, partial rankings). Efficiency, numerical stability, and handling sparse and streaming data are practical concerns.

Algorithms:

- Newton–Raphson: Fast local convergence by using Hessian; efficient when $n$ is small or the Hessian has exploitable structure. Hessian for BT has entries that depend on pairwise weights; it is typically dense unless comparisons are limited. Inverting the Hessian costs $O(n^3)$ in general and is impractical for large $n$.

- Minorization–Maximization (MM): Hunter (2004) proposed an MM algorithm for BT that yields simple multiplicative updates and guarantees monotone likelihood increase. Each update solves an easier surrogate optimization. MM often converges reliably even when Newton’s method is unstable.

- Iterative scaling and iterative proportional fitting: For models expressible in exponential family form, iterative scaling methods can be used.

- Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): When data are massive and possibly streaming, SGD on the negative log-likelihood provides scalable updates using only local comparison information per step. Learning rates and variance reduction techniques (e.g., Adam, RMSprop) help.

- Coordinate ascent and block updates: For PL with partial rankings, block updates over items in a ranking can be effective, exploiting the product structure of the likelihood.

Sparse and structured computations: When comparisons are sparse (each item involved in few comparisons), the gradient and Hessian computations are sparse and can be implemented in time proportional to number of observed comparisons rather than $n^2$. Exploiting sparse data structures is crucial for scale.

Parallel and distributed optimization: Since the likelihood decomposes over observations, gradient computations parallelize naturally. Parameter synchronization across workers can be managed via asynchronous SGD, distributed L-BFGS, or parameter server architectures. Care must be taken with step sizes and staleness.

Regularization and hyperparameter selection: Cross-validation, held-out log-likelihood, or information criteria (AIC, BIC) guide selection of regularization strength. For Bayesian approaches, MCMC or variational inference approximate posteriors; variational methods scale better for large item sets.

Dealing with missingness and partial observations: Incomplete rankings are handled naturally in PL by using only observed stages. For pairwise BT, missing comparisons are simply absent from the likelihood. When data are missing-not-at-random (e.g., items survive selection bias), more careful modeling of the observation process is needed.

Computational stability: Numeric underflow/overflow can occur when exponentiating large $\theta$. Implementations typically work in log-space and normalize log-weights by subtracting a maximum before exponentiation. Also, when some $w_i$ approach zero, adding a small smoothing lower bound (e.g., via prior) stabilizes computation.

Connections to spectral methods: For BT-like pairwise data, spectral ranking methods (e.g., Rank Centrality) use Markov chain stationary distributions or eigenvector computations to obtain fast, approximate estimates of ranks. These methods are computationally attractive and have provable statistical guarantees under certain sampling regimes, often serving as initialization for iterative MLE optimizers.

Concluding remarks

The Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models form a foundational pair of models for modeling pairwise and listwise preferences via latent scores. They are simple, interpretable, and supported by strong statistical theory and practical optimization tools. Understanding their likelihoods, identifiability issues, handling of ties, and computational strategies equips the reader to engage with modern research that extends these models to feature-based settings, mixtures, time-varying preferences, active sampling, and beyond. When applying these models, careful attention to data sparsity, regularization, and model validation is essential to obtain reliable and useful estimates.
