Introduction

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a practical and widely used approach to align large language models (LLMs) with human preferences. In production settings RLHF is not a single algorithm but a pipeline of stages and engineering choices that together determine the final behavior, safety, and usefulness of a model. This chapter synthesizes the common three-stage RLHF pipeline—supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling (RM), and reinforcement learning (RL) optimization—and expands into the supporting engineering choices: how to pick and reuse reference models, how to design data pipelines for sampling and logging, how to budget compute and maximize throughput, and how to manage caching, batching, and sequence-length trade-offs. We then cover the most important monitoring signals (reward, KL, and quality metrics), common failure modes (degeneration, overfitting, collapse), and practical strategies for reproducibility and hyperparameter management. The goal is to give you an end-to-end, precise, and intuition-driven view of how RLHF is run in real systems so you can read and implement research and production papers effectively.


# Three-stage pipeline: SFT, reward modeling, RL optimization

The canonical RLHF pipeline divides alignment into three stages that build on one another: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reward Modeling (RM), and Reinforcement Learning (RL) optimization. Each stage has a distinct purpose and set of design choices.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is the stage where a base pretrained language model is adapted to the task distribution and basic safe/desired behaviors using labeled examples. Typical SFT data includes human-written prompts and target responses, expert demonstrations, and filtered or cleaned logs. The objectives are usually maximum likelihood (MLE) or cross-entropy with teacher forcing. SFT serves multiple roles: it brings the base model into the domain of interest, reduces low-level generation errors (e.g., hallucinated formatting, incorrect tokenization artifacts), and provides a stable starting point for the later RL stage. Because SFT is a supervised learning problem, it is generally stable, sample-efficient, and easy to reproduce. However, SFT alone can only capture behaviors present in the labeled data; it cannot easily encode nuanced preference trade-offs or resolve ambiguous or subjective ranking signals.

Reward Modeling (RM) converts human preference judgments into a scalar reward function that the policy (the LM) will optimize. Typically, humans are shown pairs or sets of model outputs given the same prompt and asked to rank or choose the better output. These comparisons are used to train a reward model $r_\theta(x, y)$ where $x$ is the prompt/context and $y$ is the candidate response. The simplest and common training loss is a pairwise logistic loss: if $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$, train $\theta$ to increase $r_\theta(x, y_1)$ over $r_\theta(x, y_2)$ by minimizing
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\log \sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_1) - r_\theta(x,y_2)\big),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. RM is critical because it defines the objective of the downstream RL optimization; biases, inconsistencies, or low sample efficiency in RM directly affect final behavior.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) optimization then updates the SFT-initialized policy to maximize expected reward according to the reward model, while often enforcing constraints to avoid undesirable drift from acceptable behavior. Practical RLHF approaches frequently use policy gradient variants such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or off-policy algorithms adapted to sequence generation. A common objective is to maximize expected RM score while adding an extra term to penalize divergence from a reference policy (often the SFT model) via a KL penalty. For a trajectory $y$ given prompt $x$, a common composite objective optimized by stochastic gradient ascent on policy parameters $\phi$ is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r_\theta(x,y) - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\phi(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right],
\]
where $\beta$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. Intuitively, RM pushes the policy toward higher-preference outputs; the KL term constrains updates so the policy does not exploit artifacts of the reward model or stray far from human-like text. Designing the right coefficient $\beta$, or replacing the KL term with a replay or trust-region mechanism, is one of the key engineering knobs.

These stages are interdependent. Better SFT reduces the amount of RL needed; higher-quality RM data reduces reward hacking; stronger KL constraints preserve fluency and reduce risky behaviors. A successful pipeline chooses how much effort to invest in each stage given data, compute, and safety requirements.


# Reference model selection and reuse

A reference model (often called the "behavioral prior" or "reference policy") serves two roles in RLHF: it is the distribution the policy is compared against (for KL penalties or constraints) and often the source of logged samples used for training the reward model. Choosing and reusing an appropriate reference model affects stability, compute, and final performance.

The simplest choice for a reference is the SFT model: after supervised fine-tuning, we use that model's probability distribution $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in the KL penalty during RL. This is attractive because SFT is tuned to desirable base behaviors, and using it as the anchor tends to preserve fluency and basic safety. Using the immediate SFT model also simplifies data pipelines: sampled outputs for RM labeling and for RL can be traced back to a single known distribution.

However, there are other choices and trade-offs. One can use the original pretrained base model as the reference, which can increase the penalty for diverging from the general language prior and thus prevent over-specialization. Another option is to maintain a moving-average or exponential-smoothed version of the RL policy as the reference. This can enable larger RL updates while keeping the penalty meaningful relative to recent policy actions. The challenge with moving references is bookkeeping: the KL becomes a penalty to a changing target, making theoretical interpretations more complex and sometimes hindering off-policy data reuse.

Reusing a single reference model across experiments reduces storage and compute and simplifies reproducibility. But in multi-version production systems it is common to have several versions: an SFT reference for early-stage RL, a conservative production reference for final tuning, and possibly a larger-capacity reference for quality control. When you reuse a reference across models of different sizes, you must be careful: the KL metric between a small policy and a large reference can be biased and lead to unhelpful penalties. In practice, it is often best to use a reference that is the same architecture and tokenizer as the policy, differing only in its parameter weights.

Finally, the reference model is also used for sample collection strategies (see the section on data pipelines). For example, when you want to collect ranked pairs for RM, you may sample both the policy and the reference model to capture diverse behaviors. Deciding when to freeze the reference (never update it) versus when to refresh it is an operational choice that trades simplicity for flexibility.


# Data pipelines for sampling and logging

The data pipeline is the circulatory system of RLHF: it delivers prompts to models, records generated responses, queues items for human labeling, stores metadata and metrics, and feeds training jobs. The effectiveness of RLHF depends heavily on this infrastructure.

A typical pipeline starts with a prompt reservoir: this contains prompt templates, user queries, or synthetic prompts designed to probe desired capabilities. For large-scale RLHF you will want a mixture of prompts: representative production traffic, adversarial queries, and targeted prompts aimed at known weaknesses. Sampling strategies should balance exploitation (collect more data where the model is uncertain or where reward is high variance) and exploration (covering the space so the reward model generalizes).

Generation and logging components must capture not just the final response text, but the full generation metadata: model logits, per-token probabilities, temperature and sampling parameters, the exact prompt text, any system instructions, and seed or RNG state if applicable. For pairwise labeling, you must be able to reproduce the same outputs deterministically; logging the RNG seed or storing the full text output is essential. Store also version identifiers for the policy and reference model used to generate each sample.

Human labeling workflows often use interface tools that present side-by-side outputs and collect preference signals. The pipeline should perform quality control on labelers, using inter-rater agreement metrics and calibration tasks embedded as gold-standard checks. Depending on budget, you may also use automated filters to discard low-quality or toxic outputs before labeling.

For reward model training, maintain structured datasets: each data point should include the prompt, the candidate responses, the preference label, and any annotator metadata. Versioning datasets is crucial: as you collect more comparisons and retrain the RM, you must retain older data to avoid catastrophic forgetting of rare but important preferences.

Finally, you must handle throughput and latency constraints. For continuous-learning setups you may implement a streaming pipeline that ingests labeled pairs and periodically retrains the RM and then triggers RL runs. For batch-oriented development, you can accumulate large labeled datasets offline and run training on a schedule. Robustness and observability are important: the logging system should support querying examples of high or low reward, requests that caused model failures, and distributions over prompt types.


# Compute budgeting and throughput optimization

RLHF is compute-intensive. You have three main compute phases: SFT training, reward model training, and RL optimization (which itself involves generating many samples and running policy updates). Budgeting compute means deciding how to allocate cycles and how to optimize throughput to get the best return on investment.

At a high level, early stages are data- and annotation-limited: collecting high-quality RM labels is often more valuable than throwing more compute at RL. But compute efficiency is still essential. For SFT and RM, classic optimizations apply: mixed-precision training, gradient accumulation, sharded data parallelism, and appropriate batch sizes that saturate GPU/TPU memory without wasting host-GPU bandwidth. Use learning-rate schedules that converge faster and checkpoint frequently.

RL optimization has unique compute demands. First, sample complexity: policy gradient methods require many environment interactions (here, model generations scored by the RM). Each generated sequence is variable-length and costly. To maximize throughput, generate sequences in batches and use vectorized decoding where possible. If you have multiple GPUs, pipeline generation across devices or run many asynchronous generation workers feeding a central learner.

Second, there is a trade-off between on-policy and off-policy data. On-policy methods (PPO) require samples from the current policy, which tightens coupling between generation and optimization. Off-policy replay can reuse older samples to improve data efficiency, but may require importance sampling corrections and careful off-policy corrections to avoid bias. If compute is limited, designing a replay buffer to store and reuse high-value samples can substantially reduce generation load.

Third, consider model size and parallelism. Larger models generate better outputs but slow down decoding and training. Many production systems use smaller policy models for RL optimization and then distill into larger models, or use a two-model approach: a smaller policy for RL, and a larger model for evaluation or final production. Another option is to perform RL on a smaller architecture and then transfer weights or fine-tune a larger model with the RL-updated distribution.

Finally, spot/preemptible instances, mixed-precision, and pipeline/model parallelism (for very large models) help stretch budgets but introduce engineering complexity. Monitor fine-grained utilization and profile both generation and gradient steps. Often the bottleneck is not raw FLOPs but I/O—loading checkpoints, logging examples, or waiting for human labeling. Optimize the end-to-end loop, not just the GPU utilization.


# Caching, batching, and sequence length management

Generation workloads for RLHF deal with many variable-length sequences. Effective caching, batching, and management of sequence length are critical to throughput and cost control.

Caching plays two main roles. First, caching model outputs avoids re-generating the same response multiple times, which is particularly valuable when re-evaluating with a new reward model or when the same prompt appears frequently. A cache keyed by (prompt, model-version, sampling-seed, generation-params) can prevent redundancy. Second, caching past hidden states or key-value pairs accelerates autoregressive decoding: once you have computed the transformer keys and values for the prompt tokens, you can reuse them when generating multiple candidate continuations. Implementing such "context caching" is crucial for batched beam or diverse sampling.

Batching is essential to maximize hardware utilization. For training and generation, group requests by similar sequence length and tokenize them into padded batches to reduce wasted compute. Bucketing by length reduces the amount of padding required. However, in interactive systems with low latency requirements, batching must be balanced against responsiveness: you may choose smaller batches and accept lower GPU utilization to meet SLA latency targets. For offline RL generation, it's usually optimal to maximize batch sizes and generate many sequences in parallel.

Sequence length has a quadratic cost in attention for transformers. Longer prompts or longer sampled responses increase both memory and compute. In RLHF, three levers are commonly used to control sequence length: truncate or summarize long prompts, set maximum generation length limits, and use hierarchical generation (generate a short outline and then expand). Truncating prompts can remove critical context, so prefer prompt compression or summarization when you must reduce length. For reward models, you should train on the same maximum sequence lengths you plan to use during RL to avoid distribution shifts.

Finally, be cautious about padding-induced KL artifacts. When computing per-token KL between policy and reference, padding tokens should be masked out; otherwise the KL statistics will be contaminated by padding behavior. And when batching sequences of mixed length, keep careful masking so that loss and reward computations only consider real tokens.
