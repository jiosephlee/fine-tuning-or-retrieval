Introduction

In many decision-making problems we do not receive numeric rewards for each action; instead, we observe only pairwise comparisons or preferences. For example, a search engine may show the user two rankings and see which one the user prefers, or an online retailer may show two product layouts and receive a click on the preferred layout. These scenarios call for bandit models whose feedback is relative—pairwise comparisons or "duels" between actions—rather than absolute scalar payoffs. This chapter develops the theory and algorithms for such preference-based and dueling bandits, explains how they differ from standard multi-armed bandits, introduces game-theoretic notions of optimality such as the von Neumann winner and maximin strategies, and discusses both online learning (regret minimization) and offline preference learning (estimation and sample complexity). We also cover contextual extensions, where preferences depend on observed context, and survey principal algorithms and their performance guarantees.

# Multi-armed bandits vs dueling bandits: problem setup

In the canonical multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem there is a finite set of actions (arms) $\mathcal{A} = \{1,\dots,K\}$. At each time $t=1,\dots,T$, the learner selects an arm $A_t\in\mathcal{A}$ and receives a stochastic reward $r_t$ whose expectation depends on the chosen arm, say $\mathbb{E}[r_t \mid A_t = i] = \mu_i$. The learner's goal is to maximize cumulative reward or equivalently minimize regret, typically compared to the best single arm $i^* = \arg\max_i \mu_i$.

Dueling bandits replace numeric rewards with pairwise comparisons. At each round $t$ the learner selects a pair of actions (a duel) $(i_t,j_t)$ and observes a noisy outcome indicating which alternative is preferred. A common stochastic model represents the pairwise preference probabilities by a matrix $P\in[0,1]^{K\times K}$ where $P_{ij}$ denotes the probability that the user prefers arm $i$ over arm $j$ when these two are compared. Typically $P_{ij} + P_{ji} = 1$ and $P_{ii} = 1/2$. The outcome of a duel is a Bernoulli random variable with mean $P_{i_t j_t}$. The learner's objective must be redefined because there may not exist an arm that beats all others with probability greater than $1/2$; moreover, the underlying matrix $P$ may not be transitive.

Thus, dueling bandits have two main structural differences from standard MAB:

1. Feedback is relational: observations are noisy indications of preferences between pairs, rather than scalar rewards attached to a single chosen arm.

2. The notion of an optimal arm is ambiguous: there may be cycles (Condorcet paradox) so that no arm dominates all others. Hence we need alternative optimality concepts (Condorcet winner when it exists, Copeland winner, Borda winner, von Neumann winner, maximin strategies), and the algorithmic and statistical goals depend on which notion is selected.

Formally, the stochastic dueling bandit setting can be specified as follows. There is a (hidden) preference matrix $P$ with $P_{ij}\in(0,1)$ satisfying $P_{ij}+P_{ji}=1$ and $P_{ii}=1/2$. At each round $t$ the learner chooses a pair $(i_t,j_t)$, observes a single Bernoulli outcome $Y_t$ with $\mathbb{P}(Y_t = 1) = P_{i_t j_t}$ (interpreting $Y_t=1$ as "first arm wins"). The learner's performance is measured by some regret notion over $T$ rounds relative to a comparator derived from $P$ (for instance, regret relative to repeatedly comparing the best arm versus itself, or regret relative to playing a saddle-point strategy). We discuss precise regret definitions in the section on algorithms.

This basic dueling setup admits many variants: deterministic or adversarial outcomes, the ability to compare more than two arms at once, or of returning a ranking instead of a single winner. Two frequently used stochastic models for the structure of $P$ are the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model and the Plackett–Luce (PL) model, which impose parametric structure that simplifies inference and reduces sample complexity. However, most dueling-bandit algorithms are designed for the general nonparametric case.

# Contextual dueling bandits and contextual preference learning

Contextual bandit problems generalize multi-armed bandits by introducing side information (context) $x_t$ observed before the action is chosen. In the standard contextual MAB, the reward distributions depend on both the arm and the context. Similarly, in contextual dueling bandits (also called contextual preference learning), the pairwise preference probabilities depend on the context: $P_{ij}(x)$ denotes the probability that arm $i$ is preferred to arm $j$ in context $x$.

Contextual dueling bandits arise naturally in personalized systems: a user with features $x$ may prefer different items than another user. The problem is to develop a policy that maps contexts to duels (pairs to compare), or in some formulations maps contexts to a single recommended arm, using past context–duel–outcome data to improve decisions.

There are two major learning paradigms in contextual dueling settings: (1) learning a context-dependent ranking or score function and reducing preference feedback to supervised learning of pairwise comparisons; and (2) reduction-based online learning where the contextual preference learning problem is reduced to a sequence of classical contextual bandit problems or binary classification problems.

One standard reduction takes advantage of a scoring model $s(x,i)$ that assigns a score to action $i$ in context $x$ and models $P_{ij}(x)$ as a function of score differences, for example via a logistic link:

$P_{ij}(x) = \sigma\big(s(x,i) - s(x,j)\big)$

where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ (the BTL/Bradley–Terry–Luce link). Under such a model, learning preferences reduces to learning the score function $s$ via supervised pairwise logistic regression using observed duels. Alternatively, one can estimate pairwise probabilities directly for each fixed pair and context features using standard classification techniques with labels indicating the winner. These supervised approaches are natural in the offline setting (where all data is available) but must be adapted to the online contextual bandit setting where data collection is interleaved with decision-making.

Reduction-based online algorithms for contextual dueling bandits often proceed by constructing an exploration strategy that collects unbiased or importance-weighted pairwise labels and then calling an oracle for contextual classification or contextual bandit optimization. For regret guarantees, two key tools are inverse propensity scoring (IPS) to obtain unbiased estimates of losses or preferences and the use of empirical risk minimization or online learning oracles for classification/regression under costs. For example, one can reduce contextual dueling bandits to contextual bandits by interpreting a duel as obtaining feedback about the relative loss of two policies, then using a contextual bandit oracle with importance-weighted updates (this is more intricate than in the standard scalar-feedback case because the observed comparison provides only pairwise information).

Analysis of contextual dueling bandits focuses on regret bounds that scale with the complexity of the function class used to model preferences. If we use a finite policy class $\Pi$ mapping contexts to arms, and define regret relative to the best policy in $\Pi$ under the von Neumann/worst-case notion (discussed next), then reductions yield regret bounds like $O(\sqrt{T\log |\Pi|})$ or, with more structure and stronger oracles, bounds with dependence on Rademacher complexity or VC-dimension in place of $\log|\Pi|$. Contextual extensions are an active research area because the structure of preferences across contexts introduces substantial additional statistical and computational challenges.

# Von Neumann winner and maximin strategies

Because pairwise preferences may be nontransitive, the classical "best arm" notion may not exist. Game theory provides principled alternatives. Interpret the pairwise preference matrix $P$ as defining a zero-sum matrix game between two players who each choose an arm; when player 1 selects arm $i$ and player 2 selects arm $j$, player 1 obtains payoff $P_{ij}$ and player 2 obtains $1-P_{ij}$. In zero-sum games there is a minimax value and optimal mixed strategies for both players. The von Neumann winner (or von Neumann solution) is the mixed strategy (probability distribution over arms) that secures the highest guaranteed win probability against any opponent strategy.

Formally, let $\Delta_K$ denote the probability simplex over arms. The maximin (or minimax) value is

v^* = \max_{p\in\Delta_K} \min_{q\in\Delta_K} p^\top P q.

A distribution $p^*$ that attains the maximum is called a maximin strategy or von Neumann winner. Because $P$ is skew-symmetric around $1/2$ in the sense $P_{ij}+P_{ji}=1$, the value $v^*$ satisfies $1/2 \le v^* \le 1$. The interpretation is that if the learner plays arms by sampling from $p^*$ and the adversary responds with any mixed strategy $q$, the expected win probability of the learner is at least $v^*$.

Why is this concept useful? First, a maximin strategy always exists (the simplex is compact and the payoff is linear in each player’s mixed strategy), even when no single arm dominates all others. Second, it captures a conservative notion of optimality: we seek a randomized policy that ensures robustness against any competitor. Third, in many online and offline dueling bandit formulations minimizing regret relative to the von Neumann winner yields stable objectives and avoids pathological cycles.

The maximin strategy can also be characterized as the solution to a linear program:

maximize v
subject to \sum_{i=1}^K p_i P_{ij} \ge v for all j = 1,\dots,K
\sum_{i=1}^K p_i = 1
p_i \ge 0.

This LP enforces that the mixed strategy $p$ yields expected payoff at least $v$ against each pure action $j$ of an adversary. Solving this LP computes $p^*$ and $v^*$. The dual LP corresponds to the adversary's min strategy.

For a different but related notion, the Copeland score of an arm $i$ is the number or fraction of arms it beats with probability greater than $1/2$. A Copeland winner maximizes this score; unlike the von Neumann solution, Copeland winners are pure strategies. Another scalar-based objective is the Borda count, where the Borda score of $i$ is $\sum_j P_{ij}$; the Borda winner maximizes this sum. These alternatives are sometimes easier to estimate but have different robustness properties than the maximin solution. The von Neumann winner is particularly appealing for game-theoretic interpretation and for producing randomized policies that are resilient in repeated interactions.

An important observation: if there is a Condorcet winner $c$ (an arm with $P_{ci} > 1/2$ for all $i\ne c$), then the deterministic strategy that always plays $c$ is also a von Neumann winner (it wins against any opponent with probability at least $>1/2$). But when a Condorcet winner does not exist, the von Neumann strategy can be mixed over several arms and can perform strictly better in the worst-case sense than any single arm.

# Offline vs online preference data and sample complexity considerations

Preference learning can be studied in offline (estimation) and online (sequential decision-making) regimes. Offline preference learning assumes a dataset of pairwise comparisons has been collected—possibly by passive observation or active experimental design—and the task is to infer underlying rankings, scores, or an optimal policy. In the online setting, the learner actively chooses duels to minimize regret while learning the preference structure.

Offline sample complexity questions ask: how many pairwise comparisons are required to recover a target object with high probability? The target might be the ranking of all arms, the top arm under some criterion, or an approximation to the von Neumann distribution. Two main axes affect sample complexity: the noise model and the target criterion.

Under parametric models such as BTL/PL, the preference matrix $P$ is generated from a low-dimensional score vector $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^K$ where

P_{ij} = \sigma(\theta_i - \theta_j)

with $\sigma$ the logistic or softmax link. Estimating $\theta$ (or the induced ranking) typically requires $O(K \log K)$ comparisons if comparisons are chosen adaptively and roughly $O(K^2)$ if comparisons are sampled uniformly at random, though constants and logarithmic factors depend on the minimum gap between parameters. For top-1 identification under a gap $\Delta$ between the best and second-best in terms of pairwise win probability, sample complexity scales like $O((1/\Delta^2) \log(1/\delta))$ to identify the best item with probability $1-\delta$; if there are $K$ items and one wants the top item with high probability, a naive union bound yields a factor $K$ making the sample complexity $O\big((K/\Delta^2)\log(K/\delta)\big)$, but more clever tournament or elimination schemes can reduce dependence on $K$.

For nonparametric models, sample complexity depends on the separations in the matrix $P$. Identifying a Condorcet winner (when one exists) requires distinguishing each pair in which the winner has $P_{ci} > 1/2$ by at least some margin. If the minimum gap is $\Delta$, then $O\big((K/\Delta^2)\log(1/\delta)\big)$ comparisons suffice in many adaptive procedures. Ranking the whole set consistently is typically more expensive, resembling sorting with noisy comparisons; classical results on noisy sorting imply sample complexity at least $O(K\log K)$ under suitable margins.

Active sampling improves sample complexity significantly compared to passive sampling. In an active design, the learner adaptively chooses which pairs to compare based on past outcomes, focusing effort on distinguishable pairs and not wasting comparisons where the ordering is obvious. This adaptivity is crucial, for example, to achieve near-linear sample complexity in $K$ for top-1 identification rather than the quadratic complexity of exhaustive pairwise sampling.

Offline estimation of the von Neumann distribution requires recovering the matrix $P$ sufficiently well to solve the LP for the maximin strategy. In particular, to guarantee that the computed $p$ is within $\epsilon$ in expected value $v$ of the true maximin value, one needs uniform $\ell_\infty$ control of estimation errors of entries of $P$ on the order of $\epsilon$. This can require $\widetilde{O}((K^2/\epsilon^2))$ comparisons in the worst case if all pairs need to be estimated; however, if the underlying structure is low-dimensional or if one only cares about approximating $p^*$ in some weaker norm, fewer comparisons suffice. Again, active sampling can concentrate comparisons on informative pairs and lead to improved sample complexity.

In the online setting, sample complexity is linked to regret: roughly speaking, to get average regret approaching zero you must collect enough comparisons to identify the optimal strategy. For stochastic problems, typical rates are that cumulative regret grows sublinearly in $T$ (e.g., $O(\log T)$ or $O(\sqrt{T})$ depending on gap-dependent or gap-independent bounds). The precise dependence on $K$, the gaps in $P$, and the target optimality notion matters and is discussed next in the algorithmic section.

# Algorithms for dueling bandits and regret bounds

Designing algorithms for dueling bandits depends on the stochastic or adversarial setting, the optimality notion (Condorcet, Copeland, Borda, von Neumann), and whether contexts are present. We cover several representative algorithms and their regret properties, emphasizing intuition and the broad types of guarantees that are possible.

Regret definitions. Several regret notions have appeared in the literature. A common one for the Condorcet or Copeland setting is cumulative regret relative to always playing a best arm $i^*$ (if it exists). When using the von Neumann benchmark, natural regret is defined relative to the value of the maximin strategy. Let p^* be a maximin mixed strategy and consider the learner at each round sampling an arm according to some distribution p_t; then expected instantaneous regret can be defined as

r_t = v^* - \min_{q\in\Delta_K} p_t^\top P q,

and cumulative regret is \sum_{t=1}^T r_t. Another practical measure is duel-regret: the expected loss per duel compared to the outcome had we played according to the benchmark distribution. In many stochastic settings, the goal is to ensure cumulative regret grows sublinearly in $T$.

Algorithms for stochastic dueling bandits.

1. Relative UCB (RUCB). RUCB is a natural adaptation of the UCB principle to dueling feedback. RUCB maintains confidence intervals for pairwise probabilities $P_{ij}$ based on empirical comparisons and selects pairs that are most informative for identifying a Condorcet winner or for eliminating arms. The algorithm uses upper confidence bounds on pairwise wins to drive exploration. For problems where a Condorcet winner exists and gaps are $\Delta_{ij} = P_{ij} - 1/2$, RUCB achieves gap-dependent regret on the order of O\big(\sum_{i\ne i^*} (\log T)/\Delta_{i^* i}\big) where the sum is over arms that need to be distinguished from the Condorcet winner. This mirrors the classical MAB logarithmic regret scaling. The algorithmic idea is to maintain an active set of candidate winners and repeatedly compare the most ambiguous pairs.

2. Beat-the-Mean and Copeland extensions. When no Condorcet winner exists, some algorithms target Copeland or Borda winners. Beat-the-Mean is an algorithm that aggregates pairwise estimates to construct empirical Copeland scores and eliminates arms with low scores using confidence bounds. Regret guarantees are similar in flavor: gap-dependent logarithmic regret in favorable stochastic settings. The complexity constants typically depend on the number of arms that are near the top and on pairwise gaps.

3. Sparring and Doubler. A simple reduction-based approach for the von Neumann objective is "sparring": run two independent single-player MAB algorithms, say A and B, in parallel. At each round, algorithm A selects arm i_t and B selects arm j_t; the duel outcome is used as the reward for A (treating a win as reward 1) and as the complement for B. Intuitively, if both players run no-regret MAB algorithms, their empirical action distributions converge to a Nash equilibrium of the induced zero-sum game, approximating the von Neumann solution. A clean analysis requires careful consideration of feedback structure because each player only sees bandit feedback. In stochastic settings, sparring with standard stochastic MAB learners yields regret bounds like O(\sqrt{KT}) for gap-free guarantees, and gap-dependent bounds like O(\sum (\log T)/\Delta) with appropriate algorithms.

Doubler is a refinement for contexts where one wished to decouple the selection of arms between the two players or to use specialized adversarial algorithms.

4. Explore-then-Commit and tournament schemes. For top-1 identification or best-arm selection with dueling feedback, simple explore-then-commit schemes perform repeated comparisons in a tournament tree. These yield sample complexity scaling like O\big((K/\Delta^2)\log(1/\delta)\big) to identify the best arm with probability at least $1-\delta$. Such schemes are practical and achieve minimax-optimal dependence on $\Delta$ in many settings.

Regret rates and tradeoffs. Two general types of regret bounds appear:

- Gap-dependent bounds: when pairwise gaps (differences) between optimal and suboptimal arms are nonzero, algorithms can often achieve regret that grows logarithmically in $T$ with constants inversely proportional to gaps. These are the analogs of the classical Lai–Robbins logarithmic lower bounds in MAB. For stochastic dueling bandits targeting a Condorcet winner, algorithmic regret rates are often O\big(\sum_{i\ne i^*} (\log T)/\Delta_{i^* i}\big), where \Delta_{i^* i} = P_{i^* i} - 1/2.

- Gap-independent bounds: when gaps can be arbitrarily small or zero, one aims for worst-case regret bounds that scale as O(\sqrt{T}) (up to factors depending on K). For example, sparring with adversarial MAB learners yields O(\sqrt{KT}) bounds in many formulations. These rates are similar to generic bandit rates: \Omega(\sqrt{T}) is unavoidable information-theoretically in the adversarial or gap-free stochastic settings.

Lower bounds. Information-theoretic lower bounds show that dueling bandits cannot be easier than classical MAB in general. For instance, even if the true preferences are generated by embedding arms on the real line with smooth pairwise probabilities, discerning small gaps requires \Omega(1/\Delta^2) samples per distinguishing comparison. For the von Neumann objective, lower bounds on regret scale analogously to zero-sum game learning lower bounds, i.e., \Omega(\sqrt{T}) in the gap-free setting and \Omega(\log T) with gaps.

Contextual versions. In contextual dueling bandits, regret bounds depend on the complexity of the function class used to model preferences. Using finite policy classes leads to regret O(\sqrt{T\log|\Pi|}). When reducing to contextual bandit or classification oracles, the regret depends on the underlying oracle guarantees. The challenge in contextual settings is to obtain unbiased estimates for preferences under partial feedback; IPS and doubly robust estimators are common tools. The dependence on the number of arms K is often multiplicative; for large action spaces one needs structure (e.g., linear or low-dimensional parametrization) to obtain meaningful bounds.

Practical considerations. Many dueling-bandit algorithms rely on pairwise confidence intervals and elimination rules; they need to manage a potentially quadratic number of pairwise estimates. Practitioners often exploit structure: if a parametric model (BTL/PL) is reasonable, fit a global score function and compute duels analytically, which drastically reduces sample complexity and computational cost. When computational constraints preclude maintaining all pairwise statistics, randomization and sampling heuristics can prioritize promising pairs.

Summary of algorithmic landscape. Overall, dueling-bandit algorithms trade off between robustness to intransitivities and statistical efficiency. Algorithms targeting Condorcet winners can be efficient when a Condorcet winner exists and gaps are favorable. For general nontransitive preferences, methods that aim for von Neumann or maximin strategies are principled and can be implemented via sparring or game-theoretic solvers, with regret guarantees comparable to standard bandits. Contextual extensions introduce additional complexity but can be handled by reductions to classification or contextual bandit oracles, with regret controlled by the complexity of the hypothesis class and the precision of importance-weighted estimators.

Concluding remarks

Preference-based and dueling bandits extend classical bandit ideas to settings where feedback is pairwise and possibly nontransitive. They introduce new notions of optimality coming from game theory and require both algorithmic creativity and careful statistical analysis. Offline preference learning focuses on estimation and sample complexity, benefiting strongly from active sampling and parametric structure, whereas online dueling bandits emphasize regret minimization and often require reductions to standard bandit or classification oracles. The von Neumann winner and maximin strategies give a robust benchmark in the absence of a Condorcet winner, and a variety of algorithms—from RUCB-style elimination to sparring reductions—provide practical ways to learn in these environments with provable guarantees. The interplay between structure in preferences, available contexts, and the choice of optimality criterion determines both what is learnable and how efficiently it can be learned.
