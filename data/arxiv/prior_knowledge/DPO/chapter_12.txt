Introduction

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) has become a cornerstone of modern reinforcement learning and, in particular, of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) for fine-tuning large language models (LMs) toward desirable behaviors. PPO strikes a practical balance between the theoretically motivated but brittle Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) and simpler policy-gradient methods by introducing a clipped surrogate objective that prevents excessively large policy updates while remaining straightforward to implement and efficient to compute. In RLHF, PPO is used to optimize a language model's output distribution using scalar rewards derived from human preferences, learned reward models, or heuristic objectives. This chapter explains PPO in the context of open-ended text generation, with emphasis on the clipped objective and surrogate loss, advantage estimation, choices between KL-penalized and clipped variants, value function learning and reward design, on-policy sampling and rollout generation, hyperparameters affecting stability, risks such as mode collapse and reward overoptimization, and the computational costs of applying PPO at large scale.

This chapter assumes familiarity with basic policy-gradient ideas: a policy parameterized by $\theta$ that defines a distribution $\pi_\theta(a\,|\,s)$ over actions $a$ given states $s$, the idea of returns and advantages, and gradient-based optimization. For language models, "state" will typically be the prompt or partially generated sequence, and "action" will be the next-token sampling choice (or the entire generated sequence when using sequence-level treatment). We will make the mapping explicit as we proceed.

Clipped objective and surrogate loss

The central innovation of PPO is the clipped surrogate objective that stabilizes policy updates without computing second-order information or solving constrained optimization explicitly. Start with the probability ratio
r_\theta(s,a) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a\,|\,s)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a\,|\,s)},
which measures how much the new policy differs from the behavior policy that generated the data (usually the policy before the update). A standard policy-gradient surrogate objective without constraints is
L_\text{PG}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a \sim \text{data}}\left[r_\theta(s,a) \, \hat{A}(s,a)\right],
where \hat{A}(s,a) is an estimator of the advantage. Maximizing this directly can lead to large updates where the ratio becomes very large, leading to instability.

PPO introduces a clipped surrogate objective:
L^\text{clip}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a}\left[\min\left(r_\theta(s,a)\,\hat{A}(s,a),\ \text{clip}\big(r_\theta(s,a),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}(s,a)\right)\right].
Intuitively, if \hat{A} is positive, the unclipped term encourages increasing \pi_\theta(a\,|\,s); clipping limits the benefit once r_\theta(s,a) exceeds 1+\epsilon. If \hat{A} is negative, clipping prevents making r_\theta(s,a) too small (below 1-\epsilon). The objective therefore penalizes large changes only when they would improve the objective beyond a trust region controlled by \epsilon. Typical values of \epsilon are in the range 0.01 to 0.2, depending on scale and application.

Why the min? The min ensures a pessimistic estimate: when the ratio is outside the trust region, the clipped term provides a lower value than the unclipped one for the advantageous direction, so the optimizer cannot exploit large changes. The gradient of L^\text{clip} is zero when the unclipped ratio drives the surrogate further outside the clipping region, which effectively prevents pushing the policy farther in that direction for that data point. Because the objective is a simple expectation over observed transitions, it is easy to compute with mini-batches and compatible with first-order optimizers like Adam.

The surrogate formulation is "on-policy" in spirit: the expectation is taken under the data collected by \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}. In practice, PPO performs multiple epochs of stochastic gradient updates using the same batch of data, but because the clipping limits the step size per data point, PPO tolerates these multiple epochs without destabilizing learning as much as an unconstrained policy gradient would.

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)

The advantage estimator \hat{A}(s,a) is critical to variance reduction and sample efficiency. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) constructs a low-variance, low-bias compromise for advantage estimates by combining temporal-difference (TD) errors at different horizons.

Let V_\phi(s) be a learned value function (parameterized by \phi) that estimates the expected return from state s. Define the TD residual
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t),
where r_t is the reward at time t and \gamma is the discount factor. GAE defines the advantage estimator as
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},
where \lambda\in[0,1] interpolates between high-variance, low-bias Monte Carlo returns (\lambda=1) and low-variance, high-bias TD(0) (\lambda=0).

In practice, episodes are finite-length, and the infinite sum truncates at episode end. The parameter \lambda controls a bias-variance trade-off: larger \lambda reduces bias but increases variance. Typical values used in RLHF and language-model PPO are around 0.95 to 0.99, though smaller values like 0.9 can be used when rewards are noisy. Because language generation rollouts can be long and rewards may be sparse or delayed, choosing \lambda carefully helps stabilize learning; GAE also benefits from an accurate value function V_\phi.

Computation of GAE can be performed efficiently via a backward recurrence:
\hat{A}_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda \hat{A}_{t+1}
with \hat{A}_T = 0 at the end of a trajectory. The advantages are then often normalized (mean zero, unit variance) across the minibatch before being used in the surrogate objective, which prevents scale issues when optimizing with steps of fixed size.

KL-penalized vs. clipped variants

PPO originally had two main variants: the clipped surrogate (described above) and an alternative that adds a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to the objective. The KL-penalized variant writes a surrogate loss with an explicit penalty:
L^\text{KL}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[r_\theta(s,a)\hat{A}(s,a)\right] - \beta \, \mathbb{E}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot\,|\,s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\,|\,s)\big)\right],
or equivalently with the KL in the opposite direction depending on convention. Here \beta>0 controls the strength of the trust-region penalty. This variant directly discourages the policy from deviating too far in distributional terms.

Each approach has pros and cons. The KL-penalized variant provides an interpretable soft constraint; tuning \beta can target an intended average KL divergence per update. Some implementations adaptively adjust \beta to maintain a target KL. However, computing KL terms between very large output distributions for language models can be expensive, and the gradient of the KL may interact with the policy gradient in subtle ways. The clipped surrogate is simpler to implement, generally more robust in practice, and avoids explicit KL computation, but it does not provide a direct control over the expected KL divergence.

In RLHF for language models, practitioners often combine a surrogate objective (clipped) with an explicit KL constraint against a behavior or reference policy. The reference policy is typically the original pretrained language model or a supervised fine-tuned model; including a KL penalty or clipping the log-probability ratio relative to this reference helps preserve language quality and prevents the fine-tuned policy from drifting toward degenerate outputs that maximize reward but are otherwise low-quality or nonsensical. A common practical formulation for sequence-level training is:
L(\theta) = L^\text{clip}_\text{policy}(\theta) - c_\text{KL}\,\mathbb{E}\left[ D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\,|\,\text{prompt})\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\,|\,\text{prompt})\big)\right] + c_v L_\text{value}(\phi),
where c_\text{KL} and c_v weight the KL penalty and the value loss respectively. The KL term guides the policy to stay close to the reference LM; it is especially important when the reward signal is sparse, noisy, or when preserving style and factuality is critical.

Value function learning and reward design

The value function V_\phi(s) is necessary for advantage estimation and often optimized jointly with the policy. In the LM setting, "state" typically corresponds to the prompt plus the generated prefix up to time t, and the value can be either token-level (predicting expected return from each generation step) or sequence-level (predicting return for the whole generated continuation). Training the value function usually minimizes a squared error (or Huber) loss:
L_\text{value}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\big(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{R}_t\big)^2\right],
where \hat{R}_t is an estimate of the empirical return from timestep t, e.g., the discounted sum of future scalar rewards or a GAE-derived target like V_\phi(s_t) + \hat{A}_t. Stabilizing value learning requires regularization: weight decay, separate learning rates, and sometimes gradient clipping or target networks help.

Reward design is especially delicate in RLHF. Rewards may come from human preference models trained on pairwise comparisons, automatic classifiers for attributes (toxicity, helpfulness), or handcrafted heuristics. Rewards are often noisy, biased, and only loosely aligned with the end objectives (e.g., human satisfaction). Several practical considerations affect reward design:

- Reward shaping and decomposition: Breaking a scalar reward into components (helpfulness, safety, style) and combining them can make optimization more interpretable, but care must be taken to calibrate scales. Unbalanced components can dominate learning dynamics.

- Baselines and centering: Removing a baseline (e.g., average reward per prompt) reduces variance. Advantage estimators already use a state-dependent baseline via V_\phi, which is crucial.

- Reward normalization: Rewards may be normalized per-batch or per-prompt to avoid scale issues; always ensure that normalization does not leak information across updates in a way that biases learning.

- Robustness to adversarial exploitation: Learned reward models can be gamed by policies that find spurious shortcuts that increase reward without improving actual human preference. Regularization through KL penalties and holding out human-evaluated validation prompts can detect runaway behaviors.

In open-ended text generation, rewards are often sequence-level and sparse: only after a full response is generated can the reward be computed. To apply token-level PPO, one either assigns the same sequence-level reward to all timesteps (with resulting high variance) or crafts intermediate rewards. GAE can help by bootstrapping from value estimates, but the entire training loop must account for the long-range credit assignment inherent in language generation.

On-policy sampling loops and rollout generation

PPO is fundamentally on-policy: policy updates use data collected by the current or recent policy. In RLHF for LMs, this translates to periodic rollout generation where, given a set of prompts, the policy generates continuations sampled according to \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}. The standard loop proceeds as follows:

1. Collect rollouts: For a batch of prompts, sample continuations using the current policy. Sampling can be pure ancestral sampling, top-k/top-p sampling, or temperature-scaled sampling; the sampling procedure must be recorded because it affects the log-probabilities used in the ratio r_\theta.

2. Compute rewards: For each generated continuation, compute scalar rewards using the reward model or human labels. If using pairwise preference models, often the reward is proportional to the preference score; sometimes a logistic loss or the Bradley–Terry model is used to convert pairwise comparisons into scalar utilities.

3. Estimate advantages: Using the collected rewards and the value function V_\phi, compute GAE advantages \hat{A}_t, and possibly target returns for value updates.

4. Perform PPO updates: Using minibatches from the collected trajectories, perform multiple epochs of optimizing the clipped surrogate for the policy and a value loss for V_\phi. Optionally include the KL penalty against a reference policy.

5. Update reference and repeat: Optionally update the reference policy periodically or keep it fixed. Then collect fresh rollouts with the updated policy and continue.

Several practical aspects matter for rollout generation with LMs:

- Stochasticity and temperature: The degree of stochasticity in sampling affects exploration and variance. Higher temperature increases exploration but may produce lower-quality samples. Because PPO relies on the importance ratio r_\theta, it's important that the data reflect the policy being updated.

- Length handling: Rollouts can be variable-length. One must pad or mask losses appropriately and ensure the value function and advantage computations respect episode termination.

- Batch composition and prompt diversity: A diverse set of prompts reduces overfitting and mode collapse. In RLHF, prompts should reflect the distribution of real-world usage.

- Replay and on-policy constraints: PPO often repeats optimization epochs over the same data, making it only "approximately" on-policy. Strictly off-policy data (from much older policies) should be avoided unless importance sampling corrections are applied and variance is controlled.

Hyperparameters and training stability

PPO introduces several hyperparameters that crucially affect stability and performance. Understanding their roles and interactions is necessary for effective tuning.

- Clip parameter \epsilon: Controls the trust region size in the clipped objective. Small \epsilon enforces conservative updates; large \epsilon allows more change. Typical values: 0.05–0.2. For large LMs with sensitive behavior, smaller \epsilon may be safer.

- Learning rates: Separate learning rates for policy and value networks are common. The policy learning rate often needs to be small to avoid destabilizing text quality; the value network can have a larger rate but may also overfit.

- Batch size and epochs per batch: The number of trajectories per update and number of training epochs over that batch affect sample efficiency and overfitting. More epochs improve gradient estimation but risk overfitting to the batch; clipping mitigates this but does not eliminate the risk.

- GAE \lambda and discount \gamma: As discussed, these control bias-variance trade-offs. For episodic, goal-oriented tasks, \gamma near 0.99 and \lambda near 0.95 are common. For open-ended text, \gamma often is set to 1 for undiscounted returns when using sequence-level rewards, but practical implementations might use \gamma<1 to favor shorter, higher-reward responses.

- Entropy bonus: Adding an entropy regularizer encourages exploration and prevents premature convergence to deterministic policies. The coefficient must be small; for LMs entropy also influences sample diversity and fluency.

- KL coefficient c_\text{KL}: In KL-penalized variants or when including a KL constraint against a reference policy, c_\text{KL} balances reward maximization against policy deviation. Adaptive schemes can raise c_\text{KL} when the measured KL exceeds a target and lower it otherwise.

- Value loss weight c_v and reward scaling: The relative scaling of the value loss and policy loss affects learning dynamics. Poor scaling can cause the value network to dominate updates or not learn at all.

- Gradient clipping and optimizer settings: Gradient clipping (by norm) prevents exploding gradients, while optimizer parameters (Adam's \beta_1, \beta_2, eps) can influence convergence speed and stability.

Tuning these hyperparameters often requires validation with held-out prompts and human evaluation metrics, since reward signal may not perfectly reflect human preferences. Logging KL between the current policy and the reference, rollout reward distributions, and measures of output diversity helps detect problematic behavior early.

Mode collapse and reward overoptimization risks

Two central risks when applying RL to language generation are mode collapse and reward overoptimization (also called reward hacking). Mode collapse refers to the policy converging to a narrow set of outputs—often repetitive or templated—thereby reducing diversity and likely utility. Reward overoptimization occurs when the policy exploits weaknesses in the reward model, producing outputs that score highly according to the reward but are undesirable (e.g., factually incorrect content that tricks the reward model into thinking it is helpful).

Mechanisms producing these risks include high learning rates, weak KL regularization, poorly specified reward functions, and overtraining on a limited dataset of prompts. Because the reward model is an imperfect proxy for human judgment, the policy can discover adversarial strategies that maximize the proxy reward while undermining actual objective quality.

Mitigation strategies include:

- Stronger KL or reference regularization to preserve the prior language distribution and prevent extreme deviations.

- Reward model robustness: training reward models on adversarial negatives, augmenting with human labels, and evaluating on held-out human judgments reduces susceptibility to gaming.

- Entropy bonuses and minimum diversity constraints: Encouraging stochasticity or penalizing low diversity per prompt can preserve output variety.

- Early stopping and validation with humans: Regular human evaluations of samples detect degenerate behaviors sooner than automated metrics.

- Ensemble reward models and uncertainty estimation: Using an ensemble of reward predictors and penalizing high-variance or overconfident predictions reduces single-model exploitation.

- Prompt and data augmentation: Increasing prompt diversity and including counterexamples in the training set reduces overfitting to narrow prompt types.

- Conservative objective scaling: Use modest reward magnitudes and gradual optimization (small \epsilon, low learning rates) to avoid dramatic shifts.

Mode collapse manifests in quantitative signals: decreased token-level entropy, lower n-gram diversity, or collapsed response lengths. Reward overoptimization can be identified by divergence between automated reward predictions and human evaluations. Both require constant monitoring during training.

Computational costs in large-scale LM PPO

Applying PPO to large language models is computationally demanding. There are three main resource-intensive components: rollout generation, reward computation, and optimization.

Rollout generation requires sampling many continuations from the current policy, potentially with long sequences and batched autoregressive decoding. For a policy model with billions of parameters, sampling thousands of sequences per update (each several hundred tokens long) consumes substantial GPU/TPU cycles. Latency also matters when human annotators supply labels or when on-the-fly reward computation is used.

Reward computation can be particularly costly if rewards come from human evaluations, expensive classifiers, or large reward models. Running a separate reward model over each generated sequence can cost nearly as much compute as generation. Preference learning pipelines that use human annotations to train reward models add further human-in-the-loop costs.

Optimization involves backpropagation through the policy and value networks. For very large LMs, gradient computation and parameter updates require waveform-distributed training, model-parallel machinery, and careful memory management. PPO's multiple epochs per batch multiply the optimization cost relative to one-pass supervised fine-tuning.

Practical cost-reduction strategies include:

- Reduced frequency of updates: Collect larger batches before updating to amortize per-update overheads.

- Smaller surrogate models: Use a smaller value network or reward model to compute advantages and rewards while using a larger policy. For instance, the value network can be a lightweight head over frozen LM layers or a separate smaller transformer.

- Offline reward computation: Precompute rewards for fixed datasets, when feasible, to avoid repeated reward model calls. This is workable only when the policy used to generate the rollouts is static or slowly changing.

- Mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and model sharding to reduce memory and compute per device.

- Sample-efficient generation: Use techniques like nucleus sampling with constrained temperatures to produce informative rollouts with fewer tokens.

- Distributed pipelines: Overlap rollout generation, reward computation, and optimization in a pipelined manner across different clusters or pools of machines.

- Distillation after RL: Train a smaller student model to imitate the RL-improved policy, enabling cheaper deployment while paying the RL cost once.

Finally, infrastructure for PPO at scale must manage data movement, logging, and reproducibility. The on-policy nature of PPO complicates caching and reuse of data, so engineering for high-throughput generation and efficient communication between sampling and optimization workers is crucial.

Conclusion

PPO provides a practical and robust framework for fine-tuning language models with reinforcement learning signals such as those arising in RLHF. The clipped surrogate objective stabilizes policy updates and avoids costly second-order methods. Generalized Advantage Estimation reduces variance and improves sample efficiency when combined with a learned value function. Choosing between clipped and KL-penalized formulations involves trade-offs: clipping is simpler and empirically robust, while explicit KL penalties provide a more direct constraint on policy drift and are useful in RLHF to preserve language quality.

Value function learning and reward design require careful engineering: the value target, reward normalization, and reward model quality directly affect the advantages and therefore the policy updates. On-policy rollout generation is central, and the ways in which generation is performed (sampling strategy, prompt diversity, batch sizes) determine the data distribution the policy is optimized against. PPO has many hyperparameters—clip size, learning rates, GAE parameters, entropy and KL weights—which must be tuned with stability and safety in mind. The risks of mode collapse and reward overoptimization necessitate defensive design choices, including KL constraints, robust reward models, and human validation. Finally, large-scale PPO imposes significant computational costs in generation, reward evaluation, and optimization; careful engineering and approximations are often required to make RLHF with large LMs tractable.

In practice, successful PPO training for language models is part engineering and part empirical science: combining solid theoretical choices (e.g., clipping and GAE) with monitoring, validation, and iterative tuning. This chapter has provided the conceptual intuition and operational details needed to understand PPO within RLHF pipelines for open-ended text generation and to read and implement methods reported in research literature.
