Chapter 12: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for Language Models

Introduction

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a family of on-policy policy-gradient algorithms that have become a practical standard for reinforcement learning with large function approximators, including neural language models used in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipelines. In the context of open-ended text generation, PPO is used to fine-tune a base language model to produce outputs that better match human preferences or other reward signals while attempting to avoid destabilizing the pretrained model and causing catastrophic behavior.

This chapter explains the key components that make PPO effective and robust in RLHF settings. We begin with the clipped objective and surrogate loss that are central to PPO, then cover how advantages are estimated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). We compare KL-penalized and clipped variants of PPO, discuss value function learning and how rewards are designed for language tasks, and describe on-policy sampling loops and rollout generation for text. The chapter finishes by addressing practical considerations: hyperparameters and training stability, risks such as mode collapse and reward overoptimization, and the computational costs that arise when scaling PPO to large language models.


# Clipped objective and surrogate loss

At the heart of PPO is the idea of updating a stochastic policy by maximizing a surrogate objective that approximates the true policy-gradient objective but constrains updates so the new policy does not move too far from the old policy. For a policy parameterized by $\theta$, denote the old parameters as $\theta_{\text{old}}$ and let $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ be the policy probability of action $a$ in state $s$. The fundamental per-time-step importance sampling ratio is
\[
r_\theta(s,a) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}.
\]
A classic surrogate objective (from policy gradient theory) is
\[
L^{\text{PG}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[ r_\theta(s,a) \, \hat{A}(s,a) \big],
\]
where $\hat{A}(s,a)$ is an estimator of the advantage function. This objective is unbiased for small changes in policy but can be high-variance and unstable when $r_\theta(s,a)$ is far from 1, especially with large neural networks and on-policy rollouts.

PPO introduces a clipped surrogate loss to limit the effect of large policy ratios. The clipped objective for a single sample is
\[
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a}\Big[ \min\big( r_\theta(s,a)\hat{A}(s,a),\; \operatorname{clip}\big(r_\theta(s,a), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\big)\hat{A}(s,a) \big)\Big],
\]
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant (e.g., $0.1$ or $0.2$) and $\operatorname{clip}(x, a, b)$ clamps $x$ to the interval $[a,b]$. Intuitively, when $\hat{A}(s,a)$ is positive, increasing $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ above $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)$ improves the unclipped objective; the clipping prevents $r_\theta$ from growing beyond $1+\epsilon$. When $\hat{A}(s,a)$ is negative, decreasing $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ is advantageous, but the lower bound $1-\epsilon$ prevents it from shrinking too much. The outer $\min$ makes the objective a pessimistic lower bound of the unclipped surrogate, preventing updates that would exploit excessive policy ratio excursions.

Why this works in practice: The clipping introduces a trust-region–like effect but in a simpler, first-order-compatible form. Instead of explicitly constraining some distance metric like the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence or the Fisher metric, clipping acts directly on the importance weights to keep gradient steps moderate. For language models generating tokens, the per-action probability ratio is computed for each generated token, and the clipped loss accumulates across tokens in a rollout (often with token-level advantages and per-token ratios). The optimization then uses standard first-order optimizers (e.g., Adam) applied to the clipped surrogate plus auxiliary losses (value loss, entropy bonus) to produce stable updates.

Important practical details include whether to average the clipped objective per token or per sequence, how to weight per-token advantages, and how to compute the importance ratios when the policy is autoregressive (the product of token-level ratios across tokens can be numerically unstable). Typical implementations compute a sum of log-probability differences per token to form sequence-level ratios or compute token-wise surrogate losses and sum them, which tends to be more stable.


# Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)

Advantage estimation measures how much better an action is compared to the baseline value of being in the same state. Accurate advantage estimates are essential because the surrogate loss in PPO multiplies the importance ratio by the advantage estimate. A biased but low-variance advantage estimator can produce more stable learning than an unbiased high-variance one.

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) provides a continuum between high-bias, low-variance and low-bias, high-variance advantage estimators via a smoothing parameter $\lambda$. Assume we have a value function $V_\phi(s)$ approximator with parameters $\phi$ and observed rewards $r_t$ when following the old policy. Define temporal-difference residuals (TD errors)
\[
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t),
\]
where $\gamma\in[0,1]$ is the discount factor. GAE computes the advantage estimate as an exponentially-weighted sum of these TD errors:
\[
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}.
\]
In practice, rollouts are finite and the sum truncates at the end of the trajectory. The hyperparameter $\lambda\in[0,1]$ interpolates between two extremes: $\lambda=0$ gives a one-step TD residual (low variance, high bias), while $\lambda=1$ gives the Monte Carlo advantage (high variance, low bias). Choosing $\lambda$ around $0.95$ is common because it balances bias and variance well for many tasks.

Intuition: The TD error $\delta_t$ captures the immediate surprise of getting $r_t$ and transitioning compared to expected value. Summing discounted and decayed future TD errors accumulates evidence for the action’s long-term desirability while damping the influence of far-future noisy outcomes. For autoregressive language models, where episodes correspond to sequences or dialogues, GAE helps smooth noisy scalar reward signals (e.g., human preference scores) across token-level decisions so that the policy learns which token choices contributed to the final reward.

Implementation notes for language models: Because sequences may have varying lengths and rewards can be sparse (often only at the end of a sequence), GAE requires careful handling of episode termination. When a sequence ends, bootstrapping with $V_\phi$ is typically set to zero if the environment truly terminates, or to $V_\phi(s_T)$ if a continuing sequence is possible. Additionally, discounting with $\gamma<1$ is often used, though in many RLHF setups $\gamma$ is set to 1 (no discount) for episodic tasks where the entire sequence matters. Using $\gamma=1$ with $\lambda<1$ still provides temporal smoothing via $\lambda$.


# KL-penalized vs. clipped variants

PPO has multiple variants to enforce that the updated policy does not drift too far from the behavior policy. Two common approaches are the clipped surrogate described earlier and a KL-penalized objective. Understanding their differences is important for RLHF because pretrained language models carry valuable prior behavior we do not want to lose.

The KL-penalized variant augments the objective with a penalty term proportional to the KL divergence between the new and old policies:
\[
L^{\text{KL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[ r_\theta(s,a)\hat{A}(s,a) - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\big) \big],
\]
where $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the penalty. One can also reverse the KL argument order depending on theoretical or practical preferences. The penalty discourages large average Kullback–Leibler divergence across states. In practice, implementations often include an adaptive $\beta$ that increases if the observed KL exceeds a target and decreases if it is below the target.

The clipped objective avoids computing a state-wise KL and works directly on the importance sampling ratios. It is simpler to implement and has been shown empirically to perform well across many tasks. The clipping also has a clear pointwise guarantee: for each sample, the incentive to change probabilities beyond the clip region is removed.

Trade-offs and practical considerations:

- Interpretability and control: KL-penalty gives a direct handle on controlling expected KL divergence, which is a meaningful distance between distributions. This is appealing in RLHF because one often wishes to keep the fine-tuned policy close to the pretrained LM. A target KL can be enforced adaptively. Clipping indirectly constrains per-sample ratios but does not directly control the expected KL; observed KL can still vary.

- Stability: Clipping can be more robust to large, spiky advantages because the clip prevents extreme updates on a per-sample basis. KL penalties provide a smoother constraint but require careful tuning of $\beta$ or its adaptive schedule to maintain stability.

- Computational aspects: Computing KL requires summing over vocabulary probabilities if a full-distribution KL is used at each step, which can be expensive with large vocabularies. However, for autoregressive LMs we can compute token-level KL cheaply by evaluating log-probabilities already needed for the surrogate loss. Clipping only needs the ratio or log-prob difference and is slightly cheaper.

- Conservative behavior: KL penalties explicitly push the policy towards the old distribution, which can help prevent forgetting of pretrained knowledge and reduce the risk of mode collapse. Clipping is effective but sometimes allows aggregate drift over many updates because it acts locally per sample.

In RLHF pipelines, hybrid strategies are common: applying clipped surrogate updates for optimization stability while monitoring and optionally penalizing or constraining total KL from the pretrained base model. A common pattern is to include an explicit KL penalty relative to the base (reference) model in the reward or as a separate loss to ensure the fine-tuned policy does not stray arbitrarily from the base LM's behavior.
