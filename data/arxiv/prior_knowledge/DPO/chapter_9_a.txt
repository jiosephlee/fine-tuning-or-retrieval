Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a framework for training agents to make sequences of decisions to maximize an objective that is typically expressed as cumulative reward. In the context of language models and sequence generation, RL is used to fine-tune a probabilistic policy (the language model) so that its generations score highly under some reward function: for example, human preference, task-specific metrics, or constraints such as factuality. This chapter develops the foundational RL concepts needed to understand and implement policy optimization for sequence generation. We start by defining the mathematical objects—Markov decision processes, policies, returns, and value functions—and then derive one of the central optimization tools, the policy gradient theorem and the REINFORCE estimator. We then explain practicalities specific to sequences: reward-to-go and credit assignment, reward shaping and baseline techniques for variance reduction, distinctions between on-policy and off-policy learning, episodic versus continuing formulations for text, and the principal sources of variance and instability when applying RL to large language models. The aim is to provide both the equations and the intuition so you can read papers and implement algorithms with a clear understanding of where design choices come from.


# MDPs, policies, returns, and value functions

The Markov decision process (MDP) is the canonical mathematical model for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. An MDP is defined by a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is a set of states, $\mathcal{A}$ is a set of actions, $P(s' \mid s,a)$ is the transition probability from state $s$ to next state $s'$ when action $a$ is taken, $r(s,a)$ is a (possibly stochastic) immediate reward function, and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ is a discount factor that trades off immediate and future rewards. At each time step $t$ the agent observes a state $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$, selects an action $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$ according to its policy, then receives reward $r_t := r(s_t,a_t)$ and transitions to $s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t,a_t)$.

For sequence generation with language models, the "state" often encodes the tokens generated so far (the context), and the action is selecting the next token from the vocabulary. For example, if tokens are $x_1, x_2, \dots$, then after generating tokens up to $x_{t-1}$ we can define the state as $s_t = (x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$, and pick action $a_t = x_t$. The transition dynamics are typically deterministic in this representation: $s_{t+1} = (s_t, a_t)$—that is, the next state is simply the context appended with the chosen token. The reward can be provided at the end of the sequence (episodic reward) or at intermediate steps if we have token-level feedback.

A policy is a (possibly stochastic) mapping from states to distributions over actions. We denote a parametrized policy by $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$, where $\theta$ are the learnable parameters (e.g., the weights of a neural language model). The policy induces a distribution over trajectories $\tau = (s_1,a_1,s_2,a_2,\dots)$ via the initial state distribution, the action sampling $\pi_\theta$, and the transition dynamics.

The return is the cumulative (discounted) reward from a time step $t$ onward. For a trajectory $\tau$ and time $t$ the return is
$G_t = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \gamma^k r_{t+k}$,
where $r_{t+k}$ is the reward received at time $t+k$. In episodic tasks with finite horizon $T$, the sum is finite: $G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t} \gamma^k r_{t+k}$. The return quantifies how good a state or action is in terms of future rewards.

Value functions summarize expected return under a policy and are indispensable for both understanding performance and developing optimization algorithms. The state-value function $V^{\pi}(s)$ is the expected return starting from state $s$ and following policy $\pi$:
$V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_t \mid s_t = s\right].$
The action-value function $Q^{\pi}(s,a)$ is the expected return when starting from state $s$, taking action $a$ once, and thereafter following policy $\pi$:
$Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a\right].$
The advantage function captures the relative desirability of action $a$ compared to the average action under the policy:
$A^{\pi}(s,a) = Q^{\pi}(s,a) - V^{\pi}(s).$

In sequence generation, rewards are often sparse and observed only at the end of a generated sequence. That means $r_t=0$ for all intermediate steps and $r_T$ is the terminal reward. The value functions then reflect the expected terminal reward conditional on the partial sequence. For example, $V^{\pi}(s)$ tells you, given the tokens generated so far, what reward you expect if the model continues sampling according to $\pi$. Learning or estimating these quantities is central to making sensible updates to the parameters $\theta$.


# Policy gradient theorem and REINFORCE

When the policy is parametrized by $\theta$, our objective is to maximize the expected return under the policy. Define the performance objective as
$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G_1(\tau)]$,
where the expectation is over trajectories sampled according to the policy dynamics. The policy gradient theorem provides a way to compute the gradient $\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ without differentiating through the environment dynamics. The theorem states:
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t) \right].$
This result is powerful because it expresses the gradient in terms of quantities that depend only on the policy and value functions, not on derivatives of environment transitions.

REINFORCE is the canonical Monte Carlo estimator based on this theorem. If we replace $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)$ with the empirical return $G_t$ observed from a sampled trajectory, we obtain the REINFORCE estimator:
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t^{(i)} \mid s_t^{(i)}) \, G_t^{(i)},$
where $N$ is the number of sampled episodes and $T_i$ is the length of episode $i$. Intuitively, REINFORCE increases the log-probability of actions that led to high returns and decreases it for actions that led to low returns.

The derivation of REINFORCE relies on the "log-derivative trick": $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(\tau) = \pi_\theta(\tau) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau)$ and linearity of expectation. Writing $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau} [G(\tau)]$, we get
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\tau \left[ G(\tau) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau) \right],$
and because $\log \pi_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$, the policy gradient theorem follows.

While REINFORCE is unbiased, it is often high variance. The variance grows with trajectory length and with stochasticity in rewards, which creates practical challenges that we address later. Despite these challenges, REINFORCE remains a foundational algorithm and a baseline for many more sophisticated methods (actor-critic, trust-region methods, PPO, etc.). For language models, REINFORCE corresponds to sampling sequences according to the current model, computing sequence-level rewards, and using those rewards to adjust token probabilities via the gradient of the log-probabilities.


# Reward-to-go, credit assignment in sequences

A key refinement of REINFORCE for sequential tasks is the use of reward-to-go. Rather than using the full return from time 1, $G_1$, to update the gradient at every time step, reward-to-go uses the return starting at the current time step $t$, i.e., $G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} r_k$. The policy gradient theorem supports this because
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \, \mathbb{E}[G_t \mid s_t, a_t] \right],$
and empirically replacing the conditional expectation by the observed $G_t$ produces an unbiased estimator with lower variance than using the full $G_1$ at every time step. Put simply, reward-to-go assigns each action only the rewards that follow it, not rewards that preceded it, which is a more sensible credit assignment.

Credit assignment is the problem of determining which actions were responsible for observed rewards. In sequence generation, credit assignment is especially challenging because a single final reward (e.g., human preference between two sentences) must be attributed to a long sequence of token choices. Using reward-to-go partially addresses this by limiting the contribution of an action to those rewards that occur after it, but it still may be insufficient when rewards are delayed and sparse.

Several additional strategies help with credit assignment. One is to design intermediate rewards so that partial sequences receive informative feedback. For example, one could have a reward component measuring n-gram overlap with a reference, or a reward that increases as generated text becomes more fluent or less contradictory. Another strategy is to learn a critic $V_\phi(s)$ to estimate the expected return from a state and use advantage estimates $G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$ for more accurate credit assignment (this leads to actor-critic methods). Temporal-difference (TD) methods that bootstrap value estimates can further reduce variance and improve credit assignment efficiency because they combine sampled rewards with learned value predictions to attribute credit over multiple time scales.

For language models, an important practical consideration is that the state includes entire generated prefixes, leading to a huge state space and sparse visitation. This makes learning a reliable critic difficult and increases the importance of methods that reduce variance or inject stronger inductive biases—such as using pretrained LMs as initial policies and shaping rewards that reflect token-level structure.


# Reward shaping and baselines

Reward shaping and baselines are techniques to reduce variance and guide learning while preserving the optimal policy under certain conditions. A baseline is a function $b(s_t)$ subtracted from the return or $Q$-value in the policy gradient estimator. The modified gradient estimator is
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \left(Q^{\pi}(s_t,a_t) - b(s_t)\right) \right].$
Since $\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \, b(s_t)] = b(s_t) \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t) = 0$, subtracting any baseline that depends only on the state does not introduce bias but can greatly reduce variance. A common choice is the state-value baseline $b(s_t) = V^{\pi}(s_t)$ which yields the advantage function $A^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)$ as the multiplier. In practice, $V^{\pi}$ is unknown and is estimated by a learned critic $V_\phi(s)$.

Reward shaping refers to modifying the reward function by adding an extra term that encourages intermediate behavior without changing optimal policies—with some caveats. A classic form of potential-based reward shaping adds $\gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ to the reward at a transition from $s$ to $s'$, where $\Phi$ is any scalar potential function. This shaping preserves the set of optimal policies in the MDP. Intuitively, potential-based shaping redistributes reward along trajectories in a conservative way so the cumulative difference between any two complete trajectories remains the same. In sequence generation, practical shaping often departs from the strict potential-based form, because the true objective (e.g., human preference) may be complex and shaping is used heuristically to speed up learning. When shaping is not potential-based, it can alter the optimal policy and introduce bias; still, in applied work practitioners balance bias versus variance and per-step learning speed.

For sequence-level RL with language models, baselines are especially important. Without baselines, the variance of the gradient estimator can be enormous because small changes in log-probabilities get multiplied by large sequence-level returns. Using a baseline such as the expected reward of the current policy (self-critical sequence training) is common. In self-critical training, one computes the reward of a greedy decoding sequence $r(\hat{y}_{\text{greedy}})$ to serve as a baseline and then uses $r(y_{\text{sample}}) - r(\hat{y}_{\text{greedy}})$ as the advantage. This often stabilizes learning because it centers the signal around zero and focuses updates on actions that improve over the model's current deterministic output.
