Chapter 10: Preference and Ranking Models (Bradley‑Terry & Plackett‑Luce)

Introduction

Modeling preferences — the choices people or systems make when presented with alternatives — is a central problem in statistics, econometrics, and machine learning. Two foundational statistical models for preference data are the Bradley‑Terry model for pairwise comparisons and the Plackett‑Luce model for listwise (ranking) data. Both models assign a scalar “score” or “ability” to every item and translate those scores into choice probabilities via ratios or the softmax function. This chapter presents these models carefully: how they are formulated, how their likelihoods are built from observed comparisons or rankings, how identifiability issues arise and are resolved, how maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) works and what the gradients look like, and how to extend and regularize the models in practice. The presentation emphasizes intuition: why softmax over scores is a natural choice, why additive constants are irrelevant, and what practical steps help obtain stable estimated parameters.

# Bradley‑Terry model for pairwise comparisons: formulation and likelihood

The Bradley‑Terry (BT) model is a simple, widely used model for pairwise comparison data. Suppose we have $n$ items indexed by $i=1,\dots,n$. Each item $i$ is assigned a nonnegative ability parameter $w_i>0$. When items $i$ and $j$ are compared, the BT model assumes the probability that $i$ is preferred to $j$ equals
\[
\mathbb{P}(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j}.
\]
This ratio form is appealing: it respects symmetry, gives higher probability to the item with larger ability, and is invariant to scaling all abilities by the same positive constant. A convenient reparameterization is to work in the log‑ability (or score) scale. Define score parameters $s_i = \log w_i \in \mathbb{R}$. Then
\[
\mathbb{P}(i \text{ beats } j) = \frac{e^{s_i}}{e^{s_i} + e^{s_j}} = \sigma(s_i - s_j),
\]
where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function. In this form, the model depends only on score differences $s_i - s_j$, which makes the connection to logistic regression clear: the probability that $i$ beats $j$ is a logistic function of the difference in their scores.

To form the likelihood, suppose we observe a dataset of pairwise outcomes. Let $N_{ij}$ be the number of times item $i$ and $j$ were compared and let $y_{ij}$ be the number of times $i$ beat $j$ (so $0\le y_{ij}\le N_{ij}$). Under independent comparison assumptions, the likelihood factor for the pair $(i,j)$ is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{ij}(w_i,w_j) = \left(\frac{w_i}{w_i+w_j}\right)^{y_{ij}} \left(\frac{w_j}{w_i+w_j}\right)^{y_{ji}},
\]
where $y_{ji}=N_{ij}-y_{ij}$ is the times $j$ beat $i$. The full likelihood is the product over unordered pairs $(i,j)$:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = \prod_{i<j} \left(\frac{w_i}{w_i+w_j}\right)^{y_{ij}} \left(\frac{w_j}{w_i+w_j}\right)^{y_{ji}}.
\]
Equivalently, with scores $\mathbf{s}=(s_1,\dots,s_n)$,
\[
\ell(\mathbf{s}) = \log \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i<j} \big[ y_{ij}\log\sigma(s_i-s_j) + y_{ji}\log\sigma(s_j-s_i)\big].
\]
Because $y_{ji}=N_{ij}-y_{ij}$ and $\log\sigma(-x)=\log(1-\sigma(x))$, this is a sum of log‑likelihood contributions each like a Bernoulli/binomial logistic regression on the single predictor $s_i-s_j$.

Intuitively, the BT model treats comparisons only in terms of relative strengths: only the differences $s_i-s_j$ matter. This leads directly to the identifiability issue discussed below, and it also yields simple inference algorithms based on convex optimization (or iterative MM algorithms).

# Plackett‑Luce model for rankings and listwise probabilities

The Plackett‑Luce (PL) model generalizes the ratio idea to full rankings (or partial rankings, top‑k lists). Again assign a strictly positive ability parameter $w_i>0$ to each item $i$. For a listwise ranking, suppose a respondent outputs an ordered list of items, for instance a full permutation $(i_1 \succ i_2 \succ \dots \succ i_n)$ meaning $i_1$ ranked first, $i_2$ second, etc. The PL model defines the probability of that permutation as the product of sequential conditional choices: at each choice stage, the item chosen from the remaining set is drawn with probability proportional to its ability. Concretely,
\[
\mathbb{P}(i_1 \succ i_2 \succ \dots \succ i_n) = \frac{w_{i_1}}{\sum_{k=1}^n w_{i_k}} \cdot \frac{w_{i_2}}{\sum_{k=2}^n w_{i_k}} \cdot \cdots \cdot \frac{w_{i_n}}{w_{i_n}}.
\]
Because the last ratio equals 1, the product effectively runs until the second‑to‑last stage. The PL probability factorization has an intuitive “sequential choice” interpretation: the top‑ranked item is selected from the whole set proportional to its ability; then the second is selected from the remaining items in the same proportion, and so on.

If we use scores $s_i=\log w_i$, each factor becomes a softmax: e.g. the probability the first item is $i_1$ is $\exp(s_{i_1})/\sum_k\exp(s_k)$. Thus PL is naturally expressed using softmax probabilities over scores. The log‑likelihood for a dataset of observed permutations is the sum over permutations of the logarithm of such products; writing out the log gives sums of log‑softmax terms. For partial rankings (for example observed top‑k lists) one simply stops the product after $k$ factors, so PL handles incomplete lists in a natural way.

PL reduces to Bradley‑Terry on the pairwise level: marginally, the probability item $i$ is chosen over $j$ in a pairwise contest is $w_i/(w_i+w_j)$, the BT probability. More formally, if one samples a Plackett‑Luce permutation for a set {i,j} alone, the induced pairwise outcome distribution matches BT. Thus PL is a coherent listwise generalization of BT.

An important modeling assumption in PL is the independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) at each choice stage: the relative odds of two items being chosen from a set do not depend on other irrelevant alternatives outside the set of consideration, except through their removal if they are no longer available. This assumption is sometimes too strong in practice, but it simplifies the model and inference.

# Relation between score functions and choice probabilities (softmax over scores)

Both Bradley‑Terry and Plackett‑Luce tie choice probabilities to a latent score function via a softmax (or logistic) mapping. The common pattern is: assign each item $i$ a real-valued score $s_i$. Then the probability of choosing item $i$ from a set $S$ is
\[
\mathbb{P}(i \mid S) = \frac{\exp(s_i)}{\sum_{j\in S}\exp(s_j)}.
\]
For pairwise choices $S=\{i,j\}$, this reduces to the logistic function of a difference:
\[
\mathbb{P}(i\mid\{i,j\}) = \frac{e^{s_i}}{e^{s_i}+e^{s_j}} = \sigma(s_i-s_j).
\]
This softmax mapping has several attractive properties. First, it converts arbitrary scores into a normalized probability distribution over the available alternatives. Second, because the exponential is monotone, larger scores always imply higher choice probabilities; the exponential also stretches differences, so a unit change in score in a high‑score region has a multiplicative effect on odds. Third, when working with log‑abilities $s_i=\log w_i$, the softmax naturally encodes multiplicative ability comparisons in additive score differences, which often simplifies inference.

Interpreting scores is straightforward: the odds of choosing $i$ over $j$ equal $\exp(s_i-s_j)$, so the difference $s_i-s_j$ is the log‑odds. This is why many estimation procedures and diagnostics operate on the difference scale. Moreover, if scores depend linearly on item features (e.g., $s_i = x_i^\top \beta$), then the model becomes a generalized linear model where parameters $\beta$ influence choice probabilities via softmax — a multinomial logistic regression.

Although softmax is the default mapping in these models, alternative link functions or noise models lead to other choice models (e.g., Thurstone-Mosteller uses Gaussian noise so probabilities are functions of the difference through the Gaussian CDF), but the softmax/logistic mapping has computational convenience and intuitive multiplicative ability interpretation.

# Identifiability: additive shifts and normalization constraints

A core statistical issue with BT and PL models is identifiability: the data determine scores only up to an additive constant. In the BT/PL formulation with scores $s_i=\log w_i$, if we replace every score by $s_i' = s_i + c$ for some constant $c\in\mathbb{R}$, then every softmax probability is unchanged:
\[
\frac{e^{s_i'}}{\sum_{j\in S} e^{s_j'}} = \frac{e^{s_i+c}}{\sum_{j\in S} e^{s_j+c}} = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_{j\in S} e^{s_j}}.
\]
Thus the likelihood is invariant to additive shifts, and the parameters $s_i$ are not uniquely determined without a normalization constraint. In the original $w$‑parameterization this corresponds to multiplicative nonidentifiability: scaling all $w_i$ by the same positive factor leaves all probabilities unchanged.

How to fix this? There are several standard choices of identifiability constraints. One common choice is to fix one parameter arbitrarily, for example set $s_1=0$ (or $w_1=1$). Another choice is a sum constraint, such as $\sum_{i=1}^n s_i = 0$ or $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1$. A third is to use a reference group: take $s_1$ as baseline and interpret other scores relative to it. Which normalization you use does not change predicted probabilities or the relative ordering of $s_i$, but it affects the numerical values and the interpretation of intercepts in models that incorporate covariates.

Additive nonidentifiability also affects variance estimates. The Fisher information matrix is singular in the direction of the all‑ones vector because the likelihood does not vary when moving along that direction. When computing standard errors, you should either project the variance onto an identifiable subspace (for example, eliminate one parameter) or impose a constraint and compute the Fisher information within that constraint.

A related practical remark: because only differences matter, many algorithms and model formulations explicitly operate on differences (e.g., pairwise logistic regression uses contrasts $s_i-s_j$). This automatically removes the additive constant. When using numerical optimization, enforce a constraint (like mean zero) or add a regularizer that penalizes large norms and effectively picks out one solution among equivalent shifted ones.

# MLE for preference models and gradient expressions

Maximum likelihood estimation is the standard approach to estimate the score/ability parameters. Because the models are parameterized through softmax/logistic functions, the log‑likelihoods are sums of log‑softmax/logistic terms and are typically smooth. Below are explicit forms and gradients for both Bradley‑Terry and Plackett‑Luce, which are useful for implementing numerical optimization, stochastic gradient descent, or Newton methods.

Bradley‑Terry (pairwise) log‑likelihood and gradient. Using scores $\mathbf{s}=(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ and observed counts $y_{ij}$ (# times $i$ beat $j$), the log‑likelihood can be written as
\[
\ell(\mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i<j} \big( y_{ij}\log\sigma(s_i-s_j) + y_{ji}\log\sigma(s_j-s_i)\big).
\]
Differentiate with respect to $s_i$ to obtain the gradient component:
\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s_i} \;=\; \sum_{j\ne i} \big( y_{ij} - N_{ij}\cdot \sigma(s_i-s_j)\big).
\]
Here $N_{ij}=y_{ij}+y_{ji}$ is the number of comparisons between $i$ and $j$. The intuition: each observed win $y_{ij}$ pulls $s_i$ upward, while the expected wins under the current scores, $N_{ij}\sigma(s_i-s_j)$, pull it downward. Setting the gradient to zero equates observed wins to expected wins against all opponents, which is the usual moment interpretation for MLE.

The Hessian (second derivative) with respect to scores has negative terms involving $\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))$, making the log‑likelihood concave or at least well‑behaved in many cases in the score parameterization; practical optimization often uses Newton or quasi‑Newton methods or specialized MM (minorize‑maximize) algorithms (e.g., iterative scaling / Bradley‑Terry algorithms) to handle the constraints and avoid singularity.

Plackett‑Luce (listwise) log‑likelihood and gradient. For a dataset of observed permutations or partial rankings, express each observed ranking $r$ as an ordered list $(i_1,\dots,i_k)$ (where $k=n$ for full permutations or $k$ for top‑k lists). The log‑likelihood contribution from $r$ is
\[
\ell_r(\mathbf{s}) = \sum_{t=1}^k \log \frac{e^{s_{i_t}}}{\sum_{j\in R_t} e^{s_j}},
\]
where $R_t$ is the set of items remaining at stage $t$ (so $R_1$ is the full set, $R_2$ is the set excluding $i_1$, etc.). Summing over observations gives the total log‑likelihood. Differentiating, the gradient with respect to $s_i$ is
\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s_i} \;=\; \sum_{r}\sum_{t:\,i\in R_t} \Big( \mathbb{I}\{i = i_t\} - \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_{j\in R_t} e^{s_j}} \Big),
\]
where the inner sum runs over stages in ranking $r$ where item $i$ is available. The term $\mathbb{I}\{i = i_t\}$ is 1 when $i$ was selected at that stage and 0 otherwise; the subtraction is the expected selection probability under the current scores at that stage. Thus the gradient again equates observed selections to expected selections for each item across all stages.

These gradient expressions are straightforward to implement and allow use of off‑the‑shelf optimization routines. For large datasets or many items, one often uses stochastic gradient or mini‑batch optimization, computing the gradient contribution from a subset of comparisons or rankings at a time.

Existence and uniqueness of the MLE. Because the likelihood is invariant to additive shifts, the MLE is not unique unless an identifiability constraint is imposed. Beyond that, the MLE exists and is finite if the comparison graph is sufficiently connected: intuitively, every item must be connected to every other by some chain of comparisons so that its parameter is informed. If an item never loses (or never wins) compared to all others, the MLE may go to infinite values (separation), similar to separation in logistic regression. Regularization or priors are commonly used to address these issues, as described below.

# Extensions and practical regularization

Real datasets present complications that motivate extensions and regularization of BT and PL models. Below are common extensions and practical techniques to stabilize estimation and increase modeling flexibility.

Regularization and priors. A standard remedy for nonidentifiability and instability (e.g., when some items have few comparisons or complete dominance) is to add a penalty or Bayesian prior. An $L_2$ penalty on scores (ridge) corresponds to a Gaussian prior $s_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$ and breaks the translational invariance by centering scores at zero; one can enforce mean zero afterward or interpret posterior modes directly. An $L_1$ penalty can induce sparsity in score differences in certain structured formulations. In the $w$ parameterization, a Dirichlet prior is natural for Plackett‑Luce (because $w_i$ are positive and often normalized), and adding pseudo‑counts to pairwise wins is equivalent to a conjugate prior in many cases.

Handling partial rankings, ties, and missing data. Plackett‑Luce handles partial rankings natively by stopping the sequential product early. Ties require explicit modeling: one approach is to treat a tie as a simultaneous selection from a subset, with probability given by summing/adjusting the PL mass for all permutations consistent with the tie, or use explicit tie parameters (e.g., Davidson’s extension of BT introduces tie probabilities). Missing data (unobserved comparisons) are handled by simply excluding those pairs; however, if missingness is informative, one should model it explicitly.

Covariates and mixed models. Scores can be modeled as linear or nonlinear functions of item or context features. For instance, let item $i$ have features $x_i$ and parameter vector $\beta$, and set $s_i = x_i^\top \beta$. Then PL or BT become multinomial or pairwise logistic regression models, respectively. If items vary across users, mixed‑effects models introduce random effects for users and items (e.g., $s_{i,u} = \alpha_u + x_i^\top \beta + b_i$) to capture heterogeneity. Estimation here often uses likelihood approximations, penalized quasi‑likelihood, or Bayesian MCMC/variational methods.

Temporal models and dynamic abilities. In many applications (sports, online platforms) abilities evolve over time. State‑space extensions model $s_i(t)$ with random walk priors or auto‑regressive processes and update estimates sequentially via filtering/EM methods. The additive invariance still holds at each time point, so one must choose consistent normalization over time (e.g., subtract mean score at each time) to make interpretation stable.

Computational algorithms: MM, iterative scaling, and gradient methods. Although gradients are simple, specialized algorithms exploit the structure of BT/PL likelihoods. The Minorize‑Maximize (MM) principle yields iterative update rules that guarantee monotone likelihood increase and are easy to implement; classic BT iterative algorithms update $w_i$ proportionally to observed wins divided by expected wins. For large item sets and datasets, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or stochastic average gradient methods are effective. For high precision, quasi‑Newton methods (BFGS, L-BFGS) can be used on the log‑likelihood after imposing an identifiability constraint.

Model checking, goodness of fit, and diagnostics. Compare predicted choice probabilities to empirical frequencies (e.g., calibration plots), use held‑out likelihood to assess predictive performance, and examine residuals such as differences between observed and expected wins. For ranking data, one can compare predicted and observed pairwise preferences implied by the model. If the IIA assumption is violated, consider richer models (mixture PL, nested models) or add context/covariates to capture dependencies.

Mixture and context‑dependent extensions. When choice behavior is heterogeneous across users or contexts, mixture models of Plackett‑Luce components can capture multimodal preference populations. Context‑dependent choice models allow scores to depend on the set of available alternatives or other contextual features (e.g., generalized attraction models). These extensions increase flexibility at the cost of more complex identifiability and estimation issues.

Practical tips. Always check connectivity of the comparison graph; use small regularization to avoid divergence in the presence of near separation; enforce a normalization constraint for interpretability; start optimization from reasonable initial values (e.g., log of observed win rates); and validate models on held‑out comparisons or rankings. For very large item sets, exploit sparsity (most pairs are not directly compared) and use mini‑batch SGD or distributed optimization.

Conclusion

Bradley‑Terry and Plackett‑Luce models provide compact, interpretable, and mathematically elegant ways to turn item scores into choice probabilities via the softmax/logistic mapping. The models’ dependence only on score differences yields simplicity but introduces additive nonidentifiability that must be addressed by normalization or priors. Maximum likelihood estimation is straightforward via gradient or MM methods, but practice calls for regularization, handling of partial/tied data, and possibly richer extensions when the real choice behavior departs from the simple assumptions. Mastery of these models and their extensions gives a strong foundation for reading and contributing to modern literature on preference learning and ranking.
