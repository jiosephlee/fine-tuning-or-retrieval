Chapter 10: Entropy- and KL-Regularized RL; Control as Inference

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) traditionally aims to find a policy that maximizes expected cumulative reward. In many settings, however, adding regularization to the optimization objective produces policies that are more robust, more exploratory, or easier to optimize. Two broad and closely related regularization approaches are entropy regularization and Kullback–Leibler (KL) regularization (or constraints) relative to a reference policy. These regularizers introduce a preference for stochasticity, penalize large deviations from a prior policy, and admit an interpretation as probabilistic inference. Under mild assumptions these formulations admit closed-form optimal policies that are exponentials of the reward (or advantage) weighted by the reference distribution, leading to energy-based interpretations and clear roles for a temperature parameter $ \beta $ that controls the trade-off between reward-seeking and regularization.

This chapter develops these ideas systematically. We begin by introducing entropy-regularized objectives and the temperature parameter. We then treat KL constraints and show how Lagrangian duality converts constrained problems into penalized ones, deriving closed-form optimal policies of the form
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big).
\]
We explain the partition function and log-normalizer, and show the energy-based view that casts the policy as a Gibbs (Boltzmann) distribution. The role of the temperature $ \beta $ is explored in detail, revealing its effect on exploration–exploitation, stability, and trust-region-like behavior. Finally, we connect these ideas to maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning (MaxEnt IRL) and the broader control-as-inference perspective.

Entropy-regularized objectives and temperature

Standard RL seeks a policy $ \pi $ that maximizes expected cumulative reward. Consider a single-step decision problem or a state-action pair $(x,y)$ with immediate reward $r(x,y)$. A simple entropy-regularized objective augments expected reward with an entropy bonus:
\[
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d,\, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] + \beta \, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d}\big[ \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x))\big],
\]
where $d$ is a distribution over states (for example, a stationary distribution under some dynamics or an empirical distribution), $ \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)) = -\sum_y \pi(y\mid x)\log \pi(y\mid x)$ is the Shannon entropy of the policy at state $x$, and $ \beta\ge0$ is a temperature (or entropy weight) controlling the strength of the entropy bonus.

Intuition: The entropy term rewards stochasticity. It prevents premature convergence to deterministic policies that may be suboptimal due to lack of exploration, and smooths the optimization landscape. The temperature $ \beta $ scales how much randomness is favored: large $ \beta $ values prioritize entropy (more exploratory/uncertain policies), while small $ \beta $ values focus more on maximizing reward (more exploitative/deterministic policies).

For an isolated state $x$ where we optimize the local objective
\[
J_x(\pi(\cdot\mid x)) = \sum_y \pi(y\mid x) r(x,y) + \beta \, \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)),
\]
we can derive the optimal policy in closed form. Using calculus of variations or Lagrange multipliers enforcing $\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1$, the maximizer is the Gibbs distribution:
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) = \frac{\exp\!\big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\big)}{\sum_{y'} \exp\!\big(\frac{r(x,y')}{\beta}\big)}.
\]
Thus entropy regularization yields a Boltzmann policy: actions are sampled proportional to exponentiated reward scaled by inverse temperature $1/\beta$. In the limit $ \beta\to 0^+ $, this concentrates on maximizers of $ r(x,y)$; in the limit $ \beta\to\infty $, it approaches the uniform distribution.

This single-step result generalizes to multi-step MDPs when the entropy term is added at each time step. The dynamic programming (soft Bellman) equations replace maxima with soft-maxes (log-sum-exp), and the optimal policy becomes proportional to exponentials of advantage or state-action soft value functions divided by $ \beta $.

KL constraints to a reference policy and Lagrangian duality

Entropy regularization is one way to favor stochastic policies. Another way is to penalize, or constrain, deviations from a reference policy $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ using KL divergence. There are two closely related formulations.

The constrained form enforces a trust-region-like constraint:
\[
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d,\, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d}\big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))\big] \le \epsilon,
\]
where $ \epsilon\ge0 $ bounds how far the new policy can deviate, on average, from $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $. This is a natural constraint for policy improvement: it limits abrupt updates that might collapse performance or violate safety requirements.

The penalized form places the expectation of the KL into the objective:
\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d}\left[\sum_y \pi(y\mid x) r(x,y) - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))\right].
\]
Here $ \beta>0 $ trades off reward against deviation cost; the larger $ \beta $, the stronger the penalty for moving away from $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $.

These two are connected by Lagrangian duality. The constrained problem can be written in Lagrangian form by introducing a multiplier $ \lambda\ge0 $:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda) = -\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] + \lambda\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))\big] - \epsilon\Big).
\]
Minimizing the Lagrangian over $\lambda\ge0$ yields the constrained optimum. If strong duality holds, there exists $\lambda^\star$ such that solving the penalized problem with $\beta=\lambda^\star$ is equivalent to the constrained problem. Hence the penalized and constrained formulations are dual: the KL penalty coefficient plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier.

Closed-form optimal policy: $\pi^\star(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r/\beta)$

We now derive the closed-form solution under a per-state penalized KL objective. For a fixed state $x$, consider the per-state objective
\[
J_x(\pi(\cdot\mid x)) = \sum_y \pi(y\mid x) r(x,y) - \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)).
\]
Expanding the KL term,
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \sum_y \pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
Substitute and rearrange the objective:
\[
J_x(\pi) = \sum_y \pi(y\mid x)\big( r(x,y) - \beta\log\pi(y\mid x) + \beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big).
\]
Introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\eta$ to enforce normalization $\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1$, set derivatives w.r.t. $\pi(y\mid x)$ to zero:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial \pi(y\mid x)}\Big(J_x(\pi) + \eta\big(\sum_{y'}\pi(y'\mid x)-1\big)\Big) = r(x,y) - \beta(1+\log\pi(y\mid x)) + \beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \eta = 0.
\]
Solving for $\pi(y\mid x)$ yields
\[
\log\pi(y\mid x) = \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta} + \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \frac{\eta}{\beta} - 1,
\]
or, after exponentiating and absorbing constants into normalization,
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big).
\]
More precisely,
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\big)}{Z(x)},
\qquad
Z(x) = \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\!\big(\frac{r(x,y')}{\beta}\big).
\]
This is the central closed-form result: the optimal policy under a KL penalty to a reference is a reweighted version of the reference policy, where the weights are exponentials of the scaled reward. The reference policy can encode prior knowledge, a previous policy (leading to trust-region updates), or any baseline distribution.

Partition function and log-normalizer interpretation

The normalizing constant $Z(x)$ is commonly called the partition function or the log-normalizer (its logarithm is the log-partition or log-normalizer). It plays several roles.

First, $Z(x)$ ensures $\pi^\star$ is a valid probability distribution. Second, $\log Z(x)$ is the negative of the per-state optimal value up to constants. To see this, plug $\pi^\star$ back into the objective and compute the optimal value:
\[
J_x(\pi^\star) = \sum_y \pi^\star(y\mid x) r(x,y) - \beta \sum_y \pi^\star(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi^\star(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
With algebraic manipulation one finds
\[
J_x(\pi^\star) = \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Thus $\log Z(x)$ is proportional to the optimal regularized value. Equivalently, the soft value (or free energy) at state $x$ is $V(x) = \beta\log Z(x)$. This log-sum-exp structure is the smooth counterpart to the max operator: as $\beta\to 0$, $\beta\log Z(x)\to \max_y r(x,y)$; as $\beta$ increases, the soft-max becomes smoother.

From a probabilistic perspective, $Z(x)$ is also the marginal likelihood (evidence) if we treat $r(x,y)$ as an unnormalized log-probability and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as a prior over $y$. In control-as-inference, $Z(x)$ plays the role of a normalizing constant for the optimal posterior.

Energy-based view of policies and rewards

The closed-form policy admits an energy-based interpretation. Define an energy function $E(x,y)$ as
\[
E(x,y) = -r(x,y) - \beta \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x).
\]
Then the optimal policy is
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) = \frac{\exp(-E(x,y)/\beta)}{\sum_{y'} \exp(-E(x,y')/\beta)}.
\]
This is exactly a Gibbs/Boltzmann distribution over actions with temperature $ \beta $ and energy $E(x,y)$. The reward contributes a negative energy (higher reward lowers energy), and the reference policy contributes an additive energy term $-\beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that penalizes deviating from the prior. If the reference is uniform, the second term is constant and the policy reduces to $\propto \exp(r/\beta)$; if the reference concentrates mass on certain actions, the effective energy increases for actions that contradict the reference.

The energy-based viewpoint is powerful because it places policies in the same mathematical framework as many models in statistical physics and probabilistic modeling. It supports reasoning about temperature, partition functions, and sampling-based approximations. It also clarifies that learning a policy is equivalent to shaping the energy landscape so that desirable actions have low energy (high probability).

Role of $ \beta $ in exploration–exploitation trade-offs

The parameter $ \beta $ plays several interconnected roles: it scales reward relative to regularization, controls entropy indirectly, and acts as temperature in the Boltzmann policy. Consider extremes and intermediate regimes.

When $ \beta\to 0^+ $ (low temperature), the exponential weights $\exp(r/\beta)$ strongly favor the action(s) with highest reward, making the policy nearly deterministic at argmax actions. This corresponds to an exploitative regime: the policy concentrates on highest-reward choices and exploration is minimal.

When $ \beta\to\infty $ (high temperature), the exponentials flatten; if the reference is uniform, the policy tends to uniform; if the reference is nonuniform, the policy tends to the reference. This corresponds to maximal exploration and minimal sensitivity to reward.

In practice, selecting $ \beta $ sets the exploration–exploitation balance. Small $ \beta $ accelerates convergence to high-reward actions but risks getting trapped in local optima and poor estimation when reward signals are noisy. Large $ \beta $ encourages exploration and stabilizes learning by preventing overconfident updates. Temperature scheduling—annealing $ \beta $ from large to small—can allow extensive exploration early and focused exploitation later.

There is also an implicit relationship between $ \beta $ and the entropy of the optimal policy. For the KL-penalized objective, higher $ \beta $ penalizes deviation more strongly (in the penalized version, $ \beta $ was the multiplier; recall the derivation used $-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}$). If instead we view the entropy-regularized form $+\beta\mathcal{H}$, then larger entropy weight $ \beta $ increases stochasticity. Careful attention to sign and parameterization matters: some authors define the objective as reward minus $ \tau D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ (as we did) while others add $ \alpha \mathcal{H}$. The temperature often appears as $ \tau $ or $ \alpha $ inversely in different papers; interpret it through the Gibbs form: $\pi\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$, where $ \beta $ in the denominator of exponent is the temperature controlling concentration.

Trust-region interpretations and stability

KL-regularization has a natural trust-region interpretation. When optimizing policies iteratively, one can take the reference $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ to be the previous policy $ \pi_{\text{old}} $. Constraining the KL divergence between successive policies enforces gradual updates, preventing wild policy shifts that might drastically reduce performance in on-policy evaluation or in environments with sensitive dynamics.

Algorithmically, many policy optimization methods use KL regularization or constraints. For example, Relative Entropy Policy Search (REPS) enforces a bound on expected KL and solves for the optimal policy in closed form, yielding a reweighted policy with exponential weights. Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) uses a constrained optimization with a KL constraint between policies, though it uses an approximate linearized objective and quadratic constraint. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) implements a clipped surrogate objective that roughly enforces a trust region. In all these, the underlying idea is the same: control step size in policy space by bounding divergence, and obtain stability and monotonic improvement guarantees under suitable assumptions.

From the Lagrangian viewpoint, the KL penalty coefficient plays the role of a trust-region stiffness: large penalty means small steps from the reference. In practical implementations, $ \beta $ (or the dual variable) can be adapted dynamically, for instance by adjusting the Lagrange multiplier to meet a desired KL threshold. This yields algorithms that automatically modulate how conservative updates are.

Connections to maximum entropy IRL

Maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning (MaxEnt IRL) frames the inverse problem—recovering reward functions from demonstrations—using maximum-entropy principles. The core idea is to assume demonstrators act stochastically in a way that maximizes expected reward while remaining as random as possible given constraints: among distributions that match observed feature expectations, choose the maximum-entropy one.

In MaxEnt IRL, the probability of a trajectory $\tau$ under an optimal (soft) policy is proportional to $\exp(R(\tau))$ where $R(\tau)$ is cumulative reward. This parallels the control-as-inference view: the posterior over trajectories given an optimality event is a Gibbs distribution proportional to exponentiated reward. The partition function normalizes this posterior and is the marginal likelihood of optimality. Thus the same log-sum-exp, Boltzmann policy structure appears.

More concretely, if we model demonstrator behavior as sampling trajectories from
\[
p(\tau) \propto p_0(\tau)\exp\!\big(R(\tau)/\beta\big),
\]
where $p_0(\tau)$ is the trajectory distribution under some baseline dynamics and policy, then inference for rewards from demonstrations reduces to maximum likelihood estimation of $R(\tau)$, up to identifiability constraints. MaxEnt IRL and the KL-regularized policy optimization are two sides of the same coin: one infers reward from observed Boltzmann-distributed behavior, the other computes a policy that is Boltzmann-distributed given known rewards and a prior.

Control as inference

The control-as-inference framework makes the probabilistic connection explicit by augmenting the model with an optimality variable. Consider a graphical model where trajectories $\tau$ are generated by environment dynamics and policy, and there is a binary optimality variable $O$ whose likelihood depends on cumulative reward. One can define
\[
p(O=1\mid\tau) \propto \exp\!\big(R(\tau)/\beta\big).
\]
Performing inference to compute $p(\tau\mid O=1)$ yields a posterior over trajectories proportional to $p(\tau)\exp(R(\tau)/\beta)$, which is exactly the MaxEnt distribution. Consequently, optimal control reduces to finding a policy whose induced trajectory distribution matches this posterior. When using a KL penalty to a reference policy $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $, the reference appears as the prior over actions in the graphical model.

This interpretation clarifies why minimizing KL divergence to a posterior (or reweighting a prior by exponentiated reward) is natural: we are performing Bayesian conditioning on the event that trajectories are good. The variational perspective provides algorithmic tools: approximate inference (e.g., variational methods, importance sampling) gives approximate control algorithms. It also suggests ways to incorporate observation noise, latent variables, and hierarchical priors.

Putting it all together: examples and implications

Consider a concrete episodic MDP with deterministic dynamics for simplicity, where the reward is the sum of stagewise rewards $R(\tau)=\sum_t r(x_t,y_t)$. Suppose we hold fixed dynamics and seek an improved policy that stays close (in expected KL) to the current policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$. The KL-penalized objective per state and time step yields the local updates
\[
\pi^\star(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{Q^{\text{soft}}(x,y)}{\beta}\Big),
\]
where $Q^{\text{soft}}(x,y)$ is a soft state-action value that composes immediate reward and future soft values via soft Bellman backup. Algorithms such as REPS or methods derived from policy iteration with entropy/KL regularization exploit this closed form to construct improved policies analytically, often combined with function approximation to represent $Q^{\text{soft}}$.

A practical implication is that policy improvement with KL regularization can be viewed as Bayesian updating: the prior is the reference policy, the likelihood is $\exp(Q/\beta)$, and the posterior is the updated policy. This yields a principled way to interpolate between prior knowledge and reward-driven adaptation. When the reference is the previous policy, this update produces a natural, tempered improvement step.

Another implication arises for exploration: using an explicit entropy bonus or keeping a high $ \beta $ makes it likely the policy will continue sampling diverse actions, improving gradient estimates and reducing variance. At the same time, too high a $ \beta $ can prevent convergence to high-reward policies. Thus algorithmic designs often include schedules or adaptive mechanisms for $ \beta $ and for dual variables enforcing KL constraints.

Conclusion

Entropy and KL regularization provide principled and practically useful ways to shape policy learning. They lead to policies that are Gibbs distributions proportional to a reference times the exponentiated reward, with a partition function (log-normalizer) that plays the role of a soft value function. Lagrangian duality links KL-constrained problems to penalized formulations, yielding interpretable multipliers that control trust-region behavior. The energy-based perspective unifies rewards and priors into a single energy function, and the temperature $ \beta $ dictates the degree of concentration and exploration. Finally, the control-as-inference viewpoint and connections to MaxEnt IRL show that regularized control is fundamentally a probabilistic inference problem, which opens avenues for leveraging inference techniques for control and for interpreting demonstrations as samples from Boltzmann policies.

Understanding these relationships equips the reader to read and derive algorithms that balance exploration, prior knowledge, and reward maximization while maintaining stability and interpretability. Whether used to design trust-region policy optimization methods, to justify entropy bonuses, or to frame IRL problems, the entropy- and KL-regularized formulations form a central part of modern RL theory and practice.
