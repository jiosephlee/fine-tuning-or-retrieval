Introduction

Preference modeling formalizes how choices, comparisons, and rankings arise from underlying utilities or scores. Two classical probabilistic models that map latent scores to observable preferences are the Bradley–Terry model for pairwise comparisons and the Plackett–Luce model for full or partial rankings. These models are widely used in psychology, economics, recommender systems, and machine learning because they are interpretable, mathematically tractable, and extendable to richer settings. This chapter presents the construction of these models, their statistical estimation through maximum likelihood, identifiability issues and how to resolve them, ways to incorporate regularization and calibration of latent scores, extensions to handling ties and indifference, asymptotic properties such as consistency, and practical optimization and computational considerations for fitting the models to data.

Bradley–Terry pairwise preference model

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model describes the probability that one item is preferred over another using latent positive-valued scores. Let there be $n$ items indexed by $i=1,\dots,n$. Associate each item $i$ with a latent positive parameter $w_i>0$, often interpreted as a skill, utility, or attractiveness. For a pairwise comparison between items $i$ and $j$, the BT model defines
\[
P(i\ \text{beats}\ j) = \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j}.
\]
Equivalently, one can work on a log-score scale by writing $w_i = \exp(\theta_i)$ with real-valued $\theta_i\in\mathbb{R}$. Then
\[
P(i\ \text{beats}\ j) = \frac{\exp(\theta_i)}{\exp(\theta_i)+\exp(\theta_j)} = \frac{1}{1+\exp(\theta_j-\theta_i)}.
\]
This logistic form shows that BT is closely related to logistic regression for pairwise differences.

The BT model has a simple interpretation: the odds that $i$ beats $j$ equal $w_i/w_j$. When $w_i=w_j$, outcomes are equally likely; larger differences increase the win probability. The model assumes independence of comparisons conditional on the latent parameters (i.e., no context effects or sequence effects), and it is invariant to multiplying all $w_i$ by a common positive constant, a point we will return to under identifiability.

Data are often observed as counts: for each unordered pair $(i,j)$ we observe $y_{ij}$ successes of $i$ over $j$ and $y_{ji}$ successes of $j$ over $i$. Under the BT model, each comparison between $i$ and $j$ is a Bernoulli trial with success probability $p_{ij}=w_i/(w_i+w_j)$, independent across repeated comparisons. The log-likelihood for parameters $w$ (or $\theta$) becomes a sum over observed pairwise outcomes:
\[
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} \big( y_{ij} \log p_{ij} + y_{ji} \log p_{ji} \big) = \sum_{i<j} \big( y_{ij} (\theta_i - \log(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_j})) + y_{ji} (\theta_j - \log(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_j}))\big),
\]
which simplifies using $y_{ij}+y_{ji}=n_{ij}$, the number of comparisons between $i$ and $j$.

Plackett–Luce ranking model and generalizations

The Plackett–Luce (PL) model generalizes BT to model full rankings (listwise choice). It posits that each item $i$ has a positive score $w_i$, and a permutation (ranking) over a set of items is generated by an iterative selection (multinomial choice) process known as "selection without replacement": at the first position choose item $i$ with probability proportional to $w_i$; remove $i$ and repeat among the remaining items for the second position, and so on.

Formally, for a ranking that orders items as $(i_1, i_2, \dots, i_k)$ over a subset of items $S = \{i_1,\dots,i_k\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, the PL probability is
\[
P(i_1 \succ i_2 \succ \cdots \succ i_k) = \prod_{t=1}^k \frac{w_{i_t}}{\sum_{j\in S_t} w_j},
\]
where $S_t$ denotes the set of items not yet selected at stage $t$ (initially $S_1 = S$). For a full ranking over all $n$ items, this product runs to $n$ and the denominators are sums over decreasing subsets.

When $k=2$ and the ranking is just a pairwise preference $i_1\succ i_2$, the PL model yields $w_{i_1}/(w_{i_1}+w_{i_2})$, so PL reduces to BT on pairs. PL can also model top-$k$ lists (partial rankings), and observed lists of varying length, by truncating the product appropriately and interpreting absent items as unranked at the bottom.

Generalizations and extensions include:
- Context-dependent PL where scores depend on covariates or on both item and user features, i.e., $w_{i,u}=\exp(\phi(x_{i},u)^\top\beta)$.
- Mixtures of PL components to capture heterogeneity across users or contexts, leading to finite mixture models with identifiability caveats.
- Nested or stagewise PL where choices occur within categories or under constraints, and the denominators sum only items available at each stage.
- The Multinomial Logit (MNL) interpretation: PL arises when choices follow a Luce choice axiom applied sequentially, and equivalently if each item has a random utility $U_i=\theta_i+\varepsilon_i$ with i.i.d. Gumbel noise, then choosing the maximum yields MNL; sequential application gives PL.

Maximum likelihood estimation for preference models

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the most common approach for fitting BT and PL. For BT, with observations $y_{ij}$ as counts, the log-likelihood in terms of $\theta=\log w$ is
\[
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i<j} \big( y_{ij}(\theta_i - \log(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_j})) + y_{ji}(\theta_j - \log(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_j}))\big).
\]
It is convenient to express derivatives. The gradient with respect to $\theta_i$ is
\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_i} = \sum_{j\neq i} \big( y_{ij} - n_{ij} \frac{e^{\theta_i}}{e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_j}} \big) = \sum_{j\neq i} \big( y_{ij} - n_{ij} p_{ij} \big),
\]
where $n_{ij}=y_{ij}+y_{ji}$. Setting gradients to zero yields the MLE equations, nonlinear in $\theta$. No closed-form solution exists in general, but the log-likelihood is concave in $\theta$ up to an additive constant constraint (see identifiability), so global optimization methods apply.

For PL, given observed rankings (possibly partial), the log-likelihood is a sum over rankings of terms of the form $\sum_{t} \log w_{i_t} - \log \sum_{j\in S_t} w_j$. Writing in log-parameters $\theta_i=\log w_i$, the log-likelihood becomes
\[
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{\text{rankings}} \sum_{t} \big( \theta_{i_t} - \log \sum_{j\in S_t} e^{\theta_j} \big).
\]
The gradient w.r.t. $\theta_i$ is the observed count of times item $i$ appears in any position minus the expected count under the model, where expectations involve softmax terms over relevant subsets. The log-likelihood is concave in $\theta$ (in fact, PL is a member of the class of exponential family models with convex negative log-likelihood), so MLE is unique up to additive constants on $\theta$.

Algorithms for computing the MLE include iterative scaling, minorize–maximize (MM) algorithms, iterative reweighted least squares (IRLS) variants, gradient ascent (or descent on negative log-likelihood) with line search, and quasi-Newton methods like BFGS. For PL specifically, the MM algorithm popularized by Hunter provides a simple multiplicative update on the $w$ values that guarantees monotone improvement and converges to the MLE under connectedness conditions.

Identifiability and equivalence up to additive constants

Both BT and PL are invariant to multiplying all scores $w_i$ by a common positive scalar. In log parameters, $\theta_i$ are identifiable only up to an additive constant: if $\theta$ is a parameter vector, then $\theta + c\mathbf{1}$ produces identical probabilities for any scalar $c\in\mathbb{R}$. Therefore the parameter space has a one-dimensional subspace of equivalent representations. To ensure identifiability one typically imposes a constraint such as $\sum_i \theta_i = 0$ (zero-sum constraint) or fixes one parameter, e.g., $\theta_1 = 0$, or enforces $\prod_i w_i = 1$ or $\sum_i w_i = 1$ depending on interpretation.

Identifiability also depends on the data: the comparison graph must be connected. For pairwise data, construct an undirected graph with nodes for items and edges for pairs that were compared at least once. If the graph has multiple connected components, parameters across different components cannot be compared — each component's scores are independent up to separate additive constants. Thus a necessary and sufficient condition for a unique MLE (up to the global additive constant) is that the comparison graph be connected and that sufficient variation in comparisons exists to estimate relative strengths.

Regularization and calibration of latent scores

In practice, data are often sparse or noisy, and unconstrained MLE can overfit or produce extreme parameter estimates, particularly when some items never lose or never win (leading to infinite MLEs). Regularization stabilizes estimation by introducing a prior or penalty. Common approaches include ridge (L2) and L1 penalties on $\theta$ or penalizing deviations from a baseline. For example, maximize penalized log-likelihood
\[
\ell(\theta) - \lambda \sum_i (\theta_i - \mu)^2,
\]
where $\mu$ may be zero or another reference. In the multiplicative $w$ scale, a natural prior is Gamma on $w_i$, which corresponds to an L2-type penalty on $\theta$ but with different curvature near zero. Bayesian formulations place priors on $w$ or $\theta$; conjugate priors for BT are available in certain parameterizations.

Calibration refers to mapping latent scores to interpretable scales, for instance converting log-skill differences to expected win probabilities or to Elo-like rating systems. Because of the additive invariance, calibration adds meaning by fixing a reference and possibly rescaling. One may enforce a scale (e.g., standard deviation of $\theta$ equals one) or choose an interpretable unit such as Elo points by mapping a specified log-odds difference to a predetermined number of points.

Regularization also resolves infinite estimates arising from "separation" events (an item undefeated). Adding a small pseudocount to each pair or a prior on $w$ prevents parameters from diverging. From a Bayesian perspective, the posterior remains proper with appropriate priors even when the likelihood alone would be unbounded.

Handling ties and indifference

Real-world comparisons often result in ties or reported indifference. Several approaches extend BT/PL to handle ties.

For pairwise ties, one option is to introduce an explicit tie parameter $\tau\ge0$. The Davidson model generalizes BT by specifying probabilities for three outcomes between $i$ and $j$:
\[
P(i\ \text{wins}) = \frac{w_i}{w_i + w_j + \tau\sqrt{w_i w_j}}, \quad
P(j\ \text{wins}) = \frac{w_j}{w_i + w_j + \tau\sqrt{w_i w_j}}, \quad
P(\text{tie}) = \frac{\tau\sqrt{w_i w_j}}{w_i + w_j + \tau\sqrt{w_i w_j}}.
\]
The $\sqrt{w_i w_j}$ term ensures symmetry and sensible behavior: when $w_i=w_j$ ties are relatively more probable according to $\tau$. The tie parameter can be estimated jointly with $w$ via MLE.

For PL and ties in rankings (e.g., partial orders or weak orders), one can adapt the generative process to allow groups of items to be tied at the same rank. One model treats ties as simultaneous selection: at a stage pick a subset of items according to weights, where the probability of selecting subset $T$ from set $S$ is proportional to some product or sum of weights; multiple formulations exist, but many become combinatorially complex. A practical approach is to treat tied items as having been ordered arbitrarily (random tie breaking), and correct standard errors for the ignored tie structure, or to use models specifically designed for ties, such as extensions of PL that assign probability proportional to a symmetric function over tied items. Another approach is to model an explicit threshold on latent utilities: declare items tied when their latent utilities differ by less than a tolerance, but such models complicate likelihoods and inference.

Indifference in choice modeling can also be modeled by introducing a third decision such as "no preference", handled similarly to ties with an extra parameter or by modeling utilities with measurement noise leading to probability mass for indifference.

Consistency and statistical properties

Statistical properties of the MLE for BT and PL mirror those of general maximum likelihood estimators under regularity conditions. Under correct model specification, the MLE is consistent: as the amount of data increases (e.g., number of independent comparisons grows), the estimator converges in probability to the true parameters, modulo the additive constant indeterminacy. More precisely, if data are generated from a BT or PL model with true parameter $\theta^*$ and the comparison graph is sufficiently connected and sampling covers edges appropriately, then the MLE $\hat\theta$ (with an identifiability constraint) satisfies $\hat\theta \xrightarrow{p} \theta^*$.

Asymptotic normality also holds: $\sqrt{N}(\hat\theta - \theta^*)$ converges in distribution to a multivariate normal with covariance given by the inverse Fisher information matrix projected onto the identifiable parameter subspace. Estimating standard errors requires care because of the additive invariance; one must compute covariance in the constrained parameter space (e.g., by fixing one parameter or using a generalized inverse).

However, asymptotic results assume the number of items $n$ is fixed while the number of comparisons grows. When both $n$ and the number of observations per pair grow, rates and consistency can differ. In modern applications with very large item sets and sparse comparisons, classical asymptotics may be less informative. Recent research studies high-dimensional regimes, the effect of graph topology on estimation error, and minimax rates for ranking from comparisons.

Consistency can fail if the model is misspecified, for example when context effects or dependence between comparisons violate model assumptions. Robustness studies consider quasi-likelihood or estimation under partial misspecification.

Optimization and computational considerations

Fitting BT and PL at scale requires attention to optimization algorithms, numerical stability, and data sparsity.

Concavity and global optima. The log-likelihood for BT and PL, when written in $\theta=\log w$, is concave (strictly concave on the identifiable subspace) which implies any local maximizer is global within that subspace. This is a major computational advantage. For BT, the Hessian is negative semidefinite; for PL, similar properties hold. These permit use of stable optimization methods.

Algorithms. Common methods include:
- Iterative scaling / MM algorithms. An MM algorithm constructs a surrogate function that is easier to maximize. For BT, Hunter's MM updates for $w$ are multiplicative and take the form
\[
w_i^{(t+1)} = w_i^{(t)} \frac{\sum_{j} y_{ij}}{\sum_{j} n_{ij} \frac{w_i^{(t)}}{w_i^{(t)} + w_j^{(t)}}},
\]
which intuitively scales $w_i$ by observed wins over expected wins. These updates guarantee monotone increase in likelihood and converge under connectedness.
- Gradient-based methods. Compute gradient and optionally Hessian (or Fisher information) and apply Newton or quasi-Newton methods. Newton's method converges rapidly near the optimum, but each iteration can be expensive because the Hessian is dense for PL (since denominators involve sums over many items). Quasi-Newton (BFGS, L-BFGS) provides good practical performance with moderate memory.
- Iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). For BT, because of logistic-like form, IRLS-like updates approximate the problem as weighted least squares and can be efficient.
- Stochastic gradient descent (SGD). In very large-scale problems with streaming comparisons or massive item sets, SGD on the negative log-likelihood with minibatches is effective. Careful learning rate schedules and variance reduction techniques (e.g., Adam, SVRG) improve convergence.
- Coordinate ascent or projected gradient methods when constraints (e.g., sum-to-zero) are imposed.

Computational complexity. The cost per iteration depends on the data and model. For BT with sparse pairwise counts, computing the gradient costs O(m) where m is the number of observed pair types (nonzero edges). For PL with full rankings over n items and r rankings, naive gradient computation can be O(r n^2) because each ranked list involves denominators summing over remaining items, but with appropriate bookkeeping these can be reduced to O(r n) when lists are long: computing cumulative denominators and their contributions allows linear-time updates per list. For partial rankings or top-k lists, costs scale with total observed positions.

Sparse and distributed implementations. In recommender and web-scale settings, item sets are large but each ranking or comparison involves a small subset. Sparse data structures and map-reduce or parameter-server architectures help distribute gradient computations. Ensuring consistent parameter updates in distributed environments may require asynchronous algorithms or locking, with trade-offs in convergence speed and accuracy.

Dealing with degeneracies and numerical issues. When items have extreme observed records (always win or always lose), unconstrained MLE can diverge. Regularization or priors (e.g., add small pseudocounts) mitigate this. In implementations, working in the log-parameterization avoids underflow/overflow when $w$ vary across many orders of magnitude. Computing denominators as log-sum-exp improves stability for large negative or positive $\theta$.

Model selection and diagnostics. Comparing BT and PL versions, or choosing model complexity (e.g., mixture components, covariate effects), uses likelihood-based criteria (AIC, BIC), cross-validation, and predictive performance measures on held-out comparisons or rankings. Residual analysis can check fit; for PL, one can compute expected position counts and compare to observed frequencies. Goodness-of-fit tests and bootstrap methods provide uncertainty quantification.

Practical tips. Center parameters to enforce identifiability (e.g., subtract mean of $\theta$ each iteration). Initialize parameters sensibly, for example by method-of-moments approximations: for BT, take $\hat\theta_i = \log \sum_j y_{ij} - \log \sum_j y_{ji}$ as a crude start. Use sparse linear algebra when calculating Hessian-vector products for Newton-like methods. Monitor convergence using both parameter changes and changes in log-likelihood. When fitting mixtures, use multiple restarts to avoid poor local optima.

Conclusion

The Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models provide foundational probabilistic frameworks for interpreting pairwise and listwise preference data through latent scores. Their conceptual simplicity—the mapping from positive scores to choice probabilities via ratios—yields interpretable parameters and tractable inference. While both models are invariant to a common scaling of scores, practical identifiability is handled through constraints or priors. Maximum likelihood estimation, augmented with regularization or Bayesian priors, furnishes consistent and asymptotically normal estimators under standard conditions. Extensions to accommodate ties, covariates, mixtures, and partial rankings broaden applicability, but they introduce statistical and computational complexities that require careful modeling choices.

From an applied standpoint, efficient algorithms—MM updates, quasi-Newton methods, and stochastic optimization—combined with sparse implementations make BT and PL suitable for large-scale use. Understanding the models’ assumptions, identifiability constraints, and the nature of data (connectivity of comparisons, ties, and sparsity) is crucial to obtaining reliable estimates and valid uncertainty quantification. Armed with these models, researchers can interpret preferences, compare items or agents, predict rankings, and build downstream decision systems grounded in a probabilistic theory of choice.
