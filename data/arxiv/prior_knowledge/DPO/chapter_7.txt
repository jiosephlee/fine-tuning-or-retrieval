Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies how an agent should act in an environment to maximize cumulative rewards. Unlike supervised learning, where the correct outputs are provided for training examples, RL focuses on sequential decision making: the agent's actions influence not only immediate rewards but also the future states from which it will receive further rewards. This chapter presents the essential mathematical and conceptual foundations you will need to read many research papers in modern RL. We begin by formalizing the environment as a Markov decision process (MDP). We then define the objective functions used in RL, introduce how returns are computed in both episodic and continuing settings, and explain different ways to define policies, including parameterized stochastic policies. Building on those definitions, we derive the policy gradient theorem and the REINFORCE algorithm, and we examine common variance-reduction techniques such as baseline subtraction and advantage estimation. Finally, we discuss two practical regularization tools—entropy regularization and Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalties—that appear frequently in contemporary RL methods.

# Markov decision processes (states, actions, rewards, policies)

The Markov decision process (MDP) is the standard mathematical model for the environment in reinforcement learning. An MDP is defined by a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of states, $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of actions, $P$ is the transition probability kernel, $R$ is the reward function (or distribution), and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ is the discount factor (more on $\gamma$ later). At each discrete time step $t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ the agent observes a state $S_t \in \mathcal{S}$, selects an action $A_t \in \mathcal{A}$ according to its policy, then the environment emits a next state $S_{t+1}$ sampled from the transition distribution $P(\cdot \mid S_t, A_t)$ and a scalar reward $R_{t+1}$ sampled from some reward distribution or given by a deterministic function $r(S_t, A_t, S_{t+1})$.

The Markov property is the key structural assumption: the next state $S_{t+1}$ and future rewards depend on the past only through the current state and action $(S_t, A_t)$, not on earlier history. This assumption makes dynamic reasoning tractable because it allows us to compress all relevant information into the current state variable.

The policy is the agent's behavior rule. A policy $\pi$ specifies how the agent chooses actions given states. In the general case we consider stochastic policies, which are conditional probability distributions $\pi(a \mid s)$ giving the probability (or density) of selecting action $a$ when in state $s$. If the policy is deterministic, we write $a = \mu(s)$ for some function $\mu: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$. Policies can be stationary (the same at all time steps) or time-dependent; in most RL theory and practice we focus on stationary policies.

Two other central concepts are the state distribution induced by a policy and the transition dynamics under a policy. Starting from some initial state distribution $S_0 \sim \rho_0$, the policy together with the transition kernel induces a joint distribution over state-action trajectories $(S_0, A_0, S_1, A_1, \dots)$. Many objective functions and expectations in RL are defined with respect to this induced trajectory distribution. Understanding how changing the policy changes that distribution is the heart of policy optimization.

# Return, discounted reward, and episodic vs continuing tasks

The core objective in RL is to maximize cumulative reward over time, but there are multiple mathematically convenient ways to formalize "cumulative". The return is the total reward (or weighted total) from a given time step onward. Given a sequence of future rewards $R_{t+1}, R_{t+2}, \dots$, the undiscounted finite-horizon return from time $t$ to time $T$ is simply $\sum_{k=t+1}^{T} R_k$. However, in infinite-horizon problems we typically use a discounted return to ensure the sum remains finite and to prefer immediate rewards over far-future rewards.

The discounted return from time $t$ is defined as
$G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{k} R_{t+k+1}$,
where $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor. The factor $\gamma^k$ geometrically downweights rewards $k$ steps into the future. When $\gamma$ is close to 1, long-term rewards are valued almost as much as immediate rewards; when $\gamma$ is small, the agent is more short-sighted. Discounting has technical benefits: if rewards are bounded and $\gamma < 1$, then $G_t$ is almost surely finite under reasonable assumptions.

Two broad categories of RL problems are episodic and continuing. In episodic tasks, interaction naturally breaks into episodes of finite length: the environment contains terminal states (or an explicit time limit) after which the process resets. For a finite episode of length $T$, one often considers the return $G_t = \sum_{k=t+1}^T R_k$ (no discounting is necessary for finiteness), or the discounted form if one wants to emphasize earlier rewards. In continuing tasks, interactions never end; the agent continuously collects rewards in an infinite-horizon process. For continuing tasks, discounting with $\gamma < 1$ is typically used both to express preference for near-term reward and to ensure that expected returns remain finite.

It is important to realize that discounting is not only a mathematical convenience; it also encodes a modeling choice about time preference. In some domains the proper objective is average reward per time step rather than discounted sum; average-reward formulations exist and are used in some research, but the discounted-return formulation is the standard starting point in much of modern RL because it makes many derivations simpler and aligns well with episodic tasks.

Two fundamental value functions quantify the quality of states and state-action pairs under a policy. The state-value function $V^{\pi}(s)$ is the expected return when starting in state $s$ and following policy $\pi$ thereafter:
$V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[ G_t \mid S_t = s \big].$
The action-value function $Q^{\pi}(s,a)$ is the expected return after taking action $a$ in state $s$ and then following $\pi$:
$Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[ G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \big].$
These functions satisfy recursive Bellman equations; for the discounted infinite-horizon case,
$V^{\pi}(s) = \sum_{a} \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a) \big( r(s,a,s') + \gamma V^{\pi}(s') \big),$
which expresses $V^{\pi}$ in terms of immediate reward plus discounted value of the next state.

Understanding returns and value functions is crucial because nearly every RL algorithm aims to find a policy that maximizes some function of these returns—most often the expected discounted return from the start distribution.

# Policy parameterization and stochastic policies

A central question in policy-based RL methods is how to represent policies. Policy parameterization means expressing the policy as a function $\pi_{\theta}(a \mid s)$ whose behavior depends on a finite-dimensional parameter vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Parameterization enables optimization: we can adjust $\theta$ to increase expected return using gradient-based methods or other numerical optimizers.

There are many choices for parameterization. For discrete action spaces, a common choice is a softmax (categorical) policy derived from a score (or preference) function $f_{\theta}(s,a)$:
$\pi_{\theta}(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp(f_{\theta}(s,a))}{\sum_{a'} \exp(f_{\theta}(s,a'))}.$
The function $f_{\theta}$ might be a linear function of features or the output of a neural network. Softmax ensures the policy assigns positive probability to each action and smoothly depends on $\theta$.

For continuous action spaces, policies are often represented as conditional probability densities, commonly Gaussian:
$\pi_{\theta}(a \mid s) = \mathcal{N}\big(a \mid \mu_{\theta}(s), \Sigma_{\theta}(s)\big),$
where the mean $\mu_{\theta}(s)$ and covariance $\Sigma_{\theta}(s)$ may be parameterized functions of state. For computational simplicity, many implementations use a diagonal covariance with state-independent variance or a learned scalar standard deviation.

Stochastic policies are particularly important for exploration and for deriving policy gradients. A deterministic policy might be optimal in some MDPs, but learning deterministic policies directly is harder for some gradient formulas because the gradient of the expected return with respect to deterministic mapping parameters must account for the dependence of future states on actions. Stochastic policies make the expectation over trajectories differentiable with respect to policy parameters, and they ensure that the agent continues exploring different actions unless the policy collapses to a point mass.

When using parameterized stochastic policies, we must think about two kinds of randomness: the environment's randomness (transition dynamics and reward noise) and the policy's own stochasticity. The combined randomness defines a distribution over trajectories, and the expected return is an expectation with respect to that distribution. Optimization proceeds by estimating gradients of this expectation with respect to $\theta$ and using stochastic gradient ascent.

Lastly, policy parameterizations induce a manifold of policies. The choice of parameterization affects optimization ease and representation capacity. For example, using a neural network with many parameters may allow representing a wide range of policies but introduces nonconvexity. Smooth parameterizations such as softmax or Gaussian ensure the log-probability derivatives used in policy-gradient estimators are well-defined, which is crucial for algorithm derivations.

# Policy gradient theorem and REINFORCE

Policy-gradient methods directly optimize the policy parameters by performing gradient ascent on an objective equal to expected return. Let $J(\theta)$ denote the expected discounted return starting from an initial state distribution $\rho_0$ under policy $\pi_{\theta}$:
$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{t} R_{t+1} \Big],$
where a trajectory $\tau$ denotes the sequence $(S_0,A_0,S_1,A_1,\dots)$ and $p_{\theta}(\tau)$ is the trajectory probability induced by $\pi_{\theta}$ and the environment dynamics.

The policy gradient theorem provides an expression for the gradient of $J(\theta)$ that is amenable to Monte Carlo estimation. The simplest and most widely cited form is
\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\bigg[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t) \, Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(S_t, A_t) \bigg].
This identity says that the gradient of expected return can be written as an expectation of the score function $\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t)$ times the action-value function $Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(S_t, A_t)$. Intuitively, if taking action $A_t$ in state $S_t$ leads to above-average return (large $Q$), we should increase the log-probability of that action; conversely, for below-average actions we should decrease it. The derivation uses the "log-likelihood trick" (also called the score-function identity) and the fact that the environment dynamics do not depend on the policy parameters $\theta$, allowing the gradient to be pushed inside the expectation over trajectories.

Although the theorem is stated in terms of the true action-value $Q^{\pi}$, in practice we rarely know $Q^{\pi}$ exactly. One basic Monte Carlo algorithm that follows directly from the theorem is REINFORCE, introduced by Williams. REINFORCE uses sample returns to estimate $Q^{\pi}$ and performs stochastic gradient ascent with the following unbiased estimator:
For a sampled episode (or partial trajectory), compute the empirical return $G_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T-1} \gamma^{k-t} R_{k+1}$ and update parameters by
\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t) \, G_t,
where $\alpha$ is a step size. If using infinite-horizon discounted returns, $G_t$ is defined with discounting; for episodic tasks $G_t$ can be the finite-horizon sum. REINFORCE is an on-policy Monte Carlo method: it requires complete sampled returns following the current policy, and its gradient estimate is unbiased but typically has high variance.

The key strengths of REINFORCE and other policy-gradient methods are generality and simplicity. They work with continuous actions, can handle parameterized stochastic policies naturally, and directly optimize the policy without requiring value-function approximation unless used for variance reduction. Their main weakness is variance: naive Monte Carlo estimates of $Q$ can be noisy, making convergence slow or unstable. The next sections describe techniques to reduce this variance and improve learning.

# Baseline subtraction and advantage estimators

A major practical challenge for policy-gradient methods is variance in gradient estimates. A standard and principled variance-reduction technique is to subtract a baseline from the action-value before multiplying by the score function. Concretely, the policy gradient theorem implies that for any function $b(s)$ independent of the action $a$,
\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\bigg[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t) \big( Q^{\pi_{\theta}}(S_t,A_t) - b(S_t) \big) \bigg].
Subtracting $b(S_t)$ does not introduce bias because $\mathbb{E}_{A_t \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid S_t)}[ \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t) ] = 0$. Intuitively, subtracting a baseline centers the $Q$ values, so that actions with above-average returns still receive positive weight but their magnitude is reduced relative to raw returns, which reduces variance.

A commonly used baseline is the state-value function $V^{\pi}(s)$, which yields the advantage function:
A^{\pi}(s,a) = Q^{\pi}(s,a) - V^{\pi}(s).
Using the advantage rather than raw $Q$ expresses how much better an action is compared to the average action at that state. Plugging the advantage into the gradient formula often results in much lower variance because it removes the portion of $Q$ that depends only on state and not on the chosen action.

In practice, we usually do not have access to the true $V^{\pi}$ either, so we approximate it with a learned value function estimate $V_{\phi}(s)$ parameterized by $\phi$ (commonly via regression using sampled returns or TD methods). Combining a parameterized value baseline with a policy parameterized by $\theta$ leads to actor–critic architectures: the actor (policy) is updated using policy-gradient steps with the advantage estimated using the critic (value function). Typical actor-critic updates use the gradient estimator
\hat{g}_t = \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(A_t \mid S_t) \, \big( G_t - V_{\phi}(S_t) \big)
for Monte Carlo targets $G_t$, or they use TD-based advantage estimates to reduce variance further.

A particularly effective low-variance advantage estimator is the generalized advantage estimator (GAE). GAE constructs an exponentially-weighted average of $n$-step TD residuals to trade off bias and variance. Define the one-step TD error (TD residual)
\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma V_{\phi}(S_{t+1}) - V_{\phi}(S_t).
Then the GAE is defined as
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},
where $\lambda \in [0,1]$ interpolates between high-variance low-bias Monte Carlo ($\lambda \to 1$) and low-variance high-bias TD(0) ($\lambda = 0$). Using GAE in actor–critic algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) often improves sample efficiency and stability. The fundamental idea behind baseline subtraction and advantage estimation is to retain unbiasedness (or controlled bias for variants) while reducing estimator variance to make policy optimization practical.

# Entropy regularization and KL penalties in RL objectives

When optimizing policies, especially parameterized ones like neural networks, it is common to add regularization terms to the objective to shape learning dynamics and encourage desirable behavior. Two widely used regularizers in modern RL are entropy regularization and Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty or constraint. Both control the distributional properties of the policy and help with exploration and stable policy updates.

Entropy regularization adds the expected policy entropy to the objective, encouraging stochasticity in the policy. The entropy of a policy at state $s$ is
\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot \mid s)) = - \sum_{a} \pi(a \mid s) \log \pi(a \mid s)
(for discrete actions; for continuous actions the sum becomes an integral and care must be taken about densities). The entropy-regularized objective becomes
J_{\text{ent}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t \big( R_{t+1} + \beta \, \mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid S_t)) \big) \Big],
where $\beta \ge 0$ weights the entropy bonus. Intuitively, adding entropy encourages the policy to remain exploratory by avoiding premature convergence to deterministic or highly concentrated distributions. This is particularly useful early in training or in multimodal action-value landscapes, where the policy should explore multiple promising actions to discover the best ones. Entropy regularization modifies the policy gradient by adding a term $\beta \nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{H}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid S_t))$, which can be computed analytically for common parametric families.

A related concept, used especially in trust-region policy optimization methods, is to penalize or constrain the KL divergence between the new policy and some reference policy (often the old policy before the update). Consider a KL penalty in the optimization objective:
\tilde{J}(\theta) = J(\theta) - \alpha \, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi_{\text{old}}}} \big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid s) \,||\, \pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s)) \big],
where $\alpha > 0$ controls the strength of the penalty and $d_{\pi_{\text{old}}}$ is a state distribution under the old policy. The KL term discourages the new policy from moving too far from the old one in a single update, which can stabilize training by preventing large destructive policy changes. An alternative is to enforce a hard constraint instead of a penalty, e.g., maximize $J(\theta)$ subject to $\mathbb{E}_{s}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta} || \pi_{\text{old}})] \le \delta$, as in Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO). Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) implements a practical surrogate that approximately enforces a trust region by clipping the policy ratio or by adding a KL penalty and tuning it adaptively.

Both entropy regularization and KL penalties can be viewed from a probabilistic inference perspective: entropy encourages high-entropy posterior policies and KL penalty constrains a new posterior to stay close to a prior (the old policy), leading to exploration–exploitation trade-offs that are well-behaved. In practice, adding an entropy bonus is straightforward and often included as a regularization term in actor–critic implementations; KL penalties or trust-region constraints are slightly more complex but yield greater stability when performing multiple gradient steps with the same batch of data.

It is important to tune the strengths of these regularizers carefully. Excessive entropy weight can prevent convergence to good deterministic behavior, while insufficient weight may lead to premature convergence and poor exploration. Similarly, overly tight KL constraints may slow learning, while too loose constraints can permit destabilizing updates.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has laid out the mathematical and conceptual building blocks for policy-based reinforcement learning. You should now understand what an MDP is, how returns and value functions are defined, why stochastic policy parameterizations are used, how policy gradients are derived and estimated (REINFORCE), how baseline subtraction and advantage estimators reduce variance, and how entropy and KL regularization shape policy learning. These ideas are the foundation for more advanced algorithms you will encounter in research papers: actor–critic methods, TRPO and PPO, modern variants that combine value-function learning with high-capacity function approximators, and methods that integrate exploration bonuses or constraints. With these fundamentals in hand, you will be able to read, implement, and critically evaluate many contemporary RL approaches.
