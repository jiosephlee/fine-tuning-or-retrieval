Introduction

Probability and statistics provide the language and tools for formulating models of data, for evaluating how well those models fit observed data, and for optimizing model parameters so that the models are most consistent with observations. Many modern machine learning methods are built directly on likelihood-based principles: we write a probability distribution for the data given parameters, form the log-likelihood, and maximize that objective (or minimize its negative). To reason correctly about these procedures we need a clear understanding of random variables and probability functions, of expectations and variances, of inequalities that control how random quantities behave, and of the mathematical properties of maximum likelihood estimators. We also need to understand how choices of parameterization can create non-identifiability or equivalence classes of functions that lead to the same predictive behavior but different parameter values.

This chapter builds those foundations. We begin with the basics of random variables, probability mass and density functions, and conditional probability. We then cover expectation, variance, and key inequalities (Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen) that are used repeatedly in proofs and intuition about concentration. Next we introduce log-likelihood and the maximum likelihood principle, derive gradients of log-likelihood objectives, and explain how to analyze them. We then specialize to binary classification models, introduce the logistic (sigmoid) function and its likelihood, and show how the binary cross-entropy loss relates to negative log-likelihood. After that we present important asymptotic properties of MLE—consistency and asymptotic normality—and sketch how the Fisher information controls estimator variance. Finally, we discuss identifiability and equivalence classes of parameterizations: what they mean, why they matter, and typical examples (label switching, scaling symmetries, and reparameterizations).

# Random variables, probability mass/density functions, conditional probability

A random variable is a mapping from outcomes of a random experiment to numerical values. Formally, if we have a sample space $\Omega$ of possible outcomes, then a real-valued random variable $X$ is a function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. The distribution of $X$ encodes the probabilities of different values (or ranges of values) that $X$ can take.

When $X$ takes values in a discrete set (for example, integers or a finite set), its distribution is described by a probability mass function (pmf) $p_X(x)=\Pr(X=x)$. The pmf satisfies $p_X(x)\ge 0$ for all $x$ and $\sum_x p_X(x)=1$. For continuous-valued random variables, we generally describe the distribution using a probability density function (pdf) $f_X(x)$ such that probabilities of events are obtained by integration: for any measurable set $A$, $\Pr(X\in A)=\int_A f_X(x)\,dx$, and $f_X(x)\ge 0$ with $\int f_X(x)\,dx=1$. When a variable has both discrete and continuous parts, its law can be described by a mixture of mass and density.

Conditional probability describes the probability of an event given that another event has occurred. For discrete variables, the conditional pmf of $X$ given event $B$ with $\Pr(B)>0$ is $\Pr(X=x\mid B)=\Pr(X=x\cap B)/\Pr(B)$. More generally, the conditional distribution of $X$ given another random variable $Y$ is a function $p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)$ such that for each fixed $y$, $p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid y)$ is a pmf or pdf and for measurable sets $A$ we have $\Pr(X\in A\mid Y=y)=\int_A p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,dx$ (when densities exist). The chain rule for joint distributions follows immediately: for discrete variables $p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)$ and for continuous variables $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)$, with straightforward continuous analogues.

Bayes' rule relates conditional probabilities in the two directions and is crucial for inference: for events or values with nonzero probability,
\[
p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)}{p_X(x)}.
\]
Here $p_X(x)=\int p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)\,dy$ is the marginal distribution of $X$. Bayes' rule is the algebraic core of Bayesian inference, but it is also useful for manipulating likelihoods and conditional statements in frequentist work.

It is important to distinguish between the joint distribution $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$, marginals $p_X(x)$ and $p_Y(y)$, and conditionals $p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)$. When modeling, we commonly specify $p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x;\theta)$, the conditional distribution of outputs given inputs parameterized by $\theta$. This is the likelihood viewed as a function of data for fixed $\theta$, and it's the starting point for maximum likelihood estimation.

# Expectation, variance, and common inequalities

Expectation (or mean) is the fundamental averaging operator for random variables. For a discrete variable $X$ with pmf $p(x)$ and a function $g(X)$, the expectation is
\[
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p(x),
\]
provided the sum converges. For a continuous variable with density $f(x)$, the expectation is
\[
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\int g(x)f(x)\,dx.
\]
Expectation has linearity: for any scalars $a,b$ and functions $g,h$, $\mathbb{E}[ag(X)+bh(X)]=a\mathbb{E}[g(X)]+b\mathbb{E}[h(X)]$. Linearity holds regardless of independence or other structure.

Variance measures dispersion around the mean. The variance of $X$ is defined as
\[
\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2],
\]
which can be expanded to the common identity
\[
\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2.
\]
Variance is nonnegative and equals zero if and only if $X$ is almost surely constant. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance and has the same units as $X$.

Several inequalities provide bounds and intuition about how random quantities behave. Markov's inequality is a simple but useful bound for nonnegative random variables: if $X\ge 0$ almost surely and $a>0$, then
\[
\Pr(X\ge a)\le \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.
\]
Markov's inequality follows immediately from $\mathbb{E}[X]\ge a\Pr(X\ge a)$ and is often used to convert bounds on expectations into tail bounds.

Chebyshev's inequality refines this for arbitrary random variables by using variance: for any $a>0$,
\[
\Pr\big(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge a\big)\le \frac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{a^2}.
\]
Chebyshev's inequality shows that if variance is small then the variable concentrates near its mean. It is particularly useful in proofs of the weak law of large numbers.

Jensen's inequality connects convexity and expectation. If $\phi$ is a convex function and $X$ is a random variable with $\mathbb{E}[|X|]<\infty$, then
\[
\phi\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)\le \mathbb{E}\big[\phi(X)\big].
\]
For a concave $\phi$ the inequality reverses. Jensen's inequality is essential when reasoning about transformations of expectations, such as deriving lower bounds or proving the convexity/concavity of risk functionals.

Other concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Bernstein, Chernoff bounds) give exponentially decaying tails for sums of independent bounded or sub-Gaussian variables; these are advanced tools that are often used in theoretical analyses of estimators and learning algorithms. The basic idea behind these bounds is to control the moment generating function or to use martingale methods, but Markov/Chebyshev/Jensen are the most basic tools one should master first.

# Log-likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

The likelihood function is the joint probability (or density) of observed data viewed as a function of the model parameters. Suppose we observe data points $D=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ that are modeled as independent draws from a distribution with density or pmf $p(x;\theta)$ parameterized by $\theta\in\Theta$. The likelihood is
\[
L(\theta;D)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i;\theta).
\]
Because products of many factors can be numerically small, and because optimization is simpler, we usually work with the log-likelihood:
\[
\ell(\theta;D)=\log L(\theta;D)=\sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i;\theta).
\]
Maximizing the likelihood and maximizing the log-likelihood are equivalent because the logarithm is a strictly increasing function.

Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the parameter value $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}$ that maximizes the log-likelihood:
\[
\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta\in\Theta}\ \ell(\theta;D).
\]
Optimizing the log-likelihood often involves computing gradients and Hessians. The score function is the gradient of the log-likelihood:
\[
s(\theta)=\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;D)=\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla_\theta \log p(x_i;\theta).
\]
Setting the score to zero gives the first-order condition for a (local) maximum or minimum. The observed information matrix is the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood:
\[
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\theta)=-\nabla_\theta^2 \ell(\theta;D).
\]
At a local maximum, the observed information is positive semidefinite; at a strict local maximum it is positive definite.

When deriving gradients, it is often convenient to use the identity
\[
\nabla_\theta \log p(x;\theta)=\frac{\nabla_\theta p(x;\theta)}{p(x;\theta)},
\]
so that expectations with respect to the model can be computed. For example, the expected score under the model satisfies
\[
\mathbb{E}_\theta[s(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[\nabla_\theta \log p(X;\theta)\big]=\nabla_\theta \int p(x;\theta)\,dx=\nabla_\theta 1 = 0,
\]
so the score has mean zero under the generative distribution. This property underpins many theoretical results about MLE.

Log-likelihood methods extend naturally to conditional models used for supervised learning. If the training data are pairs $(x_i,y_i)$ modeled with conditional density $p(y\mid x;\theta)$ (and possibly with an independent marginal model for $x$ or treating $x$ as fixed), the conditional log-likelihood is
\[
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i\mid x_i;\theta),
\]
and we maximize this with respect to $\theta$. This conditional likelihood viewpoint is the standard one for discriminative models such as logistic regression, neural classifiers, and conditional random fields.

# Binary classification likelihoods and the logistic (sigmoid) function

In binary classification we have labels $y\in\{0,1\}$ (or sometimes $\{-1,1\}$). A common approach is to model the conditional probability of the positive class given features $x$ using a function parameterized by $\theta$. The canonical logistic model specifies
\[
\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x;\theta)=\sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big),
\]
where $f(x;\theta)$ is a real-valued score (often linear in parameters, e.g., $f(x;\theta)=\theta^\top x$) and $\sigma$ is the logistic (sigmoid) function:
\[
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
The logistic function maps real numbers to $(0,1)$, is monotone increasing, and has the useful identity
\[
\sigma(-z)=1-\sigma(z),
\]
so it produces complementary probabilities for the two classes. Algebraically, $\sigma(z)$ can also be written as $e^{z}/(1+e^{z})$, which will appear naturally when deriving likelihoods.

Given $\Pr(Y=1\mid x;\theta)=\sigma(f(x;\theta))$, the conditional probability mass function for label $y$ is
\[
p(y\mid x;\theta)=\sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big)^y\big(1-\sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big)\big)^{1-y},\qquad y\in\{0,1\}.
\]
This compact form is convenient for log-likelihood derivations. For a dataset $(x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n$, the conditional log-likelihood is
\[
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n \big[y_i\log\sigma(f(x_i;\theta))+(1-y_i)\log\big(1-\sigma(f(x_i;\theta))\big)\big].
\]
If $f(x;\theta)=\theta^\top x$ (linear logistic regression) then $f$ is linear and the log-likelihood is a concave function of $\theta$, which guarantees a unique global maximizer if the design matrix satisfies mild conditions.

The derivative of the logistic function is simple and crucial for gradient-based optimization:
\[
\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big).
\]
This relation simplifies gradients because the derivative can be expressed in terms of the sigmoid itself. Using the chain rule, for a parameterized score $f(x;\theta)$,
\[
\nabla_\theta \sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big)=\sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big)\big(1-\sigma\big(f(x;\theta)\big)\big)\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta).
\]
This structure yields clean expressions for the score function and the gradient of the conditional log-likelihood, as we show next.

# Binary cross-entropy loss and relation to log-likelihood

The negative conditional log-likelihood for binary labels is commonly called the binary cross-entropy loss (or logistic loss). Given the model $p(y\mid x;\theta)$ above, the negative log-likelihood for one example is
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(\theta;x,y)=-\big[y\log\sigma(f(x;\theta))+(1-y)\log\big(1-\sigma(f(x;\theta))\big)\big].
\]
A common alternate form (when labels are in $\{0,1\}$) writes the loss as
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(\theta;x,y)=-y\log p_\theta(x)-(1-y)\log(1-p_\theta(x)),
\]
where $p_\theta(x)=\sigma(f(x;\theta))$. For labels encoded as $y\in\{-1,1\}$ there is an equivalent expression using the logistic function of the product $yf(x;\theta)$.

The name cross-entropy comes from information theory: for a true label distribution $q$ (which for a single example puts probability 1 on the observed label and 0 on the other) and a predicted distribution $p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$, the cross-entropy is $H(q,p_\theta)=-\sum_{y} q(y)\log p_\theta(y\mid x)$. For the one-hot true label this reduces exactly to the negative log-likelihood above. Minimizing the expected cross-entropy over the data-generating distribution is equivalent to minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the true conditional distribution to the model, which motivates using cross-entropy for probabilistic classification.

To see the gradient of the negative log-likelihood, compute the derivative for a single example with $p_\theta=\sigma(f(x;\theta))$. Using the chain rule and the derivative $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, one gets
\[
\nabla_\theta \ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(\theta;x,y) = -\bigg(\frac{y}{p_\theta}-\frac{1-y}{1-p_\theta}\bigg)\nabla_\theta p_\theta.
\]
Because $\nabla_\theta p_\theta=\sigma'(f)\nabla_\theta f=\;p_\theta(1-p_\theta)\nabla_\theta f$, the factor simplifies:
\[
-\bigg(\frac{y}{p_\theta}-\frac{1-y}{1-p_\theta}\bigg)p_\theta(1-p_\theta)\nabla_\theta f = -(y-(p_\theta))\nabla_\theta f,
\]
so the gradient is
\[
\nabla_\theta \ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(\theta;x,y) = \big(p_\theta-y\big)\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta).
\]
This compact form is central: the gradient is the prediction error $p_\theta-y$ times the derivative of the score. For a linear score $f(x;\theta)=\theta^\top x$ we get the familiar gradient $(\sigma(\theta^\top x)-y)x$.

Summing over all training examples yields the total gradient for the negative log-likelihood:
\[
\nabla_\theta \ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(\sigma(f(x_i;\theta))-y_i\big)\nabla_\theta f(x_i;\theta).
\]
Setting this to zero gives the likelihood equations for logistic regression; because the negative log-likelihood is convex in $\theta$ for linear $f$ there is a unique minimizer. The derivation above shows why stochastic gradient descent or other first-order optimization methods have a simple interpretation in probabilistic classification: each example contributes an error term times the local derivative of the model.

The connection between cross-entropy and log-likelihood goes beyond algebra. Maximizing likelihood is equivalent to minimizing average cross-entropy loss on the training set. In expectation (over the true data distribution), the expected negative log-likelihood equals the cross-entropy between the true conditional distribution and the model distribution, and minimizing that expected loss is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between the true and model conditional distributions. Thus cross-entropy is a principled objective for probabilistic classification.

# Properties of MLE: consistency, asymptotic normality

Maximum likelihood estimators enjoy desirable large-sample properties under regularity conditions. Two of the most important are consistency and asymptotic normality.

Consistency means that as the number of independent observations $n\to\infty$, the MLE $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}$ converges in probability to the true parameter value $\theta_0$ (assuming the data are generated from the model $p(x;\theta_0)$). Formally,
\[
\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}\xrightarrow{p}\theta_0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.
\]
Intuitively, with more data the likelihood concentrates around the parameter that generated the data, and the global maximizer of the likelihood converges to that generating parameter. Proving consistency requires assumptions such as identifiability (the true parameter is unique in producing the distribution), that the parameter space is compact or that the likelihood is well behaved, and that the log-likelihood satisfies a uniform law of large numbers allowing exchange of limits. Consistency gives reassurance that with enough data the MLE will recover the true model (up to identifiability issues discussed below).

Asymptotic normality describes the distribution of the scaled estimation error $\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}-\theta_0)$ as $n$ becomes large. Under standard regularity conditions (differentiability of the log-likelihood, non-singularity of the Fisher information, and some moment conditions), the MLE is asymptotically normal:
\[
\sqrt{n}\big(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}-\theta_0\big)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\theta_0)\big),
\]
where $\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)$ is the Fisher information matrix evaluated at the true parameter. Consequently,
\[
\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}}\approx\mathcal{N}\Big(\theta_0,\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\theta_0)\Big)
\]
for large $n$. This approximation provides standard errors and confidence intervals for $\theta$.

The Fisher information has two equivalent forms under regularity: the expected information and the observed information. For a single observation with log-likelihood $\ell(\theta;X)=\log p(X;\theta)$, the Fisher information matrix is
\[
\mathcal{I}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;X)\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta;X)^\top\big] = -\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta^2 \ell(\theta;X)\big],
\]
where the expectations are taken under $p(x;\theta)$. For independent observations, Fisher information adds: the information for $n$ i.i.d. observations is $n\mathcal{I}(\theta)$, which is why the estimator variance scales as $1/n$.

Derivation intuition: a Taylor expansion of the score around the true parameter gives
\[
0=\nabla_\theta \ell(\hat\theta)=\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_0)+\nabla_\theta^2 \ell(\tilde\theta)(\hat\theta-\theta_0),
\]
for some $\tilde\theta$ between $\hat\theta$ and $\theta_0$. After scaling by $\sqrt{n}$ and using that $\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_0)$ is a sum of i.i.d. mean-zero terms (so by the central limit theorem it is approximately Gaussian with covariance $n\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)$) and that $\nabla_\theta^2 \ell(\tilde\theta)/n$ converges to $-\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)$, one obtains the asymptotic normality result above.

The asymptotic covariance $\mathcal{I}^{-1}(\theta_0)/n$ is the Cramér–Rao lower bound: under regularity conditions, no unbiased estimator can have smaller asymptotic covariance than the inverse Fisher information. MLE is asymptotically efficient in many settings, attaining this bound.

In practice, we estimate the Fisher information at the MLE using either the observed information $-\nabla^2\ell(\hat\theta)$ or the sample covariance of the scores, and use that to form approximate standard errors and confidence intervals. The adequacy of asymptotic approximations depends on $n$ and on how well the regularity conditions hold.

# Identifiability and equivalence classes of parameterizations

Identifiability concerns whether different parameter values produce distinct probability distributions. A model is identifiable if the mapping from parameters to distributions is injective: formally, if $p(x;\theta_1)=p(x;\theta_2)$ for all $x$ implies $\theta_1=\theta_2$. If this property fails, different parameter values can be observationally indistinguishable, and consistent estimation of a single true parameter value becomes impossible—only the equivalence class of parameters that give the same distribution can be inferred.

There are two related but distinct notions to keep clear: identifiability of the parameterization and identifiability of the function or predictive distribution. Sometimes we do not care about recovering the parameter $\theta$ itself, but rather about recovering the predictive distribution or function $x\mapsto p(y\mid x;\theta)$. In such cases, different $\theta$ that induce the same function are considered equivalent and are part of the same equivalence class. For example, in neural networks with redundant parameterizations (e.g., permutations of hidden units, sign changes combined with other weight changes), many distinct parameter vectors yield the same input-output mapping.

Non-identifiability arises in many common settings. A simple example is the mixture of two Gaussians with identical components except for label order: the mixture
\[
p(x)=\pi\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_1,\Sigma)+ (1-\pi)\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_2,\Sigma)
\]
is observationally identical to the same model with labels swapped: replacing $(\pi,\mu_1,\mu_2)$ by $(1-\pi,\mu_2,\mu_1)$ yields the same distribution. Thus the parameterization has a permutation symmetry (label switching), and the true parameter cannot be uniquely identified without imposing additional constraints (such as ordering constraints on the means). In Bayesian inference this leads to multimodal posteriors corresponding to permuted labelings.

Scaling non-identifiability is another common phenomenon. Suppose a model has parameters $A$ and $B$ that enter multiplicatively as $f(x)=A\cdot B\cdot x$. Multiplying $A$ by $c$ and dividing $B$ by $c$ leaves $f(x)$ unchanged. Thus $A$ and $B$ are not separately identifiable—only their product is. The parameters then lie on a one-dimensional manifold of equivalent values. In practice this can cause ill-conditioning in optimization and infinite Fisher information in directions orthogonal to the identifiable subspace.

Reparameterization invariance: if we reparameterize the model by a bijective transformation $\phi:\Theta\to\Theta'$ and write $\theta'=\phi(\theta)$, then the model is identifiable in $\theta$ if and only if it is identifiable in $\theta'$. However, non-bijective transformations can collapse different parameter values to the same representation and introduce non-identifiability. It is therefore important to understand when parameter redundancy arises from modeling choices versus when it is an inherent lack of information in the data.

Identifiability is also essential for interpreting confidence intervals and asymptotic normal approximations. Asymptotic normality of the MLE requires a nonsingular Fisher information matrix. But if the model is non-identifiable along some directions, the Fisher information will be singular and the asymptotic distribution will be degenerate or undefined in those directions. In such cases one can often restrict attention to an identifiable reparameterization or to the identifiable functionals of the parameter (for example, the predictive probabilities rather than the raw parameter vector).

Practical remedies for non-identifiability include imposing constraints (e.g., fix one parameter, impose ordering, fix scale), using regularization that breaks symmetry, or redefining the parameters to focus on identifiable combinations. For example, in mixtures one may impose an ordering on the component means (e.g., $\mu_1<\mu_2$) to remove label switching for point estimation. In deep networks, weight symmetries often remain but are usually not a problem for prediction; however they complicate interpretation of learned weights.

A useful conceptual distinction is between parameter identifiability and model identifiability. Parameter identifiability asks whether the point $\theta_0$ is the unique maximizer of the expected log-likelihood. Model identifiability (or identifiability of the mapping from inputs to outputs) asks whether the predictive distribution $p(y\mid x;\theta)$ is uniquely determined by the parameter. When interpreting estimation results, always ask whether you require recovery of the exact parameter vector, or whether recovery of the predictive function suffices; your answers affect the appropriateness of MLE and the interpretation of uncertainty.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has covered the probabilistic and statistical basics that support likelihood-based modeling and inference. We began with the formalism of random variables, pmfs/pdfs, and conditional distributions; introduced expectation, variance, and key inequalities; and then connected those ideas to the log-likelihood and maximum likelihood estimation. Focusing on binary classification we derived the logistic function, the form of the conditional likelihood, and the binary cross-entropy loss and its gradient. We then discussed the large-sample properties of MLE—consistency and asymptotic normality—and emphasized the role of Fisher information. Finally, we examined identifiability and equivalence classes of parameterizations, explaining how symmetries and redundancies in parameter representations affect estimation and interpretation.

Armed with these concepts you should be able to read and understand likelihood formulations of classification and regression models, to derive gradients of log-likelihoods for optimization, and to reason about when parameter estimates are meaningful versus when only the induced predictive distributions are identifiable. These foundations also prepare you for more advanced topics—Bayesian inference, penalized likelihoods, generalized linear models, and modern deep learning architectures—where the same probabilistic principles appear with additional structure.
