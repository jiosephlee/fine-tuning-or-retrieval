Introduction

Probability and information theory form the mathematical backbone for reasoning about modern machine learning systems, especially for language models and the techniques used to align them with human values, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). This chapter develops the core probabilistic concepts you will need: random variables, expectations, likelihoods, entropy, mutual information, and divergences such as Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. We will explain common losses used in training models, like cross-entropy and binary cross-entropy, and the elementary nonlinearities used in classification, namely the logistic (sigmoid) and logit functions. Practical numerical issues appear frequently when working with exponential families and normalized probabilities; we therefore introduce partition functions, the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability, and the role of normalization constants. Finally, we tie these tools together by discussing convexity and Jensen’s inequality, which underpin many inequalities and optimization arguments used in machine learning.

The goal is to give clear intuition and derivations so you can read and reason about papers that use probabilistic modeling, reward modeling, and divergence-based objectives such as those appearing in RLHF.

1. Random variables, expectations, and log-likelihoods

A random variable is a function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome in a probability space. In machine learning we treat data, model predictions, and labels as realizations of random variables. If we write $X$ for an input (for example, a text prompt) and $Y$ for an output (for example, the next token or a human preference label), then the joint distribution over $(X,Y)$ is written as $p(X,Y)$. When modeling, we often posit a parameterized conditional distribution $p_\theta(Y\mid X)$; the model's parameters are collectively $\theta$.

Expectation is the basic averaging operator for random variables. If $Z$ is a random variable with probability mass function (pmf) or probability density function (pdf) $p(z)$, then its expectation is
\[
\mathbb{E}[Z] = \sum_z z\, p(z) \quad\text{(discrete case)}
\]
or
\[
\mathbb{E}[Z] = \int z\, p(z)\,dz \quad\text{(continuous case)}.
\]
Expectations are linear: $\mathbb{E}[aZ + bW] = a\mathbb{E}[Z] + b\mathbb{E}[W]$.

Log-likelihoods arise when we fit parameters $\theta$ to observed data. Given a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a model $p_\theta(y\mid x)$, the likelihood is the product of per-example probabilities under the model: $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)$. Because products of many small numbers underflow and because optimization over products is inconvenient, we typically work with the log-likelihood,
\[
\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i).
\]
Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) chooses $\theta$ to maximize $\ell(\theta)$; equivalently, it minimizes the negative log-likelihood (NLL). For probabilistic language models, the NLL per token is the canonical loss used in pretraining and fine-tuning.

It is often useful to consider the expected log-likelihood under some data distribution $q(x,y)$. The population negative log-likelihood (per example) is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim q}[-\log p_\theta(y\mid x)].
\]
Minimizing this population expectation would make $p_\theta(y\mid x)$ close to the true conditional distribution $q(y\mid x)$ if the model family is sufficiently flexible.

2. Cross-entropy and binary cross-entropy losses

Cross-entropy is a measure of the difference between two probability distributions. For discrete distributions $q$ (true distribution) and $p$ (model distribution) over the same support, the cross-entropy of $q$ relative to $p$ is defined as
\[
H(q,p) = -\sum_x q(x)\, \log p(x).
\]
This is an expected negative log-probability: if the true data generating distribution is $q$, then $H(q,p)$ is the expected value of the negative log-likelihood of samples under the model $p$. When $p$ is parameterized and we minimize $H(q,p_\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$, we are performing maximum likelihood estimation (if $q$ is the empirical distribution from data).

Cross-entropy decomposes into the entropy of $q$ plus the KL divergence from $q$ to $p$:
\[
H(q,p) = H(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p),
\]
where $H(q) = -\sum_x q(x)\log q(x)$ is the entropy of $q$. Because entropy does not depend on the model parameters, minimizing $H(q,p_\theta)$ is equivalent to minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p_\theta)$, i.e., the forward KL divergence.

Binary cross-entropy (BCE) is the special case for binary random variables. Suppose $y\in\{0,1\}$ and the model predicts probability $p_\theta(y=1\mid x)=\hat{p}$. The BCE loss for a single example is
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\hat{p}; y) = -\bigl(y\log\hat{p} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})\bigr).
\]
This is exactly the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution that puts mass $1$ on the observed class $y$ and the model's Bernoulli distribution. Minimizing BCE pushes $\hat{p}$ toward the observed labels in expectation. BCE is commonly used for binary classification tasks and for learning reward models in RLHF when human judgments are binary (prefer A over B).

3. Logistic (sigmoid) and logit functions

The logistic (sigmoid) function maps a real-valued score to a probability in $(0,1)$. It is defined as
\[
\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
It has the convenient property that $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$. The logistic function is used in binary classification and in reward modeling because we can interpret a real-valued score $s(x)$ as the log-odds of the positive class: the model predicts probability $\sigma(s(x))$.

The logit function is the inverse of the sigmoid; it maps a probability $p\in(0,1)$ to log-odds in $\mathbb{R}$:
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}.
\]
Log-odds are additive: if events have independent evidence contributions, log-odds sum. In many modeling situations we model the logit (or a score that we treat as a logit) as a linear or neural function of inputs: $\operatorname{logit}(\hat{p}) = s_\theta(x)$, implying $\hat{p}=\sigma(s_\theta(x))$.

Connection to binary cross-entropy: suppose the model outputs a score $s$ and predicts $\hat{p}=\sigma(s)$. The BCE loss becomes
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s; y) = -\bigl(y\log\sigma(s) + (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(s))\bigr).
\]
Using identities of the sigmoid, this simplifies to the convex function
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s; y) = \log(1+e^{-s}) \quad\text{if } y=1,
\]
and
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s; y) = \log(1+e^{s}) \quad\text{if } y=0.
\]
A unified expression is
\[
\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s; y) = \log\bigl(1 + e^{-(2y-1)s}\bigr),
\]
which is often used in analyses because it explicitly shows convexity in $s$.

In preference modeling, suppose we have two outputs $y_A$ and $y_B$ and scores $s_A$ and $s_B$. A common model for the probability that A is preferred over B is the Bradley–Terry model:
\[
P(A\succ B) = \sigma(s_A - s_B).
\]
This expresses the preference probability in terms of a difference of log-odds scores and is convenient because it reduces pairwise preference learning to binary logistic regression.

4. KL divergence (forward vs. reverse) and properties

The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from another. For discrete distributions $q$ and $p$ over the same support, the forward KL divergence (also called $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p)$) is
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p) = \sum_x q(x)\, \log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}.
\]
This is nonnegative and equals zero if and only if $q=p$ almost everywhere. The forward KL is an expectation under $q$ of the log-ratio $\log(q/p)$:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right].
\]

There is also the reverse KL divergence, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\;\|\;q)$, which swaps the roles of $p$ and $q$:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\;\|\;q) = \sum_x p(x)\, \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].
\]
Although both are called “KL divergence”, they behave differently and lead to different optimization characteristics.

Intuition and practical consequences. Minimizing forward KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p_\theta)$ tends to make $p_\theta$ cover the support of $q$: regions where $q(x)>0$ but $p_\theta(x)$ is small produce large penalties because the expectation is under $q$. This leads to “mode-covering” behavior: the model is encouraged to assign probability mass wherever the data does. In contrast, minimizing reverse KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\;\|\;q)$ is often “mode-seeking”: the expectation is under $p_\theta$, and if the model places mass in regions where $q$ is small, it is punished heavily, so the optimizer avoids placing mass in low-$q$ regions, potentially collapsing onto a single mode of $q$.

These behaviors have concrete implications. When training a generative model with maximum likelihood (minimizing forward KL between empirical data and model), the model tends to spread probability to match all modes in the data. When using reverse-KL objectives, such as some forms of variational inference that minimize $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z)\;\|\;p(z\mid x))$ or mode-seeking divergences in some adversarial setups, the learned approximations may concentrate on the most probable modes.

Other properties. KL divergence is not symmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\;\|\;q)$ in general. It also does not satisfy the triangle inequality and hence is not a metric. However, it is convex in the pair of distributions (jointly convex) and satisfies nonnegativity via Gibbs' inequality:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p) \ge 0.
\]
KL divergence appears naturally when deriving maximum likelihood and variational objectives because it measures excess expected log-loss, as shown by the cross-entropy decomposition earlier.

In alignment and RLHF contexts, KL penalties are used to keep a fine-tuned policy close to a pretrained base policy. For example, a policy optimization objective might include a term $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot\mid x))$ or the reverse $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x))$, depending on whether we want to penalize deviations weighted by the new policy's mass or by the base policy's mass. Choosing which direction matters: penalizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\;\|\;\pi_{\mathrm{base}})$ discourages the learned policy from placing mass where the base policy had low mass (mode-seeking with respect to the base), while $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\mathrm{base}}\;\|\;\pi_\theta)$ penalizes failing to cover the base policy's support (keeping coverage).

5. Entropy and mutual information (intuition)

Entropy quantifies uncertainty in a random variable. For a discrete random variable $X$ with distribution $p(x)$, the (Shannon) entropy is
\[
H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) = \mathbb{E}[-\log p(X)].
\]
Entropy is maximized for a given support by the uniform distribution and measures the average number of bits required to encode samples from $X$ under an optimal code. In modeling, entropy shows up in regularization and in exploration: a high-entropy policy is more random and explores more, while a low-entropy policy is more deterministic.

Mutual information measures how much knowledge of one variable reduces uncertainty about another. For random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint distribution $p(x,y)$ and marginals $p(x)$, $p(y)$, the mutual information is
\[
I(X;Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(x,y)\;\|\; p(x)p(y)\bigr) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X).
\]
Intuitively, $I(X;Y)$ is the reduction in uncertainty about $X$ provided by observing $Y$. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $I(X;Y)=0$. Mutual information is nonnegative and symmetric.

In language modeling and alignment, mutual information can be used to quantify how much a prompt reveals about the intended reply, or how much a reward signal is informative about desirable behavior. In some approaches to intrinsic motivation, agents are rewarded for actions that increase mutual information between latent variables or future observations, encouraging exploration that yields informative outcomes.

6. Log-sum-exp trick and numerical stability

Many expressions in probability and machine learning involve log of sums of exponentials. A common pattern is computing the log-partition function or normalizer:
\[
\log\sum_{i=1}^n e^{a_i}.
\]
Directly computing the exponentials $e^{a_i}$ can overflow or underflow when the $a_i$ are large in magnitude. The log-sum-exp (LSE) trick is a numerically stable way to compute this expression by factoring out the maximum exponent. Let $m = \max_i a_i$. Then
\[
\log\sum_{i=1}^n e^{a_i} = m + \log\sum_{i=1}^n e^{a_i - m}.
\]
Now all exponents $a_i - m$ are nonpositive, so $e^{a_i - m}\le 1$, preventing overflow. This also improves numerical precision when some $a_i$ are much smaller than $m$; those terms effectively vanish in the sum and do not cause catastrophic cancellation.

The LSE trick is used when computing softmax normalization, log-likelihoods for categorical distributions, and partition functions for energy-based models. For softmax logits $a_i$, the normalized probabilities are
\[
p_i = \frac{e^{a_i}}{\sum_j e^{a_j}}.
\]
A stable way to compute $p_i$ uses the same subtraction by $m$: compute $b_i = a_i - m$, then $p_i = e^{b_i} / \sum_j e^{b_j}$.

The LSE function is also convex and smooth, and serves as a smooth approximation to the maximum:
\[
\log\sum_i e^{a_i} \approx \max_i a_i \quad\text{(tight when one $a_i$ dominates)}.
\]
This connection shows why the softmax tends to concentrate mass on the largest logits as they spread apart.

7. Partition functions and normalization constants

Many probabilistic models define unnormalized densities or energies of the form
\[
\tilde{p}(x) = e^{-E(x)},
\]
where $E(x)$ is called the energy function. To make $\tilde{p}$ a valid probability density, we must normalize by the partition function (also called normalization constant):
\[
Z = \sum_x e^{-E(x)} \quad\text{(discrete)}, \qquad Z = \int e^{-E(x)}\,dx \quad\text{(continuous)}.
\]
The normalized probability is $p(x)=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-E(x)}$.

Partition functions are central because they connect energy-based representations with probabilities. However, computing $Z$ is often intractable because it requires summing or integrating over a combinatorially or continuously large space. This intractability is a key reason why learning with energy-based models, undirected graphical models, or arbitrary unnormalized densities can be challenging in practice.

In exponential family models, the partition function is a log-normalizer that depends on parameters in a tractable way for certain sufficient statistics. For example, consider a categorical distribution parameterized by logits $a_i$. The probability of category $i$ is
\[
p_i = \frac{e^{a_i}}{\sum_j e^{a_j}}.
\]
Here the partition function is $Z=\sum_j e^{a_j}$ and $\log Z$ is the log-partition function; it appears in log-likelihoods and in gradients.

From a statistical physics viewpoint, $\log Z$ is closely related to free energy and plays a role in variational decompositions. In variational inference, we often approximate intractable partition functions or lower-bound them using tractable surrogates; the ELBO (evidence lower bound) is one such bound that turns an intractable marginal likelihood into an optimization objective.

In RLHF and policy regularization, partition functions appear when you exponentiate rewards to form Boltzmann policies. A Boltzmann (a.k.a. Gibbs) policy with reward $r(x)$ and inverse temperature $\beta$ takes the form
\[
\pi(x) = \frac{e^{\beta r(x)}}{Z}, \quad Z = \sum_x e^{\beta r(x)}.
\]
Here $Z$ ensures the policy is a probability distribution. The temperature $\beta$ controls the sharpness of the distribution; larger $\beta$ produces more deterministic selections of high-reward items.

8. Jensen’s inequality and convexity basics

Convexity is a central property in optimization and probability. A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is convex if for all $x,y$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$,
\[
f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda) y) \le \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y).
\]
Intuitively, the line segment between any two points on the graph of $f$ lies above the graph. Convex functions have many useful properties: local minima are global minima, and they admit inequality-based bounds.

Jensen’s inequality connects convexity with expectations. If $f$ is convex and $X$ is a random variable, then
\[
f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)].
\]
For concave functions the direction of the inequality reverses. Jensen’s inequality underpins many results in information theory. For example, it is used to prove nonnegativity of KL divergence: by applying Jensen's inequality to the convex function $f(u) = u\log u$ or via Gibbs' inequality, one shows
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\;\|\;p) = \mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\log\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\bigr] \ge 0.
\]

Jensen’s inequality also explains why the log-sum-exp is an upper bound on the maximum: because exponential is convex, we have
\[
\exp\bigl(\mathbb{E}[Z]\bigr) \le \mathbb{E}[\exp(Z)],
\]
which upon taking logs yields
\[
\mathbb{E}[Z] \le \log\mathbb{E}[e^{Z}].
\]
If $Z$ is a random variable taking values $a_i$ with probabilities $1/n$, this gives $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i a_i \le \log(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i e^{a_i})$, and rearrangements lead to relations between arithmetic means, log-sum-exp, and maxima.

Jensen’s inequality often appears in deriving variational lower bounds. For an intractable marginal likelihood $\log p(x) = \log\int p(x,z)\,dz$, introducing an approximate posterior $q(z)$ yields
\[
\log p(x) = \log\int q(z)\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\,dz \ge \int q(z)\log\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\,dz,
\]
where the inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality applied to the concave log function. The right-hand side is the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which is tractable when $q$ is chosen appropriately.

Putting it together: applications to language models, rewards, and RLHF

All of the above concepts are used in concert when reasoning about alignment and RLHF. During supervised fine-tuning of a language model, we minimize cross-entropy (i.e., negative log-likelihood) between the empirical token distribution and the model's distribution; this is equivalent to minimizing forward KL. When training reward models from human preference data, we often use binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid over score differences (Bradley–Terry or logistic preference model). In RLHF, the policy optimization objective typically combines expected reward (to encourage desired behavior) with a KL penalty relative to the pretrained policy (to prevent catastrophic drift). The KL term can be either forward or reverse depending on implementation details and desired behavior. Temperatures and Boltzmann policies connect rewards to probability distributions via partition functions, and numerically stable computations entail using log-sum-exp when evaluating log-probabilities or normalizers.

Entropy regularization is also used in policy learning to encourage exploration and avoid premature convergence to deterministic policies; it appears as a term like $-\tau H(\pi(\cdot\mid x))$ in the objective. Mutual information can be used to enforce informative behaviors or constrain models to preserve certain dependencies. Jensen’s inequality and convexity arguments appear in deriving ELBOs, in proofs about KL nonnegativity, and in justifying approximations.

Summary and practical notes

This chapter has introduced the probability and information-theoretic primitives needed to understand many methods in modern language modeling and alignment. Key takeaways are:

- Expectation and log-likelihood provide the language for average loss and maximum likelihood estimation.
- Cross-entropy is the expected negative log-likelihood and is minimized by maximizing likelihood; binary cross-entropy is its Bernoulli specialization.
- The sigmoid and logit relate scores to probabilities, and logistic losses are convex in the score.
- KL divergence measures discrepancy between distributions; forward and reverse directions have different optimization effects (mode-covering vs. mode-seeking).
- Entropy quantifies uncertainty; mutual information quantifies dependence.
- Partition functions normalize unnormalized models but are often intractable; log-sum-exp is the numerically stable way to compute log normalizers and softmax probabilities.
- Jensen’s inequality and convexity are foundational for many proofs and variational approximations.

Keep these tools in mind when reading research on RLHF and reward modeling: tracking which divergence is being minimized, how probabilities are normalized, and how numerical stability is achieved will often be crucial to both understanding and reproducing results.
