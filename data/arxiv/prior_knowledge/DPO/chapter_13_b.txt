# Estimating latent scoring functions from comparisons

At the heart of preference-based methods is the problem of estimating latent scoring functions from pairwise comparisons. Suppose there exists an underlying scoring function $f$ such that for any two objects (actions, trajectories) $x$ and $x'$, the probability that $x$ is preferred to $x'$ is modeled as a function of score difference: for example, the Bradley-Terry model posits
\[
P(x \succ x') = \frac{\exp(f(x))}{\exp(f(x)) + \exp(f(x'))} = \sigma(f(x)-f(x')),
\]
where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))$ is the logistic function. Alternatively, Thurstone's model assumes that observed utilities have Gaussian noise, leading to
\[
P(x \succ x') = \Phi\!\big(f(x)-f(x')\big),
\]
with $\Phi$ the Gaussian CDF. In these parametric models, comparisons reveal noisy measurements of score differences, and estimating $f$ reduces to solving a pairwise logistic or probit regression.

In practice, $f$ may be parameterized using features and parameters, $f_\theta(x)$, or modeled nonparametrically using kernels or Gaussian processes. The likelihood of observed comparisons $(x_i,x'_i,y_i)$ under a Bradley-Terry model is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \prod_i \sigma\big( f_\theta(x_i) - f_\theta(x'_i) \big)^{y_i} \big(1-\sigma(f_\theta(x_i)-f_\theta(x'_i))\big)^{1-y_i},
\]
where $y_i=1$ indicates $x_i$ was preferred. Maximizing this likelihood (or its log) yields an estimator for $\theta$. Regularization is often used to avoid overfitting; for high-dimensional features, penalized likelihood or Bayesian priors stabilize estimates.

Nonparametric methods include preference Gaussian processes, where $f$ is endowed with a GP prior with covariance $k(x,x')$, and the pairwise likelihood is incorporated to produce a posterior over functions. Inference is typically done via Laplace approximation, expectation propagation, or variational methods because the likelihood is non-Gaussian. Posterior uncertainty over $f$ is valuable: it guides active query selection by highlighting where additional comparisons will reduce uncertainty in rankings or policy choice.

When the domain is action or trajectory spaces with structure, exploiting that structure improves estimation. For contextual problems, $f(x)$ often decomposes as $f(x)=g(x,a)$ where $x$ denotes context and $a$ action; modeling $g$ with context-action features allows efficient generalization across contexts and actions. In RL, if $f$ is meant to be a Markov reward function, one should model $f(s,a)$, and use comparisons between trajectories to infer per-step reward values; this is an inverse reinforcement learning flavor of PbRL.

Identifiability issues arise: scoring functions are only determined up to additive constants under pairwise-difference models. More serious is the fact that comparisons determine ordering and relative differences only to the extent allowed by the model; if preferences do not align with a difference of a scalar utility (e.g., preferences are context-dependent or inconsistent), any single scalar $f$ will be misspecified. Robust estimation recognizes model misspecification, uses flexible models (e.g., context-aware, nonparametric), and evaluates goodness-of-fit by held-out comparisons.

Finally, sample complexity for estimating $f$ depends on function class complexity, comparison noise, and the design of queries. Active selection of informative pairs accelerates learning; for example, comparing items with similar estimated scores yields more informative outcomes for refining ordering near decision boundaries. Methods exploit uncertainty estimates to choose pairs that reduce entropy over the ranking or minimize regret.


# Online vs. offline preference feedback

Preference feedback can be gathered in two operational regimes: online, where the learner interacts adaptively and queries comparisons during learning, and offline, where a logged dataset of comparisons is given beforehand. Each regime introduces different opportunities and challenges.

Online preference feedback enables adaptive query selection. The learner can choose which comparisons to request based on current uncertainty, leading to active learning strategies that dramatically reduce the number of comparisons needed to achieve a target accuracy. In dueling bandit settings, online algorithms can balance exploration (choosing informative pairs) and exploitation (comparing currently promising options) to minimize regret or speed identification. Online PbRL benefits from the ability to preferentially request comparisons among policies or trajectories that are near the decision boundary, or to probe unknown regions of the state-action space that matter for policy improvement.

However, online interactions can be costly, especially when humans provide preferences, and there are safety concerns when exploring poor policies in physical environments. To mitigate these concerns, algorithms may constrain exploration via conservative updates, batch queries, or simulated feedback. Online setups also require mechanisms for handling delayed or noisy human responses, and modeling annotator inconsistency.

Offline preference feedback eliminates the cost and risk of online querying, but imposes limitations due to selection bias and coverage. A logged dataset may only contain comparisons among a subset of items or policies, collected by some behavior policy or through naturalistic user interactions. As in offline contextual bandits, unbiased evaluation and learning require either overlap between the logging distribution and the target decisions or strong modeling assumptions. Importance weighting can correct for sampling bias when the propensity of each logged comparison under the logging policy is known or estimable, but this increases variance.

Offline-to-online transfer is a pragmatic approach: learn an initial model or policy from offline comparisons, then refine online with a small number of active preference queries. This leverages the cost-efficiency of offline data while allowing the learner to resolve remaining uncertainties adaptively. Another transfer strategy is to use offline comparisons to pretrain a reward model that is then used to simulate environments for safe online policy optimization.

A further distinction is the type of preference feedback. Offline data may include implicit preferences (e.g., clicks, dwell time) that are biased proxies for true preferences, while online querying can elicit explicit choices. The modeling of implicit feedback requires additional assumptions linking behavior to preference (e.g., position bias in ranking), and correcting for such biases is an active area of research.


# Sample efficiency and exploration

Sample efficiency—how many comparisons or interactions are required to learn a good policy or identify a winner—is a central concern in preference-based learning. Compared to scalar rewards, pairwise comparisons carry limited information per observation: a single binary comparison reveals only a bit about the difference between two items. Thus, naive approaches that estimate all pairwise probabilities $P_{ij}$ independently are sample-inefficient, scaling poorly with the number of arms or the complexity of trajectory spaces.

Improving sample efficiency requires three key ingredients: structural assumptions, active exploration, and variance reduction techniques. Structural assumptions could be parametric models for the latent scoring function, context-action generalization, or exploiting transitivity approximations. When $f$ lies in a low-dimensional function class (parametric or RKHS), each comparison informs many pairwise relations, enabling faster learning.

Active exploration designs queries to maximize expected information gain or reduce policy regret. In dueling bandits, algorithms that prioritize comparisons between arms with close estimated win probabilities can rapidly resolve ambiguities that affect choice. Information-theoretic criteria such as maximizing mutual information between comparison outcomes and unknown parameters, or minimizing posterior entropy, guide optimal query selection in Bayesian frameworks. Thompson sampling adapted to pairwise comparisons is another practical exploration strategy: sample a candidate scoring function from the posterior and query comparisons that best disambiguate between high-probability hypotheses.

Variance reduction methods adapt importance weighting or doubly robust estimators to preference settings. For offline evaluation, doubly robust estimators can combine a learned preference model with importance sampling to yield lower-variance estimates of policy performance. In online learning, maintaining confidence bounds around pairwise estimates allows algorithms to avoid unnecessary exploration of pairs that are already clearly dominated.

Lower bounds on sample complexity reveal that certain problems are inherently hard. For example, identifying a Condorcet winner in a tournament of size $K$ with gap parameter $\Delta$ (minimum margin against the second best in pairwise probabilities) requires at least on the order of $\Omega(K \Delta^{-2})$ comparisons in the worst case. For contextual or trajectory spaces with high-dimensional features, sample complexity depends on the complexity (e.g., VC dimension or Rademacher complexity) of the scoring function class.

In RL settings, sample efficiency is even more challenging because comparisons over trajectories must disentangle rewards across timesteps. If one assumes a Markov reward structure with low-dimensional parameterization, techniques from inverse RL and reward learning can reduce sample needs. If not, methods focus on active policy comparison in policy space, treating policies as arms and using pairwise tournament algorithms to identify good policies with fewer environment interactions.


# Connections to ranking and choice theory

Preference-based learning is deeply connected to classical ranking and choice theory from economics and social choice. Models like Bradley-Terry and Thurstone originate in psychometrics and choice modeling, formalizing how latent utilities give rise to stochastic comparisons. Social choice theory studies aggregation methods for individual preferences into collective decisions, providing solution concepts (Condorcet, Borda, Copeland) and insights about paradoxes and impossibility results that are relevant for designing preference-based algorithms.

In ranking, the goal is to produce an ordering of items consistent with pairwise comparisons or partial rankings. Rank aggregation algorithms, such as spectral methods (e.g., Rank Centrality), maximum likelihood estimation under Bradley-Terry, and other graph-based procedures, provide tools for inferring global rankings from noisy pairwise data. The spectral perspective treats pairwise comparison outcomes as edges in a graph, and the stationary distribution of a random walk on this graph can be used as a ranking score; such methods are computationally efficient and robust.

Choice theory explores how choice probabilities relate to latent utilities and how context effects (e.g., attraction or compromise effects) violate simple difference-based models. These phenomena caution that human preferences may not adhere to the utility-difference assumptions underlying many PbRL models. Accounting for context-dependent or history-dependent preferences requires richer models, such as random utility models with covariates, context-specific utilities, or models allowing intransitivities.

Econometric identification issues appear when inferring utilities from observed choices. Without experimental control over which comparisons are made, inferring cardinal utilities is generally impossible; at best one can recover ordinal information or scale-restricted cardinal estimates. This aligns with the practical approach of using reward functions learned from preferences as approximate surrogates for guiding policy, coupled with uncertainty-aware optimization.

Game-theoretic ideas from tournament solutions and equilibrium concepts link choice aggregation to robust policy selection. The Von Neumann winner can be seen as a minimax-optimal mixed strategy in a zero-sum game defined by pairwise margins, providing a bridge between choice aggregation and strategic robustness. Similarly, notions of regret minimization in learning correspond to converging to equilibria in repeated games, and learning dynamics (e.g., regret-matching) resemble social choice updating processes.

Finally, ranking metrics and evaluation criteria from information retrieval—such as NDCG, MAP, and precision-at-k—offer task-specific objectives in settings where the aim is to present a ranked list rather than a single winner. Preference-based feedback often arises naturally in such tasks (click-through data yields noisy pairwise signals), and translating pairwise observations into listwise objectives is an active area linking PbRL with practical ranking systems.

Conclusion

Preference-based learning bridges bandit and RL frameworks with the reality that many systems can provide only relative judgments. Contextual and dueling bandits, tournament solution concepts like the Von Neumann winner, and PbRL formulations provide models and algorithms to operate without absolute rewards. Estimating latent scoring functions, choosing between online and offline modes, and designing sample-efficient exploration strategies are central technical challenges. Deep links to ranking and choice theory enrich the theoretical foundations and highlight practical pitfalls, such as non-transitivity and context effects. With growing interest in human-in-the-loop learning, preference-based methods are essential tools for designing systems that optimize for human values when scalar rewards are unavailable or unreliable.
