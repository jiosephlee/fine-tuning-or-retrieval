Introduction

Reward modeling from preferences is a practical and widely used approach to teaching agents what behavior is desirable by asking humans to compare pairs (or sets) of trajectories, responses, or system outputs and express which they prefer. Instead of explicitly defining a scalar reward function for every possible state and action, preference-based reward modeling treats reward as a latent scoring function that induces the observed preferences. This chapter explains how to formulate such latent score functions, how to fit them using a probabilistic model of preferences (often leading to a binary-cross-entropy objective over score differences), and practical considerations that arise in training, evaluating, and deploying reward models. We will cover normalization and calibration strategies, overfitting and generalization issues, architectural choices, robustness to label noise, metrics for evaluation, and fundamental limitations and under-specification that practitioners should keep in mind.

Formulating reward as latent score functions

At the heart of preference-based reward modeling is the assumption that each candidate behavior or output can be assigned a scalar latent score, often denoted $r(x)$ for an example $x$, such that higher scores correspond to preferred items. A human preference between two items $x^A$ and $x^B$ is modeled as a noisy observation of the ordering of these latent scores. Concretely, we assume there exists some underlying scoring function $r:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ and that when a human compares $x^A$ and $x^B$ they are more likely to prefer the item with the higher score. This latent-score perspective makes the problem a supervised learning task: given comparisons and their outcomes, estimate the function $r(x)$.

The latent-score formulation is flexible. The argument $x$ can represent a single-step output (e.g., a language model completion) or a full trajectory (sequence of states and actions). In sequential decision-making, the total return can be considered a sum of per-step rewards, but when comparing trajectories we often directly model the trajectory-level score, perhaps with structure that decomposes across time. The latent-score view also naturally accommodates stochastic and inconsistent human feedback by treating observed preferences as noisy indicators of the underlying score ordering.

Binary cross-entropy over score differences

A common probabilistic model for pairwise preferences assumes that the probability a human prefers $x^A$ to $x^B$ is a monotonic function of the score difference $r(x^A)-r(x^B)$. The canonical choice is the Bradley–Terry or logistic model:

$P\big(x^A \succ x^B\big) = \sigma\big(r(x^A)-r(x^B)\big)$,

where $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ is the logistic sigmoid. Under this model, maximum likelihood estimation of $r$ given a dataset of labeled pairwise comparisons reduces to minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss on labels that indicate which item was preferred. For a dataset of pairs $(x^A_i,x^B_i)$ with binary labels $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ where $y_i=1$ means $A$ was preferred, the negative log-likelihood (equivalently the cross-entropy loss) is

$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i \left[ y_i \log \sigma\big(r(x^A_i)-r(x^B_i)\big) + (1-y_i)\log \big(1-\sigma\big(r(x^A_i)-r(x^B_i)\big)\big)\right].$

This objective is attractive because it is convex in the score differences and differentiable with respect to parameters when $r$ is parameterized by a neural network. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to assign higher scores to items that humans prefer, and the gradient only depends on the difference of scores, which is aligned with the binary-outcome nature of the data.

The logistic model is not the only choice; alternatives include the Thurstone–Mosteller Gaussian model, which assumes additive Gaussian noise on scores, or models that include ties and abstentions. However, the logistic/Bernoulli formulation is common because it pairs conveniently with standard optimization tools and directly uses binary cross-entropy.

Normalization strategies (e.g., zero-mean per context)

Score differences are invariant to adding a constant to both scores: replacing $r(x)$ by $r(x)+c$ leaves $\sigma(r(x^A)-r(x^B))$ unchanged. This indeterminacy is inevitable and harmless for pairwise predictions, but it becomes important when we want calibrated scores or when we combine scores across contexts. Practical reward modeling introduces normalization to address scale and bias issues.

One straightforward normalization is to center scores relative to a context. If comparisons are made within a context $c$ (for example, prompts or initial states), we can enforce that the mean predicted score over candidates in the same context is zero: for all contexts $c$, enforce $\frac{1}{|S_c|}\sum_{x\in S_c} r(x)=0$, where $S_c$ is the set of items paired within context $c$. Enforcing zero-mean per context removes arbitrary additive offsets and helps when using the reward model to rank or compare candidates within the same context, which is usually the relevant operation in downstream selection or reinforcement learning.

Another common normalization is standardization across a batch or dataset to control the scale. For example, dividing score differences by a learned temperature parameter or by the batch standard deviation stabilizes the magnitude of gradients and provides numerical regularization. When training a policy with policy gradient methods using a learned reward model, the reward scale affects the learning rate implicitly; hence a consistent normalization helps transferability between datasets and training runs.

Normalization also interacts with calibration: if the downstream system expects rewards on a particular scale (e.g., for reward-weighted regression or for RL algorithms with specific entropy or KL targets), calibrating the mean and variance of $r$ matters. In practice, a combination of per-context centering and a global learned temperature is common.

Overfitting, generalization, and calibration

Because reward models are trained on a limited set of human comparisons, they can overfit to spurious regularities in the labeled pairs. Overfitting manifests as a reward model that predicts preferences correctly on training pairs but fails to capture human judgments on novel items or is easily exploited by a policy to produce high-scoring but undesired outputs. Generalization of reward models is therefore crucial: we need models whose learned scores reflect human preferences on examples outside the training set distribution.

Regularization and validation are the basic defenses. Holding out a validation set of preference pairs and monitoring metrics like accuracy or cross-entropy helps detect overfitting. However, preferred items may come from different distributional regimes than training data due to policy optimization; extrapolation remains a major issue.

Calibration refers to whether the predicted probabilities align with empirical frequencies. Under the logistic model, perfect calibration means $P(y=1\mid r(x^A)-r(x^B)=z)=\sigma(z)$. Calibration matters if we interpret scores probabilistically or use predicted probabilities to combine uncertain feedback signals. Calibration can be improved through temperature scaling or isotonic regression on a held-out calibration set. But standard calibration methods correct for systematic mis-scaling only and do not fix deeper generalization failures.

A related and particularly important failure mode is reward hacking: when downstream optimization exploits quirks of the reward model to produce outputs that score highly but are misaligned with human intent. This can occur easily if the reward model picks up on easy-to-exploit features that correlate with human preferences in the training set but do not capture the intended concept. A robust evaluation pipeline and techniques like adversarial preference elicitation, active learning with human-in-the-loop, and conservative policy optimization can help mitigate this risk.

Architectures for reward models (LM head vs. lightweight heads)

Architecturally, reward models for language and decision-making systems are often built on pretrained models such as large language models (LLMs). There are two common strategies: attaching a lightweight head on top of a frozen or fine-tuned base model, or fine-tuning the whole model including an LM head.

A typical lightweight architecture freezes most of the pretrained model parameters and adds a small pooling layer and a scalar regression head that outputs $r(x)$. For sequence inputs, one may pool token embeddings (e.g., take the final hidden state corresponding to a special token or mean-pool) and pass this pooled vector through one or two feedforward layers to produce a scalar. This design reduces computational cost, lowers the risk of catastrophic forgetting, and often requires fewer labeled preferences to train because the base model's representations are already useful.

An alternative is to use the LM head to parameterize $r(x)$. For autoregressive models, one can compute the log-probability of the continuation given the prompt and use it as a feature, or append a learnable scalar head to the decoder output and fine-tune the entire network. Training the LM head and base model jointly can yield higher accuracy when the preference data is abundant and the domain diverges sufficiently from pretraining. Joint fine-tuning can also result in better alignment of internal representations with the scoring objective, potentially improving generalization to novel examples. However, it is more computationally expensive and increases the risk of overfitting and loss of general language capabilities.

A hybrid approach is to allow limited fine-tuning of later layers or to use adapters: small, trainable modules inserted into a mostly frozen network. Adapters offer a balance, permitting task-specific adaptation while retaining the bulk of pretraining. Ultimately, the choice of architecture depends on the amount of preference data, compute budget, and the desired trade-off between task performance and preserving pretrained behavior.

Training dynamics and label noise robustness

During training, the dynamics of learning from pairwise data exhibit some distinctive behaviors. Because the loss depends only on differences $r(x^A)-r(x^B)$, early training often quickly learns coarse ranking structure but can struggle to refine fine-grained distinctions where human preferences are noisy. The gradient for a pair is proportional to $\sigma(-y_i (r(x^A_i)-r(x^B_i)))$, which saturates when the predicted difference is large in magnitude. As a consequence, once the model becomes confident about many comparisons, gradient magnitudes decrease and learning slows. This saturation can be desirable to stabilize the model, but it can also prevent correction of subtle systematic errors.

Label noise—disagreement among annotators or mistaken labels—is a pervasive issue. The logistic noise model implicitly assumes stochasticity in human choices and has some robustness: if a comparison has contradictory labels across annotators, the maximum likelihood estimator will fit to the noisy distribution. Nevertheless, systematic biases in labels (for example, annotator demographics skewing preferences) can bias the reward model. Techniques for robustness include collecting multiple annotations per pair and aggregating via majority vote or probabilistic models of annotator reliability, using loss functions less sensitive to outliers (such as margin losses), or employing regularization and early stopping.

Active learning can improve data efficiency and robustness. By selecting the most informative pairs—typically those where the current model is uncertain—one maximizes the information gained per human label and focuses labels where disagreement matters. Pair selection strategies must also avoid sampling bias that skews the data distribution to unrepresentative regions; mixing in random or diverse pairs helps.

Evaluation of reward models (AUC, correlation with humans)

Evaluating reward models requires multiple complementary metrics because no single number captures all desired properties. Standard metrics include pairwise accuracy or area under the ROC curve (AUC) on held-out preference data, which measure the model's ability to predict human choices. AUC is appealing because it is threshold-invariant and robust to class imbalance; it quantifies the probability that a randomly chosen preferred item receives a higher score than a dispreferred one. However, high AUC on held-out pairs does not guarantee good performance on distribution-shifted examples encountered during policy optimization.

Correlation metrics such as Spearman or Pearson correlation between model scores and mean human ratings (when absolute ratings are available) provide a sense of whether scores preserve rank order beyond pairwise outcomes. Calibration metrics examine whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. More task-specific evaluations include how well the reward model predicts human-ranked lists, agreement on multi-way comparisons, and performance when the reward model is used as the objective to train a policy—does the resulting policy produce behavior that humans prefer?

Robust evaluation also requires adversarial or stress tests. One should evaluate the reward model on examples produced by candidate policies that are optimized against the model itself; if the model assigns high scores to degenerate or undesirable behaviors, this indicates exploitable failures. Human-in-the-loop evaluation, where annotators judge outputs from policies trained using the reward model, provides a final check.

Limitations and under-specification

Despite their practical utility, reward models trained from preferences face fundamental limitations and under-specification. First, preference data often leaves many aspects of the desired utility unspecified. Humans may prefer outputs for reasons that are incidental to the intended objective, and preference data typically lacks counterfactual coverage: we only observe labels on sampled pairs and not on the full space of possible behaviors. This under-specification means multiple reward functions can be consistent with observed preferences, and only some of them correspond to the true intent.

Second, reward models extrapolate poorly outside the support of the training distribution, a problem exacerbated during optimization when policies push into new regions. Overfitting to spurious correlations is common, and the model may be unstable under adversarial optimization. Third, human preferences can be inconsistent and context-dependent, which makes the concept of a single scalar reward function approximate at best. Modeling context dependence explicitly helps but does not eliminate the issue.

Finally, practical human feedback is costly, which limits dataset size and diversity; active learning and simulated annotators can help but are imperfect. Because of these limitations, practitioners should combine reward modeling with other alignment techniques: conservative policy optimization, interpretability checks, adversarial testing, and continuous human oversight. Recognizing that a learned reward is only an imperfect proxy for human values is essential for safe deployment.

Conclusion

Reward modeling from preferences provides a principled and practical framework to turn human comparisons into scalar objectives usable for training agents and ranking systems. The core idea—modeling a latent scoring function and fitting it by maximizing likelihood under a logistic or similar model—yields a simple binary cross-entropy objective that is easy to optimize and to scale. Yet real-world application requires careful attention to normalization and calibration, architectural choices that trade adaptation against stability, and training protocols that are robust to label noise and avoid overfitting. Evaluation must be multifaceted, including held-out AUC, correlation with human ratings, and stress tests against optimized policies. Finally, fundamental issues of under-specification and extrapolation remain unavoidable, so reward models should be used as part of broader systems with human oversight and conservatism. When applied thoughtfully, preference-based reward modeling is a powerful tool for aligning models to human judgments, but it must be handled with humility about its limitations and proactive defenses against failure modes.
