Introduction

Policy optimization methods are central to modern reinforcement learning (RL) for continuous and high-dimensional control problems. Rather than computing value functions alone and deriving a policy indirectly, policy optimization directly searches in the space of parameterized policies to maximize expected return. In practice, policy gradient methods combined with value-function critics—so-called actor‑critic algorithms—are the most widely used class of methods because they can handle large function approximators (neural networks) and continuous action spaces efficiently.

This chapter explains the principal practical policy optimization algorithms used in contemporary RL research and applications: Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), and the widely used Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). We will examine the mathematical derivations behind the constrained policy updates that TRPO uses, the surrogate objectives and clipping mechanisms of PPO, and the role of actor‑critic architectures and bootstrapped value function learning in making policy-gradient methods practical. We will discuss the bias–variance tradeoffs that arise from bootstrapping and using baselines, and the various sources of instability in training (reward scaling, normalization, bootstrapping failures). Finally, the chapter will end with concrete practical hyperparameters and implementation considerations that matter when you implement these algorithms.

# Trust region methods and constrained policy updates (TRPO)

At the core of trust region approaches to policy optimization is the intuition that while we want to take steps in policy parameter space that increase expected return, a single large update can catastrophically degrade performance because the new policy behaves very differently from the old one. Trust region methods control how far the policy can move in one update, ensuring monotonic improvement (or at least avoiding large regressions) under certain approximations.

Start from the objective of maximizing expected discounted return under a policy parameterized by $\theta$:
$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r_t \right]$,
where $\tau$ denotes trajectories sampled from policy $\pi_\theta$ and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor. The policy gradient theorem gives an unbiased gradient estimator:
$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_\theta}, a\sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a) \right]$,
where $d^{\pi_\theta}(s)$ is the discounted state visitation distribution and $Q^{\pi_\theta}$ is the action-value function.

TRPO formulates a constrained optimization problem that maximizes a first-order approximation (a surrogate objective) to the change in performance while imposing a constraint on the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and the new policy $\pi_\theta$. A commonly used surrogate is
$L_\theta(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}, a\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|s)} A^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s,a) \right]$,
which uses importance sampling to evaluate how a new policy would fare under data collected from the old policy. TRPO solves:
maximize $\;L_\theta(\theta)$
subject to $\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}} \left[ \mathrm{KL}\left( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|s) \,\|\, \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s) \right) \right] \leq \delta$,
for some small $\delta$ (the trust region size).

Why KL? The KL divergence is a natural measure of dissimilarity between stochastic policies and, when small, allows second-order Taylor approximations of the expected return change to be reliable. Under a quadratic approximation to the KL constraint and a linear approximation to the surrogate objective, the constrained problem becomes a quadratic program. TRPO uses the conjugate gradient algorithm to compute a (approximate) natural gradient step by solving a linear system involving the Fisher information matrix (the Hessian of the KL objective). The step is then scaled (line search) to ensure the empirical KL constraint is satisfied.

The key intuition: instead of taking the raw gradient step that might change the policy too much in distributional terms, TRPO takes a step that is large in the ascent direction but restricted so the new policy remains close to the old one in KL distance. This mitigates collapse, large performance drops, and poor approximations that occur when function approximators are pushed too far in a single update. TRPO produced a robust algorithm that outperformed naive policy gradient and vanilla natural gradient methods in many early deep RL benchmarks.

However, TRPO is computationally heavy: it requires computing Fisher–vector products, conjugate gradient iterations, and line searches, and it enforces the KL constraint across full-batch updates. The complexity motivated simpler approximations—most notably PPO.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): clipping and surrogate objectives

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was developed to capture the benefits of trust-region-style updates while being simpler to implement, more sample-efficient per update, and more amenable to mini-batch stochastic optimization. PPO replaces the hard constrained optimization of TRPO with a surrogate objective that either clips the probability ratio or penalizes large KL divergences. The most commonly used variant uses clipping.

Define the probability ratio
$r_\theta(s,a) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|s)}$.
Using the same surrogate expectation as TRPO, PPO's clipped objective is:
$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \min\left( r_\theta(s,a) \hat{A}, \; \mathrm{clip}(r_\theta(s,a), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A} \right) \right]$,
where $\hat{A}$ is an estimate of the advantage (typically from a critic) and $\epsilon$ is a small constant, often $0.1$ or $0.2$. The clipping operator prevents $r_\theta$ from moving outside $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in its contribution to the objective: improvements beyond that are truncated. If $\hat{A}$ is positive, increasing $r_\theta$ above $1+\epsilon$ does not further increase the objective; if $\hat{A}$ is negative, decreasing $r_\theta$ below $1-\epsilon$ does not further decrease the objective. Thus the clip discourages excessively large policy updates in either beneficial or harmful directions.

Why clipping? The clipping surrogate is a simple, implementable way to prevent destructive updates: it limits how much the policy can change its action probabilities per sample in terms of the ratio $r_\theta$. Unlike TRPO's KL constraint, clipping is a soft, sample-wise mechanism that is directly compatible with stochastic gradient descent and minibatching. Despite being a heuristic, PPO with clipping performs comparably to TRPO across many domains, with far simpler code and computation.

Alternative PPO variants include a KL-penalty objective:
$L^{\text{KL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ r_\theta \hat{A} - c \; \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\|\pi_\theta) \right]$,
where $c$ is a penalty coefficient that is sometimes adapted online so that the observed KL stays near a target value. This variant has more direct ties to TRPO and can be interpreted as a Lagrangian relaxation of the TRPO constraint.

PPO's empirical success arises from a few practical points. First, the clipping prevents step sizes that are too large while allowing many meaningful updates. Second, PPO is friendly to modern stochastic optimizers (Adam) and minibatched gradient updates, so it scales well with neural networks and parallelized sample collection. Finally, combining the surrogate policy objective with a value-function loss and an entropy bonus forms a stable multi-loss objective:
$L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) - c_v (V_\theta(s) - \hat{V})^2 + c_e \, \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) \right]$,
where $c_v$ and $c_e$ weigh the value loss and entropy bonus respectively, and $\hat{V}$ is a target return (e.g., Monte Carlo or bootstrapped target). The entropy term encourages exploration early in training.

# Actor‑critic architectures, value function learning, and bootstrapping

Actor‑critic methods split the learning task into two interacting components: an actor (the policy) and a critic (a value function estimator). The actor updates the policy parameters in a direction suggested by policy gradient estimates, while the critic estimates value functions used to reduce variance of the policy gradient and provide bootstrapped targets.

The value function can be the state-value $V^\pi(s)$, action-value $Q^\pi(s,a)$, or advantage $A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$. In practice, many actor‑critic algorithms learn a parametric approximation $V_\phi(s)$ with parameters $\phi$ to approximate $V^\pi(s)$. The critic is trained by minimizing a temporal-difference (TD) error; for 1-step TD:
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{TD}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{s,a,s'} \left[ \left( V_\phi(s) - \left( r(s,a) + \gamma V_\phi(s') \right) \right)^2 \right]$.
This objective bootstraps because it uses the critic's own estimate $V_\phi(s')$ on the right-hand side as the target. Bootstrapping yields lower variance, efficient sample use, and faster learning in many tasks, but it introduces bias when the function approximator is imperfect.

Bootstrapping is closely linked to multi-step returns and eligibility traces. Instead of 1-step TD, one can use $k$-step returns or $\lambda$-returns. A popular practical choice is generalized advantage estimation (GAE), which computes advantages as a weighted sum of $n$-step TD residuals:
$\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$,
where $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$. The GAE parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$ interpolates bias and variance: $\lambda=0$ yields 1-step TD (low variance, higher bias), and $\lambda\to 1$ approaches Monte Carlo returns (higher variance, lower bias). Using GAE with a bootstrapped value function usually produces more stable advantage estimates for policy updates.

The interaction between actor and critic can be synchronous (both updated in lockstep on the same batch of transitions) or asynchronous/parallel (multiple actors collect data while a central learner updates). Critic learning stabilizes policy updates because the critic provides lower-variance advantage estimates compared to raw returns, but if the critic is poor or biased, it can steer the policy update wrongly. Hence accurate critic estimation, regular updates, and techniques to limit critic-induced bias are important.

# Variance and bias tradeoffs, value baselines

The policy gradient estimator typically has high variance because returns are stochastic and the policy gradient relies on sampling actions and trajectories. To reduce variance without introducing bias, we use baselines. The basic observation is that subtracting any function $b(s)$ from the action-value term in the policy gradient does not change the expected gradient:
$\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) b(s) \right] = b(s) \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a|s) = 0$.
Hence using the state-value as baseline yields the advantage estimator $A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$, which measures how much better an action is compared to the expected action. Subtracting a baseline reduces variance while preserving unbiasedness, assuming the baseline is independent of the action.

Despite variance reductions, practical algorithms must handle the bias–variance tradeoff introduced by bootstrapping in the critic. Bootstrapping reduces variance because the critic approximates future returns, but when the critic is inaccurate it can introduce bias into the advantage estimates. Multi-step returns and GAE provide knobs ($k$ and $\lambda$) to trade variance for bias: smaller $k$ or $\lambda$ gives more bootstrapping (lower variance, higher bias), while larger values rely more on actual returns (higher variance, lower bias).

Another source of variance is the sampling distribution used to estimate expectations. On-policy methods sample trajectories from the current policy and use them directly; off-policy importance sampling can reweight older data but introduces high-variance importance weights. Actor‑critic methods usually operate on on-policy mini-batches (PPO) or use off-policy corrections (e.g., importance sampling, Retrace, V-trace) to mitigate this.

Practical variance reduction also includes normalizing advantages (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation), using larger batch sizes, or using baselines learned to predict returns with lower variance. The baseline itself can be regularized or constrained to prevent overfitting, which would otherwise reduce variance artificially by absorbing useful signal.

# Stability issues: reward scaling, normalization, and bootstrapping failures

Training instability is one of the major practical obstacles in deep RL. Several interacting issues are common:

Reward scaling. The magnitude of rewards directly affects gradient magnitudes for policy and value updates. If rewards are too large, value losses blow up and policy gradients become large, leading to unstable updates; if too small, learning is slow. Many environments have reward scales that vary widely. Practical solutions include reward clipping (clip rewards to a bounded range), reward normalization (divide by running standard deviation), or environment-specific scaling (rescaling rewards so typical returns are of order 1). Note that clipping can distort optimal policies (it changes the optimization objective), whereas normalization tries to preserve structure while stabilizing learning.

Observation and advantage normalization. Input features that have widely differing scales impair optimizer performance. Normalizing observations (subtract running mean, divide by running std) often helps. Advantage normalization—subtracting the batch mean and dividing by batch standard deviation for estimated advantages—helps maintain stable policy gradient magnitudes and is commonly used with PPO. A subtle point: advantage normalization introduces a dependence between samples in a minibatch (because normalization uses batch statistics), but the empirical benefits usually outweigh this complication.

Bootstrapping failures and value collapse. Because the critic bootstraps from its own predictions, errors can propagate and amplify. A classic failure mode is value function collapse, where the critic outputs extremely large magnitudes or collapses to a constant, invalidating advantage estimates and producing useless policy updates. Causes include high learning rates for the value network, insufficient regularization, or poorly scaled rewards. Remedies include clipping the value loss gradients, using separate learning rates for actor and critic (often the critic requires a larger or smaller rate depending on tasks), adding trust-region-like constraints on the value updates, and monitoring value predictions for drift.

Catastrophic policy updates. Even with clipping or trust regions, sometimes policy updates shift mass to previously poorly visited regions that have unknown or low returns; exploration noise and high entropy early on help prevent collapse. Early stopping based on KL (stop updating when the empirical KL between new and old policies exceeds a target) is another practical safeguard.

Off-policy bootstrap mismatch. When using replay buffers or off-policy data with an on-policy algorithm, the bootstrapped targets may be biased because the state-action distribution does not match that of the current policy. Techniques to handle this include importance sampling corrections, off-policy value-estimation algorithms (Retrace, V-trace), and limiting how old the data can be (stale data causes mismatch).

Numerical issues. Softmax/log-prob computations, log-likelihood ratios, and KL computations can overflow or underflow for extreme logits; use numerically stable formulations (log-sum-exp, clipped probabilities). Gradient clipping (global norm clipping) prevents extreme updates.

# Practical hyperparameters and implementation considerations

While theory guides algorithm design, hyperparameters and implementation details often determine whether an algorithm works well in practice. Below are practical recommendations and explanations for common choices. These are starting points — tasks vary, and hyperparameter tuning is often necessary.

Policy architecture and action parameterization. For continuous actions, policies are typically Gaussian with state-dependent mean and either (a) state-dependent diagonal variance (predicted by the network) or (b) a network predicts mean while log-standard-deviations are treated as global parameters. Predicting variance per state can improve expressiveness but sometimes destabilizes learning; many practitioners use fixed (but learnable) log-stds.

Learning rates and optimizers. Adam is the de facto optimizer for both actor and critic; small learning rates are commonly used. Typical PPO learning rates are in the range $3\mathrm{e}{-4}$ to $3\mathrm{e}{-5}$ for the policy and similar or slightly higher/lower for the value network. Use separate optimizers or separate parameter groups for actor and critic so they can have different learning rates and weight decay settings.

Batch size, minibatches, and epochs. PPO is often implemented in an on-policy minibatch SGD fashion: collect $N$ steps of experience (e.g., per actor or in aggregate), form a dataset of size $T$ (e.g., $T = 2048$ steps), then perform multiple epochs of minibatch updates (e.g., 3–10 epochs) over this dataset with minibatch sizes (e.g., 64–512). More epochs with the same data tend to improve sample-efficiency but can lead to overfitting to the batch and greater policy shift; the clipping mechanism mitigates this. Choose $T$ such that the batch contains sufficient on-policy diversity but is not so large that updates are slow or excessively stale.

Clipping parameter $\epsilon$. Typical values are $0.1$ or $0.2$. Smaller $\epsilon$ means more conservative updates; larger $\epsilon$ allows more aggressive policy changes. If you see too-slow learning, increase $\epsilon$ slightly; if you observe instability, reduce it.

Entropy coefficient. An entropy bonus encourages exploration and prevents premature collapse to deterministic policies. Typical values range from $0.0$ (for environments requiring determinism) to $0.01$–$0.02$ for continuous control. Annealing entropy coefficient over time is a reasonable strategy: higher early on, lower later.

Value loss coefficient and normalization. When combining policy and value losses, weight them to balance training signals. Typical PPO setups use a value coefficient $c_v$ around $0.5$ and a small coefficient for entropy, but these require tuning per environment. Normalizing the value target (or normalizing returns) and clipping value network updates (or output) helps prevent runaway values.

GAE $\lambda$ and discount $\gamma$. Standard choices are $\gamma \in [0.98, 0.999]$ depending on the horizon and $\lambda \in [0.9, 0.98]$ for GAE. Lower $\lambda$ reduces variance by relying more on bootstrapping; higher $\lambda$ can increase sample-efficiency when rewards are less stochastic.

Gradient clipping and max-norms. Clip gradients by global norm (e.g., 0.5–1.0) to prevent exploding updates. Also clip policy ratio values numerically when computing log probabilities.

Normalization of observations and advantages. Track running mean and variance of observations and normalize inputs to the networks. Normalize advantages within each update batch (subtract mean, divide by std, plus numerical epsilon).

Early stopping with KL. In PPO variants using a KL penalty or even with clipping, computing the mean KL between old and new policies after each optimization epoch and stopping further epochs if KL exceeds a target (e.g., $0.01$–$0.03$) is a practical way to avoid excessive policy change.

Parallelism and data collection. Modern implementations use vectorized environments to collect trajectories in parallel (e.g., 8–256 parallel envs) to improve throughput and reduce correlation between successive steps collected by a single process. When using many environments, make sure batch sizes scale appropriately.

Diagnostics and monitoring. Log and monitor: policy loss, value loss, entropy, mean KL divergence between successive policies, value predictions and their means/variance, advantage statistics, and episode returns. Visualization helps diagnose learning issues: e.g., sudden spikes in KL or value loss indicate instability; negative correlation between value loss and returns may suggest critic misestimation.

Miscellaneous implementation tips. Use numerically stable operations for probabilities and KL computations. When computing ratios, evaluate them in log space when possible: $r = \exp(\log \pi_\theta - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}})$ computed stably. Seed and deterministic settings help with reproducibility but expect some run-to-run variability. Modularize code so you can swap advantage estimators, value-loss weights, or clipping strategies easily.

Final remarks

Policy optimization with actor‑critic methods is a balance between principled mathematical derivations (policy gradient theorem, natural gradients, trust regions) and pragmatism (clipping, normalization, careful hyperparameter choices). TRPO provided a rigorous constrained-update viewpoint that motivated PPO's simpler surrogate objectives. Actor‑critic architectures combined with bootstrapped critics and variance-reducing baselines enable scalable learning, but also introduce bias and instability that must be controlled with normalization, small steps, and diagnostic monitoring.

When reading research papers, pay attention to the choices authors make around clipping vs. KL penalties, advantage estimation method (GAE parameters), normalization procedures, network architectures, and hyperparameters. Small implementation choices and tuning often explain much of the empirical performance differences between algorithms. Understanding the derivations and the practical stability concerns together will enable you to both implement these algorithms and critically read and reproduce results from the literature.
