Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) traditionally formulates an agent’s objective as maximizing expected cumulative reward. This classical picture leads to deterministic optimal policies in the limit of full information and ignores the value of stochasticity for exploration and robustness. Entropy- and Kullback–Leibler (KL)-regularized variants of RL amend this by explicitly rewarding stochasticity or penalizing divergence from a reference behavior. These regularizations produce several practical and theoretical benefits: they yield softer (probabilistic) optimal policies, provide a direct handle on exploration versus exploitation, enable trust-region-style stability guarantees, and connect RL to probabilistic inference. In this chapter we develop the mathematical forms of entropy- and KL-regularized objectives, derive their optimal policies using Lagrangian duality, interpret the normalization constant (partition function) and its log as a value function, and place the whole framework in the energy-based / control-as-inference perspective. We also discuss the role of the temperature parameter \( \beta \), how KL constraints implement trust regions, and the relation of these ideas to maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning (IRL).


# Entropy-regularized objectives and temperature

Entropy-regularized RL augments the usual expected-reward objective with a term that rewards the randomness (entropy) of the policy. For a stationary Markov decision process (MDP) with states \(x\) and actions \(y\) we often consider, for a single-step decision, maximizing
\[
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim d,\, y \sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[ r(x,y) \big] + \beta \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim d}\big[ \mathcal{H}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\big)\big],
\]
where \(r(x,y)\) is the reward function, \(d\) is some state distribution (e.g., occupancy or initial-state distribution), \(\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)) = -\sum_y \pi(y\mid x) \log \pi(y\mid x)\) is the Shannon entropy, and \(\beta > 0\) is a scalar called the temperature or entropy weight. In the multi-step, discounted or average-reward MDP setting, the entropy term can be added per time step, leading to a cumulative objective that trades off reward and policy entropy across trajectories.

Intuitively, the entropy term encourages policies that spread probability mass rather than concentrating it on a single action. This has multiple consequences. First, when the reward landscape is flat or noisy, the entropy term prevents premature collapse to arbitrary deterministic choices and thus supports exploration. Second, entropy leads to smoother gradients when optimizing parameterized policies, which can make policy optimization more numerically stable. Third, by keeping a policy stochastic, the agent remains robust to modeling errors or non-stationarity.

The parameter \( \beta \) calibrates how strongly the agent values entropy relative to reward. Small \( \beta \) means the agent cares mainly about reward and will concentrate probability mass on high-reward actions; large \( \beta \) emphasizes entropy, producing near-uniform action distributions. In the extreme limit \( \beta \to 0 \), the entropy-regularized objective recovers the standard expected-reward maximization and typically yields deterministic optimal policies. In the opposite extreme \( \beta \to \infty \), the objective is dominated by entropy and the optimal policy tends to uniformity (ignoring reward). Because \( \beta \) plays the same mathematical role as inverse temperature in statistical mechanics, it is commonly referred to as temperature; sometimes authors use the inverse \( \tau = 1/\beta \) for similar interpretations.

Entropy regularization can be applied in several variants. One can regularize the marginal action entropy \(\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot\mid x))\) per state, or regularize the entropy of the entire trajectory distribution. For temporally extended settings, an additive per-step entropy term gives rise to a dynamic programming operator that uses softmax (log-sum-exp) instead of hard max. This replacement yields "soft" Bellman backups and value functions that are smooth functions of rewards, which is crucial to deriving closed-form optimal policies in the next sections.


# KL constraints to a reference policy and Lagrangian duality

An alternative to adding an entropy bonus is to impose a constraint on the KL divergence between the learned policy and a given reference (or prior) policy. The typical constrained optimization view is: maximize expected reward subject to a bound on KL distance from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Formally, for each state \(x\) or averaged under some state distribution, one might write
\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot\mid x)} \ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)]
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \le \epsilon,
\]
or in expectation over states,
\[
\max_{\pi} \ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim d}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)]
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim d}\big[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))\big] \le \epsilon.
\]

This formulation encodes a trust-region idea: the new policy should not deviate too far from the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) as measured by KL divergence. The reference policy can be the current policy in an iterative policy improvement algorithm, which implements a conservative update, or it can be a fixed prior capturing known safe behaviors or behavioral cloning targets.

To solve the constrained problem one commonly forms the Lagrangian, introducing a nonnegative multiplier \( \lambda \) for the KL constraint. For the per-state constrained problem the Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi, \lambda) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \lambda\Big(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})-\epsilon\Big).
\]
Rearranging terms and dropping constants yields an unconstrained objective of the form
\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \lambda\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}).
\]
Because \( \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\log\pi(y)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)]\), the Lagrangian expands to
\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[r(x,y) + \lambda\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\big] - \lambda\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\log\pi(y)],
\]
which is equivalent to maximizing reward plus a weighted entropy term and an expected log-prior term. If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is uniform, the expected log-prior is constant and the Lagrangian reduces to the entropy-regularized form. More generally, KL-regularized objectives smoothly interpolate between reward, entropy, and prior alignment depending on \(\lambda\). Solving the primal constrained problem is often done via dual ascent on \(\lambda\) while solving for \(\pi\) in the inner maximization, or by directly optimizing the unconstrained Lagrangian with a chosen \(\lambda\).

This Lagrangian viewpoint is powerful because it shows that a KL constraint is dual to adding a KL penalty (or entropy-like terms) in the objective. It also yields closed-form optimal policies for the inner maximization, as the next section demonstrates.


# Closed-form optimal policy: \(\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}\)

One of the most useful analytic results in entropy- and KL-regularized RL is that the optimal policy of the Lagrangian (or the entropy-regularized objective) has a softmax form relative to a reference policy. Consider the Lagrangian form
\[
\max_{\pi(\cdot\mid x)} \ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[ r(x,y) \big] - \beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big),
\]
where we have set \(\beta = \lambda\) for notational consistency. The optimization is over the probability simplex for \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\). This is a convex optimization: the expected reward is linear in \(\pi\) and the KL divergence is convex, so the objective is concave in \(\pi\) (we maximize minus a convex). One can derive the optimal \(\pi^*\) by calculus of variations or by forming the Lagrangian for the normalization constraint \(\sum_y \pi(y\mid x)=1\). Doing so yields the stationary condition that the optimal probability mass for each action \(y\) satisfies
\[
0 = r(x,y) - \beta\big(\log \pi^*(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big) - \nu,
\]
where \(\nu\) is the Lagrange multiplier enforcing normalization. Solving this for \(\pi^*\) gives
\[
\log \pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y) + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) - \frac{\nu}{\beta}.
\]
Exponentiating and absorbing the normalization constant \(Z(x)=e^{\nu/\beta}\) yields the classic closed-form solution
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big).
\]
Written more compactly,
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big).
\]

This is a Boltzmann-type distribution that reweights the reference policy by an exponentiated reward. High-reward actions are exponentially favored relative to the reference; the temperature \( \beta \) controls how peaked that reweighting is. If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is uniform, this reduces to the familiar Gibbs distribution
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big).
\]
If \(\beta\) is small, the exponentiation sharpens probabilities toward the maximal-reward actions; if \(\beta\) is large, the exponentiation is shallow and the reference policy dominates.

This optimal policy holds in both single-step and per-state inner optimizations occurring during dynamic programming-style updates. In temporally extended problems with entropy added per step, the optimal policy at state \(x\) has the same softmax form with the Q-function \(Q(x,y)\) in place of one-step reward:
\[
\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}Q(x,y)\big).
\]
Thus, in the full dynamic setting, exponentiated action-values and the reference policy determine action probabilities. This form is central to many modern algorithms, including soft actor-critic (SAC) and relative entropy policy search.
