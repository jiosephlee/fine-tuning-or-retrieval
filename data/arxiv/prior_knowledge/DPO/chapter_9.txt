Introduction

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a practical framework that has become central to aligning large language models and other generative systems with human preferences. At a high level RLHF converts human judgments about model outputs into a numerical reward function, then uses reinforcement learning (RL) to update a policy so that it produces outputs that receive higher human preference scores. The approach separates three stages: (1) collecting human preference data, typically as pairwise comparisons; (2) training a reward model that maps an input and candidate output to a scalar reward consistent with the human comparisons; and (3) fine‑tuning a policy using RL to maximize that learned reward while often constraining deviation from a reference model. Each stage contains many design choices that materially affect performance: how to collect and format annotations, the architecture and loss for the reward model, whether to fine‑tune a language model or train from scratch, how to normalize and regularize rewards, and which RL algorithm to use. This chapter explains each stage in detail, with intuition and the key mathematical formulations used in practice, and then discusses practical recommendations and persistent challenges such as instability and distribution mismatch.

# Collecting pairwise preference data and human annotation protocols

The most common form of human feedback used in RLHF is pairwise preference data: for a given prompt or context $x$, two candidate responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ are presented to a human annotator, who indicates which response they prefer (or sometimes that they are equal). Compared with absolute ratings (e.g., a score from 1 to 5), pairwise comparisons are easier for humans to perform consistently because the task reduces to relative judgment and avoids scale calibration issues. Pairwise data also leads directly to simple probabilistic models for the reward function and robust training objectives.

A typical annotation protocol begins by sampling a prompt $x$ (drawn from some distribution of interest) and generating candidate responses $y_1,y_2$ from one or more models. The candidates are then shown to an annotator under blinded conditions (the annotator should not know which model produced which response unless model identity is part of the evaluation). Clear annotation instructions are essential: they define the axes of preference (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, accuracy, style) and provide examples of edge cases. Instructions should include what to do when both answers are acceptable, when both are wrong but for different reasons, and how to handle hallucinations, offensive content, or sensitive topics.

Human annotation protocols often include quality control mechanisms. These include qualification tests for annotators, periodic calibration tasks where ground‑truth preferences are known to the experimenter, and inter‑annotator agreement metrics to detect ambiguous prompts. For ambiguous or borderline comparisons, allowing a "no preference" option can be useful; these ties can be modeled explicitly or discarded. Annotation policies should also specify whether annotators are allowed to edit responses, ask clarifying questions, or use external tools (usually not). When collecting preferences at scale, one must balance annotation cost versus the marginal utility of more labels; oftentimes a few tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand comparisons are used in practice, depending on model size and task complexity.

When designing the candidate generation process, diversity matters. If all candidate responses are extremely similar or come from a narrow set of checkpoints, the reward model will not learn meaningful structure. It is common to include responses from supervised fine‑tuned (SFT) models, various RLHF checkpoints, random sampling, temperature variation, and even adversarial examples crafted to expose known failure modes. Another important practice is active sampling: as the policy evolves, sample comparisons targeted where the reward model is uncertain or where the policy frequently visits, so annotations remain informative.

# Reward modeling from preferences: architectures and objectives

Once pairwise preference data is available, the next step is to train a reward model $R_\theta(x,y)$ parameterized by $\theta$ that assigns a scalar reward to a response $y$ for context $x$. The usual approach is to model the probability that $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ as a logistic function of the difference in predicted rewards:
$$
P_\theta(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(R_\theta(x,y_1) - R_\theta(x,y_2)\big) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\big(-\big(R_\theta(x,y_1)-R_\theta(x,y_2)\big)\big)}.
$$
This formulation is equivalent to a Bradley–Terry or Thurstone model: only reward differences matter for preference probabilities. Given a dataset of comparisons $D=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_1,y^{(i)}_2, z^{(i)})\}$ where $z^{(i)}=1$ if $y_1$ was preferred and $0$ otherwise, the standard maximum likelihood objective is the binary cross‑entropy loss
$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_i \Big[z^{(i)}\log P_\theta(y^{(i)}_1\succ y^{(i)}_2\mid x^{(i)}) + (1-z^{(i)})\log\big(1-P_\theta(y^{(i)}_1\succ y^{(i)}_2\mid x^{(i)})\big)\Big].
$$
Because this loss depends only on differences $R_\theta(x,y_1)-R_\theta(x,y_2)$, reward model training is invariant to adding an input‑dependent constant to $R_\theta$, but not to scaling; i.e., multiplying $R_\theta$ by a positive scalar changes the sharpness of the model's predicted probabilities.

Architecturally, reward models are almost always implemented with the same family of neural networks used for the policy, typically transformers for language tasks. A common design is to take a pretrained language model encoder and add a small scalar head. For sequence outputs $y$ the model computes a representation $h(x,y)$, e.g., the final hidden state corresponding to a sequence end token or a pooled representation, then applies a linear projection to produce $R_\theta(x,y)=w^\top h(x,y)+b$. Using pretrained checkpoints for the encoder improves sample efficiency and stability. Other architectural choices include whether to share weights between the reward model and the policy (not typical for safety reasons), whether to use attention over the prompt separately, and whether to model reply length explicitly. Some work also uses ensemble reward models to reduce variance and mitigate overfitting to specific annotators.

Beyond the basic logistic objective, several practical modifications are common. Regularization (weight decay, dropout) and early stopping reduce overfitting to limited annotation data. Label noise models can be applied if annotators are imperfect; for example, modeling per‑annotator reliability or incorporating an explicit noise parameter in the likelihood can help. In some settings annotators provide graded scores rather than pairwise comparisons; those can be modeled with regression losses or ordinal models, but pairwise logistic loss remains a robust default. Finally, calibrating the reward outputs (e.g., to have zero mean or unit variance over some baseline distribution) is sometimes applied before using rewards in RL; this affects learning dynamics and is discussed below in the section on normalization.

# The standard RLHF three‑stage pipeline: SFT → reward model → RL fine‑tuning

A widely used practical pipeline for RLHF has three distinct stages: supervised fine‑tuning (SFT), reward modeling, and RL fine‑tuning. Each stage has a clear role and helps control instability and distribution drift.

1. Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT). In the first stage, a pretrained base model (e.g., a large language model pretrained with self‑supervision) is fine‑tuned on human‑generated demonstrations or curated high‑quality data using standard supervised learning (maximum likelihood). This creates a reasonable initial policy $\pi_\text{SFT}$ that produces coherent and mostly safe outputs. SFT is important because direct RL from a randomly initialized or purely pretrained base can lead to degenerate behaviors; supervised learning anchors the model to human language and prevents catastrophic divergence.

2. Reward model. Next, collect human preference data comparing outputs from various models and contexts. Train a reward model $R_\theta$ as described earlier to predict those preferences. Because the reward model is learned and approximate, it can contain errors; keeping the RL stage bounded relative to the SFT policy mitigates over‑optimization of reward model errors.

3. RL fine‑tuning. Using $R_\theta$ as the reward function, perform policy optimization to obtain a new policy $\pi$ that produces outputs expected to maximize human preference. In practice RL is done against sequence outputs (token sequences), and the reward is usually computed once a full response is generated (or via per‑token shaping approximations). A common approach is to use policy gradient methods (e.g., PPO) with a KL penalty to keep the learned policy close to $\pi_\text{SFT}$:
$$
\mathcal{J}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R_\theta(x,y) - \beta \,\text{KL}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{SFT}(\cdot\mid x))\big],
$$
where $\beta$ is a scalar that trades off reward maximization against staying near the SFT policy. The KL term functions as a trust‑region or regularizer, preventing the policy from exploiting reward‑model idiosyncrasies or producing degenerate outputs. In practice one usually uses either an explicit KL penalty in the objective or a hard KL constraint enforced approximately by the RL optimizer. Additionally, entropy bonuses and reward normalization (discussed below) are often added to stabilize learning.

This three‑stage pipeline is modular: one can iterate (collect more comparisons on new model outputs and retrain the reward model), and in practice several rounds of data collection and RL fine‑tuning yield progressive improvements. The design balances human supervision (SFT and preference labels) with automated optimization (RL) while keeping the learned policy anchored to human behavior.

# Reward normalization, baselines and partition function considerations

Rewards from the learned reward model can have arbitrary scale and additive offsets because the preference loss depends only on differences. These degrees of freedom have concrete implications for RL optimization and for regularization terms like KL penalties, so careful normalization is important.

Because the logistic preference model is invariant to adding a context‑dependent constant $c(x)$ to $R_\theta(x,y)$, the absolute level of $R_\theta$ for a given $x$ is not identified by the data. In RL training, adding such a constant affects value estimates, advantage computations, and the interaction with KL regularization when the KL term is compared to reward magnitudes. A typical normalization is to zero‑center rewards per batch or per prompt distribution so that the expected reward under the current policy is near zero:
$$
\tilde R(x,y) = R_\theta(x,y) - \mu,\quad \mu = \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi} [R_\theta(x,y)].
$$
Similarly, scaling rewards to have unit variance across a batch or dataset (divide by empirical standard deviation) stabilizes learning rates across stages and models.

Baselines are essential in policy gradient methods to reduce variance. If the return for a sequence is $G$, policy gradient estimates typically use $G - b(x)$ where $b(x)$ is a baseline depending on context. The simplest baseline is the mean reward across a batch or a learned value function $V_\phi(x)$ trained to predict expected reward under the current policy. Using a learned critic allows for advantage estimation:
$$
A(x,y) = G(x,y) - V_\phi(x),
$$
and reduces gradient variance substantially, which is crucial for sequence spaces with high variance returns.

The “partition function” or scaling of the reward model matters when we combine reward with regularizers that have absolute units. For example, if we optimize
$$
\mathbb{E}[R_\theta(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathbb{E}[\text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})],
$$
the relative magnitudes of $R_\theta$ and the KL determine the learned policy behavior. Because $R_\theta$ can be multiplied by a scalar without changing its fit to human preferences, one must choose a scale that makes $\beta$ interpretable; common practice is to normalize $R_\theta$ such that its standard deviation under sampled prompts and responses equals 1, and then tune $\beta$ (or equivalently the target KL) empirically.

Another partition‑function consideration arises when reward values are used in exponentiated weighting schemes (e.g., rejection sampling or importance sampling with weights proportional to $\exp(R/\tau)$). There the additive constant affects normalization but doesn't change relative probabilities; however the scale (temperature $\tau$) strongly affects concentration and variance. If rewards are too large, $\exp(R/\tau)$ becomes peaky and importance weights explode, creating high variance. Hence tempering or clipping of exponentiated rewards is often required.

In summary, although the reward model is trained only to predict preferences, once used for RL the numeric properties of its outputs matter. Practical steps include zero‑centering per context or per batch, scaling to unit variance, using baselines or learned critics for advantage estimation, and carefully tuning KL penalties or temperature parameters to achieve stable tradeoffs.

# Common RLHF algorithms (REINFORCE, PPO) and empirical practices

Two policy optimization methods commonly used in RLHF are REINFORCE (policy gradient) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Both operate on policies that generate token sequences, but they differ in how updates are applied and how they trade off exploration and stability.

REINFORCE is the canonical episodic policy gradient. For a trajectory (sequence) $y$ with return $G$, REINFORCE updates policy parameters $\phi$ with gradient
$$
\nabla_\phi J = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\phi}\big[(G-b)\,\nabla_\phi \log \pi_\phi(y \mid x)\big],
$$
where $b$ is a baseline (often the mean return or a learned value). REINFORCE is simple and unbiased, but suffers from high variance in sequence spaces, and naive application typically requires many samples. When episodes are entire generated responses, variance is high; therefore REINFORCE is less used in large language model RLHF except in simpler or simulation settings.

PPO is a more practical, stable method which performs multiple epochs of minibatch updates using a clipped surrogate objective that limits the size of policy updates. For a per‑token parameterization of the policy, PPO defines the probability ratio
$$
r_\phi(y \mid x) = \frac{\pi_\phi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\phi_\text{old}}(y\mid x)},
$$
and maximizes the clipped surrogate
$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{PPO}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \min\big( r_\phi A,\, \text{clip}(r_\phi, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A\big) \Big] - c_\text{KL}\,\text{KL}(\pi_\phi \| \pi_\text{ref}) + c_H \, \mathcal{H}(\pi_\phi),
$$
where $A$ is the advantage estimate, $\epsilon$ is the clipping parameter (e.g., 0.1–0.2), $c_\text{KL}$ is a coefficient for an auxiliary KL penalty relative to a reference policy (often the SFT policy), and $c_H$ scales an entropy bonus $\mathcal{H}(\pi)$ to encourage exploration. PPO is widely used in RLHF because the clipping keeps updates conservative and multiple passes over collected data improve sample efficiency compared with single‑step REINFORCE.

Empirical practices in RLHF with these algorithms include the following. First, compute rewards at the sequence level and then distribute them over tokens via advantage estimation strategies, e.g., Monte Carlo returns or value‑bootstrap returns. A learned critic $V_\phi(x)$ is commonly trained to predict expected sequence reward under the current policy; using temporal‑difference updates or batch regression stabilizes the advantage estimates. Second, the KL term is often treated specially: rather than a fixed penalty coefficient, one can adapt the coefficient to keep the average KL per prompt close to a target value, or clip updates when the observed KL exceeds a threshold. This adaptive KL control helps prevent runaway divergence from the SFT policy.

Other practical tips: use PPO with mini‑batches and multiple epochs over the same collected rollouts to maximize sample efficiency; perform gradient clipping to reduce ill‑conditioning; apply reward scaling and advantage normalization (subtract mean and divide by standard deviation across the batch) to stabilize training; and maintain a replay buffer or fresh on‑policy rollouts depending on the desired bias–variance tradeoff. For language models, it's common to treat the policy as autoregressive token distributions and compute per‑token log probabilities; the trajectory log probability is the sum of token log probabilities, and the policy gradient naturally decomposes to per‑token contributions.

Finally, hardware and throughput considerations matter: generating long responses is expensive, so practitioners often cap response length, batch prompts efficiently, and precompute reward model evaluations where possible. Using mixed precision, distributed rollouts, and offloading the reward model to separate workers helps scale RLHF to large models.

# Challenges: sample efficiency, instability, and distribution mismatch

RLHF is powerful but faces several fundamental challenges in practice. Three of the most salient are sample efficiency, training instability, and distribution mismatch between the data used to train the reward model and the distribution visited by the policy during RL.

Sample efficiency. Human annotations are expensive. Training an expressive reward model and then using RL to optimize a policy across a vast combinatorial output space requires many samples (generated responses) and correspondingly many human comparisons. The classical sample‑inefficiency of RL is exacerbated by long, structured outputs. To mitigate this, practitioners use pretrained models and SFT to start near reasonable policies; use transfer learning and shared encoders for the reward model; employ active learning to request annotations for the most informative comparisons; and use synthetic or proxy reward signals where appropriate. Another strategy is to reuse unlabeled model outputs with semi‑supervised methods or to augment pairwise labels with scalar ratings or critique explanations to improve learning per annotation.

Instability. Learning can be unstable in several ways. The reward model is an approximation of human preferences and contains errors; if the policy exploits these errors (“reward hacking”) the agent may produce outputs that score highly under the reward model but are undesirable under true human preferences. To limit this, standard practice includes keeping a strong KL constraint to the SFT policy, regularizing the reward model, using early stopping, and iterating: collect new human labels on policy outputs and retrain the reward model to close loopholes. Optimizer instability arises as well: large policy updates can lead to mode collapse or degenerate outputs. Conservative algorithms like PPO with clipping and adaptive KL control are empirical mitigations, as are advantage normalization and gradient clipping.

Distribution mismatch. The reward model is trained on comparisons between candidate responses from earlier policies and particular prompt distributions. During RL, the policy shifts and starts visiting regions of the input–output space that were not represented—or were underrepresented—in the preference data. Because reward models are only empirically accurate on their training distribution, predictions on out‑of‑distribution outputs can be arbitrarily wrong, causing optimization to push the policy toward nonsensical or unsafe behaviors. Addressing distribution mismatch requires iterative data collection (relabeling new policy outputs), adversarial or targeted sampling to expand coverage, and using uncertainty estimates (e.g., ensembles or Bayesian approaches) to detect when the reward model is extrapolating.

Other persistent problems include evaluator bias and annotator variance: annotators may have systematic preferences that do not generalize to the user population, or they may disagree leading to noisy supervision. There is also the problem of multi‑objective alignment: human preferences can be context dependent and tradeoffs (e.g., helpfulness vs. safety) may be hard to encode in a single scalar. Multi‑label or hierarchical reward models can help but complicate RL.

Finally, evaluation itself is difficult: relying solely on the learned reward function for monitoring is circular. Independent evaluations, held‑out human annotations, red‑team testing, and automated checks are needed to detect unwanted behavior. Iteratively adding targeted comparisons from adversarial prompts, making reward models robust, and using conservative RL objectives remain the main practical defenses.

Conclusion

RLHF provides a pragmatic route to align complex generative models with human judgments by converting pairwise preferences into a differentiable reward model and using RL to improve policy outputs. The three‑stage pipeline (SFT → reward modeling → RL fine‑tuning) is widely used because it combines practical stability with the flexibility of RL. However careful attention to annotation design, reward normalization, baseline and advantage estimation, choice and tuning of policy optimization algorithms, and mitigation against instability and distributional drift is essential for success. Researchers and practitioners must iterate between data collection, reward model training, and safety‑oriented testing to maintain alignment as policies grow more capable.
