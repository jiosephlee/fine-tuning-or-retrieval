Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a principled way to optimize agents that act to maximize long-term objectives. When the agent is a language model (LM) producing sequences of tokens, RL supplies the mathematical and algorithmic machinery to tune the model to optimize sequence-level rewards that are hard or impossible to express as token-wise supervised losses. This chapter builds the foundational RL concepts and tools needed to understand methods for fine-tuning language models against reward signals: the Markov decision process (MDP) formalism, policies and returns, value functions, policy gradient methods including REINFORCE, techniques for credit assignment across long sequences, reward shaping and baselines, distinctions between on-policy and off-policy learning, episodic versus continuing formulations for text tasks, sources of variance in sequence-level RL, and practical stability challenges when applying RL to large language models.

The presentation emphasizes intuition together with precise formulas so that you can read and reason about contemporary RL-for-sequence-generation papers and implementations.

1. MDPs, policies, returns, and value functions

Reinforcement learning problems are most commonly formalized as Markov decision processes (MDPs). An MDP is a tuple \((\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)\) where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the state space, \(\mathcal{A}\) the action space, \(P(s' \mid s, a)\) the transition dynamics, \(r(s,a)\) a reward function, and \(\gamma \in [0,1]\) a discount factor. At discrete time steps \(t=0,1,2,\dots\) the agent observes state \(s_t\), chooses action \(a_t\), receives reward \(r_t = r(s_t,a_t)\), and moves to state \(s_{t+1}\) distributed according to \(P(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)\).

When the agent is a language model, the MDP is instantiated in terms of sequence generation. A common choice is to treat the partially-generated token sequence as the state. Formally, let tokens come from a vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\); then states can be prefixes \(s_t = (x_1,\dots,x_t)\) and actions are selecting the next token \(a_t = x_{t+1} \in \mathcal{V}\). The transition is deterministic given the token chosen: \(s_{t+1} = (s_t, a_t)\). Rewards can be sparse (e.g., nonzero only when the sequence ends) or dense (token-level rewards), and \(\gamma\) can be set to 1 for finite-horizon episodic generation tasks or to a value \(<1\) in continuing formulations.

A policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) defines the agent's behavior: the probability of selecting action \(a\) in state \(s\). In sequence generation with a neural language model, \(\pi\) is typically a parameterized conditional distribution \(\pi_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})\), often implemented with a softmax over logits computed by the model.

The central optimization goal is to find policy parameters \(\theta\) that maximize expected cumulative reward. Define a trajectory (episode) \(\tau = (s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots,s_T)\) where \(T\) may be finite. The return at time \(t\) is the discounted cumulative future reward
\[
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \gamma^k r_{t+k}.
\]
For finite-horizon tasks with episode length \(T\) and \(\gamma=1\), \(G_t = \sum_{k=t}^T r_k\). The performance objective (expected return from start states) is
\[
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[G_0].
\]

Value functions characterize expected returns from states or state-action pairs under a policy \(\pi\). The state-value function is
\[
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[ G_t \mid s_t = s \right],
\]
and the action-value function is
\[
Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[ G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a \right].
\]
They satisfy the Bellman equations
\[
V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a\mid s) \left[ r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a) V^\pi(s') \right],
\]
and
\[
Q^\pi(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a) \sum_{a'} \pi(a'\mid s') Q^\pi(s',a').
\]

In sequence generation, value functions measure expected future sequence-level reward given the current prefix (and possibly a chosen next token). Because transitions are deterministic (concatenation) and the environment is usually fully observable, the state representation reduces to the token history; nevertheless, the expected reward depends on the stochastic future choices of the policy.

2. Policy gradient theorem and REINFORCE

Directly maximizing \(J(\theta)\) by gradient-based optimization requires expressing the gradient \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) in terms of expectations that can be estimated from sampled trajectories. The policy gradient theorem provides exactly this.

Write the objective as an expectation over trajectories:
\[
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[G_0(\tau)],
\]
where the trajectory density under the policy and environment is
\[
p_\theta(\tau) = p(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t).
\]
Differentiating using the log-derivative trick leads to
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[ G_0(\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \right].
\]
This identity is the basis of the REINFORCE algorithm. A commonly used variant rewrites the gradient as an expectation over time steps with reward-to-go:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) G_t \right],
\]
where \(G_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T-1} \gamma^{k-t} r_k\) is the return-to-go from time \(t\). Sampling trajectories from \(\pi_\theta\) and forming Monte Carlo estimates yields the REINFORCE gradient estimator:
\[
\widehat{\nabla}_\theta J = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \, G_t.
\]
This estimator is unbiased, meaning its expectation equals the true gradient, but it typically has high variance, which motivates variance reduction techniques discussed below.

In language models, the policy \(\pi_\theta\) is a product of token-level conditional distributions:
\[
\pi_\theta(\tau) = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t}).
\]
Thus the score function gradients \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\) are exactly the same backpropagated gradients used in supervised training: the gradient of the log-probability of the chosen token. REINFORCE uses those same quantities weighted by return signals to nudge the model toward sequences with higher reward.

3. Reward-to-go, credit assignment in sequences

A central difficulty in sequence generation is credit assignment: attributing how much each token decision contributed to the final sequence-level reward. Returns-to-go \(G_t\) partially address this by associating the future cumulative reward from time \(t\) with the action taken at \(t\). Using \(G_t\) instead of the full episode return \(G_0\) reduces variance because it avoids attributing rewards that occurred before time \(t\) to action \(a_t\).

However, \(G_t\) still may be a noisy signal: many actions early in a long sequence may have little marginal influence on the final reward, and random future token choices add variance. In language tasks where reward is only available at the end (for example, human preference score or task success), the return-to-go at early steps equals the same scalar as at the start, providing no per-token discrimination. Additional methods aim to improve credit assignment.

One approach is temporal-difference (TD) learning combined with bootstrapped value-function approximations, which can provide lower-variance, biased estimators of expected returns. For example, actor-critic methods learn a parametric critic \(V_\phi(s)\) and use temporal-difference targets like
\[
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t),
\]
to form advantage estimates. Advantage functions
\[
A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)
\]
measure the extra value of taking \(a\) versus the average action at \(s\). Using advantage estimates instead of raw returns concentrates learning signal on meaningful deviations from typical performance at each state, improving learning speed and stability.

For sequence generation, learned critics can be implemented as additional neural networks conditioned on the token prefix (often sharing representations with the policy model). Critics allow estimation of per-prefix value and thus more informative per-token gradients. Another technique is to shape intermediate rewards to provide denser feedback (discussed in the next section).

4. Reward shaping and baselines

Reward shaping modifies the reward function to provide more informative intermediate signals, which can accelerate learning while preserving the optimal policy if done carefully. Potential-based reward shaping constructs shaped rewards
\[
r'_t = r_t + \gamma \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t),
\]
for some potential function \(\Phi\). Such shaping leaves the relative ordering of policies unchanged and does not change the optimal policy under standard assumptions. However, designing an appropriate potential \(\Phi\) for language tasks is nontrivial.

More commonly in sequence-level RL practitioners introduce auxiliary dense rewards that correlate with final objectives (e.g., next-token likelihood, n-gram overlap with reference, heuristic quality scores) but must ensure that these additions do not encourage degenerate behaviors (like copying or reward hacking).

Baselines are another crucial variance-reduction technique closely related to value functions. Subtracting a baseline \(b(s_t)\) from the return in the policy gradient estimator preserves unbiasedness so long as \(b(s_t)\) does not depend on the chosen action \(a_t\). The resulting gradient estimator is
\[
\widehat{\nabla}_\theta J = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \left( G_t - b(s_t) \right).
\]
Choosing \(b(s_t) = V^\pi(s_t)\) yields the advantage form where the estimator has minimal variance among all baselines that are functions of \(s_t\). In practice, simple baselines such as moving averages of returns, value networks trained to minimize mean-squared error, or per-token baselines (e.g., the expected reward under the current policy approximated by Monte Carlo) are used. For language models, a common practical baseline is the reward of greedy decoding (or a reference policy), referred to as "self-critical sequence training" in some literature. Subtracting the reward of a deterministic baseline sequence yields stable updates and directly optimizes improvements over the baseline behavior.

5. On-policy vs. off-policy learning

RL algorithms can be categorized by whether they require data generated by the current policy (on-policy) or can reuse data collected under different policies (off-policy). On-policy methods, such as REINFORCE and most actor-critic algorithms, estimate gradients using episodes sampled from \(\pi_\theta\). They are simple and unbiased with respect to the current policy, but sample-inefficient: new trajectories must be sampled whenever \(\theta\) changes significantly. Off-policy methods, like Q-learning and importance-sampled policy gradient estimators, can leverage previously collected experience and thus can be more sample-efficient.

In sequence generation with large LMs, on-policy approaches are common because the policy is represented by the LM and generation from it is cheap relative to collecting human feedback. However, on-policy sampling becomes expensive when human oracles must score outputs. Off-policy techniques permit reusing logged data (for example, previously rated model outputs), but they introduce estimation bias when the data distribution differs from the current policy. Importance sampling corrects for distribution mismatch: given trajectories from behavior policy \(\mu\), the gradient expectation can be rewritten with importance weights
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[ w(\tau) G_0(\tau) \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \right],
\]
with importance weight \(w(\tau) = p_\theta(\tau)/p_\mu(\tau) = \prod_t \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)}\). These products can have extremely high variance, especially in long sequences, so practical off-policy RL for sequences relies on stabilized techniques: truncated importance sampling, per-decision importance weights, trust-region constraints, or learning value estimators that are trained off-policy. Methods like importance-weighted actor-critic and experience replay with conservative updates are also applied.

For language models, logs of human ratings or preference comparisons form valuable off-policy datasets. Algorithms that can safely exploit these logs (such as inverse propensity weighting with variance control, or batch-constrained optimization) are critical to practical systems where collecting fresh labels is expensive.

6. Episodic vs. continuing tasks in text generation

Most text generation tasks are naturally episodic: generating a sentence, a response, or a document constitutes an episode with a clear termination when an end-of-sequence token is produced or a length budget is reached. In episodic problems, it is common to set the discount factor \(\gamma=1\) and treat each generation as a finite-horizon MDP. The return is then the sum of rewards across the generated tokens or, often, a single terminal reward.

Continuing tasks with \(\gamma<1\) are less common in generation, but there are cases where a stream of tokens corresponds to an ongoing process (for example, an interactive dialogue where the agent persists across turns). Discounting in continuing tasks places more emphasis on immediate rewards and can simplify infinite-horizon analyses by ensuring finite returns.

The episodic framing simplifies credit-assignment because episodes are independent samples for Monte Carlo estimators. It also aligns with many evaluation protocols: a model is judged by the quality of each completed output. When terminal rewards are sparse, the episodic setup intensifies the need for variance reduction strategies and for techniques such as reward shaping, baselines, or auxiliary supervised objectives to stabilize learning.

7. Variance sources in sequence-level RL

Variance in the gradient estimator is the primary practical obstacle to efficient RL for sequence generation. Key sources of variance include:

- Stochasticity of policy sampling: When \(\pi_\theta\) places mass across many tokens, sampled trajectories vary widely, producing noisy returns and gradients.

- Sparse or delayed rewards: If rewards are only provided at the end of an episode, all intermediate actions share the same scalar outcome, leading to large variance in how much each token is blamed or credited.

- Long horizons: Importance-weight products and accumulated noise scale unfavorably with sequence length. Actions early in a long sequence have more uncertain influence on terminal rewards.

- High-dimensional action spaces: Language modeling has very large vocabulary sizes; small changes in logits can alter token probabilities and thus produce high-variance gradients.

- Nonstationarity of policy: On-policy updates change \(\pi_\theta\), meaning that prior samples become less informative; frequent policy changes can amplify variance when mixed with bootstrapped critics.

- Reward noise and human variability: When rewards come from human raters, labels themselves have variance. This label noise directly inflates gradient variance.

Reducing variance is therefore a major focus. Techniques include baselines and advantage estimation, value function bootstrapping, generalized advantage estimation (GAE), reward shaping, using deterministic baselines like greedy decoding, and combining RL updates with supervised fine-tuning as a regularizer.

Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) interpolates between high-bias, low-variance TD methods and low-bias, high-variance Monte Carlo returns. It forms exponentially-weighted sums of temporal-difference residuals controlled by a parameter \(\lambda\), yielding advantage estimates
\[
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l},
\]
where \(\delta_t\) are TD residuals. GAE is widely used in practice because it gives a tunable bias-variance tradeoff.

8. Stability challenges in RL for LMs

Applying RL to large language models introduces additional stability challenges beyond generic RL issues. Several practical failure modes and mitigations are important to understand when reading RL-for-LM papers.

Reward hacking and misalignment. Highly expressive models can find degenerate strategies that maximize the reward proxy without genuinely improving desired behavior. For example, if a reward function overweights repetition or n-gram matches, the model may produce verbose repetitions or copy training data. This problem is exacerbated when rewards are imperfect proxies for human preferences. Careful reward design, human-in-the-loop evaluation, and techniques such as constrained optimization or explicit penalty terms help mitigate reward hacking.

Catastrophic policy updates. Policy gradient updates can dramatically change token probabilities, causing catastrophic degradation in generation quality. Trust-region methods like TRPO or Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) constrain policy updates by penalizing or clipping large changes in the policy distribution. In practical LM fine-tuning, KL-penalized objectives or limiting the step size on logits are common to preserve fluency and prevent collapse.

Mode collapse and reduced diversity. RL objectives that prioritize a narrow set of high-reward outputs can reduce the diversity of generated text. Regularization terms encouraging high entropy or constraints relative to a reference policy (e.g., KL regularization toward the pretrained LM) counteract collapse and maintain naturalness.

High compute and sample cost. Estimating sequence-level rewards for many samples, especially when human feedback is required, is expensive. Strategies to reduce cost include using learned reward models trained from human labels, active learning to select informative samples, and off-policy reuse of previously collected rated outputs.

Unstable critic learning. When advantage-based actor-critic methods are used, a poorly trained critic leads to biased advantage estimates and unstable policy updates. Shared representations between policy and critic, careful learning-rate schedules, and target networks or trust-region updates for critics can help. In many LM settings, practitioners prefer simple policy-gradient with strong baselines because critics can be difficult to fit reliably on sparse human-labeled data.

Scale and optimization dynamics. Large transformer LMs have many parameters and complex loss landscapes. RL updates interact with pretrained behavior in nontrivial ways; fine-tuning may require low learning rates, warm restarts, or mixed objectives combining supervised likelihoods and RL rewards to stabilize training.

Practical recipes in many successful RL-for-LM applications combine multiple of the above mitigations: use a KL penalty to the pretrained policy to prevent catastrophic drift, employ a learned reward model trained from human preferences, fine-tune with PPO or another trust-region-aware optimizer, use baselines like greedy decoding or learned value networks, and limit episode lengths and batch sizes to control variance.

Conclusion

This chapter presented the core reinforcement learning foundations required to understand and evaluate sequence-level optimization of language models. We began with MDPs and the mapping from sequence generation to states, actions, transitions, and rewards. We derived the policy gradient theorem and the REINFORCE estimator and explained how reward-to-go and value functions support improved credit assignment. Reward shaping and baselines were discussed as key variance-reduction tools. We contrasted on-policy and off-policy learning and explained why episodic formulations are typically preferred in generation tasks. We examined the multiple sources of variance specific to long, high-dimensional sequence decisions and surveyed practical stability challenges when applying RL to large language models, including reward hacking, catastrophic updates, and critic instability.

Understanding these concepts equips you to read and reason about the many variants and empirical tricks used in RL-based LM fine-tuning: why baselines and KL penalties appear, how preference-based reward models fit into pipelines, what off-policy corrections buy you, and what limitations remain. With this foundation, the reader can more confidently study techniques such as Proximal Policy Optimization for language, reward-model-based RL from human preferences, and hybrid supervised + RL objectives that are prominent in contemporary research and production systems.
