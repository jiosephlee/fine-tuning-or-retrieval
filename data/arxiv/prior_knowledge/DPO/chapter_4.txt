Introduction

Transformer architectures form the backbone of most contemporary large language models (LLMs) used in alignment pipelines. Their modular design—built from attention mechanisms, feed-forward networks, normalization, and clever positional handling—enables high-capacity modeling of sequences and flexible scaling to billions or trillions of parameters. This chapter explains the essential components and best practices for training and deploying transformer models. We cover self-attention, multi-head attention, and feed-forward blocks, positional encodings and masking strategies, decoder-only versus encoder–decoder topologies, initialization and normalization choices that affect stability, parameter scaling and efficiency tradeoffs, methods for adapting pretrained models (fine-tuning, adapters, LoRA), mixed-precision and distributed training strategies, and inference optimizations such as key-value caching. The goal is to provide both the conceptual intuition and the technical detail needed to understand and evaluate research literature and to guide practical implementation choices.

Self-attention, multi-head attention, and feed-forward blocks

Self-attention is the mechanism that allows each token in a sequence to attend to other tokens, producing context-dependent representations. Given an input token sequence represented as vectors in a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ (where $n$ is sequence length and $d$ is model dimensionality), the standard scaled dot-product self-attention computes queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$ via learned linear projections:
$Q = X W_Q$, $K = X W_K$, $V = X W_V$, with $W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}$ (often $d_k = d_h$ for convenience). Attention scores are computed as the scaled dot product
$A = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$,
and the attended outputs are
$Y = A V$.
Intuitively, each token's query vector compares against keys of all tokens to produce a distribution of attention weights; those weights mix the value vectors to produce a context-aware output for each position. The scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$ counteracts the growth of dot products with dimensionality, stabilizing gradients and softmax behavior.

Multi-head attention extends this by running several parallel attention "heads". Each head uses separate projections $W_Q^{(h)}, W_K^{(h)}, W_V^{(h)}$ and typically a dimension $d_k = d / H$ where $H$ is the number of heads. The outputs of the heads are concatenated and projected:
$\text{MHA}(X) = \text{Concat}(Y^{(1)},\dots,Y^{(H)}) W_O$.
Multi-heads allow the model to represent different types of relationships (local vs. global, syntactic vs. semantic) in parallel subspaces, improving expressivity and facilitating learning.

The attention block is usually followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN). The common form is a two-layer MLP applied independently to each token position:
$\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \, \phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2$,
with a nonlinearity $\phi$ such as GeLU. The FFN expands the dimensionality to $d_{ff}$ (e.g., $4d$ in the original Transformer) and then projects back to $d$. This structure allows the model to apply richer, tokenwise nonlinear transformations that complement the cross-token mixing done by attention.

Transformer layers normally sandwich attention and FFN sublayers with residual connections and normalization:
$x' = x + \text{MHA}(\text{Norm}(x))$,
$x'' = x' + \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(x'))$.
The order of normalization (pre-norm vs. post-norm) and the use of residual scaling influence training stability, which we address later.

Positional encodings and causal masking

Because self-attention treats positions in a sequence symmetrically (it is permutation-invariant except for positional information), transformers must inject position information. Two main approaches are used: fixed positional encodings and learned positional embeddings.

Fixed positional encodings, as introduced in the original Transformer, use sinusoids:
$\text{PE}_{pos,2i} = \sin(pos / 10000^{2i/d})$, $\text{PE}_{pos,2i+1} = \cos(pos / 10000^{2i/d})$.
These provide a structured basis that allows the model to infer relative positions via linear operations. Learned positional embeddings allocate a learned vector for each position up to some maximum length. Learned embeddings can capture position-specific idiosyncrasies and often perform slightly better in practice, but they require careful handling of extrapolation to longer sequences at inference.

More recent architectures use relative positional encodings, which encode relative distance between tokens rather than absolute position. Relative encodings can be implemented by adding a bias to attention logits based on index difference or by modifying key/value projections. Relative schemes can improve generalization to varied sequence lengths and often simplify modeling of long-range dependencies.

Causal masking enforces autoregressive behavior: when generating or training a decoder-only model, each position should attend only to itself and previous positions. This is achieved by masking the attention logits $Q K^\top$ with a lower-triangular mask before softmax, setting future positions to $-\infty$. For tasks requiring bidirectional context (e.g., classification), full attention without causal masking is used.

Decoder-only vs. encoder–decoder setups

Transformer architectures come in two primary topologies: decoder-only (autoregressive) models and encoder–decoder (seq2seq) models. Decoder-only models consist of stacked decoder layers with causal masking and are trained to predict the next token given previous tokens. They are particularly suited for open-ended generation and the dominant choice for large LMs used in alignment and instruction-following tasks. Their input and output share a common vocabulary and representation, simplifying pretraining and scaling.

Encoder–decoder models contain an encoder that processes the source sequence into contextual representations and a decoder that generates the target sequence attending to encoder outputs. The decoder typically has cross-attention layers that attend to encoder keys and values. Encoder–decoder architectures excel at conditional generation tasks where the model must transform one sequence into another—machine translation and many generation-with-conditioning tasks. They can incorporate bidirectional context in the encoder and autoregressive decoding in the decoder, offering effective modeling when input and output are distinct.

Choosing between the two depends on the task: decoder-only models are simpler for unconditional or many-instruction-following tasks and are widely used for alignment research because they easily model long continuations. Encoder–decoder models can be more sample-efficient for conditional tasks and may benefit where encoder-only pretraining (e.g., masked language modeling) is used.

Initialization, normalization, and training stability

Proper initialization and normalization are central to stable transformer training, especially at large scale. Two related decisions are the placement and type of normalization (pre-norm versus post-norm) and the initialization of residual branches.

Layer normalization was originally placed after the residual addition (post-norm). However, post-norm transformers can exhibit unstable training dynamics at depth. Pre-norm—applying LayerNorm before the attention and FFN blocks—has become the standard because it leads to better gradient flow and more stable training for deep stacks. With pre-norm, gradients propagate more directly through identity residual paths, attenuating the vanishing/exploding problem.

Residual weight initialization and scaling also affects stability. When residual branches produce outputs with variance comparable to the identity path, deep networks can blow up. Common remedies include scaling the residual outputs by small factors at initialization (e.g., multiplying the output of each block by a constant near zero that is gradually undone during training), using initialization schemes like Xavier/Glorot or Kaiming adapted to the activation functions, and careful choice of learning rate schedulers and warmups. The famous learning-rate warmup strategy—starting with a very small learning rate and linearly increasing it to the target value—reduces early-step instability due to large gradients interacting with randomly initialized projections.

Normalization variants and modifications like RMSNorm (root mean square normalization) or the use of scaled initialization for the final projection of attention (orthogonal initialization or smaller standard deviation) can further improve stability and throughput. In very large models, techniques such as gradient clipping, Adam optimizer variants, and adaptive learning-rate schedules tailored to batch size are critical. Practical recipes for training billion-parameter models include long linear warmups, cosine or linear decay, careful optimizer hyperparameters (betas, epsilon values), and periodic evaluation to detect divergence early.

Parameter scaling and efficiency considerations

Transformer model capacity scales with depth, width, and number of heads. Empirical scaling laws suggest that model performance improves predictably with parameter count and compute, but returns diminish and resource costs grow steeply. Designers must balance model size with efficiency needs.

Depth (number of layers) tends to be effective at increasing expressivity, but deeper models require careful initialization and normalization. Width (hidden dimension $d$) increases per-layer capacity and improves performance but raises memory and compute per token. Head count trades off attention subspace granularity and the per-head dimension; too many heads with tiny $d_k$ can reduce expressivity, whereas too few heads might limit the ability to represent diverse interactions.

Efficiency techniques include architectural changes like using gated linear units (e.g., SwiGLU) in FFNs to boost expressivity at similar parameter counts, replacing dense attention with sparse or low-rank approximations for long sequences, and using reversible layers to save activation memory. Parameter-efficient architectures such as mixture-of-experts (MoE) can scale capacity by activating only a subset of parameters per token.

Memory and compute budgets also motivate checkpointing strategies (activation recomputation) and sharding across devices. Model parallelism schemes—tensor parallelism that splits matrix multiplications across accelerators, pipeline parallelism that partitions layers sequentially, and data parallelism for batch splitting—are commonly combined to scale training to many devices.

Fine-tuning vs. adapters/LoRA

Adapting pretrained transformers to downstream tasks can be done via full fine-tuning or parameter-efficient alternatives. Full fine-tuning updates all model parameters and typically achieves strong performance but requires storing a separate copy of the model per task and can be costly for very large models.

Adapters insert small task-specific modules (often two-layer bottleneck MLPs) into each transformer layer. During adapter tuning, the original model weights are frozen and only the adapter parameters are learned. Adapters are compact, enabling many task-specific components to be stored and swapped with minimal storage overhead.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) injects low-rank updates into the weight matrices of attention or feed-forward projections. For example, a weight $W$ may be adapted by adding a low-rank decomposition $W + \Delta W$ where $\Delta W = A B$ with $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ and $r \ll d$. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained, which dramatically reduces trainable parameters while allowing effective adaptation. LoRA is often applied to query and value projections in attention layers and to some FFN weights.

Adapters and LoRA are attractive in alignment pipelines because they permit many task-specific behaviors to be layered onto a shared base model while keeping storage and catastrophic forgetting under control. They also allow rapid switching of behaviors at inference time and limit exposure of the base model to potentially harmful gradient updates.

Mixed precision and distributed training

Training large transformers is enabled by mixed-precision arithmetic and distributed training. Mixed precision uses lower-precision floating types (commonly float16 or bfloat16) for most tensor computations to reduce memory bandwidth and accelerate matrix multiplies, while keeping a master copy of certain parameters or accumulators in higher precision (float32) to preserve numerical fidelity. bfloat16 is often preferred on hardware that supports it because it retains a wide dynamic range, reducing overflow/underflow risks compared to float16.

Maintaining numerical stability with mixed precision requires loss scaling to avoid gradient underflow: gradients are multiplied by a scale factor during computation and unscaled before parameter updates. Frameworks provide automatic mixed precision (AMP) tooling that manages casting and scaling.

Distributed training combines data parallelism and model parallelism. Data parallelism replicates model weights across devices and synchronizes gradients, which scales with batch size but requires gradient communication. Tensor model parallelism splits large weight tensors across devices, enabling single layers to be larger than device memory. Pipeline parallelism partitions layers across devices and streams micro-batches to keep devices utilized. These strategies are often combined into hybrid parallelism with communication-optimized libraries (e.g., NCCL, Gloo) and frameworks providing sharding of optimizer states and gradient reductions.

Efficient checkpointing and optimizer sharding (e.g., ZeRO techniques) allow memory footprint reduction by partitioning optimizer states, gradients, and parameters across worker processes. ZeRO stage 1/2/3 trade off implementation complexity and memory savings; stage 3 can significantly reduce per-device memory to enable training of much larger models.

Inference efficiency and caching

At inference time, latency and cost are dominated by autoregressive decoding for decoder-only models. A key optimization is key-value caching: when generating token by token, the keys and values computed from previous tokens for the attention layers can be cached and reused rather than recomputed. For a model with $L$ layers, at step $t$ the cached tensors have shapes proportional to $t \times d_k$, so incremental decoding scales linearly with context length unless other optimizations are applied.

Caching reduces per-step complexity from $O(t^2)$ to $O(t)$ for the attention computation across steps, yielding substantial speedups. Implementing caching requires careful memory management and data layout to avoid overheads in broadcasting caches across devices. For long-context models, attention memory grows with sequence length; strategies to manage this include windowed attention, sparse attention patterns, or compressing older key-value pairs.

Other inference optimizations include quantization (reducing weight precision to INT8 or lower), operator fusion to reduce kernel launches, batch decoding, and pipelined execution across accelerators. Quantization-aware retraining or post-training calibration can reduce quality loss. For deployment in alignment pipelines where determinism and safety checks may be needed, deterministic decoding algorithms and consistent precisions are sometimes prioritized over maximal acceleration.

Conclusion

Transformers combine self-attention, multi-head representations, and per-position feed-forward processing into a flexible, scalable architecture that underlies modern LLMs. Proper handling of positional encodings and masking determines whether models behave autoregressively or bidirectionally. Architectural choices between decoder-only and encoder–decoder affect suitability for different tasks. Training stable, deep transformers requires thoughtful initialization, pre-norm placement, warmup schedules, and potentially residual scaling. Scaling decisions involve tradeoffs across depth, width, and parameterization techniques, and efficiency techniques like MoE or reversible layers can increase capacity at lower compute cost. For adaptation, LoRA and adapters enable parameter-efficient personalization, which is valuable in alignment work. Mixed-precision and distributed training frameworks enable practical optimization of large models, while inference requires caching, quantization, and execution optimizations to meet latency and cost constraints. Understanding these components and practices equips the reader to interpret research claims about model improvements, training recipes, and deployment strategies in alignment-focused literature.
