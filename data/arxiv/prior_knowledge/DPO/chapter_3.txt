Introduction

Language modeling is the foundational probabilistic task behind most modern natural language processing systems. At its core, a language model assigns probabilities to sequences of discrete tokens (words, subwords, characters). These probabilities enable generation, scoring, and downstream conditioning for translation, summarization, question answering, and more. In this chapter we develop the formalism used throughout the field: the autoregressive factorization that reduces sequence probability estimation to a product of conditional distributions; conditional language modeling where the model predicts an output sequence given some input context; tokenization and vocabulary management, which define the discrete units the model manipulates; differences between per-token and sequence-level likelihood; maximum likelihood training (and practicalities such as teacher forcing); and likelihood-based evaluation metrics such as cross-entropy and perplexity. The emphasis is on intuition and probabilistic interpretation so you can read and reason about research papers that use these ideas.

# Autoregressive factorization of sequence probabilities

A fundamental property of discrete sequences is that you can use the chain rule of probability to decompose the joint probability of a sequence into a product of conditional probabilities. If we denote a sequence of $T$ tokens as $y_{1:T} = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)$, the chain rule gives

$P(y_{1:T}) = P(y_1) \, P(y_2 \mid y_1) \, P(y_3 \mid y_{1:2}) \cdots P(y_T \mid y_{1:T-1}).$

This exact identity is the basis of autoregressive sequence models. Instead of trying to model the combinatorially large joint distribution over whole sequences directly, we model each conditional distribution $P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})$ with a parametric function (for example, a neural network). The model's parameters are trained so that these conditional distributions, multiplied together, give high probability to sequences seen in data.

The autoregressive factorization has several practical and conceptual advantages. First, it reduces a hard structured-probability estimation problem to a sequence of simpler supervised learning problems: at timestep $t$ the model predicts the next token given the preceding tokens. Second, it supports left-to-right generation: to sample from $P(y_{1:T})$ we sample $y_1 \sim P(y_1)$, then $y_2 \sim P(y_2 \mid y_1)$, and so on. Third, the chain rule guarantees that the model's outputs define a normalized probability distribution over sequences (provided that each conditional is a valid categorical distribution), which is important for principled evaluation and likelihood-based training.

Autoregressive models can be implemented with a variety of architectures. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) naturally process sequences step-by-step and were early choices. Transformer architectures model the dependency of $y_t$ on $y_{1:t-1}$ using self-attention restricted to previous positions; they have become dominant due to their scalability and parallelizability during training. Regardless of architecture, the modeling goal is the same: produce sharp, calibrated estimates of the conditional categorical distribution over the next token.

It is important to note that the autoregressive factorization imposes a particular causal structure: predictions cannot depend on future tokens. That is well suited for generation tasks and many natural language settings, but it is only one possible factorization. Bidirectional models (like masked language models) do not define an autoregressive joint distribution in the same straightforward way.

# Conditional language modeling (p(y | x)) and sequence conditioning

Many practical tasks require modeling a conditional distribution over an output sequence $y$ given some input context $x$. For machine translation, $x$ is a source-language sentence and $y$ is the target-language translation. For summarization, $x$ is the document and $y$ is the summary. In dialog, $x$ may be the history of turns, and $y$ the next response. Conditional language modeling generalizes the autoregressive factorization to

$P(y_{1:T} \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^T P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x).$

Here the conditional distributions can depend on both previously generated tokens and the context $x$. Implementationally this is often achieved by providing $x$ to the model via an encoder (encoder-decoder models) or by concatenating $x$ and $y$ (decoder-only models with special separators or prompt tokens). Attention mechanisms are a standard way for the decoder to incorporate information from $x$, allowing the model to focus on relevant parts of the input at each generation step.

Conditioning introduces flexibility: the same model can produce different outputs for different $x$ while learning from paired examples $(x, y)$ in the training set. From a probabilistic perspective, conditional models still provide normalized distributions over outputs for each fixed $x$. This normalization is important: it enables maximum likelihood training on pairs $(x,y)$ and principled likelihood-based evaluation (e.g., conditional perplexity).

There are important practical and conceptual issues with conditioning. First, the way we represent $x$ influences model capacity and bias. If $x$ is long (e.g., long documents or multimodal inputs), you must decide how to compress or attend to it. Second, during generation the model must balance fidelity to $x$ with fluency and coherence: the conditional probability $P(y \mid x)$ encodes this tradeoff, but search procedures (greedy, beam search, sampling) can interact with model probabilities in complex ways. Third, conditioning introduces the possibility of conditional calibration issues: the model could be well-calibrated marginally but poorly calibrated when conditioned on certain inputs, which impacts downstream decision-making.

Conditional language models can be used in a wide variety of settings beyond translation or summarization. They include controlled generation via prompts or attributes, few-shot learning via prompt examples, and retrieval-augmented generation where $x$ includes retrieved passages. In all cases, the probabilistic semantics remain the same: the model defines $P(y \mid x)$ which we can use to sample, score, or optimize outputs.

# Tokenization and vocabulary management

Language models operate over discrete vocabularies of tokens. Tokenization is the process of mapping raw text into a sequence of tokens chosen from a finite vocabulary $V$. Choices about tokenization and vocabulary size strongly affect modeling efficiency, computational cost, and the model's ability to represent rare or morphologically complex words.

There are several standard tokenization strategies. Word-level tokenization treats entire words as tokens. This is simple conceptually but problematic in practice: it leads to a very large vocabulary (all observed word types) and severe out-of-vocabulary (OOV) issues when encountering rare or previously unseen words. Character-level tokenization avoids OOV by using a small alphabet, but sequences become long and modeling long-range dependencies becomes harder.

Subword tokenization methods such as Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), unigram subword models, and WordPiece strike a practical balance. They build a vocabulary of subword units—frequent words remain single tokens while rarer words are split into subwords. BPE constructs vocabulary by iteratively merging frequent symbol pairs. Unigram models learn a probabilistic tokenization and choose a fixed vocabulary by maximizing likelihood. WordPiece, used in many early transformer models, is similar to BPE with a likelihood-based selection. Subword tokenization provides compact vocabularies (tens of thousands of tokens) while allowing open-vocabulary representation: any string can be decomposed into subwords.

Tokenization also determines how special symbols (start-of-sequence, end-of-sequence, padding, separators) are represented. These special tokens are critical for modeling sequence boundaries, conditional inputs, and batch processing. Vocabulary management includes deciding which tokens to include (punctuation, casing, special characters), whether to lowercase text, and how to handle multilingual data where scripts vary.

A practical consideration is that tokenization can change the effective length distribution of sequences and affects per-token loss and perplexity. For example, using subword tokens will typically produce shorter sequences (fewer tokens per sentence) than character tokens, which impacts the numeric values of per-token cross-entropy and perplexity. When comparing models, always report what tokenization was used.

Finally, vocabulary size is a key hyperparameter. Larger vocabularies can reduce the number of tokens needed to represent text but increase the cost of the final softmax layer (both memory and compute). Techniques such as adaptive softmax, sampled softmax, and hierarchical softmax have been used to mitigate this cost for very large vocabularies, though modern GPUs and transformer optimizations make large softmaxes tractable for many models.

# Per-token vs sequence-level likelihood

When training and evaluating language models, we can look at probabilities at different granularities. Per-token likelihood focuses on the conditional probabilities of individual next tokens $P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x)$ and typically aggregates a loss (negative log-probability) per token across a dataset. Sequence-level likelihood treats the entire sequence $P(y_{1:T} \mid x)$ as the atomic object and considers its joint probability.

These perspectives are tightly linked by the chain rule: the sequence log-likelihood is the sum of token-level log-likelihoods,

$\log P(y_{1:T} \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x).$

From a training standpoint, maximum likelihood for a dataset of paired examples $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$ is achieved by maximizing the sum of sequence log-likelihoods, which is equivalent to minimizing the average per-token negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy). Thus per-token training objectives are consistent with sequence-level objectives under teacher forcing (we discuss teacher forcing below).

However, per-token metrics can obscure sequence-level behavior. For instance, a model might predict each next token with moderate probability but still assign low joint probability to a full sequence because small per-token errors multiply over long sequences. Conversely, thinking only in sequence-level terms obscures which tokens the model struggles with. Many evaluation and diagnostic tools use per-token analysis (e.g., token-level accuracy, loss curves for particular positions) while overall scoring uses sequence likelihood or perplexity.

Another important difference arises in generation and decision-making. Some tasks care about finding the most likely sequence under the model, i.e.,

$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{y} P(y \mid x) = \arg\max_{y} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x).$

Finding $\hat{y}$ requires search (beam search, greedy decoding, or more complex discrete optimization) because the space of sequences is huge. A model with good per-token likelihood does not necessarily make search simple or guarantee that the highest-probability sequence is the most desirable output by human judgment, which leads to tradeoffs between likelihood, diversity, and other quality metrics.

# Maximum likelihood training for sequence models

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the canonical training method for autoregressive and conditional language models. Given a dataset of $N$ paired examples $\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, MLE chooses parameters $\theta$ to maximize the conditional log-likelihood:

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log P_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)}).$

Using the autoregressive factorization, the objective becomes

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log P_\theta(y_t^{(i)} \mid y_{1:t-1}^{(i)}, x^{(i)}).$

In practice this is minimized as the average negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy loss) per token. Training uses gradient-based optimization (stochastic gradient descent or variants such as Adam). For neural sequence models, the conditional distributions are implemented as softmax outputs over the vocabulary, and the loss per timestep is the negative log-probability assigned to the ground-truth next token.

A crucial practical detail is how we feed the previous tokens during training. The standard approach, called teacher forcing, conditions on the ground-truth prefix $y_{1:t-1}$ rather than on tokens the model itself would have generated. Teacher forcing makes training stable and efficient because it converts the problem to supervised next-token prediction. However, at generation time the model must condition on its own sampled or predicted tokens, which can cause exposure bias: the model never learned to recover from its own mistakes and may drift into low-probability regions of sequence space.

Several techniques address exposure bias and related sequence-level problems. Scheduled sampling mixes ground-truth and model-generated prefixes during training so the model learns to handle its own errors. Reinforcement learning or sequence-level training objectives (such as minimum risk training or policy gradient methods) directly optimize non-differentiable sequence-level metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) by treating generation as a decision process and optimizing expected reward. These methods can improve task-specific metrics but are often less stable and can harm likelihoods.

Regularization and optimization details are also important. Large transformer models require careful learning rate schedules (warmup followed by decay), weight decay, dropout, and large batch sizes to train stably. Gradient clipping is commonly used. For conditional models, data balancing across domains or tasks can affect the learned conditional distributions; for example, overrepresentation of certain input forms can bias outputs.

Finally, MLE has desirable theoretical properties: it yields consistent estimators under correct model specification and sufficient data. In practice models are misspecified but MLE remains a robust and scalable training principle that underlies most modern language models.

# Perplexity and likelihood-based evaluation

Likelihood is the natural quantity for evaluating probabilistic models. For a test set of examples, we can compute the average negative log-likelihood per token (cross-entropy):

$H = -\frac{1}{\sum_i T_i} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \log P_\theta(y_t^{(i)} \mid y_{1:t-1}^{(i)}, x^{(i)}).$

Perplexity (PPL) is an exponentiated and more interpretable form of this metric defined as

$\text{Perplexity} = \exp(H) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^M \log P_\theta(w_j)\right),$

where $M$ is the total number of tokens and $w_j$ ranges over tokens; in conditional settings the probabilities are conditional on the appropriate $x$ and prefix. Perplexity can be interpreted as the effective branching factor of the model: a perplexity of $k$ suggests the model is as uncertain as if it had to choose uniformly among $k$ alternatives at each time step. Lower perplexity indicates better predictive performance.

Perplexity has a direct relation to cross-entropy and information theory: the negative log-likelihood (in nats if natural logs are used, bits if base-2 logs) measures the expected information content per token under the model. In particular, if we use base-2 logarithms, the average negative log-likelihood equals the cross-entropy in bits per token; perplexity is then $2^{\text{bits-per-token}}$.

While perplexity is a principled and widely used metric, it has limitations. It depends on tokenization: different tokenization choices yield different perplexities that are not directly comparable. Perplexity also measures the model's average predictive quality, which does not always correlate perfectly with human judgments of generated text quality for tasks like summarization or dialog. Models with low perplexity may still produce outputs that are unfaithful, repetitive, or irrelevant, especially in conditional generation where fidelity to $x$ matters. For tasks with specific utility metrics (translation accuracy, factual correctness), direct task-specific evaluation is necessary.

Likelihood-based evaluations also enable statistical comparisons and calibration checks. Per-token log-probabilities allow per-example or per-position analyses to identify failure modes. When paired with human evaluation, likelihood metrics can help diagnose whether problems are due to model uncertainty, dataset mismatch, or decoding strategy.

Generation procedures interact with model probabilities. Greedy decoding or beam search searches for sequences that maximize the model's joint probability, while sampling procedures (temperature-controlled sampling, top-k or nucleus sampling) trade off diversity and probability. Changes in decoding can profoundly affect empirical quality even when the underlying perplexity is unchanged, so evaluation should consider both model likelihoods and decoding behaviors.

Conclusion

This chapter established the probabilistic foundations for modern sequence modeling and language modeling. Autoregressive factorization reduces sequence probability estimation to next-token prediction, which in turn supports principled maximum likelihood training. Conditional language modeling generalizes this to input-conditioned distributions, enabling a wide range of NLP tasks. Tokenization and vocabulary choices define the discrete units the model operates on and materially affect performance and evaluation. Understanding the relationship between per-token and sequence-level likelihood clarifies training objectives and generation strategies. Finally, likelihood-based metrics like cross-entropy and perplexity provide principled evaluation but must be interpreted carefully in light of tokenization and task-specific goals. These ideas form the basis for reading and understanding the majority of research papers on modern language models.
