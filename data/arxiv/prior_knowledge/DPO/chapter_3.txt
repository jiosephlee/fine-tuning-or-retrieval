Introduction

Autoregressive language models based on the Transformer architecture are the dominant paradigm for conditional sequence modeling in modern natural language processing. These models learn to represent the conditional distribution over a target token sequence given some context (which may be empty for unconditional generation, a prefix for continuation, or a source sequence for tasks such as translation). In practice this learning is accomplished by maximizing the likelihood of observed training sequences under an autoregressive factorization. This chapter explains how tokenization and vocabulary choices enable modeling arbitrary text, how the autoregressive factorization and teacher forcing define the training objective, how sequence log-probabilities are computed and affected by length, practical decoding algorithms (greedy, beam, temperature scaling, top-k and nucleus sampling), problems that arise from the mismatch between training and generation (exposure bias) and ways to mitigate it, the contrast between per-token and sequence-level objectives, and concrete considerations for computing and caching log-probabilities efficiently in Transformer models.

Tokenization and vocabulary (BPE / WordPiece)

Before any probabilistic model can operate on text, raw strings must be converted into discrete tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary. Two widely used subword tokenization algorithms are Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) and WordPiece. Both methods are data-driven and produce a tradeoff between vocabulary size and the length of token sequences that represent inputs and outputs.

BPE starts with a vocabulary of individual characters (or bytes) and iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent symbol pairs into new symbols, stopping when a target vocabulary size is reached. WordPiece uses a similar idea but formulates merges in a likelihood framework that greedily adds tokens that maximize the likelihood of the training corpus. The result of both methods is a vocabulary that includes whole words, common morphemes, and rarer characters or byte-level pieces for out-of-vocabulary content.

Subword tokenization has several crucial advantages. It limits the vocabulary size to a manageable number (e.g., 10k–200k) while allowing an open vocabulary for novel words, since any word can be decomposed into subword units. It also balances modeling efficiency: frequent words appear as single tokens, which reduces sequence length and computational cost, while rare words are decomposed so they remain representable.

Design choices: vocabulary size trades off sequence length and model capacity. Larger vocabularies reduce average token count per sentence but increase embedding and softmax matrix sizes. Special tokens (e.g., 〈bos〉, 〈eos〉, 〈pad〉, and task-specific markers) are included and treated as atomic tokens. Tokenization intricacies affect probability assignments: tokenization is part of the model’s input/output mapping, so any probability on text is implicitly a probability on token sequences induced by the tokenization scheme.

Autoregressive factorization p(y | x) = ∏ p(y_t | x, y_<t)

The central probabilistic assumption in autoregressive modeling is to factor the conditional distribution over a target sequence $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$ (which are tokens, not characters) given context $x$ into a product of conditionals:

$p(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).$

This exact factorization is purely mathematical (the chain rule of probability) and not an approximation: any joint can be decomposed this way. What is learned is a parameterized model of each conditional factor $p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$. Transformers implement this via causal self-attention in the decoder or masked language modeling in decoder-only architectures: at step $t$, the model’s representation and output softmax depend only on the context tokens $x$ and the previously generated tokens $y_{<t}$, enforced either by masking future positions during training and inference or by causal attention during incremental decoding.

The parameterization produces logits $z_t$ at each step, which are converted to conditional probabilities with a softmax:

$p_\theta(y_t = v \mid x, y_{<t}) = \frac{\exp(z_{t,v})}{\sum_{v'} \exp(z_{t,v'})},$

where $v$ indexes tokens in the vocabulary.

Teacher forcing and maximum likelihood training

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) trains the parameters $\theta$ to maximize the probability assigned to ground-truth target sequences in the training set. With the autoregressive factorization, the negative log-likelihood loss for a single pair $(x, y)$ is

$\mathcal{L}(\theta; x, y) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).$

In practice, training uses teacher forcing: when computing each conditional $p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, the model conditions on the true previous tokens $y_{<t}$ rather than its own sampled predictions. Teacher forcing stabilizes and speeds up training because the model sees gold histories and learns to predict the next token under correct context. Gradients flow through the model normally since the inputs to each step are observed tokens.

Implementation detail: the loss is computed in parallel by shifting the target sequence by one position and applying a masked cross-entropy across the sequence length dimension. Padding tokens are masked out so they don’t contribute to the loss. Common extensions to the basic cross-entropy include label smoothing, which replaces the hard one-hot targets with a soft distribution to improve generalization and calibration.

Sequence log-probabilities and length effects

The log-probability of a generated sequence $y$ under the model is the sum of per-token log-probabilities:

$\log p_\theta(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).$

This additive form is convenient numerically and is what is computed when assessing model likelihoods. However, because this sum grows (typically more negative) with sequence length, raw sequence probabilities impose a strong length bias: shorter sequences can have higher (less negative) average log-probability because they accumulate fewer negative terms. Consequently, maximum-probability decoding (argmax over sequences) tends to prefer short outputs (sometimes the empty string or a short safe phrase).

To compensate, several normalizations are used during decoding and scoring. Common approaches include length normalization, which divides the log-probability by $T^\alpha$ or by $T$:

$\text{score}(y) = \frac{1}{T^\alpha} \log p_\theta(y \mid x)$

or a length penalty added to the score. Beam search implementations often use such normalization to avoid favoring overly short hypotheses. Another consideration is per-token average log-probability:

$\frac{1}{T}\log p_\theta(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),$

which compares fluency normalized by length but may overly favor longer sequences if per-token probabilities are high.

Decoding strategies: greedy, beam, temperature, top-k/p

Decoding is the algorithmic process of transforming the conditional distributions $p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$ into a single sequence (or set of sequences). Strategies trade off computational cost, optimality under the model, and diversity.

Greedy decoding selects the most probable next token at each step:

$y_t = \arg\max_v p_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}).$

It is fast but myopic: local optimality does not guarantee a globally high-probability sequence.

Beam search maintains $B$ partial hypotheses (beam width) at each step and expands each by all candidate tokens (or a top subset), keeping the top $B$ by cumulative score. Beam search is an approximate method to find high-probability sequences under the model; larger beams find better sequences but incur extra computation. Beam search uses a scoring function (often length-normalized cumulative log-probability) to compare hypotheses of different lengths. Practical implementations also apply pruning, diversity-promoting penalties, and end-of-sequence handling to produce coherent outputs.

Sampling-based methods generate tokens stochastically from the next-step distribution. Two commonly used temperature and truncation mechanisms modify sampling to control randomness:

- Temperature scaling rescales logits $z$ by $1/\tau$ before softmax: $p_\tau(v) \propto \exp(z_v/\tau)$. When $\tau<1$, the distribution becomes sharper and sampling is more conservative; when $\tau>1$, it becomes flatter and more random. $\tau \to 0$ approximates greedy decoding.

- Top-k sampling restricts the support to the $k$ most probable tokens and renormalizes the distribution over them before sampling. Top-p (nucleus) sampling selects the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds $p$ and samples from that set. Top-p adapts dynamically to the distribution’s shape and often produces better-quality diverse generations than fixed top-k.

These decoding methods produce different tradeoffs: beam search tends toward mode-seeking deterministic outputs; sampling allows diversity and creativity but risks incoherence. Hybrid strategies combine beam search with sampling or use stochastic beams to explore diverse high-probability regions.

Exposure bias and mitigation strategies

Exposure bias refers to the mismatch between training (teacher forcing) and generation (model conditions on its own predictions). Because training only sees gold histories, the model never learns to recover from its own mistakes. At inference time, an early mistake can lead to inputs the model has never been trained on, producing compounding errors.

Several mitigation strategies have been proposed:

- Scheduled sampling alternates between teacher forcing and feeding the model’s sampled or greedy predictions as inputs during training, gradually increasing the proportion of self-fed tokens. This exposes the model to its own errors but introduces bias in the gradient estimator because targets are no longer strictly conditionally independent.

- Data augmentation techniques create corrupted prefixes and train the model to continue from them, or generate synthesized histories that reflect likely model errors.

- Professor forcing uses an adversarial objective to match the hidden-state dynamics under teacher forcing and free-running (sampling) modes, encouraging the model’s internal behavior to be similar in both settings.

- Fine-tuning with sequence-level objectives such as reinforcement learning (policy gradient, REINFORCE) or minimum risk training optimizes evaluation metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) at the sequence level while sampling from the model, thus directly adapting the model to its generation-time behavior. Self-critical sequence training (SCST) uses the model’s own greedy decoding as a baseline to reduce variance during policy-gradient updates.

Each mitigation has tradeoffs: scheduled sampling can hurt convergence and introduce bias, adversarial methods are complex and can be unstable, and RL-based fine-tuning often requires careful reward design and variance reduction.

Per-token vs. sequence-level objectives

The standard MLE loss optimizes per-token log-probabilities summed across positions. This local objective is statistically efficient and produces calibrated conditional distributions if the model class is well-specified. However, many downstream metrics of interest (e.g., BLEU for translation) are non-differentiable, sequence-level and non-local. Two principal families of alternatives address this mismatch.

Sequence-level training directly optimizes expected task reward:

$\mathcal{L}_{\text{seq}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[R(y)],$

where $R(y)$ is the reward (higher is better). The gradient can be estimated by policy-gradient methods:

$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{seq}} \approx -\sum_{i} (R(y^{(i)}) - b) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x),$

where $y^{(i)}$ are sampled sequences and $b$ is a baseline to reduce variance. Self-critical methods set $b$ to the reward of the baseline greedy output.

Minimum risk training optimizes empirical expected loss over a sampled candidate set and directly minimizes expected metric loss. These sequence-level objectives align training and evaluation but are typically higher variance, require sampling or beam-derived candidate sets, and may lead to degenerate behavior if the reward is poorly designed or sparse.

A hybrid approach starts with MLE pretraining to obtain a reasonable model and then fine-tunes with sequence-level objectives. This combines the stability and coverage of MLE with direct optimization of task metrics.

Computing and caching log-probabilities

During both training and evaluation, computing per-token and sequence-level log-probabilities efficiently is crucial. On GPUs, training computes all per-token logits in parallel by masking future tokens and feeding the entire sequence at once to the Transformer encoder/decoder stack. The per-token log-probabilities are obtained from the output logits via cross-entropy computation.

During autoregressive generation, naive recomputation of all hidden states at each step would be quadratic in sequence length. Transformers support incremental decoding via key/value caching: at each decoding position $t$, the model computes the new attention keys and values for the current token and appends them to a cached buffer of past keys and values for each self-attention layer. Subsequent steps reuse the cached keys/values so that attention only needs to compute interactions between the new token and previous cached entries rather than recomputing all pairwise interactions. This reduces cost per step from $O(t)$ to $O(1)$ per previous token in the attention operations and overall from $O(T^2)$ to $O(T)$ for a generated sequence of length $T$.

Concretely, consider a decoder self-attention layer that maps inputs to queries $Q_t$, keys $K_t$, and values $V_t$. For incremental decoding, one stores $K_{1:t-1}$ and $V_{1:t-1}$. At step $t$, compute $Q_t$ from the current token, compute attention weights with the concatenated $[K_{1:t-1}; K_t]$, and compute the context as a weighted sum over $V_{1:t}$. Only $K_t$ and $V_t$ need to be computed and appended to the cache. The softmax over vocabulary at each step produces the conditional distribution $p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$, from which $\log p_\theta(y_t)$ is extracted.

When computing sequence log-probabilities for a candidate sequence (e.g., to score beams or to compute loss on a dataset), it is efficient to compute the whole sequence in one forward pass with masking on training hardware. For arbitrary candidate lists (different sequences per example), batching and padding with attention masks is used. Numerical stability: use log-sum-exp tricks when aggregating probabilities across vocabularies or for marginalization.

Practical tips: when scoring many candidate sequences (as in minimum risk training or reranking), reuse cached encoder outputs for the shared context $x$, and, when possible, evaluate multiple candidates in parallel by packing them into a single batch, taking care with attention masks and position encodings. For long outputs, incremental decoding with caching is essential for real-time generation.

Conclusion

Autoregressive Transformer language models are trained with maximum likelihood under an autoregressive factorization that decomposes sequence probabilities into products of conditional token probabilities. Tokenization with BPE or WordPiece bridges raw text and finite vocabularies, trading off vocabulary size and sequence length. Teacher forcing makes MLE practical and efficient but creates exposure bias because the model is not trained on its own generated prefixes; several mitigation strategies (scheduled sampling, adversarial matching, sequence-level fine-tuning) attempt to address this. Sequence log-probabilities are sums of per-token log-probabilities, and length effects must be handled in decoding via normalization or penalties. Decoding strategies span deterministic mode-seeking (greedy, beam) and stochastic sampling (temperature, top-k, top-p), each with strengths and weaknesses. Finally, efficient computation of log-probabilities and generation hinges on batching in training and on key/value caching for incremental decoding at inference. Understanding these components and their interactions is essential for reading and designing research in modern autoregressive language modeling.
