# Entropy and mutual information (intuition)

Entropy quantifies the uncertainty or randomness of a distribution. For a discrete random variable $X$ with distribution $p$, the entropy is
$H(X) = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x)$.
Entropy is measured in nats if natural logs are used (or bits if base-2 logs are used). Maximum entropy occurs for the uniform distribution over a finite set, reflecting maximal uncertainty. Zero entropy occurs when the distribution is deterministic (a point mass).

Entropy has multiple operational interpretations. One interpretation is in coding theory: $H(X)$ is the expected number of nats needed to encode a sample of $X$ using an optimal prefix code. Another is in terms of unpredictability: higher entropy means greater unpredictability of outcomes. In machine learning, entropy regularly appears as a regularizer: maximizing entropy of a policy encourages exploration by preventing premature convergence to a deterministic behavior, while minimizing entropy of a predictive distribution can indicate confident predictions.

Mutual information measures the amount of information one random variable contains about another. For random variables $X$ and $Y$, mutual information is defined as
$I(X;Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_{X,Y}\,\|\,p_X p_Y\big)$,
or equivalently
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X)$.
Mutual information is always nonnegative and is zero precisely when $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Intuitively, mutual information quantifies how much knowing $Y$ reduces uncertainty about $X$. If $I(X;Y)$ is large, observations of $Y$ are informative about $X$.

In language models and representation learning, mutual information is often used to encourage learned representations to capture salient information about inputs or targets. For example, maximizing mutual information between input text and a latent representation can encourage the representation to retain informative structure. In RLHF, mutual information concepts can help understand how much information a human preference signal gives about desired behaviors and how much the model's outputs reveal about underlying intent.

Entropy and mutual information are linked: mutual information is the reduction in entropy obtained by conditioning. This viewpoint provides a useful way to think about rewards and constraints: a policy that achieves high expected reward but low entropy is narrowly focused and predictable; balancing reward and entropy is a common objective when searching for robust or exploratory policies.


# Log-sum-exp trick and numerical stability

When working with probabilities and exponential functions, numerical overflow and underflow are frequent problems, because exponentials can produce extremely large or small numbers that exceed floating-point limits. The log-sum-exp (LSE) trick is a simple algebraic identity that stabilizes computations of expressions of the form
$\log\sum_{i} e^{a_i}$.
Directly computing $e^{a_i}$ and summing can overflow or underflow when some $a_i$ are very large or very negative. The LSE trick rewrites the expression using the maximum of the $a_i$ values. Let $M=\max_i a_i$. Then
$\log\sum_{i} e^{a_i} = M + \log\sum_{i} e^{a_i - M}$.
Because each exponent $a_i - M$ is now $\le 0$, the exponentials are in $[0,1]$ and numerical overflow is avoided. Subtracting $M$ also avoids underflow when $M$ is very large and other $a_i$ are much smaller.

This trick appears everywhere: softmax computations, marginal likelihoods, normalization constants, and log-likelihoods. For example, when computing the log of a mixture model density given log component scores $a_i$, one uses LSE to compute the log-sum in log space stably.

A related numerically stable computation is for the softmax function. If we need probabilities
$p_i = \frac{e^{a_i}}{\sum_j e^{a_j}}$,
we can compute them by subtracting the maximum before exponentiating:
$p_i = \frac{e^{a_i - M}}{\sum_j e^{a_j - M}}$,
which is numerically safe.

When implementing losses like cross-entropy with logits, the same idea yields stable formulas. For binary cross-entropy with logit $s$ and label $y$, compute
$\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(s;y) = \max(0, -s) + \log\big(1 + e^{-|s|}\big) + (1-y)s$
or more simply using identities that avoid computing $\sigma(s)$ directly. Many deep learning libraries implement these numerically stable forms internally, but understanding the trick helps you reason about numerical behavior when reading papers or implementing new algorithms.


# Partition functions and normalization constants

A partition function (or normalization constant) is a scalar that ensures an unnormalized score function becomes a valid probability distribution. Suppose a model assigns an unnormalized positive score $\tilde{p}(x) = e^{-E(x)}$ to each configuration $x$, where $E(x)$ is called an energy (this is common in energy-based models). To convert $\tilde{p}$ into a proper distribution, we divide by the partition function
$Z = \sum_x e^{-E(x)}$
(for discrete domains) or
$Z = \int e^{-E(x)} \, dx$
(for continuous domains). The normalized distribution is then
$p(x) = \frac{e^{-E(x)}}{Z}$.
Computing $Z$ exactly is feasible only in small or highly structured problems; in most realistic high-dimensional models (including language models over sequences), $Z$ is intractable because it involves summing or integrating exponentially many terms. The intractability of partition functions is central to the design of approximate inference and learning methods.

Partition functions appear in maximum entropy models and log-linear models, where the log-probability is linear in parameters up to the log partition function:
$\log p_\theta(x) = \theta^\top \phi(x) - \log Z(\theta)$,
with $Z(\theta)=\sum_x e^{\theta^\top \phi(x)}$. The gradient of the log partition function has a useful form: $\nabla_\theta \log Z(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\phi(X)]$. This identity connects moment matching and maximum likelihood: the optimal $\theta$ under maximum likelihood sets model expectations $\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\phi(X)]$ equal to empirical expectations of $\phi$.

When $Z$ is intractable, several strategies are used. One is to avoid computing $Z$ by working with ratios of probabilities where $Z$ cancels, as in maximum likelihood gradients for energy-based models expressed as expectations under the model. Another is to approximate $Z$ or its gradients using Monte Carlo methods such as importance sampling, Annealed Importance Sampling, or thermodynamic integration. Variational methods introduce a tractable lower bound to the log partition function, and pseudo-likelihood approaches sidestep $Z$ by using conditional distributions that are easier to normalize.

In RLHF and reward modeling, partition functions appear in the context of reward-weighted distributions. For example, to form a distribution proportional to $\pi_{\text{base}}(x)\exp(\beta r(x))$—a common Boltzmann policy construction—the normalization constant is $Z = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{base}}}[e^{\beta r(X)}]$. Computing or estimating this $Z$ is necessary if one wants to evaluate or sample exactly from the Boltzmann policy; in practice, importance sampling or approximate methods are used.

Understanding partition functions helps explain why certain divergences or objectives are chosen: some objectives allow $Z$ to cancel or be approximated more easily, which makes them preferable in large-scale settings.


# Jensen’s inequality and convexity basics

Jensen’s inequality is fundamental whenever an expectation interacts with a nonlinear function. It states that for any convex function $\varphi$ and any random variable $X$,
$\varphi\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big) \le \mathbb{E}\big[\varphi(X)\big]$.
If $\varphi$ is concave, the inequality direction reverses. Equality holds when $X$ is almost surely constant or when $\varphi$ is affine on the support of $X$.

Jensen’s inequality underlies many important results in probability and information theory. A classic application is the nonnegativity of KL divergence (Gibbs’ inequality): by taking $\varphi(u)=u\log u$ (which is convex for $u>0$) and applying Jensen’s inequality to the distribution of $q(x)/p(x)$ under $p$, one derives $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0$. Another application is that the log function is concave, so
$\log\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]$,
which is often used to bound log-marginal likelihoods.

Convexity theory more broadly provides critical intuition about optimization. A function $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ is convex if for all $x,y$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$,
$f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda) y) \le \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda) f(y)$.
Graphically, the line segment between any two points on the graph of a convex function lies above the graph. Convexity implies useful properties: any local minimum is a global minimum, and subgradients and gradients give useful first-order lower bounds on function values. These properties make convex functions tractable for analysis and optimization.

In machine learning, many loss functions are convex in simple models (e.g., logistic loss in linear models), enabling guarantees about global optimality. However, deep neural networks are nonconvex, so the convex toolbox provides only partial insights. Still, Jensen’s inequality and convexity arguments are invaluable for deriving bounds and understanding expectations of nonlinear functions, such as when deriving variational lower bounds or upper bounds on partition functions.

A concrete use of Jensen’s inequality is deriving the evidence lower bound (ELBO) in variational inference. For a model with latent variable $Z$ and observations $X$, the marginal log-likelihood satisfies
$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,Z)}{q(Z)}\right] \ge \mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,Z)}{q(Z)}\right]$,
where $q$ is any proposal distribution. The inequality follows from concavity of $\log$ and yields a lower bound that is optimized in variational methods.

Intuitively, Jensen’s inequality tells us that "the function of an average is better (for convex functions) or worse (for concave functions) than the average of the function". In probabilistic modeling this governs whether approximations are optimistic or pessimistic and explains why replacing expectations by point estimates introduces bias in a particular direction.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has introduced the probabilistic and information-theoretic tools that appear throughout the study of language models, reward modeling, and alignment procedures like RLHF. Random variables and expectations allow us to formulate losses and objectives as averaged quantities. Cross-entropy and log-likelihood connect empirical training objectives to information-theoretic distances such as KL divergence, whose directionality governs qualitative behavior of approximations. Logistic and logit functions provide numerically useful parametrizations for binary probabilities, and entropy and mutual information quantify uncertainty and informativeness. Practical numerical issues—exemplified by the log-sum-exp trick and partition function considerations—are essential for reliable implementation. Finally, inequalities such as Jensen’s provide the theoretical scaffolding for many bounds and variational methods used in modern machine learning research. With these tools in hand, you will be prepared to read and critically evaluate papers that propose new algorithms for training and aligning language models.
