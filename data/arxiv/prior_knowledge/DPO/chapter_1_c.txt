# Jensen’s inequality and convexity basics

Convexity is a fundamental concept in optimization and probability. A function $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is convex if for any $x,y$ and any $\lambda\in[0,1]$,
\[
\phi(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \le \lambda \phi(x) + (1-\lambda)\phi(y).
\]
Geometrically, the line segment between two points on the graph of $\phi$ lies above the graph. If the inequality is strict for $x\neq y$ and $0<\lambda<1$, the function is strictly convex. Concave functions are those whose negatives are convex.

Jensen’s inequality connects convexity and expectations. If $\phi$ is convex and $X$ is a random variable, then
\[
\phi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)].
\]
For concave $\phi$, the inequality reverses. Jensen’s inequality explains many pivotal facts in information theory and statistics. For example, applying Jensen to $\phi(t)=-\log t$ (which is convex on $(0,\infty)$) yields Gibbs’ inequality and hence the nonnegativity of KL divergence:
\[
\mathbb{E}_P\left[-\log\frac{Q(X)}{P(X)}\right] \ge -\log\left(\mathbb{E}_P\left[\frac{Q(X)}{P(X)}\right]\right) = -\log 1 = 0.
\]
More directly, the nonnegativity of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_P[\log(P/Q)]$ follows from convexity of $t\log t$ or via Jensen arguments.

Convexity also underlies why maximum likelihood for exponential families is a convex optimization problem: the negative log-likelihood is convex in the natural parameters because it includes the log partition function, which is convex. This guarantees a unique global minimum and well-behaved gradients. When optimizing stochastic objectives (e.g., empirical risk), convexity provides convergence guarantees for gradient-based algorithms; when objectives are nonconvex, as in deep learning, such guarantees generally do not apply, but convexity arguments still help understand local behavior and design surrogate losses.

Jensen’s inequality is used throughout probabilistic derivations: deriving variational bounds, upper- and lower-bounding log partition functions, or proving inequalities between divergences and entropies. A classic application is deriving the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) in variational inference. For a model with latent variable $Z$ and parameters $\theta$, the marginal log-likelihood $\log p_\theta(X)$ can be written using any proposal distribution $q(Z)$ as
\[
\log p_\theta(X) = \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(X,Z) - \log q(Z)] + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_\theta(\cdot\mid X)),
\]
and since $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\ge 0$, we get the ELBO
\[
\log p_\theta(X) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_\theta(X,Z) - \log q(Z)].
\]
This inequality is a direct consequence of Jensen’s inequality applied to the concavity of the log or equivalently to the nonnegativity of KL divergence.

Understanding convexity and Jensen’s inequality equips you to interpret bounds, variational approximations, and why certain surrogate losses are valid. Many proofs of optimality and convergence rely on these simple but powerful principles.

Conclusion

This chapter collected the essential probabilistic and information-theoretic tools you will need to understand and reason about language models, preference models, and algorithms used in alignment and RLHF. Random variables, expectations, likelihoods, cross-entropy, and binary cross-entropy provide the basic language for modeling and training. The logistic and logit functions link scores to probabilities in binary and pairwise preference models. KL divergence in its forward and reverse forms explains qualitative differences between mode-seeking and mode-covering approximations and motivates regularization terms. Entropy and mutual information quantify uncertainty and dependence. The log-sum-exp trick and careful handling of partition functions are necessary for stable and correct numerical computation, and Jensen’s inequality with basic convexity explains why many bounds and optimization properties hold. Together, these elements form the backbone of the probabilistic reasoning you will encounter in alignment literature and RLHF algorithms.
