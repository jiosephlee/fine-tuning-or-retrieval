Introduction

Human preference data are the backbone of many modern supervised and reinforcement learning systems that seek to align models with human values and judgments. Whether used directly as labels for supervised fine-tuning or as reward signals for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), these datasets must be carefully constructed so that they accurately reflect the judgments we intend models to learn. Building such datasets is not merely a matter of collecting answers; it requires carefully designed prompt–response generation protocols, annotation instructions and training, statistical handling of disagreement, mitigation of systematic biases and label noise, and attention to ethical and safety concerns. This chapter explains how preference datasets are created and quality-assured, with an emphasis on intuition and practical methods so you can read and evaluate research that uses these data.


# Prompt–response pair generation protocols

The first step in creating a preference dataset is generating the items on which humans will express preferences. In the context of language models, this typically means generating prompt–response pairs: a prompt (or context, instruction, or question) and one or more candidate responses produced by different models or by the same model under different settings.

There are several common generation protocols, each with different trade-offs. One simple protocol is model self-play or self-generation: sample prompts from a distribution (e.g., user queries, curated instructions, or randomly drawn contexts) and generate candidate responses from two or more models (or from a single model with different decoding strategies). Another approach is “human-in-the-loop” generation, where human annotators author prompts designed to probe particular behaviors (e.g., adversarial prompts, nuanced moral dilemmas), or the prompts come from real user interactions logged with consent. A third hybrid is to seed prompts from naturalistic logs and then use automated or human augmentation to expand the set.

When designing generation protocols, consider (1) the prompt distribution you want the model to perform well on; (2) diversity of prompts in content, difficulty, and style; and (3) how the responses will be sampled. Responses can be sampled deterministically (e.g., beam search), stochastically (e.g., top-k or nucleus sampling), or by varying model temperature or architecture. Sampling diversity matters: if responses are nearly identical, preference labels will be noisy because annotators see little to distinguish them. If responses differ widely in quality, you may get many trivially consistent labels but fewer informative comparisons for learning fine-grained preferences.

A related design decision is pairing: do you compare responses to the same prompt (intra-prompt comparisons), or responses across different prompts (cross-prompt)? Most preference datasets use intra-prompt pairwise comparisons because they isolate relative preference independent of prompt difficulty. Finally, consider how many responses per prompt you want (pairwise versus multiway comparisons), and whether you want anchors (gold-quality or intentionally poor responses) to calibrate annotators.


# Pairwise comparisons vs. graded ratings and rankings

There are three principal ways to collect human preference information: pairwise comparisons, graded ratings, and full or partial rankings. Each has different cognitive load for annotators and different statistical properties for downstream modeling.

Pairwise comparisons ask annotators to choose which of two responses is better for a given prompt (or to indicate a tie). The cognitive task is simple: compare two items and pick the preferred one. Pairwise comparisons typically produce high-quality, low-variance labels because relative judgments are easier and more consistent than absolute judgments. Statistically, pairwise data are well-suited to models based on pairwise probability (e.g., Bradley–Terry). With enough comparisons, pairwise data can be aggregated into a latent score per response or per model.

Graded ratings ask annotators to assign a numerical score to a single response along one or more criteria (e.g., helpfulness from 1 to 5, factuality from 1 to 3). Ratings allow richer information per item (multiple axes and magnitude), but they introduce scale interpretation issues: different annotators may use the scale differently (one person's 4 is another's 3). Ratings are efficient when you can train annotators to align scales and when you want multi-dimensional judgments, but they often require more careful calibration and may lead to higher inter-annotator variance.

Rankings require annotators to order multiple (three or more) responses for the same prompt. Rankings provide more information per annotation because a single ranking encodes several pairwise comparisons, but they impose higher cognitive load and are slower. Partial rankings (e.g., top-k) are a compromise: ask for the best 2 responses out of 5. Statistical models for rankings (e.g., Plackett–Luce) can convert rankings into latent utilities.

From a modeling standpoint, pairwise comparisons are particularly appealing for RLHF because they directly yield relative preference signals for reward learning. The Bradley–Terry model provides a common parametric form: if each response $i$ has latent score $s_i$, the probability that response $i$ is preferred to response $j$ is modeled as
$P(i \succ j) = \frac{\exp(s_i)}{\exp(s_i) + \exp(s_j)}$.
Pairwise likelihoods are simple to optimize and robust, and they naturally accommodate ties with small modifications. If you collect graded ratings instead, you might convert them to pairwise labels by comparing scores, but be mindful that the conversion can amplify scale disagreements.

In practice, a hybrid approach is common: use graded ratings to screen or triage items and pairwise comparisons to obtain high-fidelity signals where it matters most, or use rankings for dense information in evaluation sets. Consider the downstream objective: if you need a reward function for RLHF, pairwise data are often the most straightforward match.


# Annotation guidelines and rater training

High-quality human labels begin with clear, well-structured annotation guidelines and thorough rater training. Annotation guidelines translate high-level goals (e.g., "prefer helpful, harmless, and honest responses") into operational criteria annotators can apply consistently. Good guidelines include a clear description of the task, a precise definition of the axes being judged (what counts as "helpful", "factual", "concise", etc.), positive and negative examples illustrating edge cases, instructions on how to resolve ambiguous cases, and a list of forbidden behaviors (e.g., guessing when unsure).

Rater training should combine reading the guidelines with hands-on practice. In a training session, annotators should label a curated set of calibration examples with detailed feedback. These calibration items should cover common failure modes and borderline cases. Provide explanations of the intended labeling for each calibration example, so annotators learn the nuanced reasoning expected. Iterative calibration—where annotators receive feedback across several rounds—reduces variance and improves agreement.

Design the interface to minimize cognitive burden and bias. For example, present both responses side-by-side with the prompt visible, allow annotators to highlight or quote supporting text, and give options for "no preference" or "both bad" where appropriate. Specify whether annotators should prefer brevity over comprehensiveness, when to prefer safety over directness, and how to weigh conflicting criteria. If multiple axes are being judged, decide whether to collect a single overall preference or separate per-axis ratings—the latter yields richer data but increases complexity.

Compensation and motivation matter: underpaid annotators tend to rush, increasing label noise. Provide fair pay, include intermittent quality checks that provide feedback, and consider mechanisms to reward careful work (bonuses, progressive responsibilities). Monitor annotator behavior for patterns of low effort (straight-lining, always choosing the left option) and retrain or remove annotators who underperform.


# Inter-annotator agreement and reliability

No matter how clear the guidelines, annotators will disagree. Measuring inter-annotator agreement (IAA) is essential for both instrument validation and understanding label reliability. Simple percent agreement is easy to compute but fails to account for agreement by chance. Agreement metrics that account for chance include Cohen's kappa (for two annotators), Fleiss' kappa (for many annotators), and Krippendorff's alpha (which handles arbitrary numbers of annotators and missing data and supports different measurement levels—nominal, ordinal, interval). For ordinal data or rankings, measures like Spearman's rho or Kendall's tau quantify rank correlation.

Krippendorff's alpha is widely recommended for complex annotation setups because of its flexibility. It is defined as
$\alpha = 1 - \dfrac{D_o}{D_e}$,
where $D_o$ is the observed disagreement and $D_e$ is the disagreement expected by chance. Values near 1 indicate strong agreement; values near 0 indicate agreement no better than chance. Interpreting the numeric value should consider task difficulty: some domains (e.g., subjective literary quality) naturally yield lower alpha than more objective tasks.

For pairwise comparisons, compute consistency measures like the fraction of comparisons where annotators agree, or use more advanced models that estimate annotator reliabilities directly (see the next section). When you have multiple annotators per item, you can estimate the consensus label via majority vote, weighted vote by annotator skill, or probabilistic models such as Dawid–Skene. Importantly, agreement analysis can guide improvements: low agreement on particular prompt types suggests the need to refine guidelines, add calibration examples, or treat those prompts separately.

One must be cautious interpreting kappa-like metrics when prevalence or bias is extreme. For rare positive classes, kappa can be low despite high apparent practical agreement. Hence inspect confusion matrices and per-class agreement in addition to aggregate statistics.


# Label noise, biases, and debiasing strategies

Label noise arises from random error (careless annotators), systematic biases (annotator demographics, phrasing effects), or ambiguity in the task. Biases can enter at many points: prompt sampling bias (over-representing particular topics), annotator selection bias (demographic skew), framing effects (order in which responses are presented), anchoring (presence of an anchor response), and social desirability bias (annotators report what they think the requester wants). Label noise and bias degrade model performance and can lead to models inheriting or amplifying undesirable behavior.

A first line of defense is annotation design: randomize presentation order and left/right position to avoid position bias, anonymize model identities, and avoid leading language. Use stratified prompt sampling to capture diverse topics and difficulty levels. Where possible, recruit annotators whose demographics match the intended user population or ensure sufficient diversity to detect population-specific preferences.

Statistical debiasing strategies include label aggregation models that account for annotator reliability and bias. The Dawid–Skene model is an early EM-based approach that estimates a confusion matrix per annotator and infers the latent true labels. More modern hierarchical Bayesian models or item-response theory (IRT) approaches treat annotator skill and item difficulty as latent variables. For pairwise comparisons, the Bradley–Terry model with annotator-specific parameters can model both item scores and annotator idiosyncrasies. In the Bradley–Terry extension, annotator $a$ may have a bias parameter $b_a$ so that
$P_a(i \succ j) = \frac{\exp(s_i + b_a)}{\exp(s_i + b_a) + \exp(s_j + b_a)}$,
or more flexibly allow annotator-specific sensitivity.

Another practical technique is reweighting examples during training to correct for sampling biases. If some prompt types are over-sampled relative to the target distribution, downweight them or upweight underrepresented types. Post-hoc calibration techniques, such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression, can correct score scaling mismatches between the learned reward function and actual probabilities.

Active learning can mitigate annotation inefficiency by selecting the most informative comparisons (e.g., where the current model is uncertain or where annotator disagreement is expected), thereby reducing the number of labels needed to learn a robust reward model.

Finally, explicitly model label noise in downstream training: use loss functions robust to noisy labels (e.g., label smoothing, generalized cross-entropy), or use a small high-quality calibration dataset (trusted labels) to regularize learning on the larger noisy dataset.


# Offline vs. online data collection

Preference data can be collected offline or online, and each mode has distinct advantages and pitfalls.

Offline collection means generating prompts and responses in a controlled environment (simulated users, crowdsourced prompts, or pre-collected logs) and then annotating them in batches. Offline protocols allow for careful prompt design, quality control, and rigorous annotation guidelines. They are safer for collecting sensitive or adversarial prompts because annotators can be protected, and you have time to craft rubrics for difficult cases. Offline data are easier to audit and curate, and they are common in initial research and model development.

Online data collection involves gathering human preferences from live interaction systems—real users interacting with a deployed model and providing implicit or explicit feedback (clicks, thumbs up/down, session duration, follow-up messages). Online feedback has the advantage of being from real users in natural contexts and can capture long-term preferences and behaviors. However, online data are subject to confounding factors: presentation bias, selection bias (which users choose to provide feedback), and temporal non-stationarity (users' expectations change as models improve). Moreover, obtaining explicit feedback at scale can be difficult without incentivizing engagement.

A compromise is to use a human-in-the-loop system: online aggregation pipelines where annotators review a subset of user interactions, or where model outputs that triggered safety flags are prioritized for human annotation. In RLHF pipelines, it is common to use a mixture of offline curated comparisons (for stable reward learning) and online preference signals (for continual adaptation), carefully balancing distributional shifts.

When using online signals, it is crucial to account for bandit and counterfactual estimation issues: user feedback depends on the policy used to present responses, so naive estimation of preference distributions is biased. Techniques from off-policy evaluation, inverse propensity scoring, and contextual bandits can help de-bias online signals, but they require logging sufficient metadata (which response was shown, what alternatives were possible, timestamps, and user covariates).
