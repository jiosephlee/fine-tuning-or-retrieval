Introduction

Quantization is the process of mapping continuous or high-precision values to a discrete set of representable values. In machine learning and signal processing, quantization enables efficient storage and computation by reducing the number of bits used to represent numbers. However, quantization inevitably introduces errors. To design effective quantization schemes we must understand tools for measuring and controlling these errors, techniques for choosing quantization scales, and mathematical models that describe how quantization noise propagates through computations such as deep neural networks.

This chapter develops the mathematical tools commonly used for reasoning about quantization and discretization error. We begin by reviewing norms and measures that quantify signal magnitude and error. We then explain common scaling heuristics that choose the quantizer’s dynamic range, and study properties of rounding and clipping operations including their biases. Next we introduce quantization noise models and compute variances associated with typical quantizers. We analyze the bias–variance trade-offs introduced by quantization choices, and finally examine how quantization error propagates across layers and channels in deep networks. Throughout, emphasis is placed on intuition, practical formulas, and simple derivations so you can apply these techniques when reading research papers or designing quantized systems.

# Norms and measures (L1, L2, max, mean absolute value)

When reasoning about signals and quantization error, we must choose ways to measure size and error. Several norms and measures are commonly used, each with different geometric meaning and sensitivity to outliers.

The $L_2$ norm (Euclidean norm) of a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is defined by $\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}$. The squared $L_2$ norm, $\|x\|_2^2 = \sum_i x_i^2$, is convenient because it is additive across orthogonal components and directly relates to energy in signal processing. The $L_2$ norm is sensitive to large entries due to the squaring: a few large components dominate the norm. Many analytic results (e.g., variance calculations, Parseval-type equalities) use $L_2$ because of its mathematical convenience and connection to Gaussian noise models.

The $L_1$ norm (taxicab norm) is $\|x\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|$. The $L_1$ norm measures total absolute magnitude and is less sensitive to large outliers than $L_2$. It appears naturally when discussing sparsity (e.g., Lasso) and mean absolute deviation. When quantifying average absolute error, the $L_1$ norm scaled by $1/n$ gives the mean absolute error (MAE): $\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\|x\|_1$.

The max norm (uniform norm or $L_\infty$) is $\|x\|_\infty = \max_{i} |x_i|$. This measure matters for range-based quantizers that choose a scale to cover the maximum absolute value. If you set the quantizer range to include $\|x\|_\infty$, no clipping occurs, but that choice can be wasteful if the maximum is an outlier: many small-magnitude values will be represented with coarse resolution.

Another common quantity in quantization work is the mean absolute value (mean-abs), defined as $\mathrm{mav}(x) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|$, which is simply the $L_1$ norm divided by $n$. Mean-abs is a robust, low-cost statistic for scale estimation and is often used to set quantizer ranges when one wants to ignore a few large outliers.

In statistical terms, for random variables we often use expectations: the root mean square (RMS) is $\mathrm{rms}(X) = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}$ and the mean absolute value is $\mathbb{E}[|X|]$. For a zero-mean random variable, RMS equals the standard deviation $\sigma$.

Choosing which norm or statistic to use depends on the goal. If you care about minimizing mean-squared error (MSE), the $L_2$ norm is the natural measure. If you want robustness to outliers or to minimize mean absolute error, $L_1$-based statistics are better. If the quantizer must avoid clipping at all costs, $\|x\|_\infty$ is the relevant measure. Practical quantization design typically balances these concerns: using max-based scaling avoids clipping but may increase rounding noise for most values; using mean-based scaling reduces average quantization error but allows some clipping.

# Scaling heuristics (mean-abs scaling, RMS scaling, max-based scaling)

A uniform quantizer with $b$ bits typically maps a real-valued input to $2^b$ discrete levels across a symmetric interval $[-\alpha, \alpha]$ (for signed values). The choice of $\alpha$, the scale or clipping threshold, determines the trade-off between quantization step size and clipping. Small $\alpha$ yields fine resolution near zero but causes clipping of outliers; large $\alpha$ prevents clipping but increases the quantization step (worse rounding error) for typical values. We discuss three common heuristics for selecting $\alpha$: max-based scaling, mean-abs scaling, and RMS (standard deviation) scaling.

Max-based scaling chooses $\alpha = \max_i |x_i|$ (or an empirical maximum plus small safety margin). This guarantees no clipping on the calibration set and is simple and robust. However, if the maximum is an outlier, the quantization step $\Delta = 2\alpha / (2^b - 1)$ will be large, increasing quantization noise for most samples. Thus max-based scaling minimizes worst-case clipping but can be suboptimal for average-case error. For example, if almost all values lie in $[-1,1]$ but one sample is $100$, using $\alpha=100$ wastes resolution.

RMS scaling sets $\alpha$ proportional to the root mean square: $\alpha = k \cdot \mathrm{rms}(x) = k \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i^2}$. Typical choices are $k \in [2,4]$, tuned so that only a small fraction of values are clipped. RMS scaling is natural when aiming to minimize mean-squared quantization error because $\mathrm{rms}$ reflects energy and is directly related to the variance of typical values. RMS scaling is optimal in some analytic settings where inputs are Gaussian: for a fixed clipping probability, choosing $\alpha$ proportional to standard deviation minimizes MSE.

Mean-abs scaling uses $\alpha = k \cdot \mathrm{mav}(x) = k \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |x_i|$. Mean-abs is more robust to heavy tails than RMS because it does not square values. It often provides a good trade-off between ignoring a few large outliers and representing the bulk of the distribution with adequate resolution. The constant $k$ is tuned empirically (common values are $k \approx 4$ or chosen to achieve a target clipping fraction).

When choosing a scaling heuristic consider the downstream cost function. If the application optimizes MSE (e.g., mean-squared quantization error affecting gradients), RMS scaling is aligned with that objective. If the system must avoid rare catastrophic clipping (e.g., in control signals), max-based scaling may be necessary. For deep networks, layers and channels have differing distributions; per-channel scaling (discussed later) can capture amplitude differences better than a single global scale.

A practical approach often used in quantized neural networks is to collect a calibration dataset, compute a chosen statistic (max, RMS, mean-abs) per tensor (or per channel), and set $\alpha$ as that statistic times a safety factor. The safety factor can be selected to bound the fraction of clipped values, trading off clipping versus rounding noise.

# Rounding functions and clipping operators: properties and bias

Two basic operations define most quantizers: clipping (or saturation) and rounding. Clipping truncates values outside a representation interval to the nearest representable boundary, while rounding maps values within the interval to the nearest discrete quantization level.

Consider a symmetric uniform quantizer with levels $q_k = \left(-\alpha + k\Delta\right)$ for $k=0,\dots,2^b-1$, where $\Delta = 2\alpha/(2^b-1)$. Define the clipping operator $C_\alpha(x) = \mathrm{clip}(x, -\alpha, \alpha)$ that sets $x$ to $-\alpha$ if $x < -\alpha$ and to $\alpha$ if $x>\alpha$. After clipping, the rounding operator $R_\Delta(\cdot)$ maps a value to the nearest quantization level. For analysis one can treat the full quantization operator as $Q(x) = R_\Delta(C_\alpha(x))$.

A key property of rounding in a symmetric uniform quantizer is that for any value inside the interval $[-\alpha, \alpha]$, the rounding error (quantization residual) $e(x) = Q(x) - x$ lies in $[-\frac{\Delta}{2}, \frac{\Delta}{2}]$. If rounding is unbiased in expectation for a given distribution, then $\mathbb{E}[e(x)] = 0$ (conditioning on no clipping). Unbiasedness holds if the distribution of $x$ is locally uniform at the scale of $\Delta$ or if the rounding rule is symmetric and the distribution is symmetric about level midpoints. However, in practice, rounding can introduce bias when the distribution is not centered on quantization bins or when systematic tie-breaking is used.

Clipping introduces bias more directly. If some fraction of the input mass lies beyond $\alpha$, those values get mapped to $\pm \alpha$, pulling their expectation toward the edges. The clipping bias is $\mathbb{E}[C_\alpha(x) - x]$ for the clipped portion; this term is generally nonzero and can be large if tails are heavy. For example, if $x$ is heavy-tailed, clipping at a finite $\alpha$ will significantly bias the mean. In neural networks, clipping activations or weights will change forward activations and gradient statistics, possibly harming training if not accounted for.

An important subtlety arises from nonlinearities preceding or following quantizers. If a nonlinear function $f$ is applied after quantization, the expectation mismatch $\mathbb{E}[f(Q(x))] - f(\mathbb{E}[x])$ can cause systematic errors beyond the raw quantization bias. When analyzing bias it's helpful to decompose the mean error into two parts: rounding bias (zero-mean if rounding is symmetric and distribution conditions hold) and clipping bias (typically nonzero).

To reduce bias, practitioners may use strategies like stochastic rounding (see below), mid-tread versus mid-rise quantizers, or offsetting bins. Stochastic rounding replaces deterministic rounding to the nearest level with a probabilistic choice: if $x$ lies between levels $q_k$ and $q_{k+1}$, round to $q_k$ with probability proportional to proximity and to $q_{k+1}$ with complementary probability. Stochastic rounding preserves expectation exactly: $\mathbb{E}[Q_{\mathrm{stoch}}(x)] = x$ for any $x$ inside the unclipped interval, eliminating rounding bias at the price of additional variance. Clipping bias cannot be removed by stochastic rounding inside the interval but can be mitigated by expanding $\alpha$.

In summary, rounding tends to be near-unbiased under mild conditions, but clipping introduces deterministic bias. The combined quantization bias depends on the distribution’s tails relative to the chosen scale. Understanding these biases is crucial when quantization interacts with nonlinear operations or when the downstream metric is sensitive to systematic shifts.

# Quantization noise models and variance analysis

To analyze the impact of quantization, it is useful to model the quantization error as noise. The classic additive noise model writes the quantized output as $Q(x) = x + e$, where $e$ is the quantization error (residual). For uniform quantization without clipping and assuming inputs are not correlated with quantization errors, a common simplifying assumption is that $e$ is a random variable uniformly distributed on $[-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]$. Under that model, the mean of $e$ is zero and its variance is $\mathbb{V}[e] = \frac{\Delta^2}{12}$.

This uniform-distribution model is exact if inputs are uniformly distributed over a quantization cell or if one uses dithering (adding uniform noise before quantization and subtracting it afterwards). In many practical situations the uniform model gives good approximate predictions for mean-squared quantization error. For a $b$-bit uniform quantizer with dynamic range $[-\alpha,\alpha]$, $\Delta = 2\alpha/(2^b-1) \approx 2\alpha / 2^b$ for large $b$, and the per-sample variance of quantization noise is approximately
\[
\sigma_e^2 \approx \frac{\Delta^2}{12} \approx \frac{(2\alpha)^2}{12\cdot 2^{2b}} = \frac{\alpha^2}{3\cdot 2^{2b-2}} \approx \frac{\alpha^2}{3}\cdot 2^{-2b}.
\]
More compactly, $\sigma_e^2 \propto \alpha^2 / 2^{2b}$, highlighting that quantization noise power scales quadratically with dynamic range and exponentially with bit count.

If rounding is stochastic, the uniform residual assumption holds exactly inside the unclipped interval: stochastic rounding ensures $\mathbb{E}[e|x]=0$ and when $x$ is random and its position within a quantization cell is approximately uniform, $e$ is approximately uniform. Deterministic round-to-nearest also produces residuals bounded by $\pm\Delta/2$, but the distribution of $e$ may not be uniform and can be correlated with $x$.

When clipping occurs, the additive noise model must be augmented: for clipped samples, the residual $e(x) = \pm \alpha - x$ can be arbitrarily large (bounded only by how large $x$ is), so variance formulas derived under the uniform assumption underestimate error. A practical way to handle clipping is to decompose error variance into two parts: the variance due to rounding within the interval and the variance due to clipping events. Let $p_\text{clip}$ denote the probability that $|x|>\alpha$. If the unclipped part has variance $\sigma_{e,\text{round}}^2 \approx (1-p_\text{clip})\Delta^2/12$ and the clipped part contributes $\sigma_{e,\text{clip}}^2 = \mathbb{E}[(C_\alpha(x)-x)^2 \mid |x|>\alpha]\cdot p_\text{clip}$, the total variance is their sum. Often one picks $\alpha$ so that $p_\text{clip}$ is very small, making clipping contribution negligible.

We also must consider bias and correlations. The additive noise model assumes $e$ is independent of $x$ or has zero mean conditioned on $x$. In practice correlations between $x$ and $e$ can occur, especially for deterministic nearest rounding where $e$ is a deterministic function of $x$. These correlations affect downstream computations: for example, when multiplying a quantized weight by an activation, covariance terms between quantization errors and inputs affect the expected product. For analysis it is common to assume independence for tractability, but when high fidelity is needed one must account for correlations.

For vector or tensor quantization, one often measures total quantization noise power using $L_2$ terms. If $x$ is a vector and we quantize each component independently with the same $\Delta$, the total expected squared error is $n \Delta^2/12$ (again ignoring clipping and correlations). If per-channel or per-element scales are used, each channel's contribution sums to the global error.

Finally, non-uniform quantizers (e.g., logarithmic quantizers or learned quantizers) have different error statistics. A common technique for non-uniform quantizers is to linearize locally and apply similar variance approximations by mapping back into a uniform variable via the inverse transform. But uniform additive-noise approximations remain the standard first-order model for many analyses.

# Bias-variance trade-offs introduced by quantization

Quantization affects both bias and variance of estimators and computations. Understanding this bias–variance decomposition clarifies design choices: do we prefer smaller bias (by avoiding clipping) or smaller variance (by tightening dynamic range and reducing step size)?

Decompose the quantized output $Q(x)$ into its expected value and zero-mean residual: $Q(x) = \mathbb{E}[Q(x)] + \delta$, where $\delta$ has zero mean. The bias relative to the original value $x$ is $b(x) = \mathbb{E}[Q(x)] - x$. The mean-squared error (MSE) is
\[
\mathbb{E}[(Q(x)-x)^2] = b(x)^2 + \mathbb{V}[Q(x)] = b(x)^2 + \mathbb{E}[\delta^2].
\]
Thus MSE splits into squared bias plus variance.

If we choose $\alpha$ large to avoid clipping, bias from clipping is reduced: $b(x)$ tends to zero. But $\Delta$ becomes larger, so quantization variance $\mathbb{V}[Q(x)]$ increases. Conversely, choosing $\alpha$ small shrinks $\Delta$ and reduces variance but causes clipping bias to grow. This is the fundamental bias–variance trade-off of range selection.

Stochastic rounding changes the trade-off for rounding: it eliminates rounding bias (so within the unclipped region $b(x)=\mathbb{E}[Q(x)]-x$ goes to zero), but increases variance because rounding is now a random process. Deterministic rounding can have small bias but zero additional rounding variance beyond the deterministic mapping; stochastic rounding shifts the error from bias into variance. Whether this is preferable depends on downstream operations: in iterative algorithms (e.g., gradient descent), unbiased stochastic updates may prevent systematic drift, while for single-shot computations deterministic rounding with small bias might be acceptable.

Another layer of trade-offs arises when quantization interacts with nonlinearity. Suppose we quantize inputs before applying a convex nonlinear function $f$. The bias in the output $\mathbb{E}[f(Q(x))] - f(x)$ includes contributions from both bias and variance of $Q(x)$ due to Jensen’s inequality: for nonlinear $f$, the function’s curvature makes the expected output depend on input variance. For example, if $f$ is convex and $\delta$ is zero-mean, $\mathbb{E}[f(x+\delta)] \ge f(x)$ (Jensen), so variance increases expected output. This means quantization-induced variance can systematically change downstream activations even when rounding is unbiased. Consequently, minimizing variance (e.g., by using more bits or smaller $\Delta$) can reduce such systematic shifts.

In deep learning training, bias and variance have different practical effects. Bias from clipping or systematic rounding shifts mean activations and gradients, potentially degrading accuracy or causing optimizer instability. Variance from stochastic rounding or coarse quantization injects noise that can sometimes regularize training (like dropout) and help escape local minima, but excessive variance hurts convergence and final performance. Therefore quantization strategies frequently balance these competing effects: set a scale that ensures most values are represented without clipping (reducing bias) and use enough bits or fine-grained per-channel scales to keep variance small.

Analytically, when optimizing $\alpha$ to minimize MSE for a known distribution of $x$, one can write the MSE as a function of $\alpha$ and find its minimizer. For example, if $x$ has a known distribution with density $p(x)$, the MSE is
\[
\mathrm{MSE}(\alpha) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (Q_\alpha(x)-x)^2 p(x)\,dx,
\]
and one can compute or numerically optimize this integral. In Gaussian cases closed-form or near-closed-form solutions exist for the optimal clipping threshold $\alpha$ in terms of $\sigma$ and $b$.

# Per-layer and per-channel error propagation in deep networks

In deep neural networks, quantization is typically applied to weights, activations, and sometimes gradients. Errors introduced by quantization propagate through layers via linear and nonlinear operations. Understanding how per-layer and per-channel errors accumulate is crucial for choosing where to allocate bits and how to set scales.

Consider a single linear layer computing $y = W x$, where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is a weight matrix and $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ an activation vector. Suppose $W$ is quantized to $\widetilde{W} = W + E_W$ and $x$ may be quantized to $\widetilde{x} = x + E_x$, with $E_W,E_x$ representing quantization errors. The output error is
\[
\widetilde{y} - y = E_W x + W E_x + E_W E_x.
\]
If quantization errors are small we can ignore the second-order term $E_W E_x$. Under independence assumptions and zero-mean errors, the expected squared error power at the output per output unit becomes
\[
\mathbb{E}\big[\| \widetilde{y}-y\|_2^2\big] \approx \mathbb{E}\big[\|E_W x\|_2^2\big] + \mathbb{E}\big[\|W E_x\|_2^2\big].
\]
For the first term, if errors on $W$ entries are independent with variance $\sigma_{W}^2$ and entries of $x$ have second moments, then
\[
\mathbb{E}\big[\|E_W x\|_2^2\big] = \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb{E}[E_{W,ij}^2] \mathbb{E}[x_j^2] = n m\, \sigma_W^2 \cdot \overline{x^2},
\]
where $\overline{x^2}$ denotes an average second moment. For the second term, if $E_x$ has covariance $\Sigma_x$ and $W$ is deterministic, then $\mathbb{E}\|W E_x\|^2 = \mathrm{tr}(W \Sigma_x W^\top)$, which can be bounded by $\|W\|_F^2 \cdot \max_j \mathrm{Var}(E_{x,j})$ under simple assumptions. Intuitively, large weights amplify activation quantization noise; hence heavier quantization of activations can be tolerated for layers with small weight norms, and vice versa.

Per-channel quantization exploits the observation that different output channels (filters) in convolutional networks often have widely varying magnitudes. If each output channel $k$ uses its own scale $\alpha_k$, the quantization noise variance for that channel is proportional to $\alpha_k^2/2^{2b}$ (for uniform quantization). The contribution of channel $k$ to downstream error is then scaled by how that channel participates in subsequent layers. Thus selectively allocating more bits or tighter scale to channels with larger dynamic range or greater influence can reduce overall network error.

Error accumulation through multiple layers is not merely additive because quantization errors can be amplified or attenuated by layer weights and nonlinearities. In a network of $L$ layers, a first-order analysis linearizes nonlinear activations around operating points, treating each layer as an affine operator plus noise. If layer $\ell$ introduces additive error $n_\ell$ at its output, then the total effect on the final output is approximately propagated by the product of subsequent Jacobians:
\[
\delta_{\text{final}} \approx J_L J_{L-1} \cdots J_{\ell+1} n_\ell,
\]
where $J_k$ is the Jacobian of layer $k$’s mapping with respect to its input. This shows that quantization error introduced earlier in the network is multiplied by later-layer gains, potentially leading to large effects if the product norm is large. Conversely, error introduced late in the network is less amplified. This motivates protecting early layers (or critical paths) with higher precision. In practice, however, exact Jacobians are data-dependent and nonlinear activations like ReLU can gate error propagation, making layer-wise sensitivity analysis empirical.

Another important aspect is training versus inference. During training, gradients are propagated backward, so quantization of gradients introduces additional noise that can harm convergence. Error propagation for gradients follows similar linearized formulas but runs backward through the transpose Jacobians. Empirically, some layers tolerate aggressive quantization in inference but require higher precision during training.

Finally, the correlation structure of quantization errors across channels affects propagation. If quantization errors across channels are uncorrelated, their contributions add in mean-square. If errors are correlated (e.g., due to shared scale or synchronized clipping events), worst-case amplification can occur for certain linear combinations, requiring analysis beyond simple variance sums.

In designing quantized networks, practitioners often perform per-layer or per-channel sensitivity analysis: simulate quantization on a calibration dataset and measure final-output degradation when varying bitwidth or scale for each layer or channel. This empirical approach complements analytic variance propagation and is recommended because actual networks exhibit complex interactions (nonlinearities, BatchNorm, residual connections) that challenge purely analytic models.

Conclusion

Quantization is a powerful tool for efficient computation, but it requires careful mathematical reasoning about norms, scaling, rounding, and noise. Norms such as $L_1$, $L_2$, and $L_\infty$ guide scale selection and error metrics. Scaling heuristics—max, mean-abs, RMS—offer practical ways to choose quantizer range, each with trade-offs between clipping risk and quantization variance. Rounding and clipping introduce bias and variance that must be understood, and additive noise models (uniform residuals) provide tractable approximations for quantization variance. Bias–variance trade-offs govern choices like clipping threshold and stochastic versus deterministic rounding. Finally, in deep networks, quantization errors propagate and can be amplified by weights and nonlinearities; per-layer and per-channel analyses help allocate precision where it matters most. Armed with these mathematical tools and intuitions, you should be able to interpret quantization design choices in the literature, compute first-order error estimates, and guide practical quantization experiments.
