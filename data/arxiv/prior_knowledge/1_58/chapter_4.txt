Introduction

Scaling laws for language models describe how model performance—commonly measured by metrics such as cross-entropy loss or perplexity—changes as a function of the three main resource axes available to practitioners: model size (number of parameters), dataset size (number of training tokens), and compute budget (total number of floating‑point operations used in training). Over the past several years, researchers have observed remarkably simple empirical relationships between these quantities. These relationships enable practitioners to predict how much gain in performance they can expect by increasing parameters or data, to decide how to allocate a limited compute budget optimally, and to reason about when returns will diminish. In this chapter we develop the formulation of these scaling laws, explain the theoretical and practical intuition behind them, present prescriptions for optimal allocation of parameters versus training tokens, explore implications for model design and training planning, discuss empirical validation and known limits, and interpret what it means when different setups (precision, architecture, etc.) are reported to be "equivalent."

# Formulation of model, data, and compute scaling laws (Kaplan, Chinchilla)

Early systematic studies of scaling behavior were presented by Kaplan et al. (2020) and followed by important refinements by Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla, 2022). These works fitted simple power‑law functional forms to empirical measurements of validation loss as a function of parameter count $N$, number of training tokens $D$, and total compute $C$. The basic observation is that, across many orders of magnitude, validation loss decreases roughly as a power law until one of the resources becomes too small and causes saturation or overfitting.

The Kaplan formulation focused on two axes: model size $N$ and compute budget $C$. Kaplan et al. fit an empirical law for the cross‑entropy loss $L(N,C)$ of transformer models trained on web text. Their core findings were that for fixed compute, larger models gave better performance up to a point, and that the optimal model size for a given compute budget followed a power-law scaling $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\alpha}$ for some exponent $\alpha$. The loss itself was modeled approximately as a sum of irreducible loss and scaling terms, commonly written in the separable form
$$
L(N, D) \approx L_{\infty} + a_N N^{-\alpha} + a_D D^{-\beta},
$$
where $L_{\infty}$ is an asymptotic irreducible loss (e.g., due to model class mismatch or noise in the data), and $a_N, a_D, \alpha, \beta$ are positive constants fit to data. This expresses the idea that losses have approximately additive contributions from underparameterization (insufficient model size) and insufficient data.

Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) extended and revised this picture by emphasizing the role of dataset size $D$ explicitly and analyzing a much wider sweep over $N$ and $D$ for a fixed compute budget $C = k \cdot N \cdot D$ (with $k$ depending on architecture and training regime). A key empirical result from Chinchilla is a compact scaling law for loss versus model size and dataset size:
$$
L(N, D) \approx L_{\infty} + A (N)^{-\alpha} + B (D)^{-\beta},
$$
with fitted exponents around $\alpha \approx 0.34$ and $\beta \approx 0.28$ for the specific training/settings they measured (these numerical values are dataset‑ and architecture‑dependent). One practical implication from Chinchilla was that previously trained very large parameter-count models (e.g., "GPT-3 sized") were undertrained: for a given compute budget, using fewer parameters and more data yields better loss. From the separable form one can derive an optimal tradeoff between $N$ and $D$ given compute constraints.

Another way to express scaling is via compute-centric laws without fixing either $N$ or $D$ but instead treating compute $C$ as the primary budget. A simple empirical relation is that for models where $N$ and $D$ are adjusted optimally, loss scales as a power law in compute:
$$
L(C) \approx L_{\infty} + \tilde{a} \, C^{-\gamma},
$$
where $\gamma$ is another fitted exponent. In practice, one typically sees three regimes: (1) model‑limited: increasing $N$ helps but there is ample data; (2) data‑limited: increasing $D$ helps but model is large enough; (3) compute‑limited: both must be balanced to optimally use compute. The separable error model with power laws captures these regimes and allows derivation of the optimal allocation.

Intuitively, the power-law decrease arises because each doubling of a resource (parameters or data) gives a predictable fractional improvement in loss, over a broad range. The additive separable form reflects independent sources of approximation error: finite model capacity and finite data sampling. These empirical fits are not derived from first principles but arise from consistent behavior across many experiments; they are a compact summary useful for planning and understanding trends.

# Optimal allocation of parameters vs training tokens

Given a fixed compute budget $C$, how should one allocate between model size $N$ and number of training tokens $D$ to minimize validation loss? This is the primary practical question addressed by Chinchilla and follow-up analyses. To reason about this, we need a relation tying compute to $N$ and $D$. A reasonable approximation for training transformer language models with full‑batch SGD variants and typical implementations is that compute is proportional to the product of model size and dataset size:
$$
C \propto N \cdot D.
$$
More precisely, if we count the number of floating point operations performed during training and ignore constant factors, $C \approx k \, N \, D$ where $k$ depends on sequence length, batch size, number of optimization steps per token, and architecture constants. For optimization questions the proportionality is sufficient.

Using the separable loss model
$$
L(N, D) = L_{\infty} + a_N N^{-\alpha} + a_D D^{-\beta},
$$
and the constraint $C = k N D$, we can set up a constrained optimization problem: minimize $L$ subject to the compute constraint. Substitute $D = C/(kN)$ and minimize with respect to $N$. Differentiating and setting derivative to zero yields a power-law relation for the optimal $N$ given $C$,
$$
a_N \alpha N^{-(\alpha+1)} - a_D \beta \left(\frac{C}{k}\right)^{-\beta} N^{\beta-1} = 0.
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}.
$$
Similarly,
$$
D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.
$$
These expressions show that how steeply you should increase $N$ versus $D$ with compute depends on the relative exponents $\alpha$ and $\beta$. If model improvements are gained more efficiently per parameter than per token (large $\alpha$ relative to $\beta$), then $D_{\text{opt}}$ will grow faster and vice versa.

Plugging in empirical exponents from Chinchilla (roughly $\alpha \approx 0.34$, $\beta \approx 0.28$) yields
$$
N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.45}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.55}.
$$
Thus, for those fitted exponents, the optimal strategy is to spend a bit more of your compute budget on increasing the number of training tokens than on increasing parameters: larger datasets are slightly more important than larger models, on a per‑compute basis. Numerically, the Chinchilla team reported an approximate rule that the number of training tokens should be about 20 times the number of parameters (in tokens per parameter), a concrete guideline that differs from prior practice where many models trained with far fewer tokens per parameter.

Another way to view the optimum is to balance the marginal returns: choose $N$ and $D$ so that reducing the model error component $a_N N^{-\alpha}$ by a small factor costs the same compute as reducing the data error component $a_D D^{-\beta}$ by the same amount. This equalization of marginal benefit per unit compute gives the same power-law condition and emphasizes that the scaling law prescription is a resource allocation principle.

Note that the above derivation depends on the correctness of the separable power-law approximation and on the compute proportionality assumption. If real systems introduce other overheads (e.g., more epochs per token, irregular efficiency vs $N$), the exponents or constants may shift. Still, the qualitative guidance—there exists an optimal parameter/token tradeoff and it is a power law in compute—is robust across many experiments.

# Implications of scaling laws for model design and training budgets

Scaling laws have broad practical and strategic implications for researchers, engineers, and organizations deciding how to allocate resources when building language models.

First, scaling laws give an upper-level planning tool. If you know the current loss and the fitted scaling constants for your family of models and data, you can estimate how much additional compute, data, or parameters you need to achieve a target improvement. The power-law form makes these extrapolations straightforward: halving the loss above $L_{\infty}$ requires roughly a $2^{1/\gamma}$ increase in compute under the compute‑only scaling $L(C) \approx L_{\infty}+ \tilde{a} C^{-\gamma}$. This helps to set realistic expectations for returns on additional investment.

Second, because scaling laws prescribe an optimal allocation of compute between parameters and tokens, they can prevent resource misallocation. For example, if models are undertrained (too few tokens per parameter), adding parameters without proportionally increasing data yields suboptimal returns; conversely, if datasets are immense relative to model capacity, increasing parameters may be more effective. The Chinchilla lesson—train smaller models with more data for fixed compute—led practitioners to prefer better‑trained medium‑sized models over extremely wide but undertrained ones, for many standard language modeling tasks.

Third, scaling laws inform decisions on dataset curation and data acquisition policy. If a model is data‑limited according to the scaling regime, investing in more high‑quality data will be more cost‑effective than further architecture tuning or minor parameter increases. On the other hand, when models are strongly model‑limited, efforts should shift to improving the architecture or increasing parameter budgets.

Fourth, scaling laws guide research priorities. Understanding that loss improvements scale smoothly with compute and data suggests that engineering effort might be most impactful in areas that change the constants or exponents: for instance, improving optimization to reduce the constant factors, using better tokenization or pretraining objectives that lower $L_{\infty}$, or adopting architectures that change $\alpha$ or $\beta$ to yield faster per‑resource gains. Similarly, reducing noise in data or improving pretraining tasks can reduce the asymptotic irreducible loss $L_{\infty}$, which is ultimately what matters for downstream capabilities.

Fifth, practical training budgets and timelines are affected. The steepness of the compute‑to‑gain curve means that attaining the last few fractions of loss improvement can be disproportionately expensive. Budget-constrained practitioners can use the scaling laws to choose the Pareto-efficient point—an allocation where any further loss reduction would cost much more compute per unit gain than alternative uses.

Finally, scaling laws relate to safety and societal considerations. When gains in capability scale predictably with compute and data, it becomes feasible (modulo access and cost) for different actors to forecast when certain levels of capability will be reachable. This enables planning for policy, governance, and risk assessment, but it also highlights that current trends motivate investment in public data and compute governance to manage potential risks.

# Empirical evaluation and limits of scaling predictions

While scaling laws have proven powerful and predictive across many experiments, they are empirical approximations with important limitations. A careful practitioner should understand both how and where they work and where they break down.

One limitation is domain and architecture dependence. The fitted exponents $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$, and even the appropriateness of the separable additive form, depend on architecture family (e.g., transformers vs recurrent networks), training objective, tokenization, sequence length, and the data distribution. Values reported by Kaplan, Chinchilla, and subsequent work are specific to their settings: decoder‑only transformer architectures trained with standard next‑token prediction on web text, specific model widths/depths, and particular optimizers. Different architectures or pretraining tasks can produce different exponents and constants.

Another limitation is regime changes. Scaling laws typically hold within a regime where the same failure modes dominate (e.g., parameter underfitting vs data-sampling error). When crossing into a new regime—extremely small models, extremely small datasets, or very large models where new phenomena emerge—the power-law behavior can change. For example, at very high parameter counts one may encounter optimization instabilities, new forms of overfitting, or fundamentally different generalization behavior; at very large datasets, data redundancy or noisy labels may alter the effective data exponent $\beta$.

Finite-sample and finite-precision effects also matter. Many empirical scaling experiments assume infinite-precision arithmetic or ignore numerical instability. In practice, training very large models in lower precision (e.g., fp16, bfloat16) can introduce differing noise and optimizer behavior that shift constants slightly, and very small batch sizes or microarchitectural constraints can change the effective compute per token constant.

Another important consideration is the quality and distribution of data. Scaling laws are typically fit on mixed web-scale corpora whose effective signal-to-noise ratio is a certain level. If you change to higher-quality curated data, the asymptotic irreducible loss $L_{\infty}$ may be lower and the effective $\beta$ may be different. Conversely, adding redundant or low-quality data may produce diminishing returns sooner than the law predicts.

Overfitting and evaluation metric choices are also practical limits. The separable model assumes that validation loss is a smooth function of $N$ and $D$. But when the validation set overlaps with training data or is not representative, measurements will be biased. Moreover, loss (cross-entropy) is a proxy for downstream capabilities; a small change in loss may correspond to negligible changes in some downstream tasks yet large changes in others. Thus, using loss to extrapolate capability improvements should be done with caution.

Finally, the danger of extrapolation should be emphasized. Scaling laws interpolate well within ranges that are densely sampled by data; extrapolating many orders of magnitude beyond observed experiments may be unreliable. For instance, projecting how large models trained with vastly more compute will behave can miss emergent phenomena (e.g., qualitatively new capabilities) that are not captured by smoothing power-law fits to earlier single-step metrics.

Despite these limits, careful empirical evaluation across controlled sweeps, ablation studies, and cross-validation of fits can make scaling-law-based predictions useful and actionable. Researchers should report confidence intervals, check robustness to changes in data and architecture, and be transparent about the ranges over which they fit their laws.

# Interpreting equivalence statements across different precision or architectures

A common claim in the literature and engineering practice is that two configurations—differing in numeric precision, architectural details, or hardware—are "equivalent" if they produce the same loss or the same compute-to-performance tradeoff. Interpreting such equivalence statements requires care: equivalence can mean different things depending on which variables are held fixed and which are allowed to vary.

One notion is performance equivalence: two models are equivalent if, for the same compute budget, they achieve the same validation loss $L$. Under this view, an architecture A and B are equivalent for a particular compute regime if $L_A(C) \approx L_B(C)$ over that range. This kind of equivalence is directly relevant for choosing an architecture given fixed compute; it is a pragmatic comparison but says little about internal representations or other qualitative properties.

Another notion is resource equivalence: two setups are equivalent if they achieve the same performance while using the same wall‑clock time, power consumption, or monetary cost on a particular hardware stack. For instance, training with lower precision arithmetic (fp16) may reduce memory and compute per operation sufficiently that, for a fixed blend of hardware and software, a larger model can be trained to the same loss faster than with fp32. In this sense, precision choices affect the effective compute constant $k$ in $C = kND$, shifting the tradeoff curves but not necessarily the exponents.

A third notion is sample complexity equivalence: two architectures are equivalent if they require the same number of tokens $D$ to reach a given loss for fixed parameter count $N$. This concept captures differences in how data‑efficient architectures are. If architecture B uses a more data-efficient inductive bias, it will have a smaller $a_D$ or larger $\beta$, yielding faster gains with $D$.

When precision or hardware changes, the practical effect is usually to change constant factors rather than exponents. For example, using bfloat16 instead of fp32 can halve memory usage, enabling a larger $N$ for the same hardware, or increase the effective throughput so that the same compute budget buys more parameter-updates per wall-clock hour. In terms of scaling laws, these changes typically alter the proportionality constant $k$ in $C = k N D$ or the multiplicative constants $a_N$, $a_D$ in the loss law, but do not fundamentally change $\alpha$ and $\beta$ unless the optimization dynamics or model expressivity are significantly altered by precision effects.

Architectural differences can have deeper effects. A new architecture might alter the way representation capacity scales with parameter count—changing $\alpha$—or might use parameters more efficiently so that the constants $a_N$ and $a_D$ drop. For example, introducing sparse or mixture-of-experts architectures changes the effective number of active parameters per token; these setups can change the functional relationship between nominal parameter count and actual capacity, so naive comparisons by raw parameter count may be misleading. In such cases, equivalence should be interpreted with respect to an effective parameter count or an appropriate measure of capacity.

It is also important to consider optimizer and hyperparameter interactions. An architecture might appear superior under one optimizer and inferior under another. Similarly, training longer with better learning rate schedules can change the observed scaling. Hence equivalence statements must condition on the complete training pipeline: optimizer, learning rate schedule, batch size, regularization, and evaluation protocol.

Finally, when authors report "equivalent" outcomes across different precisions or architectures, always ask: what is being held constant? If they fix wall-clock time, they implicitly fold hardware/software efficiency into the comparison. If they fix FLOPs, they emphasize theoretical compute cost rather than practical throughput. Good reporting practices should state which axes were controlled and provide raw measurements (FLOPs, wall‑clock hours, tokens seen, validation loss) so that others can interpret equivalence in the intended sense.

Concluding remarks

Scaling laws provide a compact and empirically grounded framework to reason about how language model performance depends on model size, data quantity, and compute. They give prescriptive guidance—most notably, how to trade off parameters and tokens for a fixed compute budget—and reveal that, in many settings, investing more in data can yield higher returns than simply enlarging models. At the same time, they are empirical approximations valid within certain regimes and conditioned on architecture, data quality, and training procedures. Thoughtful application of scaling-law insights, combined with careful empirical validation in one's specific context, can substantially improve the efficiency and effectiveness of model development and help navigate the cost/benefit decisions that arise when building large language models.
