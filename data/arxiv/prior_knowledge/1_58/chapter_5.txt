Introduction

Quantization is the process of mapping continuous-valued quantities (real numbers) into a finite set of discrete values. In the context of neural networks, quantization reduces the precision of weights and activations so that computation and storage can be performed with lower-bit representations. This reduces memory footprint, bandwidth, and often power consumption, enabling deployment on edge devices and improving inference throughput on specialized hardware. However, quantization is an approximation: lower-precision representations introduce error, and naive quantization can degrade model accuracy. This chapter introduces the core principles used to quantize neural networks, explains common design choices, and provides the mathematical tools you need to reason about quantization schemes used in research papers and production systems.

We begin by contrasting floating-point and fixed-point representations, because quantization is often described as converting from floating-point (usually FP32) to lower-precision formats. We then explore uniform and non-uniform quantization strategies, explain the role of scale and zero-point and the distinction between symmetric and asymmetric mappings, and describe practical decisions such as per-tensor vs per-channel quantization. Finally, we discuss rounding, clipping, and quantitative measures of quantization error, and explain why activations and weights often require different handling due to differing dynamic ranges and deployment constraints.

# Floating point (FP32, FP16, BF16) vs fixed-point representations

Floating-point and fixed-point are two fundamentally different ways to represent real numbers in finite precision. Floating-point formats represent numbers using a sign bit, an exponent field, and a significand (mantissa) field. This design provides a large dynamic range: floating-point can represent very small and very large numbers, and it does so with relative precision that scales with the magnitude of the number. Fixed-point formats instead allocate a fixed number of bits for the integer part and the fractional part (either explicitly or via an implied scaling factor), which gives a constant absolute precision across the representable range but a smaller dynamic range compared to floating-point with the same bit width.

The common floating-point formats encountered in machine learning are:

- FP32 (single precision): 32 bits total, with 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 mantissa bits. FP32 has high precision (about 7 decimal digits) and a large dynamic range (exponent bias 127). FP32 is the standard format for training and for many inference systems.

- FP16 (IEEE half precision): 16 bits total, with 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits, and 10 mantissa bits. FP16 reduces precision compared to FP32 (about 3–4 decimal digits) and has a smaller dynamic range. It is often used to speed up training and inference with hardware that supports fast half-precision arithmetic.

- BF16 (bfloat16): 16 bits total, with 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits (same as FP32), and 7 mantissa bits. BF16 preserves the dynamic range of FP32 but has less mantissa precision. This makes BF16 attractive for training because it avoids overflow/underflow issues while reducing precision cost.

Floating-point formats are convenient for both training and inference because they represent numbers approximately proportional to their magnitude; error introduced by floating-point rounding is relative. However, floating-point operations are somewhat more complex and costly in hardware than integer arithmetic, and lower-bit floating formats still consume more bits than many integer quantization schemes.

Fixed-point representations (or integer representations with an explicit scaling factor) are central to quantization for inference. A fixed-point number can be represented as an integer $n$ together with a scale factor $s$ that maps the integer to a real value: $x \approx s \cdot n$. Often the integer is stored in an $b$-bit signed or unsigned format (e.g., 8-bit signed integers in the range $[-128,127]$). The key property is that fixed-point provides uniform spacing between representable values: adjacent representable values differ by exactly $s$ (the least significant bit in real value terms). Compared to floating point of the same bit width, fixed-point has a much smaller dynamic range but constant absolute precision. This uniform spacing makes integer arithmetic efficient and deterministic, which is attractive for hardware accelerators and inference pipelines.

When quantizing neural networks we typically convert floating-point weights and activations to lower-precision fixed-point/integer representations, or to low-precision floating-point values (e.g., FP16 or BF16). Choosing fixed-point integer quantization (e.g., 8-bit integers) is common because hardware can implement fast matrix multiply–accumulate (MAC) with integer arithmetic and because integer memory traffic is cheaper. The remainder of this chapter focuses mostly on integer/fixed-point quantization because it is the dominant technique for aggressive precision reduction for inference.

# Uniform vs non-uniform quantization

Quantization schemes differ mainly in how they choose the discrete set of representable values and how they map continuous inputs to those values. A uniform quantizer places representable values at equal spacing across an interval. In contrast, non-uniform quantizers place representable values unevenly, typically to provide finer granularity where inputs are more likely or where error is more harmful.

A uniform (linear) quantizer for a real-valued variable $x$ typically defines a scale $s>0$ and possibly a zero-point $z$ (discussed below), and maps $x$ to an integer $q$ by
$$
q = \mathrm{clamp}\big( \mathrm{round}\big(\tfrac{x}{s} + z\big), q_\mathrm{min}, q_\mathrm{max}\big).
$$
The real-valued reconstruction is $\hat{x} = s\,(q - z)$ (if using zero-point). Uniform quantization is simple, efficient, and aligns well with integer arithmetic: multiply–accumulate operations on quantized integers map to scaled integer arithmetic with a single scale factor applied at the end or fused into the next layer. Many hardware accelerators are designed for uniform quantization because of these efficiency properties.

Non-uniform quantizers include logarithmic quantization, k-means (vector) quantization, and companding schemes like $\mu$-law or A-law. A logarithmic quantizer uses spacing that grows proportionally with magnitude, which matches the perceptual properties of some signals and provides relative error control. K-means quantization clusters the values of a tensor and represents each original value by its cluster centroid; this can be very effective for weights when the distribution is multi-modal. Companding first transforms the input with a nonlinear function (e.g., $\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(1 + \mu|x|)$), applies uniform quantization in the transformed domain, and then applies the inverse transformation when reconstructing. These schemes can achieve lower distortion for a given bit budget when the input distribution is highly non-uniform or has heavy tails.

Non-uniform quantization often yields better accuracy at very low bit widths (e.g., 2–4 bits), but it is more complex to implement efficiently at inference time. It may require table lookups, per-element transforms, or additional hardware support. Therefore, uniform quantization is the mainstream choice for 8-bit quantization in production systems, while non-uniform methods are explored in research and specialized accelerators.

# Scale and zero-point, symmetric vs asymmetric quantization

A quantitative description of many practical quantizers is the affine, or "scale and zero-point", mapping between real values and integer representations. Let $x$ be a real number and $q$ be the quantized integer. An affine quantizer uses a positive scale $s$ and an integer zero-point $z$ such that
$$
q = \mathrm{round}\big(\tfrac{x}{s} + z\big),
$$
with $q$ clamped to an integer range $[q_\mathrm{min}, q_\mathrm{max}]$, and the reconstructed value is
$$
\hat{x} = s\,(q - z).
$$
The scale $s$ controls the step size: two adjacent integer values correspond to real values that differ by $s$. The zero-point $z$ is the integer code that corresponds to real value zero. Zero-point makes it possible to represent zero exactly (if within range) and to use unsigned integer ranges (e.g., $[0,255]$) for tensors that can take both positive and negative values.

Symmetric quantization is a common simplification where $z$ is fixed to represent zero symmetrically: often $z=0$ for signed integer representations, and the quantization range is symmetric about zero (e.g., $q \in [-127,127]$ or $[-128,127]$ depending on convention). For symmetric quantization, we choose a scale $s$ based on the maximum absolute value:
$$
s = \frac{\max(|x|)}{q_\mathrm{max}},
$$
and set $z=0$. Reconstruction becomes $\hat{x} = s \, q$. Symmetric quantization simplifies the math (no offset), can be faster on hardware because it avoids additional additions for zero-point during inner-product accumulation, and is often sufficient for weights that are roughly zero-centered.

Asymmetric quantization allows $z$ to be nonzero. This is useful when the data range is not symmetric about zero, for example for activations like ReLU outputs that are non-negative. For unsigned integer ranges, asymmetric quantization maps the lower real bound $x_\mathrm{min}$ to $q_\mathrm{min}$ and the upper bound $x_\mathrm{max}$ to $q_\mathrm{max}$, giving
$$
s = \frac{x_\mathrm{max} - x_\mathrm{min}}{q_\mathrm{max} - q_\mathrm{min}},\qquad
z = \mathrm{round}\left(q_\mathrm{min} - \frac{x_\mathrm{min}}{s}\right).
$$
Then zero maps to $q = \mathrm{round}(\tfrac{0}{s} + z) = z$. The asymmetric formulation reduces quantization error when the dynamic range of the data is not symmetric and when you need to use an unsigned integer format for storage.

Which is better—symmetric or asymmetric—depends on the tensor type and hardware. Activations after ReLU often benefit from unsigned asymmetric quantization because their range is $[0, r_{\max}]$. Weights are often symmetric about zero and can be quantized symmetrically. Using asymmetric quantization for weights incurs a tiny additional overhead because integer arithmetic must handle the nonzero zero-point: inner products become
$$
\sum_i (s_w(q_{w,i}-z_w))\,(s_a(q_{a,i}-z_a))
= s_w s_a \left(\sum_i q_{w,i} q_{a,i} - z_a \sum_i q_{w,i} - z_w \sum_i q_{a,i} + N z_w z_a\right),
$$
where $N$ is the length of the dot product. The extra terms involving sums of $q$ values can be handled efficiently but require care in implementation. For performance reasons, symmetric quantization (zero-point zero) simplifies the arithmetic and is therefore popular for weight quantization on many accelerators.

# Per-tensor and per-channel quantization

Scale and zero-point can be computed globally for an entire tensor (per-tensor quantization) or separately for slices of the tensor (per-channel quantization). Per-tensor quantization uses a single scale $s$ (and possibly a single zero-point $z$) for the whole tensor. In contrast, per-channel quantization assigns separate scales (and zero-points) to each output channel of a weight tensor or to each input channel in some schemes. For example, a convolutional layer with weights $W$ of shape $(C_\text{out}, C_\text{in}, K_h, K_w)$ can be quantized per output channel: for each $c \in \{1,\dots,C_\text{out}\}$ compute a scale $s_c$ based on the distribution of $W[c,:,:,:]$, and quantize that slice with its own scale.

Per-channel quantization reduces quantization error when different channels have widely varying dynamic ranges. Neural network weights and gradients often have a channel-wise structure: some filters may have much smaller magnitude than others. If you use a single scale per tensor, channels with small magnitude lose precision because the scale is driven by the largest-magnitude channel. Per-channel scales keep the representational precision matched to each channel, improving accuracy, especially for weights. Per-channel quantization is widely used for weight tensors and is a major reason why 8-bit quantized models can match FP32 accuracy in many tasks.

Per-channel quantization requires that the hardware or runtime supports different scales (and possibly zero-points) per channel. For convolution and fully connected layers, this is usually straightforward because the scale factors can be applied to the per-channel outputs after accumulation or precomputed into output bias terms. Per-tensor quantization is simpler to implement and may be required on hardware that expects a single scale per tensor.

Activations are typically quantized per-tensor rather than per-channel because per-channel activation quantization would require applying different scales across spatial dimensions or channels during runtime which adds complexity and runtime overhead. However, some schemes do use per-channel activation quantization in specialized accelerators or for very low-bit quantization where accuracy demands it.

# Rounding, clipping, and quantization error measures (MSE, max error)

Quantization comprises two irreversible operations: rounding and clipping. Given a chosen representable interval $[x_\mathrm{min}, x_\mathrm{max}]$ and step size $s$, values outside the interval must be clipped to the nearest representable boundary. Values inside the interval are rounded to the nearest discrete level (or some other rounding scheme). These operations introduce noise and bias.

Rounding modes matter. The most common is nearest rounding: $q = \mathrm{round}(\tfrac{x}{s})$. Other modes include round-toward-zero, stochastic rounding (where rounding is randomized with probability proportional to the fractional part), and floor/ceil. Stochastic rounding can reduce bias accumulation during training and has been used to train networks with very low precision, but it is harder to implement deterministically in inference.

Clipping or saturation is the mapping of values outside the quantizer range to the nearest representable boundary:
$$
q = \mathrm{clamp}\big(\mathrm{round}(\tfrac{x}{s} + z), q_\mathrm{min}, q_\mathrm{max}\big).
$$
Clipping creates large errors for outliers: if a small fraction of values are very large, the scale $s$ must increase to accommodate them and therefore reduce precision for the majority of values. Choosing whether to enlarge the dynamic range to include outliers or to clip them (and accept occasional large errors) is an important practical design choice. Many calibration methods aim to choose ranges that balance clipping-induced distortion and quantization step size.

To assess the effect of quantization, several error metrics are commonly used. Mean squared error (MSE) measures the average squared difference between original and reconstructed values:
$$
\mathrm{MSE} = \mathbb{E}\big[(x - \hat{x})^2\big],
$$
where the expectation is taken over a representative dataset or tensor elements. MSE is convenient because it is differentiable (in the continuous approximation) and closely related to signal-to-quantization-noise ratio. Minimizing MSE often leads to the well-known optimal Lloyd-Max quantizer for scalar distributions.

Maximum absolute error (max error) measures the worst-case absolute deviation:
$$
\mathrm{MaxError} = \sup_x |x - \hat{x}|.
$$
This metric emphasizes the largest deviation and is sensitive to outliers. In some contexts, guarantees on worst-case error are important (for safety-critical systems), but for neural network accuracy the average-case MSE or distribution-weighted metrics are usually more relevant.

Another useful metric for quantization is signal-to-quantization-noise ratio (SQNR), defined as
$$
\mathrm{SQNR} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[x^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(x-\hat{x})^2]}\right)\ \text{dB}.
$$
Higher SQNR indicates better fidelity. SQNR analysis under uniform quantization and for certain signal models helps predict how bit width affects representational quality.

Beyond these simple metrics, practitioners also measure end-to-end model accuracy (e.g., classification accuracy, BLEU score), layerwise activation distributions after quantization, and distributional mismatch between training and inference. Layerwise measures can identify particularly sensitive layers where higher precision or special handling is required.

# Quantization of activations vs weights and dynamic ranges

Weights and activations have different characteristics that affect quantization strategy. Weights are fixed after training and can be analyzed offline: you can compute per-channel scales, cluster weight distributions, or apply quantization-aware fine-tuning to adjust the weights to compensate for quantization error. For weights, per-channel symmetric quantization is common and very effective. Because weight tensors are static, more complex quantization schemes (e.g., k-means, per-channel non-uniform) can be acceptable since quantization happens once at model conversion time.

Activations are dynamic: their range depends on input data and on layer behavior. Activation distributions may change with different inputs and can be skewed or heavy-tailed. Therefore, activation quantization typically uses calibration on a representative dataset or dynamic range estimation at runtime. Common strategies include:

- Static calibration: run representative inputs through the model in floating point to collect activation ranges per tensor or per layer, then fix scales for inference. Calibration commonly chooses $x_\mathrm{min}$ and $x_\mathrm{max}$ as some percentile of observed activation distributions (e.g., 99.99th percentile) to avoid letting occasional outliers inflate the scale and reduce precision. The chosen percentile is a hyperparameter that trades off clipping vs quantization noise.

- Dynamic quantization: compute scales at runtime for each activation tensor using a small number of batched statistics (e.g., running max or exponential moving average). This adapts to input distribution shifts but incurs runtime overhead and may complicate deployment on deterministic hardware.

- Learned quantization: insert quantization parameters as learnable variables and fine-tune the network (quantization-aware training) so that activations (and/or weights) shift their distributions to be more quantization-friendly. Approaches like “fake quantization” during training simulate integer quantization in the forward pass while keeping gradients flowing in floating point.

Activations after ReLU have a natural lower bound at zero and typically are non-negative with a skewed distribution; asymmetric unsigned quantization is appropriate in this case. Activations may also be bursty and have outliers; clipping strategies and per-tensor scales are used to contain the dynamic range.

Weights, by contrast, are often roughly zero-centered and can be quantized symmetrically. Per-channel quantization of weights is particularly helpful because convolution filters vary in magnitude across output channels. Quantization-aware training often focuses on weights as well, but because weights are static it is feasible to use per-channel and even non-uniform quantization for weights.

When performing integer inference, accumulators must be able to represent the sum of products of quantized values without overflow. For example, when using 8-bit integers for inputs and weights, the dot products can produce sums that require more bits (e.g., 32-bit accumulators). The overall scale of the result is the product of the input and weight scales, and a final rescaling or requantization is needed to bring the result back into the target integer range for the following layer. The dynamic range of activations therefore affects not just the quantization error of that layer but also the accumulator requirements and numerical stability across layers.

Finally, different layers have different sensitivity to quantization. The first and last layers are often the most sensitive, and practitioners sometimes keep them at higher precision (e.g., INT16 or FP32) or use special quantization parameters. Attention layers, layer normalization, and softmax can also be more sensitive and may require higher precision or careful implementation to preserve model quality.

Conclusion

Quantization is a rich design space balancing representational fidelity, runtime efficiency, hardware constraints, and model accuracy. Floating-point formats (FP32, FP16, BF16) offer different trade-offs of precision and range and are often the starting point for quantization. Fixed-point integer quantization with a scale and zero-point is the dominant approach for efficient inference, with choices such as symmetric vs asymmetric mapping and per-tensor vs per-channel granularity driving accuracy and implementation complexity. Uniform quantization is hardware-friendly and widely used, but non-uniform schemes can offer better fidelity at extreme compression. Rounding and clipping are the mechanisms that introduce error, and metrics like MSE, max error, and SQNR quantify the impact. Finally, weights and activations differ in their properties and therefore in how they are quantized: weights are static and amenable to per-channel and specialized schemes, while activations are dynamic and require calibration or runtime adaptation. Understanding these fundamentals will allow you to read research on quantization, implement common schemes, and make informed decisions for deploying neural networks on constrained hardware.
