Introduction

The Transformer architecture has become the dominant building block for modern large language models (LLMs) and many other sequence-processing systems. Its success stems from a flexible, modular design that uses attention to model interactions between tokens, feed-forward networks to build complex representations, and normalization and residual mechanisms to stabilize training. In this chapter we will unpack the core components of Transformer blocks and explore common architectural choices and variants that practitioners use in building and scaling LLMs. The goal is to give you an intuitive and technical understanding so you can read research papers and reason about design trade-offs.

We begin by describing the self-attention mechanism and the scaled dot-product formulation that underlies it. We then explain multi-head attention and the role of linear projections for keys, queries, and values. Positional information is essential for sequence tasks, so we cover positional embeddings and the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) approach. Next we describe the feed-forward sublayers (MLPs) and modern gated activation variants such as GLU and SwiGLU that improve expressivity. Normalization layers — primarily LayerNorm and RMSNorm — are explained with intuition for why they help and how they differ. We then examine residual connections, bias terms, and common ablation experiments that reveal which components are critical. Finally, we discuss model scaling: how width, depth, and embedding size affect capacity, computation, and training dynamics.

# Self-attention mechanism and scaled dot-product attention

Self-attention is the core operation that lets a Transformer compute interactions between all positions in a sequence. The idea is simple: to compute a representation for each position, the model forms a weighted sum of representations at all positions, where the weights depend on pairwise compatibility between positions. If the input token representations are $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ for sequence length $N$ and embedding dimension $d$, then self-attention computes for each position $i$ an output
y_i = \sum_{j=1}^N \alpha_{ij} v_j,
where $v_j$ is a value vector for position $j$ and $\alpha_{ij}$ are attention weights that sum to one over $j$.

The most common parameterization is scaled dot-product attention. We produce three linear projections from the input $X$: queries $Q = X W_Q$, keys $K = X W_K$, and values $V = X W_V$, where $W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}$ (often $d_k = d$ or $d_k = d / h$ in multi-head settings). The attention weights from query $i$ to key $j$ are computed by the dot product between the query and key, scaled and normalized by softmax:
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.
Concretely, the matrix of attention weights $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is given by $A = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)$ applied row-wise, and the output is $A V$.

There are several pieces to unpack here. First, the dot product $q_i^\top k_j$ measures compatibility: larger values mean the token at $i$ should pay more attention to token $j$. Second, the scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$ is essential to prevent the dot products from growing large in magnitude as the dimensionality increases; without scaling, the softmax can push the distribution into extremely peaky regions where gradients vanish. Intuitively, if $q_i$ and $k_j$ are random vectors with variance roughly one per dimension, then their dot product has variance proportional to $d_k$. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the magnitude stable as $d_k$ changes.

Finally, using softmax yields a convex combination of values, which has two important consequences: it is differentiable (so it can be learned end-to-end), and it normalizes the weights so that the output remains on a similar scale as the values. In practice, attention is often masked to enforce causal structure (preventing tokens from attending to future tokens during autoregressive generation) or to apply padding masks.

Beyond the basic scaled dot-product, people sometimes add bias terms, relative positional biases, or other scalings. But the core idea is always to compute content-dependent pairwise weights and form weighted sums of value vectors.

# Multi-head attention and key/query/value projections

Multi-head attention extends single-head attention by computing multiple independent attention "heads" and concatenating their outputs. The motivation is to allow the model to attend to different types of information or different positions simultaneously. Formally, with $h$ heads, we partition or project the model dimension $d$ into subspaces of dimension $d_k$ (often $d_k = d / h$). For head $\ell$ we compute
Q_\ell = X W_Q^{(\ell)},\quad K_\ell = X W_K^{(\ell)},\quad V_\ell = X W_V^{(\ell)},
and attention output for the head is
H_\ell = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_\ell K_\ell^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V_\ell.
The multi-head output is then
\text{MHA}(X) = [H_1, H_2, \dots, H_h] W_O,
where $W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ recombines the concatenated heads into a $d$-dimensional result.

There are two common parameterizations for the projection matrices. One is to learn separate $W_Q^{(\ell)}$, $W_K^{(\ell)}$, and $W_V^{(\ell)}$ for each head; the other is to implement them as single large weight matrices $W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, $W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, $W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and then split the resulting $d$-dimensional projections into $h$ chunks. Both are mathematically equivalent when $d_k = d/h$ and the large matrices are block-diagonal in their initialization, but implementation differences can affect parameter sharing and sparsity.

Why multiple heads help is partly empirical and partly intuitive. Each head has its own projection subspace, so it can specialize: one head might focus on short-range syntactic dependencies, another on long-range coreference, and another on certain token types. Multi-head attention also increases the model's expressivity by learning multiple linear maps in parallel and allowing the output projection $W_O$ to mix them.

One practical caveat is that increasing the number of heads while keeping $d$ fixed decreases $d_k$, which can reduce per-head capacity to represent fine-grained relationships. Some modern architectures decouple head count and head dimension choices (e.g., using larger $d$ to support many heads while keeping $d_k$ above a threshold).

Key, query, and value projection biases are design choices. Some implementations include biases in the linear layers (e.g., $X W_Q + b_Q$); others omit them for simplicity and minor computational savings. Empirically, biases often make little difference at scale, but they can matter in smaller models or early training. We'll discuss bias ablations later in the chapter.

# Position embeddings and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE)

Self-attention is permutation-invariant: if you permute the input tokens and their positional encodings are not provided, the attention mechanism alone cannot distinguish token order. To give the model access to token positions, Transformers incorporate positional information.

The simplest approach is absolute positional embeddings: add a learned vector $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ for position $i$ to the token embedding $x_i$, so the input to the Transformer becomes $x_i + p_i$. Learned positional embeddings are flexible and simple, and they work well for many tasks. Another common approach is sinusoidal positional encodings where $p_i$ is a deterministic function of $i$, such as using sinusoids of different frequencies. Sinusoidal encodings have the advantage of extrapolation: they can generalize to sequence lengths not seen during training because the encoding is a fixed function of position.

Relative positional encodings address the fact that in many tasks the model cares more about relative distances (e.g., "the token 3 positions to the left") than absolute positions. Relative encodings can be added as biases to attention scores or incorporated into the key/query/value transformations, giving the attention mechanism information about pairwise relative positions.

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) provide a way to inject relative position information directly into the dot-product between queries and keys by rotating subcomponents of the query and key vectors by position-dependent angles. The core idea is to apply a position-dependent orthogonal transformation $R_i$ to the raw query or key projection at position $i$. Let $q_i$ be the projected query for position $i$ before positional modification. In RoPE, we replace $q_i$ with $R_i q_i$ and similarly $k_j$ with $R_j k_j$. Because $R_i^\top R_j$ depends on the relative offset $(j-i)$, the dot product $(R_i q_i)^\top (R_j k_j) = q_i^\top (R_i^\top R_j) k_j$ implicitly contains relative position terms. A common implementation partitions each $d_k$-dimensional projection into pairs and applies 2D rotations with frequency-dependent angles derived from position $i$.

RoPE has several desirable properties. It preserves the linearity and scale of the dot product, it is fast to compute, and it allows natural extrapolation to longer sequence lengths than seen in training by evaluating $R_i$ at larger $i$. In practice, RoPE has been found to improve autoregressive modeling and is used in many modern LLMs. Compared to learned absolute embeddings, RoPE gives attention the ability to compare relative positions directly in the compatibility score without adding many extra parameters.

A practical implementation detail: when using RoPE and multi-head attention, the rotation is applied at the per-head projection level, and one must ensure the embedding dimension per head is compatible with the pairwise rotation partitioning (e.g., even dimensionality for pairing).

# Feed-forward networks (MLP) and gated activations (GLU, SwiGLU)

Each Transformer block contains, in addition to multi-head attention, a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN) or MLP that independently processes each token's representation. The standard FFN used in the original Transformer is a two-layer MLP with a nonlinearity:
\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \,\phi(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2,
where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff} \times d}$ expands the representation to a larger hidden size $d_{ff}$ (the "intermediate" or "FFN" dimension), $\phi$ is typically the GELU activation, and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{ff}}$ projects back to dimension $d$. The expansion ratio $d_{ff}/d$ is a key design choice; commonly used ratios are 4, 4.5, or higher in large models, which increases the MLP's capacity and parameter count significantly.

Gated linear units (GLU) and their modern variants introduce multiplicative gating to improve expressivity and training. A simple GLU is
\text{GLU}(x) = (W_a x) \odot \sigma(W_b x),
where $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication and $\sigma$ is a sigmoid gating function. This structure allows the network to modulate information flow: the gate $\sigma(W_b x)$ can selectively pass or suppress components of $W_a x$. GLU-style blocks can be placed instead of the standard $\phi(W_1 x)$ nonlinearity.

SwiGLU and related variants combine the benefits of GELU and gating. SwiGLU uses the activation
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \text{SiLU}(W_a x) \odot (W_b x),
where SiLU is the "swish" activation $\text{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \text{sigmoid}(z)$. In practice, SwiGLU is implemented by computing two linear projections to dimension $d_{ff}/2$ (or half of the FFN hidden dimension), applying the SiLU to one and multiplying elementwise with the other, and then projecting back to $d$ with $W_2$. Empirically, SwiGLU and related gated activations offer improved training speed and final performance compared to plain GELU for many transformer variants because they introduce multiplicative interactions that can represent complex functions more compactly.

From an intuition standpoint, gated activations help the model learn conditional computation: for each input token, different components can be turned on or off. This is particularly useful in language modeling where specific syntactic or semantic patterns may require different transformations.

When implementing MLPs with gating, the parameterization often doubles one of the projections (e.g., $W_1$ becomes two matrices $W_a$ and $W_b$), which increases parameter count slightly, but this is often offset by better parameter efficiency (better performance for the same parameter budget) because of improved representational power.

# Normalization layers: LayerNorm and RMSNorm

Normalization is a key ingredient in deep neural networks that stabilizes and accelerates training by controlling the distribution of activations. Transformers commonly use normalization before the sublayer (pre-norm) or after the sublayer (post-norm). Pre-norm (applying normalization before attention or MLP) tends to yield more stable gradients for very deep Transformers and is now the standard in many architectures.

Layer normalization (LayerNorm) operates across the feature dimension for each token independently. Given an input vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (a single token embedding), LayerNorm computes
\mu = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d x_i,\quad \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - \mu)^2 + \epsilon},
\text{LayerNorm}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} + \beta,
where $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned per-feature scaling and bias parameters, and $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. LayerNorm centers and rescales features to zero mean and unit variance, then allows the network to learn an affine transformation. This reduces covariate shift across layers and ensures gradients flow through deep stacks.

RMS normalization (RMSNorm) is a related variant that drops mean centering. Instead of subtracting the mean, RMSNorm divides by the root-mean-square of the activations:
\text{RMS}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2 + \epsilon},
\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x}{\text{RMS}(x)}.
RMSNorm has slightly lower computational cost (no subtraction) and slightly fewer learned parameters if biases are not used, while still controlling scale. Empirically, RMSNorm can match or approach LayerNorm's performance in many settings and is often used in large-scale models because of its simplicity and numerical properties.

Why normalize? Without normalization, signals can explode or shrink through many layers, making optimization difficult. Normalization ensures that the magnitude of activations entering subsequent layers is controlled, which helps optimizer hyperparameters (like learning rates) be more robust. The choice between LayerNorm and RMSNorm is often empirical: LayerNorm offers mean-centering which can be beneficial when feature means carry unwanted drift, while RMSNorm can be slightly more stable in certain large-scale regimes.

One should also be mindful of where normalization is applied: pre-norm setups apply normalization before attention and MLP (i.e., norm -> sublayer -> add residual), while post-norm applies it after (sublayer -> add residual -> norm). Pre-norm tends to be preferable for deep stacks because it reduces gradient path length variability.

# Residual connections, bias terms and common architectural ablations

Residual connections, introduced in ResNets and adopted by Transformers, add the input of a sublayer directly to its output:
\text{Output} = x + \text{Sublayer}(\text{Norm}(x)).
Residuals enable gradient flow across many layers and allow each sublayer to learn corrections on top of its input rather than needing to produce a full transformation from scratch. In Transformers, there are typically two residual paths per block: one around the attention sublayer and one around the MLP sublayer. These residuals, combined with normalization, are essential to training stability and are widely regarded as indispensable in deep Transformers.

Bias terms in linear layers are sometimes omitted at scale. For example, many large models use bias-free attention projections and MLPs, relying instead on LayerNorm's learned offset $\beta$ and other components. The rationale is that biases add parameters and small complexity but often little benefit when the model and dataset are large. Ablation studies in papers often show that removing biases produces only a marginal difference in final performance for very large models, though for smaller models or for certain tasks biases can matter. Including biases may also interact with how embeddings and logits are tied, so choices are not always independent.

Architectural ablations are systematic experiments where one removes or modifies components to see their effect. Some common ablations and findings in Transformers:

- Removing LayerNorm or residuals usually cripples training except when a replacement mechanism is provided. Residuals and normalization together are key to deep model optimization.
- Changing pre-norm to post-norm can cause instability in deep networks; pre-norm is often preferred for deep LLMs.
- Removing biases from projections often has small effect at large scale, but can slow early convergence.
- Replacing GELU with simpler activations (ReLU, tanh) may hurt sample efficiency or final performance, but gating variants like SwiGLU often improve it.
- Reducing number of heads with constant $d$ can reduce performance because per-head dimension becomes too small; conversely, more heads with larger $d$ can help but increase compute.
- Using learned absolute positional embeddings versus RoPE or relative biases can change extrapolation behavior. Absolute learned embeddings may fail to generalize to longer contexts than seen during training, whereas RoPE or relative encodings can generalize better.

Understanding these ablations is critical when designing or modifying architectures. Many choices that seem minor can affect convergence speed, stability, generalization to longer contexts, or inference efficiency.

# Model scaling (width, depth, embedding size) and implications

Scaling a Transformer means increasing its capacity by making it wider, deeper, or increasing embedding sizes. Each axis of scaling has distinct computational and representational implications.

Increasing width: by increasing the model dimension $d$ (embedding size) or the intermediate FFN size $d_{ff}$, you allow each layer to represent richer features. Wider layers increase memory and per-layer compute roughly quadratically in some cases (e.g., attention costs scale with $d$ via projections), and they increase parameter count. Wider models often yield better single-layer expressivity and can make optimization more stable if accompanied by suitable initialization and normalization.

Increasing depth: stacking more Transformer blocks (increasing the number of layers $L$) increases the effective receptive field and allows more sequential transformations. Depth increases total computation linearly with $L$ and can exponentially increase representational capacity for some functions. However, deeper models require careful design for stability (pre-norm, residual scaling, appropriate learning rate schedules) and can suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients if normalization or residuals are poorly tuned.

Changing embedding size: the embedding size (often equal to $d$) determines the interface between input tokens and the model. Larger embeddings can capture finer-grained token properties and support larger head counts in multi-head attention. The embedding size also affects the softmax output when tied with token embeddings: if the output projection is tied to input embeddings, embedding dimension directly interacts with the output logits. Very large embeddings increase memory and can slow token embedding operations.

Scaling laws: empirical studies have found predictable scaling behaviors of model performance with parameters, dataset size, and compute. For a given compute budget, the optimal allocation between width and depth may vary. A common rule of thumb is: depth gives more compositional steps and can be critical for tasks requiring many sequential transformations, while width enables richer representations per step. Parameterizing a fixed parameter budget can be done with many shallow wide layers or many narrow deep layers; the choice depends on the task, training stability, and compute trade-offs.

Practical interactions: increasing $d$ while keeping head count $h$ fixed increases per-head dimension $d_k = d/h$, which can be beneficial for attention resolution. Increasing $h$ while keeping $d$ fixed reduces $d_k$, which can harm per-head expressivity. Increasing $d_{ff}$ (the FFN expansion) often has a large impact on parameter count because $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff} \times d}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{ff}}$ scale with $d_{ff}d$. Many modern LLMs devote a significant fraction of parameters to the FFN.

Memory and compute considerations: attention has a quadratic memory and time complexity in sequence length $N$ because of the $N \times N$ attention matrix, and it scales linearly in $d$. Thus increasing width has a smaller effect on attention time complexity than increasing sequence length. Depth multiplies time and memory linearly with the number of layers for forward and backward passes. In practice, model designers balance depth, width, and sequence length to meet compute and memory budgets.

Emergent behaviors: at larger scales, models often demonstrate qualitatively different capabilities (commonly called "emergent" skills). These behaviors are hard to predict from small-scale experiments. Consequently, scaling decisions often rely on a mix of theoretical intuition, small-scale experiments, and empirical scaling laws.

Summary and practical recommendations

The Transformer block is composed of self-attention, a position-wise MLP, normalization, and residual connections. Multi-head attention enables parallel specialized attention patterns; projections for keys, queries, and values determine how information is encoded and attended. Positional encodings—learned, sinusoidal, relative, or rotary—are crucial to provide order information; RoPE is a modern alternative that encodes relative positions through rotations. The FFN benefits from gating variants like SwiGLU which improve expressivity and efficiency. Normalization layers such as LayerNorm and RMSNorm keep training stable, and residual connections are essential for gradient flow. Many small architectural choices (biases, normalization placement, activation functions, head count) can be ablated to find efficient configurations. Finally, scaling along width, depth, and embedding size changes capacity and compute in different ways and should be guided by both hardware constraints and empirical scaling behavior.

With these concepts in hand, you should now be equipped to read and interpret research papers that propose changes to Transformer components, understand why certain design choices were made, and reason about the trade-offs involved in building and scaling modern LLMs.
