Introduction

Modern machine learning and numerical computing workloads are increasingly constrained not just by algorithmic complexity but by the hardware platforms that execute them. The choices of processor architecture, on-chip and off-chip memory hierarchy, numeric precision, and specialized accelerators profoundly affect both runtime performance and energy consumption. For students learning to read research papers in this area, it is important to develop intuition about the trade-offs and quantitative models that connect algorithmic operations (for example, matrix multiply, convolution, or elementwise activation) to energy use and throughput on different hardware. This chapter explains the architectural building blocks of common accelerators (ASICs, GPUs, TPUs and other domain-specific accelerators), presents energy cost models for arithmetic and memory accesses, discusses the dominant role of memory transfers, introduces custom hardware structures such as systolic arrays and low-precision units (LPUs), examines how integer and floating-point arithmetic differ in energy and speed, and describes methodologies to estimate and compare energy consumption across designs. The aim is to give you the intuition and simple quantitative tools necessary to interpret and critique hardware-aware ML papers and to make informed design choices.

# ASICs, GPUs, TPUs and domain-specific accelerators: architectural overview

At the highest level, modern compute platforms can be categorized by their generality and specialization. Central processing units (CPUs) are the most general-purpose; graphics processing units (GPUs) introduced massively parallel SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) execution for throughput-oriented workloads; application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) are chips custom-designed for a fixed computation; and domain-specific accelerators (DSAs) such as Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) sit between GPUs and ASICs, being specialized for a narrow class of computations (e.g., dense linear algebra, inference, or convolution). Understanding their architecture helps explain why certain operations are fast or energy-efficient on one platform and poor on another.

A GPU comprises many parallel cores organized into compute units or streaming multiprocessors. Each core supports vector or SIMT (single instruction, multiple threads) execution, with a relatively deep memory hierarchy: register files, shared on-chip scratchpad (often called "shared memory"), caches (L1, L2), and off-chip DRAM. GPUs are optimized for high throughput on data-parallel workloads: they excel at large, regular operations such as batched matrix multiplication where work can be tiled to reuse data from on-chip memory. GPUs expose many arithmetic pipelines (floating-point and integer units) and rely on high memory bandwidth to feed them. Latency hiding is a key technique: many threads are scheduled so that when some stalls on memory, others keep the arithmetic units busy.

ASICs are chips custom designed for a particular application or set of kernels. Because they are purpose-built, ASICs can remove or simplify general-purpose functionality, yielding much higher energy efficiency and area utilization. For example, an ASIC designed for inference can include many multiply-accumulate units sized and arranged specifically for the target model shapes, optimized memory hierarchies, and weight storage structures. The trade-off is reduced flexibility: a change in the model architecture or numeric format may require redesign.

TPUs represent a class of domain-specific accelerators designed specifically for tensor operations. For instance, Google’s original TPU used a large systolic array of integer multiply-accumulate (MAC) units, tight integration with weight and activation SRAM, and a software stack that maps neural network layers to tiles of the array. TPUs and similar DSAs aim to maximize effective utilization for key kernels (e.g., matrix multiply, convolution) while providing enough programmability to support a family of models. They typically use on-chip SRAM rather than relying heavily on caches, and they may provide specialized instructions (e.g., fused multiply-add, activation functions) to reduce data movement.

Other domain-specific accelerators include FPGAs (field-programmable gate arrays) when partially specialized via configurable logic, vision or audio accelerators that implement convolutional primitives in hardware, and custom NPU (neural processing unit) designs that implement quantization, compression, and sparse-dedicated paths. The design choices—such as whether to place larger register files, wider interconnects, or deeper local memory—reflect the target compute-to-memory ratio of the workloads. A crucial parameter is arithmetic intensity: the number of arithmetic operations performed per byte transferred from slower memory. Architectures intended for high arithmetic intensity tasks invest in many arithmetic units and fast on-chip memory; architectures for bandwidth-bound tasks invest in memory bandwidth and interconnect.

# Energy cost models of arithmetic operations (ADD, MUL) and memory access

Energy consumption of compute is commonly decomposed into two broad categories: energy spent performing arithmetic and logic, and energy spent moving data across the memory hierarchy. While the absolute values depend on technology node and implementation details, relative magnitudes and scaling laws can be described with simple models useful for back-of-envelope calculations.

For arithmetic, energy per operation varies with the type and precision. A basic model assigns cost constants to operations: let $E_{\text{add}}(p)$ and $E_{\text{mul}}(p)$ denote the energy to perform an addition and multiplication at precision $p$ (e.g., 8-bit integer, 16-bit float, 32-bit float). Multiplication is typically more expensive than addition because multiplier circuits involve more bit-level activity and cross-coupling. Empirical CMOS measurements often show $E_{\text{mul}}(p) \approx 2$ to $5 \times E_{\text{add}}(p)$ when both are implemented at the same bit width, though this factor depends on the multiplier design (e.g., array multiplier vs. Booth multiplier) and whether fused multiply-add (FMA) reduces overhead by producing multiply and add in a single pipeline stage. Floating-point arithmetic incurs additional cost compared to integer arithmetic of the same bit width because of exponent handling, normalization, rounding, and special-case handling (NaNs, infinities). For example, floating-point multiply-add at 32-bit (FP32) may be several times more energy-consuming than integer 32-bit multiply-add.

Memory access energy often dominates arithmetic for many realistic workloads. The energy for reading or writing data depends strongly on where the data reside. On-chip register file accesses are cheap; accessing a nearby SRAM (e.g., L1 or dedicated scratchpad) costs more; and off-chip DRAM accesses are the most expensive per bit. Let $E_{\text{reg}}$, $E_{\text{SRAM}}$, and $E_{\text{DRAM}}$ denote energy per access at these levels. Typical ratios in prior technology generations might be approximately $E_{\text{reg}} : E_{\text{SRAM}} : E_{\text{DRAM}} \approx 1 : 10 : 100$ to $1 : 10 : 1000$, but absolute numbers are technology- and implementation-dependent. What matters for algorithmic choice is the relative scale: a DRAM read can cost as much energy as thousands of arithmetic operations at low bit precision.

We can express total energy for a kernel as a sum of compute and memory terms. Suppose a kernel performs $N_{\text{add}}$ adds and $N_{\text{mul}}$ multiplies and transfers $B_{\text{reg}}$, $B_{\text{SRAM}}$, and $B_{\text{DRAM}}$ bytes at the respective levels. Then a simple energy model is
\[
E_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{add}} E_{\text{add}} + N_{\text{mul}} E_{\text{mul}} + B_{\text{reg}} E_{\text{reg}} + B_{\text{SRAM}} E_{\text{SRAM}} + B_{\text{DRAM}} E_{\text{DRAM}}.
\]
This model clarifies why minimizing data movement is often the most effective way to reduce energy: reducing $B_{\text{DRAM}}$ by caching or reusing data can save far more energy than optimizing a few additional arithmetic operations.

Energy also depends on bit toggling and switching activity. For digital circuits, energy per operation often scales approximately linearly with bit-width because activity and capacitance scale with the number of bits interacting. Thus changing precision from $w$ bits to $w/2$ bits can approximately halve the arithmetic energy and corresponding local memory energy, though actual scaling can be sublinear or superlinear depending on circuit techniques (gating, bit-sliced designs, etc.). Moreover, specialized circuits (e.g., approximate multipliers) can reduce energy further by approximating arithmetic.

# Memory transfer costs: DRAM-to-SRAM and off-chip bandwidth implications

When considering performance and energy together, memory transfers—especially between off-chip DRAM and on-chip SRAM—are frequently the bottleneck. Two key reasons are energy per bit and latency/bandwidth constraints. DRAM accesses traverse long wires and off-chip interfaces, incurring high energy and latency. Bandwidth between chip and DRAM is limited by pin count and I/O signaling rates; bandwidth limitations constrain how much data a compute unit can consume per second regardless of its raw arithmetic throughput.

From a modeling perspective, it is useful to think in terms of bandwidth $BW$ (bytes per second) and energy per byte $E_{\text{DRAM}}$. If the computation requires moving $B$ bytes from DRAM, the minimum time to transfer is $t_{\text{mem}} = B / BW$ and the energy is $E_{\text{mem}} = B \cdot E_{\text{DRAM}}$. If the arithmetic can be scheduled to overlap with memory transfers, total time could be governed by the larger of computation time and transfer time; otherwise, memory stalls can leave compute units idle.

An important derived quantity is the roofline model's arithmetic intensity: $I = \text{ops} / \text{bytes}$, i.e., number of arithmetic operations performed per byte transferred from DRAM (or per byte transferred from the next slower level). The roofline model states that achievable performance is bounded by compute bound when $I$ is high and memory bound when $I$ is low: the maximum achievable ops/sec is $\min(\text{peak compute}, I \cdot BW)$. In energy terms, arithmetic intensity determines how many operations you get per unit energy spent on memory: higher $I$ amortizes the large fixed cost of transferring weights and activations.

Consider a dense matrix multiply $C = A B$ with $A$ of shape $m\times k$ and $B$ of shape $k\times n$. Naively implemented, it requires $2 m n k$ arithmetic operations (multiply and add counted separately) and $O(m k + k n + m n)$ data reads/writes. With blocked or tiled implementations, one can load tiles of $A$ and $B$ into on-chip SRAM and perform many operations per loaded byte, increasing $I$. An efficient tiling strategy trades extra on-chip memory for reduced DRAM bandwidth: larger tiles increase reuse and raise arithmetic intensity. On hardware with limited SRAM, however, tiles must be kept small, reducing reuse and making the implementation memory-bound.

Off-chip bandwidth implications also affect algorithm design. For example, on systems with limited DRAM bandwidth, it may be preferable to quantize weights to fewer bits, compress them using pruning or weight sharing, or prefetch weights to on-chip SRAM in compressed form. Each compression or quantization scheme has its own decoding cost on the compute side. The net benefit must consider whether the reduced DRAM transfer energy and bandwidth outweigh the extra compute or on-chip memory overhead required to decompress or convert formats.

Another complication is that DRAM transfers are typically performed in bursts and are organized around rows and columns with precharge/activate cycles; transferring small, scattered items can be inefficient. Therefore, hardware/software co-design often packs data into contiguous blocks to exploit DRAM burst transfers and to reduce per-transfer overhead. Additionally, memory controllers, caches, and prefetchers can hide some latency but cannot change the fundamental bandwidth and energy cost per byte.

# Custom hardware concepts (e.g., systolic arrays, LPUs) and design trade-offs

Custom hardware introduces architectural primitives that optimize for dataflow and reuse patterns characteristic of target workloads. Two illustrative examples are systolic arrays and low-precision processing units (LPUs).

Systolic arrays are spatially-arranged processing elements (PEs) that pass data rhythmically through the array, enabling high data reuse and local communication. Each PE typically contains a small local register or SRAM, arithmetic units (e.g., multiply-add), and simple control logic. For matrix multiplication, one can feed rows of $A$ and columns of $B$ into the array so that each PE repeatedly receives operands and accumulates partial sums for elements of $C$. The main advantage of systolic arrays is that they turn global memory traffic into local, short-hop communication: each data element is sent into the array once and reused many times as it propagates, greatly reducing off-chip bandwidth requirements. Latency is predictable and the hardware can be highly pipelined, offering excellent peak utilization when shapes and dataflows match the array dimensions.

Design trade-offs for systolic arrays include area and flexibility. A large two-dimensional array consumes considerable silicon area and on-chip routing resources. It is most efficient when compute sizes map well to the array dimensions; irregular shapes or sparse computations can leave many PEs idle. Systolic arrays are also sensitive to precision: supporting many numeric formats can complicate PE design and increase area. To improve flexibility, designers may tile computations over multiple smaller arrays or include additional scalar units for leftover work, but this can complexity scheduling and reduce area efficiency.

Low-precision processing units (LPUs) are specialized units optimized for narrow-bitwidth arithmetic (for example, 8-bit integer, 4-bit, or even binary operations). LPUs reduce arithmetic and local memory energy by reducing switching capacitance and allowing denser PE packing. In addition, LPUs can enable simpler datapaths and more parallelism per unit area. Hardware may expose various precision modes: INT8, INT4, or mixed-precision where accumulations occur at higher precision to preserve numerical stability. LPUs often come with specialized features such as per-lane scaling, zero-suppression for sparse values, and bit-serial datapaths which process few bits per cycle to trade time for area and energy.

Design trade-offs for LPUs include accuracy vs. efficiency and software complexity. Lower precision reduces energy and memory but introduces quantization errors; achieving acceptable accuracy may require quantization-aware training or mixed-precision techniques, adding algorithmic complexity. Furthermore, supporting many precision modes complicates hardware design and may dilute area savings. LPUs designed for integer arithmetic may require accumulators with larger bit width to avoid overflow, and such accumulators can become hot spots for energy unless carefully architected.

Other custom concepts include near-memory computing (placing compute close to large memory banks to reduce data movement), reduction trees and specialized interconnects for collective operations (e.g., all-reduce for distributed training), accelerator fabrics composed of many small domain-specific tiles, and hardware support for sparsity (index compression, sparse PE sparsity skipping). Each choice trades area, power, latency, and programmability. For example, supporting hardware sparsity skipping reduces arithmetic and memory but requires index management and irregular scheduling hardware, which increases control complexity and can underutilize datapaths on irregular sparsity patterns.

# Impacts of integer vs floating-point arithmetic on energy and speed

The choice between integer and floating-point arithmetic has a major impact on both energy consumption and performance. Integer arithmetic (fixed-point or true integer) is simpler: it avoids exponent normalization, denormal handling, rounding modes complexity, and special-case logic for NaN/Inf. Thus, an integer multiplier or adder usually occupies less silicon area, has shorter critical paths, and consumes less energy per operation than a comparable floating-point unit of the same bit width. This is why many accelerators adopt reduced-precision integer formats (e.g., INT8, INT4) for inference.

Floating-point arithmetic provides dynamic range and ease of programming without careful scaling. For training, floating-point (e.g., FP32, FP16, bfloat16) has typically been favored because gradients and model updates can exhibit a wide dynamic range and because numerical stability is crucial. FP32 arithmetic offers high precision and dynamic range but at significant energy and area cost. To trade energy for acceptable precision, many accelerators now implement half-precision floating-point (FP16), or the alternative bfloat16 format, which keeps a wide exponent but reduces mantissa bits to reduce energy while preserving dynamic range important for training.

Comparative implications for speed arise because integer units can be much denser on silicon: given a fixed area budget, a chip can host more integer PEs than floating-point PEs. This increases the aggregate arithmetic throughput and, combined with lower per-operation energy, improves energy efficiency. However, integer arithmetic sometimes requires extra software steps: scaling, zero-point handling, and calibration to maintain numerical equivalence with floating-point algorithms. For example, quantized neural networks often represent weights and activations with a scale factor and zero-point and require integer arithmetic to mimic floating-point accumulation behavior; these manipulations can cost extra operations and masks.

Mixed-precision is a common compromise: compute multiply-adds in low precision (e.g., INT8 or FP16) but accumulate in a higher precision register (e.g., INT32 or FP32) to avoid overflow and reduce accumulation error. Fused multiply-add (FMA) instructions, available in many floating-point units, can perform a multiply and add in one hardware step with a single rounding, improving both performance and numerical accuracy relative to a separate multiply then add sequence. On some specialized hardware, integer multiply-accumulate can be fused similarly, but the accumulation width matters: e.g., an INT8 multiply may be accumulated into an INT32 accumulator.

From an energy modeling viewpoint, we can express energy per operation as a function of bit width $w$. For many circuits, energy scales roughly with capacitance multiplied by voltage squared. If one scales down voltage in tandem with precision (e.g., using specialized low-voltage domains for LPUs), energy per operation can drop superlinearly with $w$. However, voltage scaling is limited by the need to maintain correct switching behavior and signal integrity. Thus, while reducing precision from 32 to 16 bits roughly halves arithmetic energy in some cases, the memory energy for storing those bits also halves, which further reduces total energy for bandwidth-bound workloads. But if the architecture cannot exploit the reduced storage (for instance, because DRAM accesses are done in fixed bursts of 64 bytes), the practical memory energy savings may be less than linear.

# Methodologies to estimate and compare energy consumption across designs

Estimating and comparing energy consumption across designs requires a careful methodology that accounts for compute, memory, data movement, and control overheads. There is no single universal method, but a typical workflow involves the following steps: (1) characterize the workload in terms of operations and data movement, (2) obtain or assume per-operation and per-transfer energy costs for the target hardware, (3) model scheduling, tiling, or dataflow that affects reuse, and (4) sum energy terms and analyze sensitivity.

First, workload characterization means counting arithmetic operations (e.g., total number of multiply-adds), and quantifying data movement at each memory level. For example, for matrix multiplication, count the number of weights read, activations read, partial sums written, and temporary buffers transferred with realistic tiling. For neural network inference, identify static data (weights) and dynamic data (activations, intermediate tensors), and note reuse opportunities across batches or sequence lengths.

Second, obtain per-operation energy costs. Where available, use measured or published numbers for the specific hardware (e.g., energy per DRAM access, per SRAM access, per FMA). If not available, use representative numbers from the literature or a technology-average. For more accuracy, decompose memory energy by bus width, number of banks, and accounting for effective utilization (e.g., wasted bits in transfers, ECC overhead). Also account for state transitions and control energy when relevant.

Third, model how software maps the workload to hardware. The same kernel can have very different energy consumption depending on tiling and reuse. Include the cost of data layout transformations, format conversions (e.g., floating-point to integer), and decompression if compression is used. Consider overlapping of computation and communication (which affects latency but not necessarily energy) and leakage/static power, which becomes important for long-running or low-utilization workloads.

Fourth, sum energy components: arithmetic energy, SRAM/BRAM energy for on-chip storage and local movement, DRAM energy for off-chip transfers, I/O and serialization costs if crossing chip boundaries, and static/leakage power integrated over execution time. Present both absolute energy estimates and normalized metrics such as joules per inference, joules per training step, or energy per operation. Include performance estimates to evaluate energy-delay product or energy per useful result, since low energy but very slow designs might be unacceptable.

Comparative analysis should include sensitivity studies: vary key parameters to understand robustness of conclusions. For example, show how energy changes with batch size, which affects activation reuse; with precision, which affects memory size and arithmetic energy; or with DRAM bandwidth, which can cause stalls that increase overall energy due to leakage and idle power. It is also informative to decompose energy into major contributors (compute vs. memory vs. I/O) to reveal where redesign efforts would yield the largest gains.

Tools to aid estimation range from simple spreadsheets implementing the formulas above to more sophisticated cycle-level or RTL (register-transfer level) simulators. High-level analytic models (e.g., roofline adapted for energy) are useful for quick comparisons. For more detailed design exploration, architects use architectural simulators that model pipelines, caches, interconnects, and memory controllers; combined with technology-specific power models (e.g., McPAT, CACTI for caches, DRAMSim for DRAM) one can estimate dynamic and leakage power. However, such detailed simulations require accurate hardware parameters and are time-consuming. In practice, researchers often balance simple analytic models for broad insight with targeted detailed simulation for final evaluation.

Finally, empirical measurement is indispensable. Prototypes, FPGA implementations, or hardware emulation can reveal non-idealities not captured in models, such as interconnect contention, unexpected memory controller behavior, or compiler-induced inefficiencies. When possible, validate model predictions with measurements, and report both model assumptions and measured data so readers can assess applicability.

Conclusion

Understanding hardware and energy models is essential for designing and evaluating efficient implementations of numerical and machine learning workloads. By decomposing energy into arithmetic and memory components, recognizing the dominant role of data movement (especially DRAM-to-SRAM transfers), and by appreciating the architectural particularities of GPUs, TPUs, ASICs, and other accelerators, you can predict the impact of algorithmic choices on performance and energy. Custom hardware primitives like systolic arrays and LPUs provide powerful levers for efficiency but carry trade-offs in flexibility and complexity. Finally, rigorous energy estimation requires careful workload characterization, realistic per-operation cost assumptions, mapping-aware modeling, and empirical validation. Armed with these tools and intuitions, you will be better equipped to read hardware-aware research and to participate in cross-layer design that optimizes both speed and energy.
