Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained with high-precision floating point formats such as 32-bit (FP32) or 16-bit (FP16) arithmetic. When deploying these models in production — to reduce memory footprint, lower latency, and decrease energy consumption — quantization is a widely used technique: model weights and sometimes activations are represented with fewer bits. Post-training quantization (PTQ) refers to methods that convert a pretrained model from high precision to lower precision without (or with minimal) retraining. PTQ is attractive because it avoids the time, cost, and complexity of fine-tuning, and it is often the only option when access to original training data or compute for retraining is limited.

This chapter gives a thorough view of PTQ for LLMs. We first explain why PTQ matters for LLMs and what makes quantizing transformer-based models challenging. We then present key calibration concepts — per-channel range calibration and activation calibration — which underpin effective PTQ. Next, we explain several PTQ algorithms that have been developed specifically for LLMs: GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant and related approaches. We cover quantization-aware linear layer designs and kernel-replacement strategies used to enable fast inference on hardware that expects integer or low-precision kernels. We analyze the limitations of PTQ when pushing to extreme low-bit widths (2-bit and 1-bit) and conclude with a discussion of the practical trade-offs between accuracy loss and deployment simplicity.

# Motivation for post-training quantization (PTQ)

The motivations for PTQ are both practical and technical. Practically, LLMs are large: modern models have billions or hundreds of billions of parameters. Storing these parameters in FP32 requires many gigabytes of RAM or flash storage. Reducing weight precision by a factor of 2–16 can yield corresponding reductions in model size and memory bandwidth requirements, enabling deployment on edge devices, accelerators with limited memory, or more cost-effective cloud instances.

Beyond storage, inference performance often depends on memory bandwidth and cache behavior. Lower-precision representations reduce bandwidth pressure (fewer bytes to fetch for the same number of parameters) and can increase effective compute throughput if hardware supports integer or low-precision matrix operations. This translates into lower latency per token and higher throughput for batch inference.

Technically, PTQ is attractive because it can be applied directly to a pretrained model: no expensive re-training, no labeled data requirement, and often little code or infrastructure change. PTQ also preserves the original model’s learned behavior as closely as possible while adjusting only numerical representations.

However, transformer-based LLMs pose additional challenges. Their inference pipelines involve large matrix multiplications (attention and feed-forward networks) and sensitive activation statistics. Small changes in weight or activation precision can disproportionately affect numerical stability (e.g., softmax inputs) or model quality (e.g., logit distributions). Unlike small classification networks, LLMs are evaluated on generative quality across many tokens and contexts, amplifying even modest quantization errors. Thus PTQ methods for LLMs must be particularly careful: they need fine-grained control (per-channel schemes), reconstruction techniques that correct systematic errors, and activation-aware strategies that cope with the wide dynamic ranges seen in transformer layers.

# Per-channel range calibration and activation calibration

Two fundamental building blocks of effective PTQ are per-channel range calibration for weights and activation calibration for layer inputs/outputs. Intuitively, quantization maps many continuous values to a small set of discrete levels. The quality of that mapping depends critically on choosing appropriate scale and offset parameters: if the scale is too large, quantization steps are coarse and quantization noise is large; if it is too small, outliers blow up error through saturation or clipping.

Per-channel range calibration addresses heterogeneity across neurons or output channels. Consider a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. A common quantization model is to represent each scalar $w$ by an integer $q$ with a scale $s$ and zero-point $z$:

$q = \mathrm{round}\left(\frac{w}{s} + z\right),\qquad \hat{w} = s(q - z).$

If a single global scale $s$ is used for the entire matrix, channels with very different dynamic ranges are forced into an identical step size, causing some channels to have very coarse quantization and others to be dominated by clipping. Per-channel quantization assigns a separate scale (and optionally zero-point) for each output channel (e.g., each column or row of $W$ depending on layout). Mathematically, for column $j$ we choose $s_j$ so that the dynamic range of values in that column is represented well. Per-channel quantization reduces the maximum per-channel quantization error and stabilizes activations downstream.

Range calibration typically proceeds by collecting representative sample statistics over a small calibration dataset (even a few hundred examples often suffices). For each weight channel or activation tensor, we measure the min and max, sometimes with robust estimators to discount outliers (e.g., trimming or percentile-based clipping). Common heuristics include symmetric quantization with scale $s = \max(|\min|,|\max|)/(2^{b-1}-1)$ for $b$ bits, or using moments to derive optimal scales under mean-squared error (MSE) approximations.

Activation calibration targets the inputs and outputs of layers during inference. Activations often have distributions whose scale depends on input data (prompt length, tokens) and model internals. To avoid catastrophic clipping or variance explosion, PTQ methods run the model on a set of calibration inputs and measure activation ranges and distributions at each quantization point. The calibration data need not be labeled; it should be representative of typical inference scenarios. Once ranges are known, activations are quantized using the same scale/zero-point formula, possibly with per-channel granularity if activations are channelized.

Advanced activation calibration goes beyond min-max and uses distribution-aware clipping, e.g., minimizing the MSE between floating-point activations and quantized activations to choose clipping thresholds, or using KL divergence measures for probabilistic outputs. Some methods also record covariances between activations and weights, information that is later used in layer-wise reconstruction approaches (see GPTQ below).

# Specific PTQ methods for LLMs: GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant and relatives

Several PTQ methods have been developed specifically to cope with the scale and particular sensitivity of LLMs. I describe three influential families: GPTQ (and Hessian-aware reconstruction methods), AWQ (activation-aware weight quantization), and SmoothQuant (and related techniques that move scale between activations and weights).

GPTQ: Hessian-aware and block-wise greedy quantization

GPTQ (short for "Hessian-aware post-training quantization for generative pretrained transformers") is a family of PTQ methods that formalize quantization as minimizing the induced error on layer outputs using second-order (Hessian) information. The central idea is that quantizing a weight vector $w$ to $\hat{w}$ perturbs the pre-activation $a = W^\top x$ by $\Delta a = (W-\hat{W})^\top x$. The downstream loss (for a given layer’s output) is approximated quadratically by

$\Delta \mathcal{L} \approx \frac{1}{2}\Delta W^\top H \Delta W,$

where $H$ is a layer-wise or block-wise empirical Hessian (or an approximation built from activation covariances). In practice, GPTQ collects activation samples $x$ from calibration runs and forms a matrix $X X^\top$ (covariance) as a proxy for Hessian structure. Using that, GPTQ implements a greedy, iterative quantization of weights in small blocks (e.g., column blocks of size 128) where it chooses the quantization of each weight element one at a time to minimize the increase in quadratic error. After quantizing an element, GPTQ updates a residual correction term to account for its effect on the remaining weights. The algorithm is computationally heavier than simple rounding but remains feasible because it operates block-wise and uses low-rank structures. GPTQ often achieves excellent accuracy at 4-bit and even some 3-bit settings for LLMs, with small quality loss.

Key components of GPTQ include:

- Collecting a calibration set of activations.
- Approximating the layer Hessian as $H \approx XX^\top$ (or adding damping).
- Block-wise greedy quantization with exact computation of error increments using the inverse of $H$ restricted to the block.
- Optionally using per-channel scales and asymmetric quantization to reduce bias.

AWQ: activation-aware weight quantization

AWQ extends the idea that quantization of weights should consider activation distributions. Instead of only using weight statistics, AWQ measures the interaction between weights and activations to choose quantizers that minimize the expected error in layer outputs. Concretely, AWQ often solves a reconstruction problem for each weight block:

minimize over quantized block $\hat{W}_b$ the empirical MSE

$\min_{\hat{W}_b} \sum_{i} \|W_b x_i - \hat{W}_b x_i\|^2,$

where $\{x_i\}$ are sampled activations. This is equivalent to weighted quantization of weights where weights with larger influence on activations are quantized more carefully. AWQ may incorporate per-row/per-column scales, and can use grouped mixed-precision (e.g., some channels in 3-bit, some in 4-bit) chosen by their sensitivity. AWQ often uses linear least-squares reconstruction per block to find a small correction (sometimes as a low-rank residual) that compensates for quantization error. The method is more computationally intensive than naive rounding but yields better fidelity in LLM outputs, particularly in low-bit regimes like 3-bit.

SmoothQuant and relatives: shifting scale between activations and weights

SmoothQuant addresses a specific practical problem: activations and weights can have imbalanced dynamic ranges that hinder efficient quantization. The trick of SmoothQuant is to move (smoothly) certain channel-wise scaling factors from activations into weights (or vice versa) so that both become more amenable to uniform quantization. Consider a matrix multiplication $Y = W^\top X$ where $W$ is weight matrix and $X$ are activations. If activation channel $j$ has very large scale compared to weight column $j$, quantizing activations finely and weights coarsely may be suboptimal. SmoothQuant introduces a diagonal scaling matrix $S$ such that

$Y = (W S^{-1})^\top (S X).$

We can choose $S$ so that $W S^{-1}$ and $S X$ have more balanced ranges; then quantize $W S^{-1}$ (weights) and $S X$ (activations) with smaller ranges. The scaling is applied only during quantization; at inference time the scale can be merged into adjacent layers to avoid runtime cost. SmoothQuant’s central contribution is demonstrating that by moving small multiplicative factors between weights and activations, one can quantize both sides in lower precision without significant accuracy loss. Related techniques include ZeroQuant and recent "activation smoothing" variants that target attention mechanisms’ sensitive softmax inputs.

These methods are often combined: e.g., SmoothQuant-style scaling to balance ranges, followed by GPTQ-style reconstruction to cluster quantized weights to minimize downstream error, and AWQ’s activation-aware blockwise profiles to assign bit-widths adaptively.

# Quantization-aware linear layers and kernel replacement strategies

Quantized models are only as useful as the runtime kernels and layer designs that can execute the quantized operations efficiently. There are several strategies for mapping quantized matrices to efficient kernels and for replacing original linear layers to accommodate low-bit arithmetic.

Quantization-aware linear layers are library-level primitives that encapsulate quantized operations while preserving numerical correctness and supporting efficient inference. A straightforward implementation stores weight matrices in integer formats (e.g., 8-bit signed integers) with per-channel scales and interprets matmul as integer matmul followed by scaling back to floating point:

Y = (W_q int) * (X fp) scaled -> accumulate in int32 -> convert to fp and multiply by scale.

To reduce runtime complexity, many systems also quantize activations, so both operands are integers and the accumulation can be performed in a smaller integer accumulator where supported by hardware. In practice, many GPUs or accelerators nonetheless perform accumulation in 32-bit integers or floating point for correctness, but still benefit from lower memory movement.

Kernel replacement strategies are necessary when the quantized representation differs structurally from the original layer. Examples include:

- Block-wise quantized GEMM: weights are stored in blocked formats (e.g., blocks of size 128x32) with block-specific scale factors. Custom GEMM kernels are written to load a block, dequantize on the fly (or accumulate in integer domain with per-block scales), and compute output. Blocked formats improve locality and amortize scale loading cost.
- Mixed-precision kernels: some kernels treat critical channels at higher precision (e.g., 8-bit or FP16) and most of the matrix at lower precision (e.g., 4-bit). The kernel orchestrates two matmuls and sums partial results.
- Kernel-level bit packing and unpacking: for sub-byte formats (4-bit, 2-bit), weights are packed tightly (two 4-bit values per byte, etc.). Kernels need to extract these values efficiently, often using tables or vectorized bitwise operations.
- Hardware-assisted integer matmul: when hardware provides fast int8 or int4 matrix multiply (e.g., NVIDIA Tensor Cores, specialized NPUs), quantized kernels map the blocked weight layout into hardware-friendly formats. This often requires additional permutations or transposes at conversion time.
- Kernel replacement with low-rank/residual decomposition: in some quantization flows, the original linear layer is replaced by a small set of operations: quantized base matrix plus a small dense residual in higher precision to correct systematic quantization error. During inference, this is implemented as two matmuls: one quantized, one high-precision, whose outputs are summed.

Designing these kernels requires careful attention to hardware characteristics: memory bandwidth, cache sizes, instruction set support for bit manipulations, and preferred blocking sizes. Kernel authors also must keep in mind numerical stability: dequantize-to-float steps may require ordering of operations to prevent loss of significance, and reduction sums must be done in an accumulator with adequate range.

# Limitations of PTQ for extreme low-bit (2-,1-bit) representations

While PTQ methods have achieved impressive results at 8-bit and 4-bit, pushing to extreme low-bit representations such as 2-bit or 1-bit presents several fundamental challenges.

First, the quantization granularity becomes extremely coarse. For $b$ bits, there are only $2^b$ levels. At 2 bits there are only 4 levels; at 1 bit there are 2. With such few representable values, the quantization error per parameter becomes large unless the weight distribution is highly compressible or supporting machinery (like heavy mixed-precision or large residuals) is in place. This coarse discretization creates bias that cannot be efficiently corrected by small reconstruction steps.

Second, the expressive capacity of linear transforms is reduced when weights are restricted to a tiny discrete set. Even if activations are full-precision, the representable linear maps are severely constrained, which can destroy critical features learned by the model. Some networks tolerate aggressive binarization if architectures are adapted during training (quantization-aware training, training from scratch with binary weights, or specialized architectures) but PTQ lacks the ability to adjust the learned representations.

Third, the combinatorial nature of selecting discrete levels across billions of parameters makes post-hoc optimization difficult. Methods that rely on local greedy improvements (like GPTQ) may get stuck in poor local minima when the decision space is so fine-grained and errors interact across the network. Furthermore, collecting enough calibration data to reliably estimate high-order interactions required to preserve generation quality across contexts becomes harder.

Fourth, hardware constraints are sometimes the limiting factor. While bit-packing for 1-bit weights is compact, the arithmetic realization (popcount-based binary convolutions, xnor-popcount) typically diverges from standard linear algebra APIs and may not map efficiently to general-purpose accelerators used for LLM inference. Implementing high-quality 1-bit inference may require custom kernels and potentially different accumulation pipelines, increasing deployment complexity.

Finally, numerical stability issues become prominent. Attention softmaxes and normalization layers can be extremely sensitive to small changes in their pre-activation inputs; large quantization noise introduced by 2- or 1-bit weights can perturb probabilities, which cascades during autoregressive generation. Without retraining to adapt the model to the quantized representations, PTQ to such low bits often incurs unacceptable quality degradation.

In summary, while PTQ can sometimes push to 3-bit or aggressive 4-bit with careful techniques and per-layer adjustments, moving to 2-bit or 1-bit usually requires quantization-aware training, architectural changes, or hybrid schemes that keep sensitive components at higher precision.

# Trade-offs between accuracy loss and deployment simplicity

PTQ sits at the intersection of competing priorities: model fidelity (accuracy), memory and compute efficiency (size and speed), and deployment complexity (engineering effort, hardware support). Practitioners must weigh these factors when selecting a quantization strategy.

On one end of the trade-off spectrum is simple uniform 8-bit post-training quantization with per-channel scaling and min-max calibration. This approach is simple to implement, often supported by standard frameworks, and provides immediate 2x reduction in memory footprint versus FP16 with minimal accuracy loss for many models. Deployment simplicity is high: runtimes and hardware typically support int8 kernels widely. The accuracy loss is often negligible for many tasks, making this approach a strong default.

Moving to 4-bit quantization improves memory and bandwidth further but increases the risk of quality degradation. Advanced PTQ methods (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant) are often necessary to preserve accuracy. These methods increase pre-deployment complexity — calibration runs, block-wise optimization, bespoke conversion pipelines, and sometimes hardware-specific kernel development — but they can reduce model size by a factor of 4 or more. The engineering cost (scripted conversion and validation) is nontrivial but still far lower than full retraining. Deployment complexity is moderate: specialized quantized kernels and careful memory layout are required, but many inference engines now provide well-tested 4-bit primitives.

At the extreme, pushing towards mixed 3/2/1-bit schemes or aggressive block-level bit allocation yields the lowest memory footprints and potentially the best throughput on custom hardware, but at the cost of significant accuracy risk and high engineering overhead. Such approaches often necessitate layer-wise sensitivity analysis to decide bit widths, per-block reconstruction, and sometimes retention of small high-precision residuals or bias correction terms. From a deployment perspective, this complexity can be prohibitive: custom kernels, compatibility issues, and the need for extensive end-to-end validation make these approaches suitable only when the cost savings justify the engineering investment.

There is also a human-factor trade-off: PTQ that yields slightly lower generation quality (e.g., somewhat worse perplexity) might still be acceptable for many downstream tasks depending on user expectations. For example, chatbots or assistants may tolerate slight fluency differences if latency and cost are substantially improved. Conversely, high-stakes applications (medical summarization, legal drafting) may require higher fidelity and therefore favor conservative quantization.

In practice, a staged approach often works best: start with a conservative PTQ (8-bit or 4-bit with robust calibration), evaluate on representative tasks, then gradually apply more aggressive PTQ and reconstruction techniques only where the benefits are clearly necessary. Measuring end-to-end generative metrics and human evaluation is important because token-level metrics like perplexity do not always capture qualitative degradation.

Conclusion

Post-training quantization is a powerful tool for deploying LLMs efficiently. By carefully calibrating per-channel ranges, taking activations into account, and using advanced reconstruction and scale-shifting techniques (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant), practitioners can often reach 4-bit or even some 3-bit operating points with acceptable quality loss. However, PTQ has limits: extreme low-bit quantization typically requires retraining or architectural adaptation. Successful deployment demands selecting the right quantization granularity for the application, investing in calibration and conversion tooling, and leveraging hardware-aware kernel implementations to realize the theoretical gains in practice.
