Chapter 9: Model Compression, Sparsity, and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed natural language processing and other domains, but their compute and memory demands are enormous. To deploy these models in resource-constrained settings (inference on-device, real-time serving, or cost-limited cloud services) and to scale further, researchers and engineers use model compression and conditional computation techniques. Compression reduces the number of parameters or their precision; conditional computation like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) activates only parts of a model for a given input. Sparsity—setting many parameters to zero—plays a central role across these techniques. This chapter surveys key ideas, trade-offs, and system implications of pruning, low-rank methods, sparsity-inducing regularizers, MoE architectures, and how quantization interacts with sparsity. The goal is to offer intuitive understanding and enough technical detail to read contemporary research papers and design experiments.

# Pruning: structured and unstructured approaches

Pruning is the process of removing parameters (weights), neurons, filters, or even entire layers from a trained neural network, with the intention of reducing storage, memory bandwidth, or compute while preserving performance. Pruning approaches fall into two broad categories: unstructured pruning and structured pruning.

Unstructured pruning removes individual weights based on some criterion, producing sparse weight matrices. A common simple heuristic is magnitude pruning: remove weights with smallest absolute value because they contribute least to the forward pass. Formally, given a weight matrix $W$ and sparsity target $s$ (fraction of weights to remove), magnitude pruning sets to zero the entries with smallest $|W_{ij}|$ until $s$ is reached. Unstructured pruning can achieve very high sparsity with modest loss in accuracy, especially in overparameterized models. The major drawback is that the resulting sparsity pattern is irregular, requiring specialized sparse matrix storage formats and sparse kernels to realize computational gains. On commodity hardware, irregular sparsity often yields modest speedups compared to the reduction in parameter count because irregular memory accesses and overheads dominate.

Structured pruning removes larger, contiguous structures such as entire neurons (rows/columns in weight matrices), attention heads in transformers, convolutional filters, or transformer layers. For instance, column pruning removes entire output neurons of a linear layer, reducing the dimension of the subsequent layer and enabling dense matrix-matrix operations on smaller tensors. Structured pruning is more amenable to acceleration on existing hardware because it preserves dense computation; it simplifies memory layout and avoids irregular memory accesses. However, structured pruning typically reduces capacity more aggressively for the same parameter reduction and may require more careful criteria and retraining.

Pruning may be performed at several points in the training lifecycle. Post-training pruning takes a trained dense model and removes parameters, often followed by finetuning to recover accuracy. Iterative pruning alternates pruning and finetuning in cycles, gradually increasing sparsity. Lottery Ticket Hypothesis work suggests that, in some cases, reinitializing a sparse subnetwork early in training can match dense performance, implying that certain sparse structures are particularly effective. One-shot pruning attempts to prune once and then finetune. Another approach is dynamic pruning during training: models learn which weights to keep in an online fashion, for example via techniques like RigL (Rigging the Lottery) that periodically remove and regrow connections based on gradient magnitude.

Choosing what to prune involves scoring elements by importance. Beyond magnitude, other criteria include gradient-based scores (sensitivity of loss to parameter perturbation), Hessian-based second-order measures (e.g., Optimal Brain Surgeon), and learned gating parameters that indicate usefulness. Structured pruning often uses group-level importance: for example, compute average magnitude across a neuron or attention head, or use reinforcement learning to find pruning policies.

From the perspective of deployment, practical pruning must consider hardware and systems constraints. Unstructured pruning yields high parameter sparsity but requires sparse kernels or hardware like specialized accelerators (TPUs with sparse support or NVIDIA sparse tensor cores) to see throughput gains. Structured pruning maps to smaller dense models and is easier to integrate but may require retraining the architecture from scratch or extensive finetuning. Hybrid approaches combine structured pruning to reduce dimensions and unstructured pruning to further compress.

# Low-rank factorization and knowledge distillation

Low-rank factorization and knowledge distillation are complementary strategies that reduce model size and computation while preserving performance.

Low-rank factorization leverages the empirical observation that weight matrices in large models often lie near low-dimensional subspaces. A dense matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ can be approximated by a product $U V$ where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ with rank $r \ll \min(m,n)$. The approximation minimizes $\|W - UV\|_F$ for a chosen $r$, using techniques like truncated singular value decomposition (SVD). In neural networks, replacing a dense linear layer with two smaller linear layers reduces multiply-adds from $mn$ to $r(m+n)$, saving computation and parameters when $r$ is small. Low-rank methods can be applied to projection matrices, embedding matrices, or attention projections in transformers.

Intuitively, low-rank factorization compresses redundancy: many output features are linear combinations of a few latent directions. In language models, embeddings for similar words or activation patterns across tokens create such redundancies. However, choosing $r$ is a trade-off: too small creates representational bottlenecks; too large yields limited benefit. One can learn $U$ and $V$ by finetuning after factorization, or incorporate low-rank constraints during training via parameterization. Advanced methods like tensor decomposition (CP, Tucker, Tensor-Train) extend low-rank ideas to multi-dimensional weight tensors.

Knowledge distillation compresses a large teacher model into a smaller student model by training the student to match the teacher's behavior. Classic distillation minimizes a combination of supervised loss and a distillation loss that compares teacher and student logits, often with temperature scaling. Let teacher outputs be $z^T$ and student outputs $z^S$, then distillation minimizes cross-entropy between soft targets $\mathrm{softmax}(z^T / T)$ and student softmax at temperature $T$ plus task loss. Distillation transfers dark knowledge—relative probabilities across classes—that helps the student generalize. Distillation can produce much smaller student models that approximately match teacher performance on downstream tasks.

Combining low-rank factorizations with distillation is powerful: after factorizing a large model to a compact parameterization, a student can be trained with distillation to recover much of the original performance. Distillation can also be applied to compress ensembles, or to distill intermediate representations (patient knowledge distillation) by matching hidden activations or attention maps, providing richer supervision than logits alone.

Both low-rank and distillation are friendly to dense hardware: they produce smaller dense matrices and networks that leverage optimized BLAS libraries. From a systems perspective, low-rank models reduce memory bandwidth and compute, and distilled students reduce latency.

# Sparse representations and sparsity-inducing regularizers

Sparsity can be encouraged during training via regularization so that the learned weights naturally become zero or near-zero. This avoids an expensive post-training pruning step and can lead to more principled sparse models.

One classical route is L1 regularization: add a penalty $\lambda \|W\|_1$ to the loss, where $\|W\|_1 = \sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. L1 encourages many weights to shrink to zero because it imposes constant pressure irrespective of weight magnitude, unlike L2 which penalizes large weights more than small ones. In practice, L1 can produce moderate sparsity, but achieving high sparsity tends to degrade performance unless $\lambda$ is large; strong regularization often harms optimization.

Group sparsity extends L1 to structured groups: use a mixed norm like $\sum_g \|W_g\|_2$ (group Lasso) to encourage entire groups (rows, columns, channels) to go to zero. Here $W_g$ is the vector of parameters in group $g$, and the penalty $\lambda \sum_g \|W_g\|_2$ drives some groups to become exactly zero, implementing structured pruning during training.

Another technique is variational dropout and Bayesian sparsity approaches. Methods like Sparse Variational Dropout place sparsity-promoting priors on weights and perform approximate posterior inference; weights with high posterior uncertainty or mean near zero are pruned. Concrete Dropout and other learned dropout scheduling can also produce effective sparsity patterns.

A different family of methods uses sparsity-inducing parameterizations. For instance, one can parameterize a weight as $W = S \odot \Theta$ where $S$ is a binary mask and $\Theta$ are underlying real-valued parameters. The mask $S$ is learned with gradient-based techniques using relaxation (e.g., Gumbel-Softmax) or straight-through estimators. These methods directly optimize mask structure but require careful training to avoid instability.

Sparsity may also be encouraged using proximal gradient methods that solve optimization problems with nonsmooth regularizers efficiently. The proximal operator for L1 is soft thresholding: after a gradient step you apply thresholding to drive small weights to zero exactly. This technique can enforce exact sparsity during training.

From the representation perspective, sparsity is not just a way to remove parameters; sparse representations can improve interpretability, robustness, and energy efficiency for downstream systems. Sparse activations (e.g., ReLU with L1 penalties on activations) can reduce dynamic compute and memory. Sparse coding and dictionary learning historically showed that many natural signals are compactly represented in sparse bases, a motivation for seeking sparse models.

However, encouraging sparsity during training can make optimization trickier. Sparse models can be less robust to hyperparameters and require longer training or careful learning rate schedules. There is also a trade-off between structured and unstructured sparsity: structured sparsity is easier to deploy but sometimes less optimal statistically than unstructured sparsity.

# Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): routing, expert capacity, and training

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a conditional computation architecture where different inputs are routed to different "expert" subnetworks so that only a subset of parameters are active per example. In the context of transformers, MoE layers replace dense feed-forward layers with a set of experts and a router that selects which experts process each token. The benefits are dramatic parameter scaling without proportional increases in per-token compute: a model can have billions of parameters spread across experts but only activate a few experts for each token or sequence.

The canonical MoE layer contains $E$ experts, each a small feed-forward network with parameters $\{f_e(\cdot)\}_{e=1}^E$, and a gating function $g(x)$ that assigns a token representation $x$ to experts. A simple soft gating uses a linear projection followed by softmax to obtain routing probabilities. For efficiency and simplicity, sparsely-gated MoE often uses top-$k$ selection: for each token, compute gating scores $s_e(x)$ and select the top $k$ experts with largest scores; then only those experts process the token and their outputs are aggregated, typically as a weighted sum normalized by gating probabilities.

Routing design is crucial. Deterministic top-$k$ gating is efficient but leads to load imbalance: some experts receive many tokens, others few. Load imbalance degrades utilization and can hurt generalization. To address this, many systems add auxiliary load-balancing losses that penalize uneven distributions. An example auxiliary loss can be the coefficient of variation of expert loads or a cross-entropy between actual expert load and uniform distribution. Another approach uses capacity constraints per expert: each expert can accept at most $C$ tokens per batch; tokens trying to route to a full expert are rerouted or dropped to secondary experts.

Capacity and routing interact tightly. If capacity $C$ is too small, many tokens will be dropped or routed to suboptimal experts, harming performance. If capacity is high, some experts can be overloaded, causing gradient noise and slower convergence. Practical MoE implementations use per-expert buffers and dynamic dispatch that enforce a capacity and allow tokens to spill over to their next-best expert. Capacity makes the model robust to extremely skewed routing distributions.

Training MoE models involves additional challenges. Since experts receive different amounts of data per batch, gradients are uneven and noisy. To mitigate, training uses large batch sizes so that each expert sees enough diverse examples per update. Load balancing also helps ensure all experts receive training signal. Gradient accumulation and synchronized updates across devices are commonly used.

Another training issue is routing discreteness. The top-$k$ operation is non-differentiable; common workarounds include using soft gating during training (e.g., Gumbel-Softmax) or backpropagating through the gating scores while the selection mechanism acts as a mask (straight-through). In practice, many implementations propagate gradients through the continuous scores even when selection is used for the forward pass; this hybrid approach works well empirically.

MoE architectures scale parameter count cheaply because experts can be specialized. They also introduce model sparsity at the layer level: only a fraction of parameters are used per token. However, MoE adds complexity in memory, communication, and software engineering, which we discuss next.

# Communication and memory overheads in MoE and large sparse models

While MoE and sparse models reduce per-token compute in principle, they can introduce significant communication and memory overheads in multi-device settings. Understanding these overheads is essential to realize practical speedups and to design cluster topology, batching, and routing algorithms.

In distributed training or inference, experts are typically sharded across devices (GPUs or TPU cores). Routing a token to an expert requires sending the token's activation across the network to the device holding that expert. For a batch of tokens, this creates all-to-all communication between devices. The cost depends on number of tokens, expert selection patterns, and network bandwidth and latency. If routing is random or balanced, communication can be roughly uniform, but skewed routing causes hotspots where some devices become bottlenecks.

Communication overheads arise in both forward and backward passes. In the forward pass, activations must be transmitted to experts and outputs transmitted back. In the backward pass, gradients for gated parameters and experts must be communicated and aggregated. When using capacity constraints with buffering, implementations may need additional coordination to determine which tokens are accepted by which expert, further increasing synchronization.

Memory overheads also grow. Each device stores the parameters of its assigned experts and possibly copies of tokenizer and embedding tables. During inference, to maintain low latency you may need to store activations and queues per expert. For large models with many experts, total model memory across devices is high even if only a subset of experts is active per token. This makes MoE memory-intensive at system level and can complicate deployment on devices with limited memory.

There are strategies to mitigate communication and memory costs. One is locality-aware routing: place experts that are likely to be co-selected on the same device, reducing cross-device transfers. However, predicting co-selection patterns requires profiling and can be brittle across workloads. Another approach is batch routing: group tokens destined for the same expert and send them in a single message, amortizing latency. Mixed parallelism strategies combine data parallelism for dense layers and model parallelism for experts; frameworks like Megatron-MoE and GShard implement such combinations.

To reduce memory movement, activation compression can be used: quantize activations before sending them to experts, or use lower-precision communication primitives. Additionally, gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory by recomputing activations during backward pass rather than storing them.

Network topology matters: high-radix, low-latency interconnects (e.g., NVIDIA NVLink, Google’s Jupiter network) reduce communication overhead. When network bandwidth is limited, the benefits of MoE can be negated by communication cost. Practical deployments must carefully balance expert count, per-expert capacity, batch size, and device allocation to achieve net wall-clock gains.

Finally, sparse models with unstructured sparsity also face systems issues: storing sparse matrices and performing sparse-dense ops require specialized kernels. Sparse weights can be compressed on disk and expanded at runtime, but then runtime expansion diminishes gains. Hardware support for sparsity (structured sparse tensor cores, compressed sparse formats in accelerator architectures) can make sparse models more efficient.

# Interaction of quantization and sparsity (sparse quantized weights)

Quantization reduces numerical precision of weights and activations to save memory and accelerate compute. Common schemes include 8-bit integer quantization, 4-bit quantization, and various non-uniform schemes (logarithmic or learned quantization). When combined with sparsity, quantization introduces new choices and challenges.

One straightforward approach is to quantize dense weights and then prune; or prune first and then quantize the remaining nonzero weights. Each ordering has implications. Pruning before quantization may produce many small-magnitude weights that quantization maps to zero anyway, potentially redundant. Conversely, quantizing first can make some small weights equal after quantization; pruning equal-valued weights may have less granularity.

Sparse quantized weights can be stored more compactly by combining sparse storage for positions with low-bit representations for values. For example, store nonzero indices in compressed sparse row (CSR) format and quantized values in 4-bit packs. This reduces memory but complicates compute because multiply-adds must unpack and convert to accumulator precision. Hardware support for low-bit arithmetic and sparse compression formats greatly affects the practical throughput.

Quantization errors interact with sparsity-induced errors. Consider a weight value $w$ that is small in magnitude and would have been pruned under unstructured pruning. If quantization maps it to zero (because it's below representable threshold), then pruning may be redundant. But quantization also introduces rounding noise to surviving weights; when a sparse pattern is enforced, these rounding errors may have nonlinear effects on network outputs. Effective quantized sparse models often require joint optimization: finetune quantized weights with sparsity constraints so the model adapts to both sources of approximation.

Another important consideration is the dynamic range across experts or layers. In MoE settings, each expert may have distinct activation scales; quantization parameters (scale and zero-point in affine quantization) may need per-expert calibration to avoid excessive clipping. Per-channel or per-tensor quantization granularity choices influence accuracy: per-channel typically yields higher accuracy but higher bookkeeping and sometimes higher memory.

Advanced techniques include mixed-precision sparse formats where indices are compressed but values use mixed precision: for instance, store weights in 4-bit but accumulate in 16-bit or 32-bit during computation. Weight sharing and codebook quantization (vector quantization) can also be combined with sparse masks: nonzero entries are quantized by mapping them to a small codebook of shared values, enabling high compression ratios. Learned quantization jointly optimizes codebook entries with sparse masks.

From a systems point of view, combining sparsity and low-bit quantization requires careful kernel design. Sparse-dense multiply-add with quantized values can be implemented by first expanding quantized values to accumulator precision and then performing dense operations on packed blocks, or by specialized sparse-quantized kernels that handle packed low-bit arithmetic and index dereferencing efficiently. Without hardware support, the overhead of unpacking and pointer chasing can erase theoretical gains.

In summary, quantization and sparsity complement each other in reducing model size and bandwidth but must be co-designed. Joint finetuning, careful choice of quantization granularity, and attention to per-expert statistics in MoE are necessary to maintain accuracy while reaping system-level benefits.

Conclusion

Model compression, sparsity, and conditional computation techniques like MoE provide a powerful toolkit to push the frontier of large models while managing compute and memory costs. Each method offers trade-offs: unstructured pruning yields high sparsity but demands specialized kernels; structured pruning is friendly to hardware but may reduce representational capacity; low-rank factorization and distillation produce dense compact models optimized for current accelerators; sparsity-inducing regularizers provide principled pathways to sparse training; MoE scales parameters cheaply but incurs communication and memory complexity; and quantization interacts nontrivially with sparsity. Understanding these trade-offs, and the system-level implications, is essential for both researchers developing new compression techniques and engineers deploying large models at scale. Continued progress will likely come from co-design of models, algorithms, and hardware so that the theoretical savings of sparsity and conditional computation translate into real-world performance and cost improvements.
