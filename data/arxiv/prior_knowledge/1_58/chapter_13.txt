Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are evaluated using a mixture of intrinsic measures that capture how well the model predicts text and extrinsic tasks that capture downstream capability (question answering, reasoning, etc.). This chapter explains the standard metrics and evaluation paradigms used in modern LLM research, describes widely used benchmark datasets, and lays out best practices for fair, reproducible comparisons. We cover how perplexity and validation loss are computed and interpreted, the important distinctions between zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned evaluation, the nature and purpose of common benchmark suites, evaluation tooling such as lm-evaluation-harness, how to report and aggregate performance scores, and how to test for statistical significance while avoiding common pitfalls such as test contamination or selection bias. The goal is to give you the intuition and the concrete methods you need to read, reproduce, and critique LLM evaluation results in the research literature.

# Perplexity and validation loss: interpretation and calculation

Perplexity and validation loss are the fundamental intrinsic metrics for generative language models trained by next-token prediction. They quantify how well the model assigns probability mass to sequences the data distribution produces. Because LLMs are generally trained to minimize cross-entropy loss (negative log-likelihood of the training tokens under the model), the validation loss and perplexity follow directly from that objective.

Cross-entropy (per-token) on a dataset of tokens can be written as
\[
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i}),
\]
where $p_\theta$ is the model's conditional token distribution, $x_i$ is the $i$th token, $x_{<i}$ are previous tokens, and $N$ is the number of tokens evaluated. This loss is typically reported on a held-out validation set that the model did not train on. Validation loss directly measures the average number of nats (natural logarithm) of surprise per token; if you prefer bits, divide by $\ln 2$.

Perplexity is a transformation of the cross-entropy that is more interpretable: perplexity is the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood,
\[
\mathrm{Perplexity} = \exp(\mathcal{L}).
\]
Intuitively, perplexity can be thought of as the "effective branching factor" of the model's predictive distribution at each step: a perplexity of $k$ suggests the model's next-token distribution is about as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among $k$ equally probable tokens. Lower perplexity indicates a better fit. Because perplexity is exponential in loss, small differences in cross-entropy can correspond to sizeable multiplicative differences in perplexity.

There are several important caveats when interpreting perplexity and validation loss. First, perplexity is sensitive to the tokenization scheme: different tokenizers (Byte Pair Encoding, SentencePiece, etc.) result in different token counts and different per-token probabilities, so perplexities are not directly comparable across models using different tokenizers unless you normalize or compute loss on the same tokenization. Second, absolute perplexity values reflect both model capacity and the difficulty of the evaluation corpus; a model can have low perplexity on one domain (e.g., well-represented web text) and much higher perplexity on another (e.g., code or scientific text). Third, perplexity captures average predictive performance, not downstream task ability: models with similar perplexities can differ substantially on reasoning, arithmetic, or structured tasks because those abilities depend on learned algorithmic behavior and knowledge that may be a small fraction of overall token-level surprise.

When computing validation loss and perplexity in practice, implementers need to handle sequence boundaries, padding, and evaluation context carefully. Compute the sum of token log-probabilities only over non-padding (or otherwise valid) tokens. For autoregressive models that use teacher forcing (conditioning on ground-truth prefixes), ensure you shift logits and targets so that the log-probability for token $x_i$ is computed from the model output conditioned on $x_{<i}$. For conditional or prompted evaluations (e.g., computing perplexity on question-answer pairs), decide whether to include prompt tokens in the loss, or to evaluate only on the target tokens; report clearly which choice was made because it greatly affects the number. Finally, report standard errors or confidence intervals (see the statistical significance section) when comparing perplexities across models.

# Zero-shot, few-shot and fine-tuned evaluation paradigms

LLM evaluation is commonly performed under three paradigms: zero-shot, few-shot (in-context learning), and fine-tuning. Each probes different aspects of model capability and generalization.

Zero-shot evaluation measures how well a model performs a task when provided a task instruction (or just the raw input) but no task-specific examples. For example, in a question-answering task, zero-shot evaluation might present "Q: What is the capital of France? A:" and then measure whether the model's first token(s) produce "Paris". Zero-shot performance reflects how much the model has internalized the task during pretraining (via implicit patterns in the pretraining data), and how well it understands and follows natural language instructions. Zero-shot is useful when you want to measure off-the-shelf capability without any extra adaptation. However, it often underestimates what the model can do when allowed to see examples.

Few-shot evaluation uses in-context learning: a small number of input-output examples are appended to the prompt before the test input, and the model is expected to complete the pattern. For instance, a prompt may include a few question-answer pairs followed by a new question. The model does not update weights; it simply conditions on the examples. Few-shot evaluation reveals how well the model can learn a new task quickly from examples and how sensitive it is to demonstration ordering, example choice, and prompt formatting. Few-shot performance can be highly variable: selecting different demonstrations or changing the prompt template can change results noticeably. Best practice includes reporting averaged performance over multiple random example sets and tempering claims by recognizing sensitivity.

Fine-tuned evaluation involves training the base model (or a copy) on task-specific data with gradient updates. Fine-tuning can be full-model, adapter-based, or head-only. This paradigm tells you the performance ceiling achievable with labeled data and optimization, and is practically important because many applications will fine-tune LLMs. Fine-tuning typically yields higher performance than in-context learning for many tasks but requires labeled data, compute, and careful hyperparameter tuning. Fine-tuning also risks data contamination if the task's test set overlaps with the model's pretraining data; therefore dataset provenance checks are essential.

When comparing models, ensure evaluations are carried out under the same paradigm. Comparing a zero-shot result of one model to a fine-tuned result of another is not meaningful unless the goal is explicitly to compare readiness for deployment with different adaptation budgets. Within few-shot evaluations, standardize the number of demonstrations (e.g., 0, 1, 5, 32 shots), the selection strategy (random vs. stratified), and report variance from different seeds and example draws. For fine-tuning, report hyperparameters, dataset sizes, early stopping criteria, and whether any validation set was used to tune hyperparameters; this transparency is crucial for reproducibility and fair comparison.

# Common benchmark suites and datasets: ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ, LAMBADA, SciQ

A widely used set of benchmark datasets targets different linguistic and cognitive capabilities. Understanding what each dataset tests, their format, and typical failure modes helps interpret model strengths and weaknesses.

ARC (AI2 Reasoning Challenge) is a multiple-choice dataset containing grade-school science questions with two subsets: ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge. ARC-Challenge consists of questions that simple retrieval or surface-level methods struggle to solve and often require multi-hop reasoning or world knowledge. Performance on ARC is reported as accuracy (percentage of correct multiple-choice answers). Models must combine factual knowledge and reasoning to excel. When evaluating LLMs with multiple-choice tasks, researchers often use the log-probability of each choice to select the best answer.

HellaSwag is a commonsense reasoning dataset for grounded next-event prediction. Given a sentence describing a situation, the model must choose the most plausible continuation from multiple options. HellaSwag was constructed to avoid superficial cues that make the task solvable by shallow language-model heuristics; thus strong performance requires deeper commonsense and world knowledge. Early models that performed well on superficially similar datasets performed poorly on HellaSwag, highlighting the dataset's robustness.

Winogrande and the original Winograd Schema Challenge are pronoun resolution tasks designed to test commonsense reasoning. Each example contains a sentence with an ambiguous pronoun and two candidate antecedents; the model must pick which candidate appropriately resolves the pronoun. Because correct resolution typically depends on commonsense inference rather than syntax, Winogrande measures a specific kind of contextual reasoning. Evaluation uses accuracy.

PIQA (Physical Interaction QA) tests physical commonsense: everyday physical knowledge like stability, object affordances, and causal mechanics. Each example provides a goal or problem and two possible solutions; the model must choose the physically plausible one. Good PIQA performance indicates grounding in practical, physical world knowledge the model has implicitly learned from text.

OpenBookQA is modeled after open-book science exams: each question requires combining a small "open book" of facts with broader commonsense knowledge. Unlike ARC, OpenBookQA questions often require identifying a relevant fact and chaining it with background knowledge. Evaluation is accuracy; typical baselines include retrieval-augmented methods that select relevant facts.

BoolQ is a dataset for yes/no (boolean) question answering over paragraphs of text. Each example provides a passage and a yes/no question, and the model must answer accordingly. BoolQ mainly tests reading comprehension and the ability to extract and reason over passage information. For LLMs, BoolQ can be evaluated in zero-shot or few-shot settings by prompting with "Yes" or "No" generation or by fine-tuning a classification head.

LAMBADA is a predict-the-last-word task where the context is a paragraph and the model must predict a specific target word or short phrase. Success requires broad discourse understanding and long-range coherence. LAMBADA is often used to probe whether models capture document-level semantics and maintain coherence over long contexts. Because LAMBADA requires exact-match prediction of the last word, evaluation is strict; some works use multiple-word tolerances or n-gram matching, but the canonical measure is accuracy.

SciQ (Science Questions) is a dataset of science questions constructed with the intention of supporting educational assessment tasks. SciQ examples are multiple-choice and can be used to probe factual scientific knowledge and simple reasoning.

When using these benchmarks, heed the following cautions. Many datasets were created prior to the large-scale pretraining era. If an LLM's pretraining data overlaps the test set (publicly available datasets that have been scraped into large corpora), reported performance may reflect memorization rather than generalization. Additionally, tasks differ in format (multiple-choice, generative, classification), so adapt evaluation methodology appropriately: for multiple-choice, compare summed log-probabilities or use constrained decoding; for generative tasks, compute accuracy through exact-match with normalization or use task-specific scorers. Also consider reporting performance by difficulty slices where available (e.g., ARC-Challenge vs ARC-Easy).

# Evaluation harnesses and toolkits (lm-evaluation-harness)

Reproducible, large-scale evaluation benefits from standardized harnesses that implement datasets, prompts, and scoring in a consistent manner. One widely used toolkit is EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness, which provides a collection of dataset loaders, standardized prompt templates for zero- and few-shot settings, and evaluation scripts that work across different model backends (transformer libraries, API endpoints, or locally hosted models). The harness reduces friction when comparing many models and ensures consistent pre-processing and scoring.

Toolkits like lm-evaluation-harness implement canonical prompt templates for many datasets and expose configuration for shot counts, random seeds for demonstration sampling, and scoring modes (e.g., greedy decoding, log-probability comparison across choices). Using such a harness helps avoid subtle differences in preprocessing that can change results, such as tokenization differences, whether to include newlines, or how to format multiple-choice prompts. It also makes it easier to run ablations (varying number of shots, prompt phrasing, and temperature) and to gather reproducible logs.

When using an evaluation harness, be explicit about the configuration: which version of the toolkit, which prompt templates, how many shots, seed values for example selection, and whether any dataset modifications were made. If you change a template to improve performance (a legitimate research step), report both the canonical and the modified template results and provide the modified templates in supplementary materials or code. Also validate that the harness's tokenization and probability computations align with the model implementation you use; mismatches in tokenization between the model and the harness will produce incorrect log probabilities and unfair comparisons.

Beyond lm-evaluation-harness, other toolkits and platforms exist for evaluation and benchmarking: Hugging Face Datasets, Hugging Face Evaluate, OpenAI Evals, and custom internal suites. Each has tradeoffs in supported datasets, licensing, and ease of extending. Regardless of the tool, the same principles apply: version-control your evaluation code, provide seed values, and release the evaluation scripts and prompt templates when publishing results so other researchers can reproduce and extend your experiments.

# Reporting metrics: accuracy, normalized scores, average across tasks

Choosing and reporting metrics requires care to fairly summarize performance across diverse tasks. The simplest task-level metric is accuracy: the fraction of examples for which the model's prediction equals the ground-truth label. Accuracy is appropriate for multiple-choice, yes/no, and exact-match tasks. For generation tasks where there are multiple acceptable answers, accuracy should be computed after normalization (lowercasing, punctuation removal, whitespace normalization) and possibly accepting multiple references.

For ranking-based multiple-choice evaluation, a robust approach is to compute the log-probability assigned to each choice:
\[
\mathrm{score}(c) = \sum_{t \in \text{tokens}(c)} \log p_\theta(t \mid \text{context}, t_{<}),
\]
and select the choice with the highest sum of token log-probabilities. This selection method avoids confounding by decoding stochasticity and directly leverages the model's internal distribution. Be careful to normalize by token length when choices have differing lengths; typically use average log-probability per token or per-character, or explicitly compute probability of the full choice without length normalization and justify the choice.

When aggregating across multiple tasks, simple averaging of accuracies (macro-average) treats each task equally; this gives small datasets or easier tasks the same weight as large or harder tasks. Alternatively, micro-averaging pools all examples across tasks and computes a single accuracy — this weights tasks by their size. Both approaches have use cases: macro-average avoids domination by large datasets, while micro-average reflects overall per-example performance. Clearly state which averaging method you use.

Normalized scores are commonly used when constructing leaderboards across heterogeneous tasks with differing chance baselines and difficulty. A common normalization maps a raw score $s$ on a task to a normalized score $s'$ in [0, 100] by subtracting a baseline (e.g., random or majority-class accuracy $b$) and dividing by the maximum achievable above-baseline (often 100% - $b$):
\[
s' = 100 \times \frac{s - b}{1 - b}.
\]
For example, transforming a 60% accuracy on a 4-choice dataset where random chance is 25% yields $s' = 100 \times (0.60 - 0.25)/(1 - 0.25) \approx 46.67$. Normalization helps when averaging across tasks with different chance levels; however, it relies on a reasonable choice of baseline. Some benchmarks use expert or human baselines instead of chance to create a "human-normalized" score.

When reporting aggregated results across many tasks, provide per-task results as well as averages. Per-task breakdowns let readers see where the model excels or fails and prevent the average from masking important weaknesses. Also report metrics like median performance and interquartile ranges across tasks to avoid sensitivity to outlier tasks. When reporting few-shot results, include the number of shots and the seed(s) used for demonstration selection and show variance across multiple sample draws.

For generative tasks with open-ended text, consider task-appropriate metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, or more recent embedding-based semantic similarity metrics; be mindful of their limitations — for many reasoning tasks, these metrics correlate weakly with correctness. In contexts where model outputs are used to answer a question with a canonical answer, prefer exact-match or human evaluation.

Finally, always report computational and environmental details alongside metrics: model size, number of parameters, compute used for inference (e.g., tokens processed per second), and the decoding method and temperature. These factors affect reproducing and interpreting results — a slightly higher accuracy may be achieved only with expensive beam search or low-temperature decoding that reduces diversity.

# Statistical significance, reproducibility, and common pitfalls

Statistical significance and reproducibility are critical for credible claims. Randomness in model initialization, dataset sampling (for few-shot demonstration selection), and nondeterministic hardware behavior (e.g., mixed-precision kernels) can produce variability. Report variability measures such as standard error, confidence intervals, or results across multiple seeds. For few-shot experiments where demonstration selection matters, run multiple replicates with different randomly sampled demonstrations and report mean and standard deviation.

Bootstrapping is a robust nonparametric method to estimate confidence intervals for accuracy or other metrics. To compute a bootstrap confidence interval for accuracy, resample the test set with replacement many times (e.g., 1000 bootstrap samples), compute the metric for each resampled set, and then take percentiles of the resulting distribution to form confidence intervals (e.g., 2.5th to 97.5th for a 95% interval). Paired tests are preferable when comparing two models on the same test sets: compute the metric difference per example and test whether the difference is significantly different from zero via a paired bootstrap or a McNemar test for binary outcomes.

Multiple hypothesis testing is a commonplace subtlety: if you evaluate dozens of tasks or many model variants and report the best-performing variant, you are at risk of false discoveries. Correct for multiple comparisons when appropriate (Bonferroni correction is conservative; false discovery rate control such as Benjamini–Hochberg is often more reasonable) or, better, pre-register the primary comparisons and treat exploratory analyses separately.

Reproducibility requires detailed reporting: random seeds, model checkpoints (with hashes), tokenizer versions, hardware, batch sizes, exact prompt templates, evaluation harness and its version, and dataset versions. Provide the code and scripts used for preprocessing and evaluation, or at least enough pseudocode and configuration details so others can reproduce results. When using black-box APIs (e.g., commercial LLM endpoints), record the API version, model name, and the date of evaluation, because API behavior and model contents can change over time.

Common pitfalls

- Data contamination: The model may have seen test examples during pretraining. This is particularly pernicious because the model can memorize answers and appear to generalize. Perform overlap checks (string match, hashing, near-duplicate detection) between pretraining corpora and test sets when possible. For widely scraped pretraining data, such checks may be infeasible; in that case, treat suspect results cautiously and, where possible, evaluate on newly created or private test sets that were guaranteed not to be in pretraining corpora.

- Test leakage through prompt engineering: If you tune prompts on the test set (e.g., selecting templates, few-shot examples, or hyperparameters based on test performance), you leak information and overfit. Use a held-out validation set for prompt tuning. When no validation is available, report the extent of prompt search and treat the final test results as potentially optimistic.

- Cherry-picking results: Reporting only the best-performing configuration (e.g., the best seed or best prompt) without reporting variance misleads readers. Always show mean and spread, and clearly mark which results are averaged versus single-shot bests.

- Incomparable settings: Comparing models evaluated with different context lengths, temperature, decoding strategies, or evaluation templates is misleading. For instance, a model evaluated with greedy decoding may appear to have higher accuracy than one evaluated at temperature 1.0. Standardize these settings when making comparisons, or report results across multiple decoding configurations.

- Mistakes in computing multiple-choice probabilities: Tokenization differences between choice strings and the model's tokenizer can alter log-probability counts. Tokenize choices with the exact tokenizer used by the model before computing per-token log-probabilities. Also, be careful whether to include a leading space or delimiter token before the choice when computing probability: design your scoring so that each choice is compared fairly.

- Over-reliance on aggregated scores: A single aggregate number (mean accuracy) hides behavior differences across tasks, dataset sub-populations, and difficulty levels. Provide per-task breakdowns, per-skill slices where possible, and calibration metrics (e.g., expected calibration error) when predictions are probabilistic and thresholds are used in decision-making.

Best practices summary

To conclude, here are concrete best-practice recommendations derived from the preceding sections. Always report the exact evaluation paradigm (zero-, few-shot with number of shots, or fine-tuned) and the prompt templates or fine-tuning hyperparameters. Use standardized harnesses (and record their versions) and provide code and seed values. For intrinsic metrics, report validation loss in nats (or bits) and perplexity, but remember to specify tokenizer and whether the loss includes prompts. For task evaluations, report per-task metrics and both macro and micro averages, and use normalized scores when aggregating across heterogeneous tasks. Provide measures of variability via repeated runs or bootstrap confidence intervals and use paired tests for model comparisons. Check for and report possible data contamination, and avoid tuning on test data. Finally, be transparent: release evaluation scripts, prompt templates, and checkpoint identifiers so others can reproduce and audit your claims.

Armed with these tools and practices, you will be able to interpret LLM evaluation results critically, design fair experiments, and present reproducible findings that the community can trust and build upon.
