Introduction

As machine learning models have grown larger and more computationally demanding, there is increasing interest in reducing the memory, compute, and energy cost of neural networks. Quantization, the process of representing weights and activations with fewer bits than typical 32-bit floating point, is one powerful approach. At the extreme of quantization are binary and ternary neural networks, which constrain weights and/or activations to a very small discrete set: binary networks use two values (commonly $\{-1,+1\}$ or $\{0,1\}$), while ternary networks use three values (commonly $\{-1,0,+1\}$). These extreme low-bit networks promise large improvements in memory footprint and opportunities for efficient bitwise or sparse computation, but they also present unique training challenges because the discrete constraints break many assumptions underlying standard gradient-based optimization.

This chapter explains the theory and practice of binary and ternary neural networks. We will first develop the formal definitions and motivations for binarizing weights and activations, then move to ternary representations and the role of zero in enabling sparsity and dynamic range. Next, we will explain quantization-aware training versus training from scratch in low precision, and describe core techniques used to train discrete networks, notably the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) and other gradient approximations. Scaling factors are critical to recovering accuracy in low-bit networks, so we examine layer-wise and channel-wise scaling (including common heuristics such as absolute-mean scaling) and how they interact with binarization/ternarization. Finally, we place these techniques in historical context by discussing seminal prior works including BinaryConnect, XNOR-Net, and Ternary Weight Networks, and highlight practical trade-offs and best practices for research and deployment.

# Binary Neural Networks (BNNs): weight and/or activation binarization

Binary Neural Networks (BNNs) constrain either weights, activations, or both to take on two discrete values. Two common conventions are using $\{-1,+1\}$ which centers the representation at zero, or using $\{0,1\}$ which is suitable when non-negativity is desired. For neural networks, the $\{-1,+1\}$ representation is often preferred because it preserves a signed notion of inhibition and excitation and simplifies arithmetic to additions and subtractions. When both weights and activations are binarized, multiplications in a neuron reduce to XNOR or bitwise operations followed by population counts, enabling orders-of-magnitude improvements in inference throughput on specialized hardware.

Formally, given a real-valued weight tensor $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, binarization maps it to a binary tensor $B \in \{-1,+1\}^{d}$. A simple deterministic binarization function is the sign function:
$B = \operatorname{sign}(W)$, where $\operatorname{sign}(x) = +1$ if $x \ge 0$ and $-1$ otherwise. For activations, a similar binarization function maps pre-activation outputs or activations to $\{-1,+1\}$ or $\{0,1\}$ depending on the chosen convention. Using $\{0,1\}$ often requires shifting and scaling or using a different activation mapping such as a hard-sigmoid followed by thresholding.

Binarization drastically reduces memory: 1 bit per weight instead of 32 bits. Compute also becomes simpler: in the simplest case of binary activations and weights, each multiply-accumulate reduces to a bitwise XNOR followed by a population count and a small integer accumulation. However, binarization also reduces representational capacity. The real-valued parameter space is projected onto a discrete hypercube, which can make it harder for the network to fit complex functions and can introduce substantial gradient estimation issues during training because the sign function is non-differentiable almost everywhere.

To offset the lost dynamic range, many practical BNNs use scaling factors combined with the binary tensor. A common representation is $W \approx \alpha B$, where $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}_{+}$ is a positive scaling factor capturing the average magnitude of $W$, and $B \in \{-1,+1\}^{d}$ encodes the sign pattern. During inference, $\alpha$ can be applied as a multiplier to the result of efficient bitwise operations. Such a decomposition retains the inference efficiency of binarized multiplications while giving the network some magnitude flexibility.

Training BNNs often involves keeping a set of underlying real-valued "shadow" parameters $W$ that are updated with gradient-based methods, while forward passes use the binarized version $B$. This allows gradient information to flow into a continuous space while enforcing discrete inference-time constraints. However, even with shadow parameters, we must confront how to backpropagate through the non-differentiable binarization function; this motivates techniques discussed later such as the Straight-Through Estimator.

The choice of whether to binarize weights only, activations only, or both presents different trade-offs. Weight-only binarization achieves memory savings for model parameters and simplifies weight storage, but still requires full-precision activations for intermediate layers, preserving much of the multiply-accumulate cost. Activation-only binarization reduces the cost of forward compute but not parameter memory. Fully binarized networks (both weights and activations) yield the largest inference speedups on specialized hardware but are also the hardest to train while preserving accuracy. Many systems adopt hybrid approaches: for instance, keeping first and last layers in higher precision while binarizing intermediate layers improves accuracy with minor additional cost.

# Ternary networks and ternary weight representations {-1,0,+1}

Ternary networks extend the idea of extreme quantization by allowing three discrete values, typically $\{-1,0,+1\}$. Introducing zero is important: it enables explicit sparsity in the weights, which has several benefits. First, sparsity can reduce the number of effective operations if the hardware or software can exploit it, producing both memory and compute savings beyond what bit-packing alone offers. Second, having a zero state introduces a way to represent small magnitudes and to prune unimportant connections without requiring a separate pruning stage. Third, the ternary representation offers a slightly richer representational capacity than binary while remaining extremely compact (about 1.58 bits per weight on average if optimally compressed).

A simple ternarization maps a real-valued weight $w$ to $\hat{w} \in \{-\Delta,0,\Delta\}$ with a thresholding rule:
\[
\hat{w} = \begin{cases}
+\Delta & \text{if } w > \theta_+,\\[4pt]
0 & \text{if } \theta_- \le w \le \theta_+,\\[4pt]
-\Delta & \text{if } w < \theta_-,
\end{cases}
\]
where $\theta_-$ and $\theta_+$ are thresholds and $\Delta>0$ is a scaling factor (which might be shared across a layer or per-channel). A symmetric choice $\theta_- = -\theta_+$ is common but not required. By pushing small weights to zero, ternary networks can find a sparser connectivity pattern that nevertheless retains most of the important sign information of weights. Like binary networks, a common factorization writes $W \approx \Delta T$ where $T \in \{-1,0,+1\}^{d}$.

Ternary networks offer a middle ground between binary and higher-precision quantization. They are particularly appealing when some connections need to be muted entirely (zero) rather than contributing a small noisy signed value. This can improve stability during training and reduce the sensitivity of the network to small gradient noise. In the inference stage, ternary operations can be implemented efficiently: one approach is to pack ternary values into small multi-bit words (e.g., 2 bits per weight) and use sparse-coded multiplication where zeros skip computation, while non-zero values still benefit from simplified arithmetic.

Choosing thresholds and scaling factors is crucial in ternary networks. Early works learned thresholds as part of training or derived them analytically from the distribution of weight magnitudes. The exact fraction of zeros created by ternarization affects both accuracy and computational savings: too many zeros and the model underfits; too few and the ternary network behaves similarly to binary, losing the sparsity advantage.

Ternary networks are often combined with other techniques such as batch normalization, residual connections, and selective full-precision layers to maintain accuracy. In many practical scenarios, ternary weights with full-precision activations (or with higher-bit activations) strike an attractive balance: they compress the model parameters significantly and allow some structured pruning-like sparsity without completely sacrificing representational richness.

# Quantization-aware training (QAT) vs training from scratch

When deploying quantized networks, there are two main paradigms for obtaining a quantized model: quantization-aware training (QAT) and training from scratch in low precision. Each approach has advantages and limitations.

Quantization-aware training begins with a real-valued network or a training process that treats quantization as part of the forward computation. In QAT, during training we simulate the effects of quantization (for example, by applying a discretization operator to weights and/or activations during the forward pass) so that the model parameters can adapt to quantization noise. The backward pass typically uses gradient approximations (such as STE) to propagate gradients through quantized operations. Because QAT exposes the model to quantized behavior during optimization, it usually yields better final accuracy than naive post-training quantization, especially at very low bitwidths like binary or ternary. QAT is especially valuable when a pretrained high-precision model is available: we can fine-tune with quantization in place, leveraging the pretrained representations as a good initialization.

Training from scratch in low precision means initializing parameters randomly and performing all training steps with quantization in the forward path (and appropriate gradient approximations). There are cases where training from scratch can achieve comparable or even superior performance to QAT from a pretrained model, particularly when the model architecture and training recipe are designed specifically for low-precision training. Training from scratch avoids the need to store a high-precision pretrained checkpoint and can lead to solutions that are better adapted to the quantization constraints. However, training from scratch can be more sensitive to hyperparameters, require more careful regularization, and can converge more slowly or be more likely to get stuck in poor local minima, especially for extremely low-bit settings like fully binary networks.

An intermediate approach is to start from a pretrained full-precision model and progressively quantize, or to use a two-stage process: first fine-tune weights with QAT in relatively higher quantization (e.g., 8-bit) and then progressively reduce bitwidth to binary or ternary while continuing fine-tuning. Progressive schemes can ease optimization by routing parameters through a sequence of easier tasks toward the final hard quantized network.

From a practical standpoint, QAT is widely used for deployment because it leverages existing pretrained models and typically requires fewer epochs to reach a satisfactory quantized accuracy. Training from scratch in low precision is an active research area and may be preferable for co-design with specialized hardware or when one wants to avoid reliance on a large high-precision pretrained model.

# Straight-Through Estimator (STE) and gradient approximations

A central challenge in training binary and ternary networks is that quantization functions such as $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$ are non-differentiable or have zero derivative almost everywhere, so standard backpropagation cannot directly compute useful gradients. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) is the most commonly used practical workaround. The STE was introduced to allow gradient signals to pass through discrete-valued units by replacing the true derivative of the discretization function with a surrogate during the backward pass.

Suppose we have a forward binarization $B = q(W)$ where $q(\cdot)$ is the sign function or a thresholding operator. We want to compute gradients of a loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $W$. Using STE, we compute the forward pass normally (i.e., using $B$), but on the backward pass we approximate
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial B} \cdot \frac{\partial \tilde{q}(W)}{\partial W},
\]
where $\tilde{q}(\cdot)$ is a differentiable surrogate for $q(\cdot)$, or often simply the identity function clipped to a range. A widely used STE is to pretend that $q(\cdot)$ is the identity within some interval and zero outside; concretely, for $q(x) = \operatorname{sign}(x)$, one STE sets
\[
\frac{d\tilde{q}}{dx} = \mathbf{1}_{|x| \le 1},
\]
i.e., pass gradients unchanged when $x$ is in $[-1,1]$ and block them outside. Another simple STE uses $\frac{d\tilde{q}}{dx} = 1$ everywhere, literally copying the gradient through the non-linearity. The choice of surrogate derivative can be considered as a form of gradient clipping or regularization for the shadow real-valued parameters.

STE is not an unbiased estimator of the true gradient, and it lacks formal guarantees; however, in practice it has proven effective for enabling gradient-based optimization to find useful discrete representations. STE can be interpreted as performing optimization in a "relaxed" continuous space while the forward pass adheres to a discrete constraint. The gradients guide the shadow weights so that their sign or thresholds produce useful quantized values.

Beyond the basic STE, researchers have developed refined gradient approximations to improve convergence and stability. For instance, soft approximations of the sign function using differentiable functions like hyperbolic tangent $\tanh(\beta x)$ with an increasing slope parameter $\beta$ can be employed: during training one may start with a small $\beta$ (making the function smooth) and gradually anneal $\beta$ to a large value so that $\tanh(\beta x)$ approximates $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ more closely. Other methods use probabilistic relaxations where weights are represented as Bernoulli or categorical distributions and optimized using reparameterization-like techniques or policy gradients; such approaches can bring in variance issues but provide alternative principled estimators.

In ternary networks, STE is used analogously, approximating thresholds with differentiable box functions or using piecewise linear surrogates for the ternarization mapping. Some works learn thresholds themselves and propagate gradients into threshold parameters; in those cases the STE must be defined consistently so that gradients can update both shadow weights and thresholds.

An important practical detail is the handling of gradient scale. Because binarized forward passes compress magnitudes to a small set, gradients computed with respect to binary variables can have different scale properties than in full precision. Maintaining separate learning rates, using adaptive optimizers, or scaling gradients by the scaling factors used in forward quantization is common.

Overall, STE is a pragmatic and widely used tool for training BNNs and ternary networks. Although it lacks rigorous theoretical justification in general, its empirical effectiveness and simplicity have made it central to most successful low-bit training recipes.

# Scaling factors, channel-wise vs layer-wise scaling (e.g., abs-mean)

When discrete representations compress values to $\{-1,+1\}$ or $\{-1,0,+1\}$, the network loses amplitude information. Scaling factors recover some of that amplitude. The typical setup writes the quantized weight tensor as
\[
\hat{W} = \alpha \, Q(W),
\]
where $Q(W)$ is the discrete sign or ternary tensor, and $\alpha$ is a positive scaling factor. The same idea can apply to activations: quantized activations may be multiplied by a learned or computed scale. Choosing how to compute and apply $\alpha$ is vital to preserving network performance.

A simple and effective choice is to choose a layer-wise scalar $\alpha$ for each convolutional or fully connected layer. One canonical method, introduced in XNOR-Net, computes $\alpha$ as the average of absolute values of weights in the layer:
\[
\alpha_{\text{layer}} = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d |W_i|,
\]
where $d$ is the number of weights in the layer. The intuition is that $\operatorname{sign}(W)$ captures sign information and the mean absolute value captures overall magnitude. During inference, one computes binary convolutions between $Q(W)$ and binary activations, and then multiplies the resulting integer output by $\alpha_{\text{layer}}$. For convolutional layers with multiple output channels, this layer-wise $ \alpha$ is shared across channels, which is cheap and simple.

Channel-wise scaling refines this by computing a separate $\alpha_c$ for each output channel (or sometimes each input channel), capturing per-channel magnitude differences:
\[
\alpha_{c} = \frac{1}{d_c} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_c} |W_i|,
\]
where $\mathcal{I}_c$ indexes the weights associated with channel $c$ and $d_c$ is the number of such weights. Channel-wise scaling can improve accuracy because different channels often develop different activation magnitudes and statistics. Using channel-wise scales increases storage marginally (one scalar per channel) but can recover a significant portion of the accuracy lost by binary quantization.

Other scaling heuristics exist beyond the abs-mean. For instance, some methods use the root-mean-square (RMS) or the $L_2$ norm:
\[
\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d W_i^2},
\]
which corresponds to matching the energy of the real-valued weights. RMS scaling can be preferable when the distribution of weight magnitudes has heavy tails. Alternatively, one can learn scaling factors as trainable parameters, allowing gradient descent to adjust the magnitude of each channel or layer to optimize performance. Learned scales can adapt to nonstationary training dynamics but add a small number of parameters.

When activations are quantized, analogous scale choices apply. For activations, dynamic ranges can vary with input data distribution and batch normalization; therefore, scaling activations often interacts with normalization layers and must be calibrated carefully. Some methods compute activation scales per batch or use moving averages to estimate ranges for inference.

Another important practical point is where to place scaling relative to other operations. If one writes a convolution with binary weights and binary activations as
\[
y = \alpha_W \, \alpha_A \, (B_W \ast B_A),
\]
then one may absorb one of the scales into a batch-normalization layer or fuse scales to reduce the number of multiplications at inference. In architectures with subsequent batch normalization and affine transforms, scaling factors can often be merged into those transforms at inference time.

Finally, combining scaling with channel-wise vs layer-wise choices also affects hardware implementation. Layer-wise scaling requires only a single multiplier per layer, which is cheaper. Channel-wise scaling increases multipliers linearly with the number of channels but still far fewer than per-weight multipliers. Choosing the granularity of scaling is therefore a trade-off between representational flexibility (favoring finer granularity) and inference cost and simplicity (favoring coarser granularity).

# Prior works: BinaryConnect, XNOR-Net, Ternary Weight Networks

Historical context helps highlight how the area evolved from conceptual proposals to practically useful architectures.

BinaryConnect (Courbariaux et al., 2015) is one of the earliest influential works on binary weights. It proposed training neural networks with weights constrained to $\{-1,+1\}$ during forward and backward passes, while preserving continuous shadow weights for parameter updates. The key idea in BinaryConnect was retaining full-precision weights for gradient accumulation while using binarized weights to propagate activations and compute gradients. BinaryConnect demonstrated that binarized weights could be used in some tasks without catastrophic harm to performance, and it popularized the use of STE-like mechanisms to allow gradients to update shadow weights.

Building on these ideas, XNOR-Net (Rastegari et al., 2016) proposed a practical and improved binarization scheme that introduced per-layer scaling factors and binarized both weights and activations. XNOR-Net demonstrated that binary networks could be implemented efficiently using XNOR and bitcount operations and that combining sign quantization with an appropriate scaling factor substantially improved accuracy over naive binarization. XNOR-Net computed a layer-wise scaling factor $\alpha$ as the mean absolute value of the weights and showed that applying this factor after efficient binary convolutions recovered large parts of the lost accuracy. XNOR-Net also highlighted practical engineering considerations, such as leaving the first and last layers in higher precision for better accuracy.

Ternary Weight Networks (TWN) (Li et al., 2016) introduced ternary quantization with values $\{-\Delta,0,\Delta\}$ and proposed a thresholding strategy that sets small-magnitude weights to zero. TWN derived thresholds and scaling factors theoretically to minimize quantization error under certain assumptions, and reported that ternary quantization often yields better accuracy than binary for a modest increase in storage. TWN and related works showed that ternary networks can often achieve near full-precision accuracy in smaller networks and are particularly useful when some sparsity is desired.

Subsequent works extended these foundational ideas in various directions. For example, Binarized Neural Networks (BNN) (Hubara et al., 2016) demonstrated binarizing both weights and activations in convolutional and fully connected networks and introduced additional training techniques such as gradient clipping and careful learning rate schedules. XNOR-Net++ and follow-up papers explored channel-wise scaling, improved activation binarization functions, and ways to integrate batch normalization more effectively. Other lines of work developed learned quantization thresholds, probabilistic binarization, improved STE variants, and hardware-aware training methods.

Collectively, these prior works established practical recipes: maintain shadow real-valued weights, use STE or similar approximations to backpropagate through discrete functions, use scaling factors (preferably channel-wise when accuracy is critical), keep first and last layers in higher precision, and combine quantization with batch normalization and appropriate regularization. While state-of-the-art full-precision networks still outperform extreme low-bit networks on many tasks, BNNs and ternary networks remain attractive in contexts where memory, power, or extreme throughput are primary constraints.

# Practical trade-offs, tips, and research directions

Understanding the trade-offs helps design training and deployment strategies. The primary gains of binary and ternary networks are reductions in memory and inference compute. For fully binarized weights and activations, multiply-accumulate operations become bitwise logical operations and integer counts, enabling very fast inference on hardware that supports bitwise parallelism. Storage drops to 1–2 bits per parameter, enabling large models to fit into small caches or embedded memory. Power consumption can fall substantially because bitwise operations and small memory transfers are cheaper than floating-point multiply-adds and large DRAM traffic.

These gains come at the cost of accuracy and more difficult optimization. On challenging datasets and large models, fully binary networks typically suffer non-trivial accuracy degradation compared to full-precision models. Ternary networks often offer a better trade-off, recovering more accuracy for a small increase in storage. Hybrid strategies—such as keeping activations or the first and last layers in higher precision, or using mixed-precision for sensitive layers—often achieve much of the memory and compute benefit while limiting the accuracy gap.

From a training perspective, effective recipes include: using batch normalization between quantized layers to stabilize activation distributions; using shadow weights and STE for gradient flow; carefully designing learning rate schedules and potentially using separate learning rates for scale parameters; initializing from pretrained models when possible; and experimenting with channel-wise scaling and learned thresholds. When deploying, consider fusing scales and batch-norm parameters to minimize runtime multipliers and exploit hardware-specific optimizations like bit-packing and zero-skipping logic.

Research directions remain active. On the theoretical side, understanding why STE works and when it fails is an ongoing topic. Alternative gradient estimators and probabilistic approaches that offer lower variance or better bias properties are being explored. On the systems side, co-designing quantization schemes for particular hardware accelerators (e.g., FPGAs, ASICs, and specialized inference engines) can produce significant end-to-end gains. There is also interest in combining quantization with network architecture search to find architectures that are naturally robust to extreme quantization.

Concluding remarks

Binary and ternary neural networks are powerful tools for extreme model compression and efficient inference. They require careful handling: appropriate scale factors, gradient approximations such as STE, and training strategies such as QAT or carefully tuned training-from-scratch recipes. Seminal works like BinaryConnect, XNOR-Net, and Ternary Weight Networks established practical techniques that remain central to modern low-bit research. For students and researchers, mastering these ideas and their trade-offs is crucial for understanding both the algorithmic and systems aspects of deploying neural networks under tight resource constraints.
