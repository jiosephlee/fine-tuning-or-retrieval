Chapter 8: Quantized Training and Optimization Strategies

Introduction

Training neural networks whose weights, activations, or gradients are represented with very low numeric precision (for example 8-bit, 4-bit, or even binary/ternary) requires more than simply replacing arithmetic with lower-precision operations at inference time. The discrete, highly non-linear mapping from real-valued parameters to quantized values alters the learning dynamics, changes gradient flow, and can introduce instabilities that prevent convergence or drastically reduce final accuracy. This chapter develops the full set of strategies used in practice to train low-bit models: how to build a quantization-aware training pipeline, how to estimate gradients through discrete quantizers, how to scale and stabilize losses and gradients, how to choose clipping and per-layer scaling heuristics, how to initialize and warm up quantized parameters, and how to measure and diagnose failure modes that are specific to low-bit training.

The goal is to provide intuition and practical recipes so you can read papers that propose new quantized training techniques, implement experiments reliably, and interpret why certain tricks are necessary. Throughout the chapter we assume the base training objective is one of the standard supervised or self-supervised losses, while the parameterization or forward pass contains quantizers that map continuous tensors to a low-bit representational set. Where appropriate we write mathematics in terms of a weight tensor $W$ and its quantized counterpart $\widehat{W}$.

# Quantization-aware training (QAT) pipelines

Quantization-aware training (QAT) is the practice of simulating low-precision numerics during training so that learned parameters and activations are adapted to the quantized domain. The high-level idea is to expose the optimizer to the same distortions that will occur at inference, thereby enabling the network to learn representations robust to quantization error.

A typical QAT pipeline maintains two copies of each parameter: a floating-point "shadow" copy $W$ that is updated by the optimizer, and a quantized copy $\widehat{W}=Q(W)$ that is used in the forward pass. The quantizer $Q(\cdot)$ depends on the chosen numeric format (uniform affine, symmetric, power-of-two scaling, ternary, binary, etc.) and may involve per-layer or per-channel scale and zero-point parameters. During forward propagation, every linear or convolutional layer uses $\widehat{W}$ (and quantized activations if activation quantization is used). During backward propagation, the gradient is computed with respect to $\widehat{W}$ but is passed back to $W$ through a gradient estimator (see the STE discussion below). The optimizer updates the shadow weights $W$, and possibly also the quantization parameters such as per-channel scales or zero-points if they are learned.

QAT pipelines vary in how they represent and update quantization parameters. Some pipelines fix scales based on calibration (running statistics collected from a batch of data, e.g., using maximum absolute value or percentile clipping), while others treat scales as learnable parameters and perform gradient updates on them. Each approach has tradeoffs: fixed calibration is simpler and often more stable, but learned scaling can recover some accuracy by adapting the dynamic range during training. A hybrid approach is common: initialize per-layer/per-channel scales from calibration and then allow small gradient-based adjustments with constrained learning rates.

An important implementation detail is when to simulate low-precision. In full QAT, both forward activations and weights are quantized in the forward pass. In partially quantized or mixed-precision training, only some components (e.g., weights) are quantized while activations or gradients remain in floating point. Mixed-precision QAT is useful because it reduces the introduced error while still imposing constraints on weights. Training with quantized activations generally makes optimization harder than weight-only quantization because activations' quantization interacts with batchnorm statistics and causes more non-linear error.

Another consideration is how strictly to enforce the discrete set during forward passes. Hard quantization maps continuous values to exact discrete levels. Soft or stochastic quantization—sampling among nearby discrete levels according to a probability distribution—is useful in some research settings because it introduces gradient signal and exploration, but it complicates implementation and often slows convergence. Deterministic hard quantization with straight-through gradient estimators (STE) is the most widely used in production QAT.

Finally, QAT requires careful bookkeeping for regularization, weight decay, and optimizer hyperparameters. Weight decay typically applies to the real-valued shadow weights $W$. If per-layer scales are learned, they should typically not be regularized in the same way as weights (e.g., different weight decay or learning rates). When using batch normalization, one must decide whether to fold batchnorm into quantized layers for inference or simulate folded layers during training to align training and inference behavior. In short, build the QAT pipeline so that forward computation closely matches inference, and make deliberate choices about which quantities are learned versus calibrated.

# Gradient estimation for discrete weights (STE variants) and regularization

The quantizer $Q(\cdot)$ is usually non-differentiable: it maps a continuous input to a discrete set. A standard practical approach to obtain gradients is the straight-through estimator (STE). The original STE, as introduced by Bengio et al., simply substitutes the backward gradient of the quantizer with the gradient of the identity function. Concretely, if $\widehat{W}=Q(W)$ and the loss is $\mathcal{L}(\widehat{W})$, the backward pass uses
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widehat{W}} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{|W| \leq T},
\]
where $\mathbf{1}_{|W| \leq T}$ is an optional clipping indicator used in some STE variants that blocks gradient flow when $W$ is outside a certain range. More simply, the plain STE uses
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widehat{W}}.
\]
This lets gradient-based optimization proceed despite the discrete forward map.

There are many variants and refinements of the STE that aim to improve gradient quality. One refinement is the use of a differentiable surrogate for $Q(\cdot)$ during the backward pass. For example, use a piecewise-linear derivative that reflects the quantization interval: if $Q$ bins inputs into steps of size $s$, define a surrogate derivative that scales gradients by a function of the distance from the nearest quantization boundary. Another approach is soft rounding: represent quantization as a weighted sum of neighboring discrete levels and use those weights to propagate gradients, effectively smoothing $Q$ during backpropagation. This allows gradients to reflect how close $W$ is to a boundary and encourages movement toward better discrete choices.

For some discrete formats (binary or ternary), one can design STEs that incorporate knowledge of the quantization-specific mapping. For binary quantization, $\widehat{W} = \alpha \operatorname{sign}(W)$ where $\alpha$ is a learned or computed scale. A common STE update for binary weights multiplies the incoming gradient by $\alpha$ and optionally masks it by an attention window that suppresses gradients for large $|W|$ values, which are already confidently assigned to a sign bit. This reduces oscillation of weights that are already strongly quantized.

Regularization plays a complementary role: because quantization effectively constrains parameters to few discrete states, the training objective should encourage the shadow weights to lie in "good" regions relative to those states. One useful regularizer is to minimize the quantization error directly: add a term $\lambda \|W - Q(W)\|^2$ to the loss. This penalizes shadow weights that are far from their quantized counterparts and encourages convergence of $W$ to quantized levels. Another helpful regularizer is to encourage magnitude separation for binary/ternary schemes: penalizing small magnitudes that cause frequent sign changes helps stabilize discrete assignments.

Entropy-based regularizers or sparsity-promoting terms can be used to steer the distribution of quantized values. For instance, in ternary networks encouraging more zeros (sparsity) can reduce overall quantization error or computation cost, so a regularizer that increases the probability mass at zero can be introduced. Conversely, when dynamic range is needed, regularization that prevents collapse to a few levels can be used.

When combining STE with regularizers, tuning the strength and schedule of regularization is critical: too strong a quantization-loss penalty can prevent the model from exploring useful real-valued configurations early in training, whereas too weak a penalty allows shadow weights to drift away from discrete levels and produce large inference discrepancies.

# Loss scaling and stabilizing techniques for low-precision training

Low-precision training often causes numerical underflow or vanishing/large gradients, especially when gradients themselves are quantized or when mixed-precision arithmetic is used. Loss scaling is a technique originally popularized in the context of mixed-precision training (FP16 + FP32) that rescales the loss to avoid gradient underflow. In quantized training, similar principles apply: increasing the effective magnitude of gradients or certain parameter updates can improve numerical stability and optimization dynamics.

Static loss scaling multiplies the loss by a constant factor $S$ during the forward pass, and the gradients are divided by $S$ before the optimizer step. This simple trick preserves the relative gradient ratios but raises the absolute magnitude seen by limited-precision representations, preventing small gradient components from being rounded to zero. In practice, choose $S$ so that the largest gradient entries are still representable without overflow; this is often done by trial or by tracking moving maxima.

Dynamic loss scaling automatically adjusts $S$ during training. Start with a large $S$ and monitor whether gradients overflow (producing NaNs or Infs). If overflow is detected, reduce $S$ (e.g., divide by 2) and skip the optimizer step for that batch. If no overflow is observed for many steps, increase $S$ slowly. Dynamic loss scaling is robust and widely used in training with small numeric ranges.

Besides global loss scaling, layer-wise or parameter-group scaling can be beneficial. Different layers have different gradient magnitudes, so a single global scale can either be too small for some layers or too large for others. A simple heuristic is to track per-layer gradient norms and apply layer-wise multipliers to normalize them to a target magnitude. This can be implemented as adaptive learning rates or by modifying the optimizer's preconditioning (e.g., per-parameter Adam or RMSProp already adapt step sizes). For quantized training, combining per-layer scaling with an adaptive optimizer often yields better stability.

Gradient clipping, discussed further below, is another key stabilizer. When a small subset of gradients has very large magnitude due to quantization noise or mismatch between forward/backward quantization, clipping prevents a single update from destroying learning progress. Clipping thresholds can be global (based on the overall gradient norm) or per-layer.

Finally, techniques such as label smoothing, larger batch sizes, or stronger data augmentations can indirectly stabilize low-precision training by smoothing the loss landscape and reducing label noise sensitivity. These are not specific to quantization but often interact with quantization effects, so treat them as part of the stabilization toolkit.

# Weight clipping, normalization, and per-layer scaling heuristics

Making the shadow weights live in a controlled range is important for quantized models because quantization maps continuous intervals to a small number of discrete levels. If shadow weights wander too far outside the intervals used in calibration or scale estimation, their quantized values saturate and learnable dynamics can stall. Weight clipping, normalization, and per-layer scaling are used to prevent such pathologies.

Weight clipping explicitly clamps shadow weights to a specified bounded interval: $W \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(W, -c, c)$ for some $c>0$. Clipping can be applied hard after each optimizer step, or softly via a penalty that encourages weights to remain within bounds. For symmetric uniform quantization with scale $s$, a natural clipping bound is the quantizer's representational limit, e.g., $c = s \cdot L$ where $L$ is the maximum representable integer magnitude. Clipping prevents weights from drifting into ranges where quantization saturates and gradient information is lost.

Normalization techniques such as weight standardization or weight normalization can be valuable. Weight standardization rescales each filter's weights to have zero mean and unit variance before convolution, which stabilizes activation distributions and improves convergence. Weight normalization re-parameterizes $W = g \frac{v}{\|v\|}$ with a scalar $g$ and vector $v$, separating magnitude and direction; this can be useful because the scalar $g$ can be quantized or handled differently than the direction. These techniques reduce per-channel variability and make per-layer scale choices more predictable.

Per-layer and per-channel scaling heuristics determine how the quantizer's scale parameter $s$ is set. A commonly used heuristic is to use the maximum absolute value of a weight tensor (or a percentile such as 99.9th) to set $s$: $s = \frac{\max |W|}{L}$ where $L$ is the maximum integer value. Percentile-based choices reduce sensitivity to outliers. Per-channel scaling (for convolutions using different scales for each output channel) often yields much better final accuracy than a single per-layer scale because weight magnitudes can vary significantly across channels.

Another practical heuristic is to tie scales of adjacent layers or to initialize scales by folding batchnorm parameters into convolution weights and then computing appropriate per-channel scales. When activations are quantized too, using matched scales between weights and activation ranges can minimize the cross-quantization error. In hardware-constrained settings, you might prefer power-of-two scales or shared scales to simplify fixed-point multiplication; such discrete constraints should be integrated into the scale-setting heuristic early in training.

It is also useful to maintain moving averages of scales and apply minor smoothing, so that scale updates are not too abrupt. Abrupt scale changes cause spikes in quantized outputs and can destabilize training. Some pipelines update scale only every few steps or apply an exponential moving average with a small decay to ensure slow, controlled changes.

# Initialization strategies and warm-up phases for quantized models

Initialization plays a more prominent role in quantized networks than in full-precision training because initial weight magnitudes determine initial quantized assignments and thus the early dynamics. Poor initialization can cause many parameters to be clipped or pushed to extreme quantized values, making recovery difficult.

A recommended strategy is to initialize shadow weights $W$ using the same schemes as for full-precision networks (Xavier/Glorot, Kaiming/He) but with additional care for per-channel variance. For per-channel quantization, scale the initialization so that each channel has similar dynamic range. That may mean applying channel-wise scaling after the standard initialization to equalize $\operatorname{std}(W_{\text{channel}})$ across channels.

Warm-up procedures are critical. The idea is to let the network parameters move in floating-point for some initial phase before enforcing the full quantized constraints. One simple warm-up schedule uses soft quantization at the start: either use higher precision activations/weights during the first few epochs or use a softened quantizer that interpolates between continuous and discrete values. For example, you can anneal the STE surrogate from an identity mapping to the full hard quantizer over the first $N$ steps, or slowly reduce the bit-width from a higher precision down to the target precision.

An alternative warm-up is to start training with quantized weights but to freeze quantization parameters (e.g., scales) and to disable aggressive regularization or weight decay for early iterations. Another practical warming technique is to train for a few epochs without activation quantization, only quantizing the weights; then, after the model has found a reasonable weight configuration, enable activation quantization. This staged quantization reduces the simultaneous shocks to both weights and activations.

Learning rate warm-up is also helpful. Use a smaller learning rate for the first few epochs and increase it gradually. Because quantization introduces high sensitivity to large updates (a single large update can change many quantized assignments), starting with conservative step sizes reduces oscillation. Pair this with conservative optimizer choices—SGD with momentum and small weight decay is still a robust default; adaptive optimizers like Adam can be used but often require tuned learning rates and weight decay settings.

When using learned scaling parameters, initialize scales using calibration data before training begins. For example, run a forward pass on a few mini-batches of real data and set initial activation ranges or weight ranges accordingly. Freezing these initial scales for a short warm-up period stabilizes early training.

Finally, when retraining from a pre-trained full-precision checkpoint (fine-tuning for quantization), a moderate number of epochs at a low learning rate with gentle warm-up and possibly only quantizing weights initially usually yields the best results. Fine-tuning allows leveraging pre-learned structure while adapting to quantization noise.

# Measuring and diagnosing training instability with low-bit parameters

Because quantized training introduces new failure modes, having systematic diagnostics is essential. There are several quantitative and qualitative signals to monitor during training to detect instability and guide remedial actions.

Monitor gradient statistics per layer and per parameter group: mean, standard deviation, maximum absolute value, and the fraction of near-zero entries. Large variance or sudden spikes in gradient magnitudes often indicate inconsistency between forward quantization and backward gradient estimation. If certain layers consistently have much larger gradient norms than others, adjust learning rates, apply per-layer scaling, or clip gradients for those layers.

Track the shadow-to-quantized discrepancy measured by $\|W - Q(W)\|$ normalized per layer or channel. A growing discrepancy indicates that shadow weights are moving away from their quantized representatives and that the model may not be learning to close the gap, which will manifest as inference accuracy loss. If the discrepancy is large and persistent, increase quantization-loss regularization, reduce learning rates, or perform stronger weight clipping.

Measure assignment stability for discrete-valued parameters. For binary or ternary networks, compute the fraction of weights whose quantized value changes between consecutive checkpoints or mini-batches. High assignment churn—many weights flipping frequently—suggests that the optimization is not settling into stable basins. Remedies include reducing learning rates, introducing a regularization term that penalizes flips, or applying a momentum-like smoothing to the quantized assignments.

Observe activation distributions and their quantized histograms. If activations frequently saturate quantization bins (many values hitting the maximum or minimum quantized level), the chosen scale is too small or data has outliers. Recalibrate scales using a percentile threshold or increase dynamic range.

Use loss landscape diagnostics like sharpness metrics. For example, track the maximum change in loss under small perturbations of weights (an approximation to local sharpness). Quantized networks often prefer flatter minima as they are more robust to discrete perturbations. If the optimizer converges to a very sharp minimum, consider stronger regularization, larger batch sizes, or explicit flatness-promoting objectives.

Watch for training catastrophes such as exploding gradients producing NaNs or Infs. When these occur, log the steps back to the event and compare parameter/gradient norms before and after. Often these catastrophes are triggered by abrupt changes in learned scales or by sudden saturation in activation quantizers. To mitigate, add gradient clipping, enable dynamic loss scaling, or smooth scale updates.

Visualizing layer-wise learning curves such as the per-layer training loss contribution, parameter norm, quantized-value histograms, and gradient norms provides intuition. Coupling these visual checks with automated alerts (e.g., when the fraction of saturated activations exceeds some threshold) speeds debugging.

Design experiments systematically: when diagnosing, change only one factor at a time (e.g., learning rate, scale updating frequency, or warm-up length). Keep a stable reference run in full precision or a higher precision quantized version to compare. This allows you to attribute observed behavior to specific changes confidently.

In summary, build a monitoring dashboard that reports (1) gradient statistics, (2) shadow vs quantized-distance, (3) assignment churn for discrete parameters, (4) activation saturation metrics, and (5) occurrences of NaN/Inf. These will help detect early signs of instability and point to targeted fixes like loss scaling, clipping, or annealing strategies.

Conclusion

Quantized training is a rich intersection of numerical analysis, discrete optimization, and empirical engineering. The techniques presented in this chapter—QAT pipelines with shadow weights, STE and its refinements, loss and gradient scaling, weight clipping and normalization, careful initialization and warm-up, and thorough diagnostics—are the practical foundation for training accurate low-bit models. The interplay among these pieces is crucial: the best results are typically achieved by combining several strategies (for example, warm-up + per-channel scales + dynamic loss scaling + STE with a consistency regularizer), rather than relying on a single trick. As you read current research papers, decompose their methods into these basic components to understand what is new and how it affects the training dynamics.
