Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a principled framework for learning to make sequences of decisions to maximize long-term reward. When applied to sequence generation and language models, RL lets us optimize models directly for application-level objectives that may be non-differentiable or not well aligned with maximum likelihood training (for example, BLEU, ROUGE, human preference scores, or correctness of downstream actions). This chapter develops the fundamentals of RL as adapted to sequence models. We begin by casting sequence generation as a Markov decision process (MDP), then derive the core policy gradient methods and the REINFORCE estimator. We explain on-policy versus off-policy learning, introduce actor-critic algorithms that use value functions to reduce variance, and explore baseline and variance reduction techniques in detail. We carefully compare per-token and per-sequence reward signals and explain their implications for credit assignment. Finally, we discuss exploration versus exploitation during decoding and how RL techniques interact with common decoding strategies used in language modeling. Throughout, the focus is on intuition, practical considerations, and the mathematical expressions you will need to read and implement research papers in this area.

# Markov decision processes (MDPs) in the context of text generation

An MDP formalizes an environment in which an agent takes actions, receives rewards, and transitions between states. To apply RL to text generation, we need to map the pieces of the MDP to the components of sequence modeling.

An MDP is usually defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the action space, $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the transition probability, $r(s, a)$ is the reward function, and $\gamma \in [0,1]$ is the discount factor. In sequence generation, states and actions are naturally interpreted in terms of tokens and partial sequences. A common and useful formulation is to treat a state $s_t$ as the sequence of tokens generated so far (including any conditioning context such as a prompt): $s_t = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_t)$. The action $a_t$ is the next token $x_{t+1}$ that the model selects. The transition is deterministic in the sense that taking action $a_t$ in state $s_t$ produces the next state $s_{t+1} = (s_t, a_t)$. The episode corresponds to generating until a special end-of-sequence token is produced or until a fixed maximum length $T$ is reached.

Two aspects of this framing deserve emphasis. First, the state contains all earlier tokens, so the Markov property holds: the future depends on the past only through the current partial sequence, given a sufficiently expressive policy. Second, the action space is typically the discrete vocabulary of tokens, which may be large (tens of thousands). These characteristics lead to high-dimensional, partially deterministic MDPs that are challenging for standard RL algorithms.

Reward design is a central modeling choice. A common setup is to assign a reward only at the end of the sequence, $r_T$, representing an evaluation metric computed on the full generated text (for example, task success, human score, or BLEU). Alternatively, one can provide intermediate or per-token rewards that reflect fluency, syntactic constraints, or partial task completion. The discount factor $\gamma$ is often set to $1$ for sequence tasks with finite horizon because we care about the total undiscounted reward over the generated sequence; however, $\gamma < 1$ can be used to emphasize earlier tokens or to stabilize learning.

Because the transition dynamics are often deterministic and the environment is known (the state evolution given an action is explicit), the hardest part of the problem is exploration and credit assignment: deciding how to assign credit to each token choice for the final outcome, and how to search or sample sequences effectively in a very large discrete space.

# Policy gradient theorem and REINFORCE

Policy gradient methods optimize a parametric policy directly by following the gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters. Let $\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ be a stochastic policy parameterized by $\theta$; in sequence modeling this is typically a softmax over vocabulary logits produced by a neural network conditioned on the partial sequence $s_t$. The objective is the expected cumulative reward from the starting state distribution, which for an episode of length $T$ can be written as

$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ R(\tau) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t) \right],$

where $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots)$ denotes a trajectory sampled from the policy. The gradient of $J(\theta)$ is given by the policy gradient theorem. A compact and frequently used form of the theorem is:

$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, G_t \right],$

where $G_t = \sum_{t'=t}^{T-1} \gamma^{t'-t} r(s_{t'}, a_{t'})$ is the return following time $t$. The intuition behind this expression is that increasing the log-probability of actions that lead to high future returns will increase expected return; the gradient aggregates contributions from all time steps weighted by their resulting future return.

REINFORCE is the Monte Carlo estimator of this gradient introduced by Williams. Concretely, given $N$ sampled trajectories $\tau^{(i)}$ from the current policy, the REINFORCE estimator computes

$\hat{g} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(a_t^{(i)} \mid s_t^{(i)}\big)\, G_t^{(i)}.$

This estimator is unbiased: its expectation equals the true policy gradient. In language models, REINFORCE allows us to backpropagate through the stochastic sampling of tokens by using the log-derivative trick. For example, if the reward is a scalar quality score of the entire generated sequence, we can sample sequences from the policy, compute the reward for each sequence, and use the REINFORCE estimator to update the policy to increase the probability of sequences with higher reward.

Despite being unbiased, REINFORCE suffers from high variance, particularly when rewards are sparse and trajectories are long. The per-step score function $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ can be large or unstable, and multiplying it by a noisy return $G_t$ results in noisy gradient estimates requiring many samples to converge. This motivates variance reduction techniques such as baselines, value functions, and actor-critic methods, which we cover next.

# On-policy vs. off-policy learning

A key algorithmic distinction in RL is whether learning uses data generated by the current policy (on-policy) or by a different policy or replay buffer (off-policy). This distinction is practically important in sequence modeling because sampling from a large model can be expensive, and one might want to reuse previously generated data or leverage demonstrations.

On-policy learning requires that the trajectories used to estimate the gradient are sampled from the same policy being updated (or at least from a policy that is sufficiently close). REINFORCE and vanilla policy gradient methods are on-policy. On-policy algorithms are simpler conceptually and avoid the bias introduced by learning from mismatched distributions, but they tend to be sample-inefficient because each update discards past experiences once the policy changes.

Off-policy learning allows using data collected by a different behavior policy, which could be an older version of the model, a human demonstrator, or another algorithm. Off-policy methods can be more sample-efficient because they reuse data, enable experience replay, and allow combining multiple data sources. However, naive use of off-policy data introduces bias: the expectation under the behavior policy differs from the target policy, and the gradient estimator becomes inconsistent unless corrected.

One standard correction is importance sampling. Suppose trajectories are sampled from a behavior policy $\mu(a \mid s)$, but we want expectations under the target policy $\pi_\theta$. For a per-trajectory importance weight, we can write

$\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[f(\tau)] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mu}\left[\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\mu(\tau)} f(\tau)\right],$

where $p_\theta(\tau) = \prod_{t} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ and $p_\mu(\tau) = \prod_{t} \mu(a_t \mid s_t)$. This yields importance weights $w(\tau) = \prod_{t} \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}$. In practice these weights can have extremely high variance, especially for long sequences, because they multiply many ratios. To mitigate this, practitioners use per-step truncated importance weights, weight clipping, or learn value-based critics that allow off-policy temporal-difference updates without full importance weighting (e.g., Q-learning or off-policy actor-critic variants). Retrace, V-trace, and other modern corrections are designed to trade off bias and variance in off-policy actor-critic learning for long-horizon tasks.

In language modeling, off-policy techniques are attractive because we can store previously sampled sequences and human preference labels in a replay buffer, but handling the mismatch between the behavior policy and the current policy is essential to avoid learning the wrong gradient direction. Many applied systems use a hybrid approach: they collect data by sampling or through human labels generated under earlier policies and then use specialized off-policy corrections (for example, importance-weighted policy gradient with clipping, or training a reward model and then performing a PPO-style constrained on-policy update that limits the policy change per batch).

# Actor-critic methods and the role of a value function

Actor-critic algorithms combine policy-based ("actor") and value-based ("critic") components. The actor is the parameterized policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ that selects actions. The critic estimates a value function $V_\phi(s)$ or action-value function $Q_\phi(s,a)$, parameterized by $\phi$, that predicts expected return. The critic's estimates are used to reduce variance of the gradient updates to the actor and to provide bootstrapped targets that improve sample efficiency.

The simplest actor-critic variant uses a state-value critic $V_\phi(s)$ and replaces the return $G_t$ in the policy gradient with an advantage estimate $A_t = G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$. The resulting gradient estimator is

$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, A_t \right].$

Training proceeds by alternating updates: update $\phi$ to reduce the squared error between predicted values and observed returns or bootstrapped targets, and update $\theta$ using the advantage-weighted policy gradient. The critic can be learned using Monte Carlo targets $G_t$ (high variance but unbiased) or using temporal-difference (TD) learning, which uses bootstrapped one-step targets such as $r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$ to train $V_\phi(s_t)$.

The advantage of actor-critic is twofold. First, subtracting the value $V_\phi(s_t)$ from the return centers the learning signal and reduces variance, because the critic captures predictable components of the return. Second, the critic can provide lower-variance, bootstrapped targets that allow learning from fewer environment interactions. In sequence generation, the critic can be a neural network that takes as input the partial sequence (and optionally the original prompt) and predicts the expected final reward. In practice, the critic may use the same transformer encoder as the policy or a separate network and share some parameters.

There are several practical actor-critic formulations used in language model RL. Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C/A3C) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an entropy bonus and a learned value function are common. PPO uses on-policy rollouts and applies a clipped surrogate objective to keep updates stable, and often includes a value function loss to train the critic jointly. Using a value function is critical in language tasks because per-sequence rewards are often sparse and delayed; the critic helps attribute credit to earlier tokens and stabilizes updates.

A further refinement is to estimate the action-value function $Q_\phi(s,a)$ directly and use it to compute an advantage $A_t = Q_\phi(s_t,a_t) - V_\phi(s_t)$. This can enable off-policy updates and more direct incorporation of future conditional action effects. However, estimating $Q$ in large discrete token spaces is more expensive, and for language many systems prefer a state-value critic with Monte Carlo or TD returns paired with policy updates.

# Baselines and variance reduction techniques

Variance is one of the main obstacles to efficient training with policy gradients. Baselines are a simple and powerful technique to reduce variance without introducing bias. For any function $b(s_t)$ that depends only on the state, the following identity holds:

$\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, b(s_t) \right] = 0.$

Therefore, subtracting a baseline $b(s_t)$ from the return $G_t$ in the gradient estimator does not change the expected gradient but can greatly reduce variance. A common choice is to use the state-value function $V_\phi(s_t)$ as the baseline, leading to an advantage estimate $A_t = G_t - V_\phi(s_t)$.

Choosing and training a good baseline is crucial. A constant baseline equal to the average return across episodes reduces some variance but not as much as a state-dependent baseline. A learned critic $V_\phi(s)$ predicts the expected return conditional on the partial sequence and is trained to minimize mean-squared error to observed returns or to bootstrapped targets. In practice, regularizing the critic and limiting how fast it changes can help avoid harmful feedback loops where a drifting baseline corrupts the gradient direction.

Beyond simple baselines, several variance reduction techniques are used in sequence model RL. An advantage estimator that trades bias and variance is generalized advantage estimation (GAE), which forms a weighted sum of $k$-step TD errors to produce an advantage estimate that interpolates between high-bias low-variance TD(0) and low-bias high-variance Monte Carlo returns. GAE is defined as

$\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}, \quad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t),$

where $\lambda \in [0,1]$ is a parameter controlling the bias-variance trade-off. In language tasks with finite horizon, the summation truncates at the end of the episode. GAE often yields much lower-variance advantage estimates and empirically speeds up learning.

Entropy regularization is another practical variance/learning-stability technique. Adding an entropy bonus to the policy objective encourages exploration and prevents the policy from collapsing prematurely to deterministic choices. The entropy term does not directly reduce the variance of the policy gradient estimator but stabilizes learning dynamics and can indirectly improve signal-to-noise by avoiding degenerate behavior.

Control variates and baselines beyond the state-value function can also be designed. For example, a learned action-dependent baseline or control variate that correlates with the return but is easier to estimate can reduce variance further, though care must be taken to maintain unbiasedness. In practice, combining Monte Carlo sampling with a strong value baseline and possibly advantage normalization (subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation of advantages across the batch) provides a robust toolkit for variance reduction.

# Per-token vs per-sequence rewards and implications

Rewards in sequence generation can be assigned at different granularities: per-sequence (sparse, computed after generating the full text) or per-token (dense, provided at each generation step). The choice strongly affects credit assignment, variance of gradient estimates, and algorithm design.

Per-sequence rewards are common because many evaluation metrics (e.g., task success, human preference scores, BLEU) require the entire output to be evaluated. If only a final scalar reward $R$ is available, then for each time step $t$ the policy gradient uses $G_t = R$ (or discounted equivalents). This makes credit assignment difficult: every token that contributed to success or failure receives the same scalar signal, so the learning algorithm must infer which token choices were responsible. REINFORCE with a learned baseline can still provide meaningful updates, but variance is high and many samples may be needed. Actor-critic methods with a critic that conditions on partial sequences mitigate this by predicting expected future reward given the prefix, thereby differentiating between prefixes that are likely to lead to good final outcomes and those that are not.

Per-token rewards assign a reward $r(s_t, a_t)$ at each step. These rewards can be dense signals like language model log-likelihoods, token-level penalties (length or repetition penalties), or partial task feedback (e.g., matching a target token or intermediate validation scores). Per-token rewards improve temporal credit assignment because the agent receives immediate feedback about each choice's desirability. This typically reduces variance and speeds learning because the returns $G_t$ are less dominated by far-future uncertainty.

However, designing per-token rewards can be challenging because local token-level feedback may not correlate well with the global objective. For example, rewarding tokens that maximize short-term fluency might hurt task success if the task requires longer-term coherence or adherence to constraints. A common hybrid approach is to combine per-sequence rewards with shaped per-token signals that encourage desirable local properties while preserving the final objective. Reward shaping must be handled carefully because arbitrary shaping can change the optimal policy. Under certain theoretical conditions, adding a shaping potential that is a difference of a potential function at consecutive states preserves optimal policies, but arbitrary shaping can introduce bias.

Another consideration is computational cost. Per-sequence rewards often require running a downstream evaluation (like executing generated code, querying a human, or computing an NLP metric), which can be expensive. Per-token rewards can be computed cheaply online if they are derived from model-internal signals. In many applied settings, a reward model is trained to approximate human judgments of entire sequences, and then that reward model is used to provide per-sequence or even prefix-based (per-token) reward estimates to guide RL training; this is the approach used in large-scale preference-based RL for language models.

In summary, per-sequence rewards align directly with many evaluation metrics but increase variance and hinder credit assignment. Per-token rewards reduce variance and improve learning speed but require careful design to align with the end goal. Actor-critic methods and learned critics are especially valuable when rewards are sparse or delayed.

# Exploration vs exploitation considerations in decoding

Exploration versus exploitation is a central dilemma in RL: the agent must explore different actions to discover high-reward sequences, but it also must exploit known high-reward actions to maximize cumulative reward. In sequence generation, exploration manifests in the decoding strategy: whether to sample tokens stochastically, use deterministic search like greedy decoding or beam search, or employ temperature and nucleus sampling to trade off diversity and quality.

During training with policy gradient methods, explicit exploration is naturally achieved by sampling from the stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$. If the policy's entropy collapses too quickly (becomes too deterministic), the agent might prematurely converge to suboptimal text patterns. Entropy regularization adds a term $-\beta \mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)]$ to the objective to encourage continued exploration; tuning $\beta$ controls how much randomness is preserved.

In practice, exploration during RL training must balance safety and practicality. Aggressive exploration might generate many incoherent or unsafe sequences that waste compute or collect poor human labels. Conversely, insufficient exploration may prevent finding rare but high-reward behaviors. Techniques used in applied language RL include initializing with a pretrained language model (which provides reasonable defaults and narrows the search space), sampling with a controlled temperature to inject limited randomness, and gradually annealing exploration strength as the policy improves.

At decoding time for deployment, exploitation is the usual priority: we want the best available sequence according to the model. Deterministic decoding like greedy search often yields one coherent output but can miss better alternatives. Beam search keeps multiple candidates but can lead to repetitive or overly generic outputs if the scoring objective (e.g., log-probability) mismatches quality. Sampling-based approaches with temperature or nucleus (top-p) sampling produce more diverse outputs, which can be useful when multiple plausible outputs exist or when stochasticity is desirable.

The relationship between exploration used in RL training and decoding at inference is subtle. If training heavily relies on sampling-based exploration, the model may learn to exploit that stochasticity in generating sequences, and its performance under deterministic decoding may not reflect its true capabilities. Conversely, training with on-policy exploration that resembles the intended decoding method (for example, training by sampling if we plan to sample at inference) helps align training and evaluation distributions. This issue is part of the on-policy versus off-policy dilemma: if the training data distribution differs from the inference decoding procedure, distributional shift can degrade performance.

A practical approach is to use RL primarily to improve the policy within the stochastic sampling regime (on-policy sampling), but evaluate and possibly fine-tune the model under the intended inference strategy. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and similar methods that limit the magnitude of policy updates are particularly helpful because they prevent the policy from shifting too far from the pretrained model, maintaining reasonable behavior while allowing exploration-driven improvements.

Finally, exploration interacts with safety: deliberately exploring unknown behaviors might generate harmful or undesirable outputs. In safety-critical or user-facing systems, one must constrain exploration using reward shaping, filtering, or human-in-the-loop checks to prevent collecting toxic or unsafe outputs during training.

# Putting it together: practical considerations and common patterns

Applying these ideas in practice to sequence models involves combining estimators, regularization, and system-level choices. A common recipe is to start from a pretrained language model to provide a reasonable initial policy, collect trajectories by sampling from the current policy, evaluate full-sequence rewards using a reward model or human labels, and then update the policy using an actor-critic method or PPO with a learned value baseline. Advantage normalization, entropy bonuses, and early stopping or KL-penalties relative to the pretrained policy help stabilize training. If using off-policy data such as human demonstrations or previously collected labeled sequences, apply importance-weighting corrections, use off-policy actor-critic variants with trace-based corrections (e.g., V-trace), or fine-tune the reward model and then perform constrained on-policy updates.

Careful reward design is essential: prefer rewards that align with downstream goals, avoid perverse incentives, and consider combining sparse objective-level rewards with dense shaping signals. Monitor metrics like policy entropy, reward variance, KL divergence to the pretrained policy, and behavior under the intended decoding method to detect undesirable shifts.

In research, many papers improve RL for language models by improving reward models (for example, preference-based learning), designing better baselines and critics that condition on prefixes, and developing more stable on-policy optimization algorithms. Understanding the core mathematics—MDP formulation, policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE, baselines, advantage estimation, and on/off-policy corrections—equips you to read and evaluate these contributions and to implement effective RL-based sequence learning systems.
