Chapter 5: Large-Scale Pretraining and Continual Training

Introduction

Large-scale pretraining has become the foundation of modern natural language processing. By training models on very large corpora with self-supervised objectives, we can learn representations and generative capabilities that transfer broadly to many downstream tasks. However, pretraining is not a single monolithic step: researchers and engineers increasingly use staged and continual training approaches to adapt base models to new domains, modalities, or capabilities. This chapter explains the principles behind pretraining objectives and transfer learning, the tradeoffs between fine-tuning and training from scratch, the rationale for stage-wise training strategies, and the practical challenge of catastrophic forgetting. We will also discuss how to mix heterogeneous data sources during training, curriculum strategies that order or weight data, and how to evaluate progress and apply early stopping during long, expensive pretraining runs. The aim is to give you both the theoretical foundations and the pragmatic tools needed to read and implement research in large-scale pretraining and continual training.

# Pretraining objectives and transfer learning

Pretraining uses self-supervised learning objectives that let models learn from unlabeled data at scale. The most common objective for generative language models is next-token prediction (causal language modeling). Given a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$, the model learns parameters $\theta$ to maximize the likelihood
\[
\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}),
\]
typically optimized by minimizing cross-entropy loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLM}}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
\]
For bidirectional or masked-language objectives (as used in BERT), the model predicts masked tokens conditioned on their context. A common masked-language objective is:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in M} \log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{\setminus M}),
\]
where $M$ is the set of masked positions and $x_{\setminus M}$ denotes the sequence with masks inserted.

Why do these objectives transfer? Self-supervised objectives encourage models to capture syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic regularities in text. These learned representations serve as useful priors: when fine-tuning on downstream supervised tasks with limited labeled data, the pretrained model only needs to adapt relatively small task-specific parts rather than learn language structure from scratch. The intuitive reason is that pretraining shapes the model’s parameter manifold so that many downstream tasks lie near regions of parameter space that produce good performance. Empirically, pretraining dramatically reduces sample complexity and improves generalization across classification, structured prediction, and generation tasks.

Transfer learning in this context means taking a pretrained model and adapting it to a target distribution or task. Two common adaptation modes are: (1) fine-tuning, where the full model (or a large subset of its parameters) is further trained on the target; and (2) feature-based transfer, where representations from the pretrained model are used as fixed features and only a small classifier is trained. The effectiveness of transfer depends on the similarity between pretraining and target data, the size and capacity of the model, and the amount of labeled data for the target. More data and compute in pretraining generally increase the model’s capacity to represent diverse tasks; however, the marginal return vs. cost and the risk of domain mismatch motivate strategies like continual pretraining to bridge the gap.

# Continual pretraining / fine-tuning vs. training from scratch

Continual pretraining refers to taking an existing pretrained model and further training it on additional data or objectives, possibly targeting a specific domain. This is distinct from fine-tuning on a supervised task: continual pretraining typically continues using self-supervised objectives (e.g., next-token or masked-token prediction) but with a different corpus. The alternative is to train a model from scratch on the combined corpus (base + new domain) or solely on the new domain.

The choice between continual pretraining and training from scratch involves tradeoffs in compute, sample efficiency, and performance. Continual pretraining is often much faster and cheaper: a well-trained base model has already learned general language structure, so the additional optimization only needs to adapt representations toward the new distribution. Empirically, starting from a large general model and continuing training on in-domain data usually yields better performance than training a smaller model from scratch on the same in-domain data, especially when in-domain data is limited. It leverages learned representations and scales effectively.

However, continual pretraining can introduce problems. One is catastrophic forgetting — the model may lose capabilities learned during the initial pretraining if the new data distribution is narrow or if the training objective overfits to the new domain. Another potential issue is miscalibration: the model's learned prior over language can shift in ways that harm certain downstream behaviors. Training from scratch on a mixture of general and domain data can avoid forgetting by exposure, but it is orders of magnitude more expensive and sometimes impractical for very large models.

Practically, a hybrid approach is common: begin with a strong general pretrained model, then either (a) fine-tune the model for downstream tasks, (b) continually pretrain on an intermediate domain corpus (e.g., code or biomedical literature) to create a specialized base, and finally fine-tune, or (c) add small adapter modules or low-rank updates during domain adaptation to limit parameter changes. The best choice depends on available compute, the size of the target corpus, and whether you must preserve broad capabilities.

# Stage-wise training (e.g., code-first then domain-specific)

Stage-wise training means structuring pretraining into sequential phases with different data distributions or objectives. A typical example is first training on a very large, general corpus to learn broad language understanding, then continuing on a corpus rich in programming code, and finally adapting further to a narrow domain (medical text, legal documents, etc.). There are several motivations for stage-wise designs.

First, curricula shaped by increasing specificity help the optimizer and the learned representations to evolve in a controlled way. Early stages expose the model to diverse linguistic phenomena and build robust low-level features (tokenization, syntax), while later stages tilt representations toward the specialized vocabulary and structural patterns of the target domain. For instance, code has distinct syntax, token frequencies, and long-range dependencies (indentation, matching brackets) which a code-focused stage can emphasize without discarding the general language prior.

Second, staged strategies facilitate modularity and reuse. If you maintain a single general base model, you can create multiple specialist variants by continually pretraining from that base. This is computationally cheaper than training multiple full models from scratch. Stage-wise training also allows intermediate evaluation: after the code stage you can evaluate whether the model has gained program synthesis capability before committing compute to a domain-specific stage.

Third, stage-wise training supports better control of catastrophic forgetting. By carefully mixing data from earlier stages into later ones (see the mixing data section), or by adding constraints (freezing some layers, using adapters), the model can retain broad capabilities while gaining specialization. Another practical advantage is data engineering: different stages can use different tokenization strategies or model heads tailored to the task (e.g., sequence-to-sequence vs. causal). When changing tokenizers, however, careful fine-grained procedures are required to avoid misaligning embeddings.

Designing a stage-wise schedule requires deciding: what data to use in each stage, how long to train per stage, whether to mix older data, and how to adjust hyperparameters. Empirical rules often used include dedicating the largest amount of compute to the most general stage that benefits many downstream tasks, then using shorter, focused stages for specialization. But these are empirical choices; reading papers and running ablations helps determine what works for a particular application.

# Catastrophic forgetting: causes and mitigation strategies

Catastrophic forgetting refers to rapid degradation on previously learned tasks or distributions when a neural network is trained on new data. In the continual pretraining context, it appears as loss of general capabilities or worse performance on tasks that the model previously handled well after prolonged training on a narrow domain.

Why does forgetting happen? At a high level, gradient-based updates modify parameters to reduce loss on current data, with no explicit mechanism to preserve performance on older data. If the new data distribution pushes the model to a different region of parameter space, representations that encoded old knowledge can be overwritten. Mathematically, successive updates can move the parameter vector $\theta$ away from values that minimized previous losses. For overparameterized models, the optimization landscape may permit multiple minima that perform differently on different distributions; new training will favor minima that fit the current distribution.

Several mitigation strategies have been developed:

1. Replay and rehearsal: Keep a buffer of examples from previous distributions and interleave them with new data during training. This directly exposes the model to older data and helps preserve capabilities. In pretraining, full replay of the original massive corpus is impractical; instead, a small sampled subset or synthetic examples can be mixed. Rehearsal is simple and effective, but requires storage and careful sampling to avoid bias.

2. Regularization-based methods: Add penalty terms that discourage parameters from moving far from their previous values, especially those parameters important to old tasks. A canonical approach is Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), which adds
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{new}}(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_i F_i (\theta_i - \theta_i^\ast)^2,
\]
where $\theta^\ast$ are parameters after old-task training and $F_i$ approximates the Fisher information, indicating parameter importance. This keeps important parameters near previous values. Other regularizations include L2 penalty toward prior parameters or KL regularization on predictive distributions.

3. Parameter isolation and architectural approaches: Allocate distinct parameters or modules for different domains. Examples include exemplar networks with gating, sparsely activated subnetworks, or appending small adapter modules to each transformer layer. During domain-specific training, update only adapters while freezing most pretrained weights. This preserves the base model and confines domain-specific changes to small, trainable components. Adapters are computationally efficient and allow multiple domain specialists to coexist in memory.

4. Low-rank or structured updates: Methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) parameterize weight updates as low-rank matrices, keeping the base weights frozen. This reduces the degrees of freedom for changes and tends to preserve prior behavior while enabling specialization.

5. Curriculum and mixing strategies (discussed below): Ensuring that pretraining data is not completely dominated by the new domain, for instance by continuing to sample some fraction of general-domain data, can reduce forgetting. Proper sampling temperatures and dynamic rebalancing can help.

6. Selective fine-tuning: Freeze some layers (typically lower layers that capture syntactic features) and only train higher layers or newly added heads. This reduces how much the representation changes.

In practice, combinations of these strategies often perform best: for example, adapters plus a small replay buffer and a modest regularization term. Measuring forgetting requires evaluation on held-out examples or tasks representative of earlier stages. If the model must retain broad capabilities while adding specialization, prefer adapter-based or replay-augmented continual training over unconstrained continued updates.

# Mixing data sources and curriculum strategies

When pretraining on multiple data sources (e.g., web text, books, code, biomedical articles), how you mix them matters. Naively shuffling all tokens and sampling uniformly can lead to dominant sources due to size disparities, causing underrepresentation of smaller but important domains. A good mixing strategy balances coverage, avoids overfitting to noisy sources, and encourages learning of rare but critical patterns.

One approach is static sampling with temperature: for each corpus $i$ with size $n_i$, sample with probability proportional to $n_i^\alpha$ where $\alpha$ is a temperature parameter (commonly between 0 and 1). $\alpha=1$ corresponds to sampling proportional to size; $\alpha<1$ upweights smaller corpora and $\alpha>1$ downweights them. This simple reweighting is widely used to control domain mixture.

Another strategy is controlled upsampling: define target fractions for each domain and oversample smaller domains to match those fractions. This ensures adequate exposure but risks overfitting if a domain is oversampled relative to its natural prevalence. To mitigate overfitting, add stronger regularization or early stopping for heavily upsampled domains.

Curriculum learning organizes the order in which data is presented across time. A chronological curriculum might start with simpler, high-quality text and move toward more complex or noisy sources. Alternatively, a domain curriculum moves from general to specific (the stage-wise design discussed earlier). There are also difficulty-based curricula where examples that the model currently finds easy are introduced first; difficulty can be measured by perplexity under the current model. Adaptive curricula can monitor learning signals and reweight examples that produce high gradient magnitudes or on which the model’s predictions are uncertain.

Mixture-of-experts-like curricula can route different data types through different parameter subsets — for instance, use specialized modules for code vs. prose — which reduces interference. Attention to tokenization is also important: mixing data with very different token distributions (e.g., code with many symbols) may benefit from a shared tokenizer that includes relevant tokens or a hybrid approach with separate subtokenizers and embeddings.

In long continual training, gradually annealing the sampling probability of the original general corpus down (but not to zero) helps prevent forgetting. Bloom-style balancing, where you hold out a fixed fraction of general data across all epochs, is effective. When adding new sources, run small probing evaluations to decide mixing ratios rather than relying only on corpus sizes.

# Evaluation during pretraining and early stopping considerations

Evaluating pretrained models is challenging because the training objective and scale make standard validation practices nontrivial. Pretraining runs are expensive, so monitoring progress and deciding when to stop is crucial for economic and scientific reasons.

Intrinsic metrics: For language modeling objectives, the primary intrinsic metric is validation loss or perplexity on a held-out validation set. Perplexity is defined as
\[
\text{PPL} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N -\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\right).
\]
A decreasing validation loss typically indicates learning, but perplexity improvements do not always correlate perfectly with downstream task performance, especially for specialized capabilities or rare behaviors.

Downstream probes: Because pretraining is intended for transfer, evaluate intermediate checkpoints on a suite of downstream tasks or probes. These can include GLUE/SuperGLUE-style tasks, code synthesis benchmarks, retrieval augmentation tasks, or domain-specific tasks relevant to your application. Running a small set of frozen-features or fine-tuned evaluations gives actionable signals about transfer quality and whether specialization stages help.

Behavioral and safety checks: For large models, monitor behaviors like toxicity, factuality, and tendency to hallucinate. Track calibration metrics (e.g., expected calibration error) and distributional shifts. These checks can reveal negative side effects of domain adaptation that are not visible in log-likelihood.

Early stopping in pretraining requires balancing compute against marginal gains. Some practical rules:

- Use validation loss on a representative held-out set as a primary stop signal, but be wary of overinterpreting tiny improvements. For very large models, small drops in validation loss can still reflect significant capability gains, so combine signals.

- Prefer stopping based on downstream task plateau rather than only intrinsic metrics when transfer is the goal. For example, if fine-tuned performance on your target tasks has converged, additional pretraining may bring diminishing returns or even harm due to forgetting.

- For continual pretraining, frequently evaluate on a set of probes from the original domain to detect forgetting. If you observe degradation, either stop, reduce the learning rate, reintroduce mixed-domain data, or switch to adapter-based updates.

- Account for randomness and noise. Large-batch pretraining can present high variance, and checkpoint-to-checkpoint variability may be large. Use smoothed metrics (moving averages) or require improvements to be sustained over several evaluations before committing.

- Use computationally cheaper proxy evaluations during training. For instance, measure perplexity on short contexts if long-context evaluation is expensive, or use smaller downstream tasks that correlate with larger benchmarks. However, ensure proxies are well-correlated with the main objectives via preliminary experiments.

Finally, logging and reproducibility are key. Store random seeds, optimizer states, learning rate schedules, and data sampling strategies. Save intermediate checkpoints and metadata so that best-performing variants can be reproduced or ensembling can be used if beneficial.

Conclusion

Large-scale pretraining and continual training form a nuanced design space combining statistical, computational, and engineering decisions. Understanding pretraining objectives, transfer mechanisms, and the tradeoffs between reusing pretrained models and training from scratch is fundamental. Stage-wise training and careful mixing of data sources enable specialization while limiting forgetting, but catastrophic forgetting remains a central practical challenge that must be mitigated through rehearsal, regularization, modular updates, or careful sampling. Finally, robust evaluation and principled early stopping are essential given the scale and expense of modern pretraining. With these concepts in hand, you will be equipped to read current literature on large models, design experiments, and implement reliable continual training pipelines.
