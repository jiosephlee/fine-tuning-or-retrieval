Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an important tool for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences and task objectives. In practice, applying RL to LLMs often requires many design decisions about where training data comes from, how it is stored and mixed, how and when reward models and policies are updated, and how to evaluate models as they change. This chapter examines the crucial distinction between online and offline data collection, explains how replay buffers are used to blend fresh and historical data, explores the dynamics and stability issues in iterative reward and policy update loops, analyzes how the source of data affects exploration and distributional shift, describes strategies for evaluation during iterative RL, and provides practical engineering guidance for building robust iterative pipelines (batching, checkpoints, and related infrastructure). The focus is on intuition and practical reasoning so you can read research papers and understand the trade-offs authors make when training RL on LLMs.

# Definitions and examples of online (on-policy) and offline (off-policy) sampling

Online and offline sampling are two broad paradigms for how training data is gathered and used when training an RL agent. In the context of LLMs, the "agent" is a generative model that produces text conditioned on prompts or conversation history, and the "environment" returns signals such as reward model scores, human preferences, or task success indicators.

Offline (off-policy) sampling means that the training algorithm uses a fixed dataset of transitions or examples collected beforehand; the policy being trained does not influence data collection during that phase. In classic RL terms, "off-policy" usually refers to algorithms that can learn from trajectories generated by some behavior policy different from the target policy. For LLMs, offline data could include human-written demonstrations, past logs of model outputs and human ratings, or datasets of human preference comparisons. The key point is that the dataset is static during training: we do not interact with the environment to collect fresh samples that depend on the current policy. For example, an offline RL approach for dialogue alignment might use a large corpus of previously collected chat logs labeled for helpfulness or safety, training a policy to imitate high-scoring responses via supervised or offline policy optimization.

Online (on-policy) sampling means the agent actively generates new data during training using the current policy, and the resulting interactions are used for subsequent updates. In on-policy RL for LLMs, the model generates responses to prompts, those responses are scored by a reward model or by humans, and then the policy is updated based on these fresh evaluations. On-policy methods treat the data as dependent on the policy itself; typical algorithms include policy gradient methods like REINFORCE or Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), where gradients are estimated from trajectories sampled from the latest policy. A concrete example is training an assistant model by repeatedly sampling responses from the current model, asking annotators to compare responses or provide scalar feedback, and then updating the model to increase expected reward under its current behavior distribution.

A hybrid view is helpful: "on-policy" and "off-policy" exist along a continuum rather than as binary buckets. Many practical systems mix the two: train with historical collected data but also periodically sample from the deployed model to obtain new preferences or failure cases. In addition, typical LLM training uses pretraining (unsupervised offline) followed by supervised fine-tuning (offline) and then RL from human feedback (online or mixed). Understanding whether data is fresh or historical, and whether it was generated by the current policy, is critical because it drives how updates should be computed and which theoretical guarantees apply.

# Replay buffers and mixing historical with fresh data

When iterative procedures generate new data over time, engineers must decide whether to discard old data or retain it for future updates. A replay buffer is a data structure that stores past interactions (trajectories, prompts and responses, reward evaluations) for reuse in training. Replay buffers play several roles: they increase sample efficiency by reusing expensive annotations, stabilize learning by providing more diverse training examples, and allow off-policy algorithms to learn from data collected under older policies.

The simplest form of a replay buffer is an append-only log of recent trajectories with a fixed maximum size. New samples push out the oldest ones. More sophisticated strategies include prioritized replay, where samples are weighted by some measure of learning utility (e.g., high advantage or high temporal-difference error), and stratified buffers that store balanced numbers of samples by prompt type or by reward value to ensure coverage of rare but important cases.

Mixing historical with fresh data addresses the bias-variance and stability trade-offs in RL for LLMs. Fresh on-policy data reflects the current policy distribution and therefore helps the learner correct present-day mistakes and drive immediate improvements. Historical offline data provides coverage and reduces catastrophic forgetting; it contains examples that may no longer appear frequently under the current policy but are still important for safety, factuality, or avoiding degenerate behavior. When combining them, one must carefully balance the mixture. If you over-weight fresh data, you risk overfitting to recent idiosyncratic behaviors or to noise in annotator feedback; if you over-weight historical data, you risk slowing adaptation and failing to exploit new opportunities.

From the perspective of learning algorithms, mixing matters for the correctness of gradient estimators and for importance sampling corrections. Off-policy corrections (e.g., importance weights) can account for differences between behavior distribution and target distribution but often have high variance when policies diverge. In language models with large action spaces, naive importance weighting is infeasible. Practical RL on LLMs therefore tends to avoid heavy importance weighting and instead uses empirical mixtures: each update is performed on a minibatch sampled from the buffer according to some schedule (e.g., 70% fresh, 30% historical), without explicit reweighting, accepting some bias for reduced variance and better sample efficiency.

Design choices for buffer retention also affect distributional coverage. For example, if your buffer retains only samples produced by a highly-optimized policy, you may lose exposure to prompt-template variations or pathological cases the policy no longer produces. A good practice is to maintain a diverse buffer that preserves examples across prompts, user styles, and reward strata. For safety-critical behaviors, storing and maintaining a curated set of adversarial or failure cases is essential.

# Iterative reward/policy update loops and stability issues

Iterative loops are central to RL for LLMs: you typically alternate between collecting data (responses), labeling that data with rewards or preference information, and updating either a reward model, the policy, or both. Common architectures include:

- Policy-only loops: keep reward function fixed (e.g., human-defined) and update policy with on-policy samples.
- Reward-model then policy loops: train a reward model on human feedback, then optimize the policy to maximize the predicted reward.
- Iterative loops where both reward models and policies are updated repeatedly: collect data using current policy, label it (human or synthetic), update the reward model, then update the policy to chase the updated reward predictions.

These loops are attractive because they allow researchers to scale human feedback: a reward model can replace repeated human evaluation. However, iterative updates introduce several stability concerns.

One key instability arises from feedback loops between policy and reward model. If the policy exploits idiosyncrasies of the reward model—ways to get high predicted reward that do not correspond to true human preferences—then the policy will produce more samples that reinforce those idiosyncrasies when the reward model is retrained on policy-generated data. This phenomenon is sometimes called reward hacking or specification gaming. Over successive iterations, small modeling errors in the reward function can be amplified, producing degenerate behaviors that score well but are undesirable.

Another stability issue is non-stationarity of the data distribution. As the policy changes, the distribution of prompts and generated responses shifts, which invalidates assumptions used in offline training. The reward model may be trained on data that no longer matches the policy's outputs, leading to unreliable reward predictions. To mitigate this, practitioners can use recurring human labels on recent policy outputs, constrain policy changes per update (e.g., via KL penalties), or use ensembles and uncertainty estimates in the reward model to detect out-of-distribution inputs.

Optimization instability also occurs when the policy is updated too aggressively. Policy-gradient estimates have high variance; when paired with a learned reward, they can produce noisy gradients that push parameters into regions producing catastrophic outputs. Regularization techniques like entropy bonuses, trust-region methods (PPO-style clipping), or conservative updates (small learning rates, gradient norm clipping) help maintain stability. Likewise, when updating the reward model, care must be taken to prevent overfitting to recent policy outputs; using cross-validation, held-out human-labeled data, and early stopping are practical defenses.

A further subtle issue is bias accumulation from bootstrapping. If the reward model is partially trained on labeled data generated by the policy optimized for that model, there is a form of circularity: the model's mistakes guide the policy, and the policy's outputs guide the model. Without careful controls such as keeping a seed dataset of trusted human labels across iterations, the entire system can drift.

Finally, the interplay between sample efficiency and stability creates trade-offs. Using more offline data from earlier iterations reduces variance and helps the reward model generalize, but can hide emerging failure modes. On the other hand, relying primarily on recent on-policy data can detect new problems early but risks overfitting and escalation of reward hacking. Designing mechanisms (e.g., replay buffers, regular human audits, conservative policy constraints) that stabilize iterative loops is a core engineering and research challenge.

# Impact of data source on exploration and distributional shift

The origin of data—online on-policy vs offline historical—has a strong effect on exploration behavior and the extent of distributional shift an algorithm must handle.

Exploration refers to the policy's tendency to try diverse or novel actions to discover higher rewards. In LLMs, exploration can mean producing varied or unexpected phrasing, strategies for instruction following, or even generating content in new genres. On-policy sampling naturally supports exploration because the policy used to collect data can be deliberately made stochastic or encouraged to explore with temperature, epsilon-greedy mechanisms, or entropy bonuses. Since updates are computed from policy-generated samples, the algorithm learns from its exploratory choices directly.

Offline datasets, by contrast, typically reflect historical behavior, perhaps collected by human demonstrators or older model versions. If the offline data lacks diversity, offline RL will struggle to discover useful behaviors not present in the dataset. Offline RL must therefore rely on either conservative policy improvement methods that do not deviate far from the behavior policy or on assumptions that offline data contains sufficient coverage. In LLM settings, offline datasets often bias models toward typical human phrasing or behavior, which can be desirable but may prevent discovering more effective or efficient strategies.

Distributional shift arises because the test-time distribution (the prompts and contexts encountered in deployment) may differ from the training data distribution. On-policy training adapts to the distribution the policy itself induces; as the policy evolves, the training distribution shifts, and continuous adaptation can keep the model well-calibrated to its own behavior. However, heavy on-policy training can also push the model into regions with little human label coverage, widening the gap between model behavior and human judgments. This creates a need for mechanisms to check or constrain the model to maintain alignment.

Offline training faces a different class of distributional shift: model behavior at deployment might step outside the support of the offline data, producing outputs for which the reward model is not reliable. Because LLMs have enormous action spaces, small changes in prompt or generation settings can lead to outputs far from any training examples. Therefore, offline approaches must use conservative techniques, such as policy constraints that keep the new policy close to the behavior policy, or model uncertainty diagnostics that flag out-of-distribution outputs for manual review.

In practice, many teams use a hybrid strategy. They start with offline data for initial policy shaping and safety constraints (ensuring the model learns basic norms and avoids catastrophic behaviors) and then move to cautious online collection with constrained updates to enable exploration of legitimate improvements. During online phases, it's common to enforce sampling strategies that promote exploration in controlled subsets of prompts—e.g., enabling higher-temperature sampling only for low-risk prompts—and to route uncertain or risky outputs to human evaluation rather than automatic reward labeling.

# Evaluation strategies during iterative RL

Evaluation during iterative RL must detect both progress on target objectives and the emergence of pathological behaviors. Designing robust evaluation pipelines is essential because training metrics (e.g., reward model scores) can mislead if the reward model itself is changing or being exploited.

One principle is to separate evaluation data and procedures from the data used for training the reward model or policy. Maintain a held-out test set of prompts and human judgments that are not used in any model updates. Periodically evaluate the policy on this set to measure true performance against human preference data. This provides an unbiased signal of alignment and helps detect reward hacking: if reward model scores improve while held-out human preference agreement declines, the system is likely exploiting flaws in the reward model.

Another useful strategy is adversarial evaluation. Design prompts and scenarios intended to probe weaknesses—ambiguous instructions, edge-case requests, or adversarially crafted inputs. Evaluate models on these sets frequently, using human raters or external validators, to catch failure modes that might not appear in generic evaluation data.

Calibration and uncertainty estimation for reward models and policies are also important. Evaluate the calibration of predicted reward scores: do higher scores correspond to higher probabilities of human preference? Use calibration plots or rank correlation metrics (Spearman rho) between reward model predictions and human labels on a validation set. Poor correlation is a red flag.

When possible, use A/B testing in production to assess real-world behavior. Sample some live traffic and route a fraction to the new policy under controlled conditions. Monitor safety metrics, user satisfaction, and downstream harms. Real-world evaluation captures distributions and interactions offline tests cannot.

Sequential and online evaluation requires choosing appropriate time granularity. Too frequent model updates can make evaluation noisy and hard to interpret; too infrequent updates slow iteration. Use rolling windows and smoothed metrics (exponential moving averages) to track trends while filtering transient variation.

Finally, incorporate human-in-the-loop audits as part of evaluation. Periodic expert review of randomly sampled model outputs catches subtle degradations or misalignment that automated metrics miss. For reward model updates, keep a seed set of human-labeled examples (a "gold set") to test whether reward model retraining has overfit to recent policy behavior. If a reward model's performance on the gold set degrades, rollback or retrain with stronger regularization.

# Practical engineering of iterative pipelines (batching, checkpoints)

Building robust iterative RL pipelines for LLMs requires careful engineering: batching and throughput choices, checkpointing and rollback mechanisms, monitoring and instrumentation, and data governance. These practical decisions determine the speed of iteration, reproducibility, and safety.

Batching is central to balancing annotation throughput, compute costs, and freshness of data. Collecting human labels is expensive, so teams often accumulate batches of generated responses before sending them for annotation. Large batches improve annotator efficiency and allow better statistical power in training, but they also increase latency between data generation and model updates, which can slow detection of new failure modes. Conversely, very small batches support rapid reaction but increase per-sample annotation overhead and can produce noisy updates. A pragmatic approach uses multiple batch sizes: a fast lane for high-priority prompts requiring quick feedback and a slow lane for bulk annotation to improve statistical reliability.

Checkpointing is essential for safety and reproducibility. Every policy and reward model update should be associated with an immutable checkpoint artifact and metadata describing the training data composition (e.g., fractions from fresh vs historical), hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics. Checkpoints allow rollback if an update produces regression or unsafe behavior. They also enable ablation studies and model comparisons. In production, a conservative deployment policy stages new checkpoints behind canary traffic, with automated rollout rules that halt deployment if acceptance criteria are not met.

Data pipelines must preserve provenance. For each sample in the replay buffer, record not only the prompt and response but also the policy version that generated it, sampling parameters (temperature, top-k), annotation source (human or synthetic), and timestamps. This metadata is crucial for debugging instability: for example, if a policy suddenly exploits a reward model, provenance helps trace whether the exploit first appeared in a particular policy version or under certain sampling conditions.

Automation of iterative loops requires orchestration systems that handle asynchronous components: generation, annotation, reward model training, policy optimization, validation, and deployment. Each step may run on different hardware with different latencies. A robust system has clear contracts: e.g., "the labeling service must return human preference comparisons within X hours" or "policy updates must not exceed a KL divergence threshold." Implementing canary tests, gating rules, and automated rollback reduces human supervision burden.

Compute considerations matter. Training large LLM policies and reward models is resource-intensive. To save compute, practitioners may fine-tune smaller policy heads or use parameter-efficient adapters, reserving full-model updates for major milestones. Another strategy is to freeze parts of the model (e.g., lower layers) and update only a smaller policy head in RL, reducing both compute and the risk of catastrophic forgetting.

Finally, safety and compliance processes should be integrated into pipelines. Build review workflows for flagged outputs, maintain curated adversarial test suites updated by red-teaming teams, and log model behavior for retrospective analysis. Ensure that human annotators have clear guidelines and that their feedback quality is monitored.

Putting it together, a typical practical pipeline might operate as follows: periodically sample a mixed batch of prompts, generate responses from the current policy with some exploration enabled, send a prioritized subset for human annotation and use the remainder for synthetic labeling or low-cost heuristics, update the reward model with a mix of new and historical labels, run validation (held-out gold set, adversarial tests), perform a constrained policy update (e.g., PPO with KL penalty relative to a safe base policy), checkpoint and evaluate on held-out test sets, and stage deployment with canary traffic and automatic rollback triggers. Throughout, maintain comprehensive logging and metadata for every item stored in the replay buffer.

Conclusion

Understanding the differences between online and offline sampling, managing replay buffers, stabilizing iterative reward-policy loops, accounting for exploration and distributional shift, evaluating continuously, and engineering solid pipelines are all critical for successful RL training of LLMs. Each choice involves trade-offs: offline data offers safety and coverage but limits exploration; online data enables adaptation but risks instability; replay buffers increase sample efficiency but can perpetuate bias. Combining thoughtful algorithmic constraints, careful evaluation, and rigorous engineering practices allows teams to build iterative RL systems that improve model behavior while controlling the risks of drift and reward hacking.
