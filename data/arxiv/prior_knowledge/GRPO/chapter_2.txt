Introduction

Information theory provides a set of mathematical concepts for quantifying uncertainty and difference between probability distributions. These concepts are central in machine learning and statistics: they underpin maximum likelihood estimation, regularization of probabilistic models, variational inference, and many model comparison techniques. In this chapter we introduce the fundamental quantities entropy and cross-entropy, define the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, and discuss its properties and relationships to likelihood and cross-entropy. We give a brief overview of symmetrized divergences, and then turn to practical matters: how to estimate KL and related quantities from data, and how KL is used as a regularizer in probabilistic modeling. The emphasis is on intuition and on technical connections that you will see when reading research papers.

# Entropy and cross-entropy

Entropy measures the average uncertainty (or information content) of a random variable under a probability distribution. For a discrete random variable $X$ with probability mass function $p(x)$, the (Shannon) entropy is defined as
\[
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x).
\]
For a continuous random variable with density $p(x)$ the analogous quantity is the differential entropy
\[
H(p) = -\int p(x) \log p(x)\,dx,
\]
noting that differential entropy has different interpretations and units and is not invariant under change of variables the same way discrete entropy is. Entropy quantifies the expected number of nats (if logarithm is natural) required to encode draws from $p$. High entropy means outcomes are more unpredictable; low entropy means outcomes are more concentrated and thus easier to predict or encode.

Cross-entropy measures the expected code length when using an encoding optimized for a distribution $q$ while the true distribution is $p$. The cross-entropy from $p$ to $q$ is
\[
H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x)
\]
(or the integral analogue for continuous $x$). Cross-entropy decomposes into entropy plus KL divergence (we will prove this below):
\[
H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q).
\]
This identity shows that cross-entropy is equal to the inherent uncertainty (entropy) of $p$ plus the extra cost incurred because $q$ does not match $p$. In machine learning, minimizing cross-entropy loss with respect to model parameters when training on samples from $p$ corresponds to trying to make the model distribution $q$ close to $p$ in the KL sense.

A concrete way to think about these quantities: imagine you must encode outcomes of a random variable $X\sim p$ using a codebook optimized for distribution $q$. If your code lengths are approximately $-\log q(x)$ then the average code length is $H(p,q)$. The minimum possible average code length is $H(p)$, achieved when $q=p$. The excess code length due to using $q$ instead of the optimal $p$ is exactly $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$.

# Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence: definition and properties

The Kullback–Leibler divergence between two probability distributions $p$ and $q$ on the same measurable space is defined as
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \| q) = \int p(x) \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx
\]
for continuous densities (or the corresponding sum in the discrete case), provided the integral exists and $p$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $q$ (so that $q(x)=0$ implies $p(x)=0$ almost everywhere). The KL divergence is also known as relative entropy. It measures how much information is lost when $q$ is used to approximate $p$. Because it is an expectation under $p$, it is often interpreted as "average surprise" under $p$ of seeing outcomes that the model $q$ assigns probabilities to.

Key properties of KL divergence:

1. Non-negativity: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\ge 0$ with equality if and only if $p=q$ almost everywhere. This is Gibbs' inequality. A short proof uses Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function $-\log$ or directly to $x\log x$.

2. Asymmetry: In general $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$. The choice of ordering matters because the expectation is with respect to the first argument. Intuitively, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ penalizes discrepancies where $p$ puts mass but $q$ does not (i.e., regions where $q$ underestimates $p$), while $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ penalizes where $q$ puts mass but $p$ does not.

3. Chain rule (additivity for conditional distributions): For joint distributions $p(x,y)$ and $q(x,y)$,
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x,y)\|q(x,y)) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x)\|q(x)) + \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(x)}\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(y\mid x)\|q(y\mid x))\big].
\]
This decomposition shows that KL divergence breaks into a marginal discrepancy plus the expected conditional discrepancy. It is useful in hierarchical and sequential models.

4. Invariance to parameterization of outcomes: KL is invariant under invertible transformations of the sample space when distributions are transformed accordingly; that is, if $y=T(x)$ is invertible, and $p_Y$ and $q_Y$ are the pushforwards, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_X\|q_X)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_Y\|q_Y)$.

5. Relationship to other divergences: KL is an $f$-divergence with $f(t)=t\log t$. Many other divergences are special cases or relatives (e.g., total variation, Hellinger, $\chi^2$) and inequalities relate them (Pinsker's inequality relates total variation to KL).

Intuition for asymmetry: Suppose $p$ places small probability mass in a region where $q$ assigns zero probability. Then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ is infinite (since $\log(p/q)$ diverges), reflecting the catastrophic underestimation by $q$ of some outcomes that actually occur. Conversely, if $q$ places mass where $p$ has none, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ does not directly penalize that beyond the normalization of $q$; however $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ would penalize such extraneous mass. This asymmetry matters in model fitting: minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ encourages $q$ to cover the support of $p$, whereas minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ encourages $q$ to concentrate on modes preferred by $p$ and can lead to mode-seeking behavior.

# Symmetrized divergences (brief overview)

Because KL is asymmetric, researchers often use symmetrized divergences when a symmetric measure of discrepancy is desired. Two common symmetrized forms are the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence and the Jeffreys divergence.

The Jeffreys divergence is simply the sum of the two directed KL divergences:
\[
D_J(p,q) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p).
\]
It is symmetric, nonnegative, and zero iff $p=q$. It inherits properties from KL but can be infinite if either directed KL is infinite.

The Jensen–Shannon divergence is defined using the mixture $m=\frac{1}{2}(p+q)$:
\[
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p,q) = \tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|m) + \tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|m).
\]
JS divergence is symmetric and always finite (bounded by $\log 2$ when using log base 2), even when the supports of $p$ and $q$ do not overlap. JS has a useful interpretation as the mutual information between a random variable drawn from the mixture and a binary label indicating which component it came from. Because JS is a smoothed symmetrization of KL it is often preferred when domain overlap issues occur. Its square root is a metric, i.e., it satisfies the triangle inequality.

Other symmetrized or alternative divergences include the Hellinger distance, total variation distance, and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). Each has different mathematical and computational properties and is useful in different contexts (e.g., MMD is kernel-based and easy to estimate from samples).

# Relationships between cross-entropy, likelihood and KL

In statistics and machine learning, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and cross-entropy minimization are closely connected to KL divergence. Suppose we have data sampled from some true distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ and we fit a parametric model family $q_\theta$ parameterized by $\theta$. The log-likelihood of parameters $\theta$ on a dataset $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log q_\theta(x_i).
\]
Dividing by $n$ and taking expectation under the empirical distribution $\hat{p}_n(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \delta_{x_i}(x)$ gives the average log-likelihood:
\[
\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\hat{p}_n}[\log q_\theta(x)].
\]
Maximizing the log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the empirical cross-entropy (because cross-entropy is $-\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log q]$). In the population limit, maximizing expected log-likelihood under $p_{\text{data}}$ is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence from $p_{\text{data}}$ to $q_\theta$, because
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|q_\theta) = -H(p_{\text{data}}) - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log q_\theta(x)].
\]
Since $H(p_{\text{data}})$ does not depend on $\theta$, minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|q_\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ is equivalent to maximizing the expected log-likelihood.

This identity explains why MLE fits models to the data distribution in the sense of KL: the fitted model $q_\theta$ attempts to match $p_{\text{data}}$ so as to reduce the KL divergence. Practically, when we use empirical averages, minimizing empirical cross-entropy approximates minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{p}_n\|q_\theta)$.

It is important to recognize the direction of KL in this equivalence: MLE corresponds to minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|q_\theta)$ (data to model). Other estimation or variational procedures may correspond to minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ (model to data), which has different consequences (e.g., mode-seeking vs. mode-covering behavior, as mentioned previously).

Another connection is that negative log-likelihood is an unbiased estimator (up to a constant) of cross-entropy. For example, when using stochastic gradient descent on the average negative log-likelihood, you are performing stochastic minimization of an estimate of cross-entropy, hence of KL.

# Estimators and practical approximations for KL

Often we need to estimate KL divergence (or cross-entropy) from finite samples or when only samples from the distributions are available and densities are unknown or intractable. Estimation strategies depend on what is known and what can be sampled. Here we describe several common approaches, their requirements, and trade-offs.

Plug-in (density estimation) estimators: If you can obtain consistent density estimates \hat{p} and \hat{q} (via kernel density estimation, histograms, or parametric fitting), plug them into the KL formula:
\[
\widehat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \int \hat{p}(x) \log\frac{\hat{p}(x)}{\hat{q}(x)}\,dx,
\]
or compute the corresponding discrete sum. This approach is conceptually simple, but suffers in high dimensions because density estimation is difficult (curse of dimensionality). Kernel density estimation (KDE) works moderately well in low dimensions with large sample sizes but becomes impractical in high-dimensional settings.

k-nearest neighbor (kNN) estimators: There are nonparametric estimators of KL divergence based on distances to nearest neighbors. A well-known approach uses ratios of volumes determined by kNN distances in samples from $p$ and $q$ to estimate local density ratios and thus the KL. kNN estimators can be less sensitive to bandwidth choices than KDE and often perform better in moderate dimensions, but they still degrade as dimension grows and require careful implementation.

Importance sampling and Monte Carlo estimation: Since KL is an expectation under $p$,
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right],
\]
if we can sample from $p$ and evaluate densities $p(x)$ and $q(x)$, we can estimate KL by Monte Carlo. If we cannot sample from $p$ directly but can sample from another distribution $r$, importance sampling gives
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim r}\left[\frac{p(x)}{r(x)} \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right],
\]
and one can estimate by averaging these weighted terms. Importance sampling can have high variance unless $r$ is chosen to be close to $p$ in the regions that matter.

Classifier- or density-ratio based estimators: Many modern methods estimate the density ratio $p(x)/q(x)$ directly, without separately estimating $p$ and $q$. If one can obtain samples from both $p$ and $q$, the density ratio can be obtained by training a probabilistic binary classifier to discriminate samples from $p$ versus $q$. Let a classifier output probability $\sigma(s(x))$ that $x$ came from $p$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $s(x)$ is a score; with equal class priors one has
\[
\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \approx \frac{\sigma(s(x))}{1-\sigma(s(x))}.
\]
This yields an estimate of the log-ratio $\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$. Methods of this kind form the basis of approaches like the KLIEP estimator (Kullback–Leibler Importance Estimation Procedure) and more recent neural estimators.

Variational lower bounds and neural estimators: Direct estimation of KL is hard when densities are complex. Variational approaches convert the KL into an optimization problem. A general template comes from the Donsker–Varadhan representation (for KL) or more general f-divergence variational forms. The Donsker–Varadhan formula states
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sup_{T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}} \left( \mathbb{E}_{p}[T(x)] - \log \mathbb{E}_{q}[e^{T(x)}] \right),
\]
where the supremum runs over measurable functions $T$ for which the expectations exist. In practice one restricts $T$ to a parametric family (e.g., neural networks) and maximizes the objective with Monte Carlo estimates of the expectations. The resulting estimator is known as MINE (Mutual Information Neural Estimation) when used to estimate mutual information (which can be written as a KL). This approach can scale to high dimensions but yields a lower bound whose tightness depends on the expressivity and optimization of the chosen function class.

Other f-divergence variational formulas express divergences in forms suitable for estimation with a discriminative network. For instance, the variational representation for $D_f(p\|q)$ allows training a neural network to estimate the divergence using samples. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) and their variants can be interpreted as minimizing certain f-divergences or their surrogates via classification objectives; this connection motivates classifier-based divergence estimation.

Practical considerations: All sample-based estimators have bias and variance trade-offs. Plug-in estimators tend to be biased due to density estimation errors. Monte Carlo estimators are unbiased (if densities are known and evaluated exactly) but can have high variance. Variational neural estimators produce lower bounds (or upper bounds depending on the representation) and are sensitive to optimization. When estimating KL between a data distribution and a model where the model assigns zero density outside a subset, KL can be infinite; in finite-sample estimation one must watch for numerically unstable log ratios and use smoothing or bounded objectives (e.g., JS) when appropriate.

# Use of KL divergence as a regularizer in probabilistic models

KL divergence is commonly used as a regularizer in probabilistic modeling to encode prior beliefs, encourage closeness to a reference distribution, or impose structure on learned distributions. Below are several major contexts where KL appears as a regularization term, with intuition and typical formulations.

Bayesian regularization and variational inference: In variational Bayesian inference, one approximates an intractable posterior distribution $p(z\mid x)$ with a tractable variational family $q_\phi(z)$. The variational objective (evidence lower bound, ELBO) can be written as
\[
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z)}[\log p(x\mid z)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z)\|p(z)),
\]
where $p(z)$ is the prior. The KL term acts as a regularizer that keeps the variational posterior $q_\phi$ close to the prior $p(z)$, preventing overfitting to the observed data. From another perspective, maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z)\|p(z\mid x))$, so the KL term enforces a balance between fitting the data (first term) and staying near the prior (second term). The strength of this regularization can be controlled by scaling the KL term (e.g., $\beta$-VAE uses $\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}$) to trade off reconstruction fidelity and latent representation properties like disentanglement.

Prior regularization for parameters or functions: In Bayesian modeling of parameters $\theta$, regularization by a prior can be expressed via KL when comparing a posterior approximation $q(\theta)$ to a prior $p(\theta)$. Minimizing the variational objective again yields a KL penalty that pushes $q(\theta)$ toward $p(\theta)$. In non-Bayesian contexts, one can encode a desired reference distribution $p_{\text{ref}}$ for model outputs and penalize $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\text{ref}})$ to encourage outputs to match certain properties (e.g., entropy constraints, smoothness).

Posterior regularization and constrained inference: Sometimes we want inferred distributions to satisfy constraints (e.g., label proportions, structured expectations). Posterior regularization frameworks add a penalty term based on KL to enforce proximity of the posterior to the set of distributions satisfying constraints. The typical formulation minimizes
\[
\min_q D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_\mathrm{prior}) + \lambda \, \mathrm{cost}(q),
\]
where $\mathrm{cost}(q)$ encodes constraint violations. The KL term ensures that the updated distribution does not deviate too far from a prior while satisfying constraints.

Regularization in generative models and likelihood-based training: In latent-variable models like VAEs, the ELBO includes KL between the approximate posterior and the prior. In autoregressive and flow-based models, KL is implicit when fitting via maximum likelihood: minimizing cross-entropy equals minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|q_\theta)$. Regularizers based on KL can also control mode coverage: for example, combining $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|q)$ with $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\text{data}})$ or other divergences can trade off mode-covering and mode-seeking tendencies.

Temperature and annealing: In practice, the relative weight of KL regularization is often adjusted, e.g., by multiplying the KL term by a coefficient $\beta$ (as in $\beta$-VAE) or by annealing the KL penalty during training. Annealing starts with a low weight (allowing the model to fit data) and gradually increases it (encouraging adherence to prior or simplicity). Such strategies help optimization escape poor local minima and balance representation learning against prior conformity.

Interpretation as information bottleneck: The KL term can be viewed as limiting the information flow between inputs and latent variables. In the information bottleneck framework, one minimizes $I(Z;X)$ subject to preserving predictive power; $I(Z;X)$ is a KL between joint and product of marginals and appears as a regularizer that prevents the latent from encoding too much information about inputs, promoting generalization.

Practical pitfalls and numerical considerations: Using KL as a regularizer requires care. If the model $q$ assigns negligible density to areas where the prior or data have support, KL may become large or infinite, destabilizing training. Bounding or smoothing densities, adding floor terms, or using alternative divergences like JS or Wasserstein may be helpful. Furthermore, in variational settings the direction $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$ matters: it tends to produce underdispersed approximate posteriors (mode-seeking) if $q$ is constrained in expressive power; more flexible variational families alleviate this. Choosing the direction and weighting of KL must be informed by modeling goals and computational constraints.

Summary

Entropy and cross-entropy quantify uncertainty and coding cost; KL divergence measures discrepancy between distributions and decomposes cross-entropy into inherent uncertainty plus approximation cost. KL is asymmetric and enjoys useful properties (nonnegativity, chain rule) that make it central to likelihood-based learning and variational inference. In practice, KL and related divergences are estimated using plug-in density estimates, kNN or KDE methods, importance sampling, classifier-based density-ratio estimation, and variational neural estimators built on representations like Donsker–Varadhan. KL is widely used as a regularizer: in variational Bayes it enforces proximity to priors, in posterior regularization it constrains posterior mass, and in representation learning it controls information flow. Understanding both the theoretical properties and practical estimation issues of KL is essential for reading and implementing modern probabilistic learning methods.
