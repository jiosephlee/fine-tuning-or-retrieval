Introduction

Reward modeling and preference learning are central techniques for aligning machine learning systems with human values and objectives. Instead of specifying a reward function by hand, which is often infeasible for complex tasks, we can learn a reward model from human judgments. A particularly effective and practical form of supervision is pairwise preference data: humans compare two trajectories, outputs, or behaviors and indicate which they prefer. From such comparisons, we can infer a latent scalar reward function that explains the rankings, and then use that learned reward to train agents by reinforcement learning or other optimization methods. This chapter develops the theoretical foundations and practical considerations for training reward models from pairwise preferences, describes interfaces and quality control for collecting reliable preference data, explains modern algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimization and Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning, and covers calibration, generalization, and handling of noise and uncertainty. We also discuss the distinction between outcome (terminal) and process (step-wise) supervision and how that shapes the learned reward and downstream behavior.

# Training reward models from pairwise preference datasets

Pairwise preference learning builds a scalar-valued reward model $r_\theta(\tau)$ from examples of human judgments of the form ``trajectory $\tau_a$ is preferred to trajectory $\tau_b$''. Here a trajectory $\tau$ may be a full episode, a partial episode, a single model output (for example, an answer to a question), or any structured object representing behavior. The basic modeling assumption is that the human compares intrinsic utilities of the two trajectories as given by an underlying reward function $r^\ast(\tau)$, possibly corrupted by noise. A simple probabilistic model of pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry or Thurstone model: the probability that $\tau_a$ is preferred to $\tau_b$ is modeled as a logistic function of the difference in reward estimates,
\[
P(\tau_a \succ \tau_b) = \sigma\big(r_\theta(\tau_a) - r_\theta(\tau_b)\big) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\big(-(r_\theta(\tau_a) - r_\theta(\tau_b))\big)}.
\]
Given a dataset of preference pairs $\{(\tau_a^{(i)}, \tau_b^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ with $y^{(i)} = 1$ if $\tau_a^{(i)}$ was preferred and $y^{(i)} = 0$ otherwise, we maximize the likelihood (or equivalently minimize the negative log likelihood) to obtain parameters $\theta$. The loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y^{(i)} \log \sigma(\Delta^{(i)}_\theta) + (1-y^{(i)}) \log(1-\sigma(\Delta^{(i)}_\theta)) \Big],
\]
where $\Delta^{(i)}_\theta = r_\theta(\tau_a^{(i)}) - r_\theta(\tau_b^{(i)})$. This is a standard binary cross-entropy loss on pairwise comparisons. Minimizing it produces a reward function calibrated to human pairwise judgments under the assumed noise model.

Two important modeling choices are the representation of the reward function and the object being compared. In many modern systems $r_\theta$ is parameterized by a neural network taking as input a trajectory or an output text, and possibly context features. The network typically produces a scalar. To avoid trivial scaling degeneracies, it is common to regularize the output (for example, by adding an $L_2$ penalty on $\theta$), or to fix the variance or scale of the logistic noise model. One can also add bias terms or monotonic transforms if the shape of $r_\theta$ needs constraints.

Pairwise losses enjoy advantageous statistical properties: they require only relative judgments, which are often easier for humans than assigning absolute numeric scores, and they scale gracefully when the number of possible outputs is large. However, pairwise data does not directly provide absolute reward values, only ordinal information; the learned reward is identifiable up to increasing affine transformations if the noise model is symmetric. If absolute magnitudes matter—in particular when using the reward to trade off with other objectives or to set stopping criteria—additional calibration steps are needed.

Beyond the logistic choice, other noise models (e.g., Thurstone/Mallows with Gaussian noise) are possible, which lead to different link functions. One may also include annotator-dependent biases by modeling an annotator-specific offset or a skill factor, so that the likelihood becomes $P(\tau_a \succ \tau_b \mid \text{annotator } j) = \sigma(r_\theta(\tau_a) - r_\theta(\tau_b) + b_j)$. Such extensions can capture systematic differences across annotators and improve fit.

# Annotator interfaces and quality control in preference data

Collecting high-quality preference data requires careful design of the annotation interface, annotator instructions, and quality control mechanisms. Humans are better at comparative judgments than absolute scoring, but they are still susceptible to biases, fatigue, anchoring, and misunderstanding. Good interfaces reduce cognitive load and encourage consistent, reproducible judgments.

The interface should present the two items to be compared side-by-side (or sequentially with easy toggling) and show the relevant context. For language tasks, both candidate responses and the prompt or conversation history must be visible; for robotics or control tasks, video clips or concise trajectory summaries should be shown. Crucially, instructions must define the evaluation criteria clearly: what does “better” mean? If the criterion is multi-dimensional (for example, helpfulness, factual correctness, harmlessness), consider asking annotators to focus on a single dimension per labeling pass or to provide pairwise comparisons per dimension. When comparisons across multiple dimensions are needed for a single judgment, consider eliciting scalar ratings on each dimension in addition to a preference, or using structured checklists that guide annotators through key failure modes.

Calibration examples and training tasks are valuable. Presenting annotators with a set of gold or near-gold comparisons, and providing feedback on their answers, improves consistency. Such training should include borderline cases and examples illustrating subtle trade-offs. Annotator qualification tests help ensure basic competence. For complex domains, hiring domain experts rather than crowd workers may be necessary, though this raises cost and scaling issues.

Quality control uses both pre-deployment and ongoing checks. Pre-deployment, qualification tests and redundancy—having multiple annotators review each pair—reduce random errors and allow estimation of inter-annotator agreement. Ongoing checks can include periodic insertion of known control pairs (honeypots), measuring time-on-task, and tracking annotator drift. Probabilistic models that infer annotator reliability, such as Dawid–Skene or Bayesian hierarchical models, can downweight unreliable annotators and impute true labels. When annotators disagree frequently on a pair, the pair may be inherently ambiguous; such ambiguity can either be treated as a signal (and modeled as noise) or filtered out.

Interface design also influences distributional properties of collected data. For example, if annotators are shown pairs sampled from top-k candidates produced by a model, the learned reward will be calibrated primarily within that local neighborhood; to learn a broadly useful reward, sampling from diverse parts of the policy space is important. Pair selection strategies (see next sections) should balance informativeness (pairs that help train the model) with coverage (pairs representative of expected deployment scenarios).

Finally, ethical and practical considerations matter. Annotator well-being should be considered when asking them to rate disturbing content. Clear compensation, reasonable workloads, and transparent instructions reduce errors and harm. Where privacy or safety concerns arise, annotation pipelines should anonymize or redact sensitive data.

# Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and pairwise losses

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a family of methods that adapt model parameters directly using preference comparisons, without explicit separate reward modeling and reinforcement learning steps. It is particularly appealing for language models, where we want to fine-tune a policy to match human preferences while avoiding the instability and sample inefficiency of RL. The key idea is to update the policy model so that it assigns higher probability to outputs that humans prefer, using a loss derived from pairwise comparisons.

Consider a base policy $\pi_\phi$ (e.g., a pretrained language model) that defines a conditional distribution over outputs $y$ given context $x$, with parameters $\phi$. Suppose we have pairwise data where, for each context $x$, an annotator preferred output $y^+$ to output $y^-$. DPO proposes to directly adjust $\phi$ so that the log-probability ratio reflects the observed preferences. One convenient loss is the cross-entropy on pairwise comparisons:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\phi) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \log \sigma\big( \log \pi_\phi(y^+_i \mid x_i) - \log \pi_\phi(y^-_i \mid x_i) - c \big),
\]
where $c$ is an offset that controls the margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs. Intuitively, this loss encourages the policy to increase the log-probability of preferred outputs relative to dispreferred ones. The loss can be interpreted as fitting a pairwise preference model where the preference is driven by the log-likelihood ratio under the policy, possibly plus a scalar reward term.

DPO can be seen as solving a constrained optimization problem: maximize likelihood on human-preferred outputs while keeping the updated policy close to the original policy (to avoid catastrophic changes). A common formulation uses a KL penalty:
\[
\max_\phi \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^+,y^-)}\Big[\log \pi_\phi(y^+ \mid x) - \log \pi_\phi(y^- \mid x)\Big] - \beta \operatorname{KL}(\pi_\phi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{base}}(\cdot\mid x)),
\]
where $\beta$ controls the trust-region size. This framed objective ensures the model embraces human preferences while preserving prior capabilities.

The DPO family includes variants that incorporate a learned reward model implicitly: instead of using raw log-probability differences, one may train a scalar function $s_\theta(x,y)$ and set the policy update to align $\log \pi_\phi(y\mid x)$ with $s_\theta(x,y)$. In that formulation, DPO can be derived as optimizing a KL-regularized expected reward objective where the reward is learned from pairwise comparisons, but the policy is updated directly by gradient descent on the preference loss.

Key advantages of DPO-style methods are simplicity and stability for language models: they avoid the high variance of policy gradient RL by using supervised updates. They also make it straightforward to incorporate pairwise data and to preserve prior behavior. Limitations include potential issues with exploration (since the policy must already produce candidate outputs to compare) and with scaling when preferences require long-horizon reasoning: DPO updates the conditional distribution directly but does not inherently solve credit assignment across long trajectories unless the pairwise comparisons are themselves structured to reflect step-wise preferences.

# Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) and filtering by reward/rule

Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) is a pragmatic technique for improving model behavior by filtering model outputs using a reward model or a rule-based filter and then fine-tuning the model on accepted outputs. The basic pipeline generates candidate outputs from a base model, uses a classifier or reward model to accept or reject each candidate, and then fine-tunes the model on the accepted set as supervised learning. That is, let $\pi_{\text{base}}$ generate outputs $\{y_i\}$ given contexts $\{x_i\}$; given a filter $f(y,x)$ that returns True for acceptable outputs (based on reward threshold, heuristic rules, or human labels), we collect the subset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{accepted}} = \{(x_i, y_i) \mid f(y_i,x_i) = \text{True}\}$ and perform maximum likelihood fine-tuning of the model on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{accepted}}$.

RFT can be motivated as approximating the distribution obtained by rejection sampling with respect to a reward function: if a base model samples from distribution $p_0$ and we accept outputs with probability proportional to $\exp(\alpha r(y))$ (for some $\alpha$), the accepted distribution is
\[
p_{\text{acc}}(y) \propto p_0(y) \exp(\alpha r(y)).
\]
Fine-tuning the model on samples from $p_{\text{acc}}$ moves it toward a policy that favors higher reward outputs while retaining some entropy from $p_0$. RFT avoids RL and uses standard maximum likelihood training, which is stable and scalable.

Filtering rules can be simple heuristics (for example, block outputs containing certain patterns), or learned reward models trained from human preference data. RFT with a rule-based filter is a common first step to reduce glaring failure modes before applying a learned reward model. RFT also provides a data augmentation mechanism: accepted samples are by construction higher-quality, so fine-tuning on them yields practical improvements, often quickly and at low cost.

There are several practical caveats. First, RFT depends on the base model generating enough acceptable examples; if the acceptance rate is very low, you need to either raise the acceptance probability (loosen the filter) or generate massive amounts of data. Second, RFT can amplify existing biases: if the reward model or rule disproportionately filters certain content, the fine-tuned model will underproduce that content. Third, because RFT is supervised, it lacks mechanisms for explicit credit assignment when acceptable outputs require multi-step planning; the filter only signals accept/reject, and if few generated sequences are acceptable, the model may not learn to iteratively improve long-range structure. Finally, RFT does not produce a calibrated reward function useful for downstream RL unless one retains the reward model separately.

One can combine RFT with active sampling: use the current model to produce candidates, filter them, fine-tune, and iterate. This loop can be efficient when the reward or filter is reliable and inexpensive. Another useful variant is to use importance weighting when training on accepted samples to correct for the sampling distribution of the base model, which can under certain conditions reduce bias in the learned policy.

# Calibration, generalization, and robustness of reward models

A learned reward model is only useful insofar as it reflects human preferences outside the narrow dataset it was trained on. Calibration, generalization, and robustness are distinct but related desiderata.

Calibration refers to the alignment between model outputs and true preference probabilities. For a pairwise logistic model, calibration means that if the model predicts $P(\tau_a \succ \tau_b) = p$, then humans would indeed prefer $\tau_a$ to $\tau_b$ roughly $p$ fraction of the time on average. Good calibration is important when reward magnitudes are used for decision-making or for trade-offs with other objectives. Post-hoc methods like Platt scaling or isotonic regression can recalibrate probabilities using held-out data with human decisions, but calibration can break under distribution shift.

Generalization concerns the reward model’s ability to evaluate trajectories from regions of state-action space not well covered by training data. A common failure mode is reward hacking: the model overfits to spurious patterns that correlate with human judgments in the dataset but not with true human preferences. For example, a model might learn that verbose answers tended to be preferred in the training set and thus give higher reward to verbosity even when verbosity is irrelevant or harmful. To improve generalization, training datasets should be diverse and actively targeted at failure modes. Techniques such as data augmentation, adversarial examples, and contrastive sampling (where hard negative examples are intentionally included) increase robustness. Regularization, early stopping, and model ensembling also reduce overfitting.

Robustness addresses both adversarial exploitation and noise in inputs. Reward models can be brittle: small changes to an input can flip its reward drastically. Defensive measures include smoothing models, ensembling, input preprocessing, and training with adversarially-generated negative examples. Certified robustness is generally infeasible for complex reward networks, but empirical robustness testing—systematic perturbations, distributional shift evaluation, and red-teaming—helps surface brittleness.

Evaluating reward models requires careful metrics beyond in-sample accuracy on pairwise tests. One should evaluate the model on held-out human comparisons from distributions similar to intended deployment, on out-of-distribution scenarios, and on downstream performance when used to train policies. Counterfactual and worst-case evaluations (e.g., checking whether optimizing the reward can produce harmful behavior) are important. When possible, retaining a holdout of human preference comparisons for adversarially selected pairs—where the policy is likely to exploit weaknesses—gives a more realistic estimate of robustness.

Another consideration is causal confounding: the reward model should not pick up on features that correlate with user satisfaction in the dataset but are not causally related. For example, if answers using certain words tended to be produced by more careful annotators, the model might prefer those words even when they are irrelevant. Designing data collection to randomize confounds where possible, and using causal techniques in model training, can mitigate this risk.

# Outcome supervision vs. process supervision (terminal vs step-wise rewards)

When learning from preferences or outcomes, a key distinction is whether annotations reflect terminal outcomes (outcome supervision) or intermediate steps/processes (process supervision). Outcome supervision assigns rewards to full trajectories or final states; process supervision provides guidance at each time step or sub-step.

Outcome supervision is often easier to elicit: humans can naturally judge which of two final outcomes is better. Many preference datasets are inherently outcome-level, where annotators compare final answers, dialogues, or completed behaviors. Training a reward model with outcome supervision produces a scalar function $R(\tau)$ that scores whole trajectories. For episodic tasks where final performance matters (e.g., finishing a puzzle, solving a math problem), outcome supervision can be sufficient. However, it raises credit-assignment challenges in long-horizon domains: the reward does not speak to which intermediate actions led to the good final outcome. When optimizing policies with RL using terminal rewards, algorithms must propagate the terminal signal backward through the episode, which can be sample-inefficient and brittle.

Process supervision addresses credit assignment by providing step-wise rewards or preferences over partial trajectories. For instance, annotators may compare prefixes, rank intermediate actions, or provide feedback on individual steps. In language tasks, process supervision could mean preferring more faithful intermediate reasoning steps or factually precise sub-answers. Learning a step-wise reward $r(s_t, a_t)$ allows direct per-step optimization and makes temporal credit assignment explicit. Moreover, process supervision can improve interpretability and reduce reward hacking, because it constrains the agent’s behavior at each step rather than only at termination.

A practical middle ground is to combine outcome and process supervision: use outcome preferences to learn $R(\tau)$ and use process annotations to decompose $R$ into step-wise components. One decomposition approach is to assume additive structure:
\[
R(\tau) = \sum_{t=1}^T r_\theta(s_t, a_t),
\]
and fit the per-step model to match outcome-level comparisons. Techniques like inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) and structured reward learning can infer stepwise rewards consistent with trajectory-level preferences by solving an identifiability-constrained inverse problem. Alternatively, one can collect pairwise preferences over partial trajectories (e.g., which of two discussion threads shows better reasoning up to step $t$) to directly supervise step-wise rewards.

Which supervision to choose depends on task characteristics. For short-horizon tasks with well-defined final objectives, outcome supervision may be sufficient and cheaper. For long-horizon and safety-critical tasks where intermediate steps matter, process supervision reduces risk and improves credit assignment. From an annotator cost perspective, process supervision is often more expensive because more judgments are needed, but targeted process supervision on critical decision points can be highly effective.

# Handling noisy reward labels and uncertainty modeling

Human preference labels are noisy: annotators disagree, make mistakes, or face ambiguous comparisons. A robust reward learning pipeline models label noise, quantifies uncertainty, and propagates uncertainty into downstream decisions.

Noise modeling starts with probabilistic models of annotator behavior. The logistic (Bradley–Terry) model implicitly assumes independent noise at the comparison level with fixed scale. To model annotator-specific noise, extend the likelihood to include annotator parameters, such as bias $b_j$ and reliability $\tau_j$:
\[
P(\tau_a \succ \tau_b \mid j) = \sigma\big(\tau_j [r_\theta(\tau_a) - r_\theta(\tau_b)] + b_j\big).
\]
Hierarchical Bayesian models put priors on $\tau_j$ and $b_j$ to borrow strength across annotators and to estimate reliabilities from limited data. If some annotators are systematically adversarial or incompetent, these models downweight their influence. Modeling ties or “no preference” responses explicitly can also improve fit in ambiguous cases.

We also need to model epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty in $r_\theta$. Aleatoric uncertainty reflects inherent label noise: if humans disagree on a comparison, no deterministic reward can perfectly explain labels. Bayesian approaches or models that output a predictive distribution (mean and variance) capture aleatoric uncertainty naturally. Epistemic uncertainty stems from lack of data in certain regions; ensemble methods, Bayesian neural networks, or Monte Carlo dropout provide uncertainty estimates that tend to grow out-of-distribution, signaling lower confidence.

Uncertainty quantification is useful in multiple ways. For data collection, active learning can prioritize querying comparisons where the reward model is uncertain or where the value of information is high, improving label efficiency. For downstream training, uncertainty-aware policies can avoid making high-stakes decisions when the reward is uncertain or can defer to humans. When optimizing policies against a reward model with uncertainty, risk-sensitive objectives—such as robust optimization against worst-case plausible rewards or Bayesian posterior sampling—can prevent catastrophic exploitation of poorly known reward regions.

Noisy labels can also be mitigated with robust loss functions and label smoothing. For example, when annotators disagree, we can use the empirical proportion of preferences as soft labels rather than forcing a binary target. Reweighting examples inversely proportional to estimated annotator noise reduces influence of unreliable comparisons. When datasets contain systematic biases, counterfactual adjustments or stratified sampling can help.

Finally, evaluation should reflect uncertainty: report confidence intervals on reward model accuracy, measure how performance varies under plausible label noise models, and conduct ablation studies where labels are perturbed. In safety-sensitive settings, require high-confidence validation on critical cases before deploying policies trained on learned rewards.

# Conclusion

Reward modeling from pairwise preferences is a powerful, practical approach to aligning machine learning systems with human judgments. Properly implemented, it leverages humans’ comparative strengths, yields flexible scalar reward models, and integrates well with supervised fine-tuning and RL. Building trustworthy reward models requires careful design of annotator interfaces and quality controls, awareness of the trade-offs between outcome and process supervision, and attention to calibration, generalization, and robustness. Direct Preference Optimization offers an efficient route to incorporate preferences directly into policy updates, whereas Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning is a pragmatic supervised approach that filters and amplifies acceptable behavior. Throughout, explicitly modeling annotator noise and uncertainty—and using those models to guide data collection and decision-making—reduces risk and improves downstream outcomes. As research advances, combining rigorous statistical modeling, active data collection, adversarial testing, and human-centered interface design will be crucial for creating reward models that generalize safely and usefully in complex real-world settings.
