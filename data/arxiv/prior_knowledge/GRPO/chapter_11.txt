Introduction

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related fine-tuning approaches have become critical tools for aligning large language models (LLMs) to human preferences. Among these methods, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a practically effective and widely used policy-gradient algorithm that balances optimization speed with stability. In the context of LLMs, PPO is typically used in an actor-critic setup: an actor (the policy) generates token sequences and a critic (the value function) estimates expected returns; gradients are computed to update the actor while the critic is trained to predict returns. However, applying PPO to autoregressive, high-dimensional policies such as LLMs requires a number of practical adjustments: clipped surrogate objectives, generalized advantage estimation (GAE), careful value-function parameterization, KL regularization toward a reference policy, per-token reward shaping, and attention to memory and compute costs. This chapter explains these components in detail, with intuition, mathematical formulas, and practical implementation guidance so a student can read, implement, or understand modern RLHF/PPO-based LLM fine-tuning papers.

# PPO objective: surrogate loss and clipping rationale

At the heart of PPO is a surrogate objective that tries to improve the policy while constraining large policy updates that can destabilize training. The surrogate loss starts from the importance-sampled policy gradient objective. Let $\pi_\theta$ denote the current policy (parameterized by $\theta$) and let $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ denote the policy that generated a batch of trajectories. For a trajectory (sequence of observations and actions) you compute advantage estimates $A_t$ (covered in the next section). The canonical surrogate objective is:
\[
L^\text{PG}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\bigg[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)} A_t \bigg] .
\]
Here the ratio
\[
r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}
\]
reweights the sampled advantage by how the new policy changes the probability of the taken action.

Why not simply maximize $L^\text{PG}$ by gradient ascent? Because large changes to $\pi_\theta$ can cause the importance weights to explode, degrade off-policy correction, and create collapse or performance collapse. Trust-region approaches like TRPO explicitly constrain the KL divergence between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. PPO provides a simpler and efficient approximation: the clipped surrogate objective. The clipped PPO loss is
\[
L^\text{CLIP}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\big[ \min\big( r_t(\theta) A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t \big) \big] .
\]
Intuition: when $A_t$ is positive, we want to increase the probability of $a_t$, but only up to a factor of $1+\epsilon$. When $A_t$ is negative, we want to decrease probability, but not below $1-\epsilon$. Taking the minimum between the unclipped and clipped objectives imposes a pessimistic bound: if the policy update would make $r_t(\theta)$ move outside the trust region $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ in the direction that improves the surrogate objective, the clipped value takes effect and prevents further improvement of that objective — thereby discouraging large policy shifts.

There are three important consequences of clipping. First, it stabilizes updates by preventing overly large importance weights and avoids the complicated second-order constraints used by TRPO. Second, it can reduce variance in practice because it limits extreme gradient magnitudes. Third, clipping introduces bias: if the optimal update requires leaving the trust region, PPO will not capture that immediately and will need multiple bounded updates instead. The common default value is $\epsilon=0.1$ or $\epsilon=0.2$, but this depends on the task and model scale.

PPO implementations typically use a loss composed of three parts: the clipped policy surrogate $L^\text{CLIP}$ (to be maximized), a value-function loss $L^\text{VF}$ (to train the critic), and an entropy bonus $L^\text{ENT}$ (to encourage exploration and avoid premature determinism). Putting signs and coefficients to form a minimization objective commonly used in code:
\[
L(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_t\big[ \min( r_t A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon) A_t ) \big] + c_1 \, L^\text{VF} + c_2 \, L^\text{ENT}.
\]
Here $L^\text{VF}$ is often a mean-squared error between value predictions and target returns, $c_1$ is its weight, $L^\text{ENT}$ is the negative entropy (so $-c_2 L^\text{ENT}$ is an entropy bonus) and $c_2$ sets its strength.

When applying PPO to LLMs, the "actions" are tokens, so $\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ is a softmax probability produced by the model at each timestep. The ratio $r_t$ is computed per-token. The clipping mechanism remains essential because language models can change token probabilities substantially from a single parameter update, especially when fine-tuning with large learning rates or small batches.

# Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to compute advantages

Policy gradient methods require advantage estimates $A_t$ that measure how much better an action $a_t$ at state $s_t$ is compared to an average baseline. A naive choice is the single-step temporal-difference (TD) error or the empirical return minus value baseline. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) provides a principled way to trade off bias and variance in advantage estimates by exponentially weighting multi-step TD errors.

Define the one-step TD residual (TD error) as
\[
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t),
\]
where $r_t$ is the reward at timestep $t$, $\gamma$ is the discount factor, and $V(\cdot)$ is the critic's value estimate. GAE constructs an advantage estimate by exponentially decaying these TD residuals:
\[
\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}.
\]
In practice trajectories are finite so the sum is truncated. The parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$ interpolates between high-bias/low-variance and low-bias/high-variance estimators. When $\lambda=0$, GAE reduces to the one-step TD error and is lower variance but more biased. When $\lambda=1$, GAE with no truncation equals the Monte Carlo advantage (return minus baseline) with the lowest bias but highest variance. Typical values used in PPO for continuous control are $\lambda=0.95$ or $\lambda=0.97$; in RLHF settings values around $0.95$ are common, though the right choice depends on reward noise and trajectory lengths.

GAE is typically computed efficiently by backward recursion. Letting $\hat{A}_T=0$ at the terminal step, iterate
\[
\hat{A}_t = \delta_t + \gamma \lambda \hat{A}_{t+1}.
\]
Then use the advantage $\hat{A}_t$ in the surrogate objective (usually normalized across the batch to zero mean and unit variance to improve stability). Normalizing advantages helps because their scale can vary across batches and tasks; dividing by empirical standard deviation reduces sensitivity to the learning rate.

In the LLM context, sequences are token sequences and rewards might be sparse or delayed (e.g., a scalar human preference given at the end of a generation). GAE can still be applied by inserting per-token rewards (discussed later) or by treating the reward as zero for intermediate tokens and nonzero at final token. If most tokens have zero reward, GAE with a moderate $\lambda$ helps propagate the delayed final reward backward through the token sequence in a stable manner, providing informative per-token advantages for policy updates.

# Value function learning and critic networks

The critic estimates the value function $V_\phi(s)$ (parameterized by $\phi$) that predicts expected return from state $s$ under the current policy. A good critic reduces variance in policy gradient updates via a baseline $V(s_t)$ and is essential for GAE computation. Designing and training the critic when the state is a partial token sequence requires careful architecture and loss construction.

Architecturally, two common choices exist for LLMs: (1) a separate critic network with its own parameters that takes in the same inputs as the policy encoder and outputs scalar values for each timestep; (2) a shared network where the actor and critic share most weights and only diverge in small heads (e.g., a value head). Sharing parameters reduces memory and can improve sample efficiency since representation learning is shared, but it risks interference between objectives and may complicate optimization; separate critics avoid interference but double model parameters.

In practice, many RLHF implementations use a "value head" attached to the transformer: after computing hidden states for each token, a linear projection plus optionally a small MLP yields $V(s_t)$ for each position $t$. When using a shared backbone, carefully tuning learning rates and loss coefficients is important to avoid destabilizing policy performance. If a large separate critic is used, it must be pretrained or warmed up; random initialization can produce poor value estimates that destabilize early policy updates.

The value loss is typically a mean-squared error between predicted value and target return:
\[
L^\text{VF}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t\big[ \big( V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{R}_t \big)^2 \big],
\]
where $\hat{R}_t$ is the empirical return or a bootstrapped target such as the TD(\lambda) return. In PPO implementations, the value target can be the advantage plus predicted value:
\[
\hat{R}_t = \hat{A}_t + V_{\phi_\text{old}}(s_t),
\]
which aligns with GAE usage.

For stabilization, a common technique is to clip the value function loss similarly to the policy clipping: prevent large deviations between $V_\phi$ and $V_{\phi_\text{old}}$ by clipping the value prediction change to a trust region. Concretely, compute the unclipped squared error and the same squared error but with $V_\phi$ replaced by a clipped version:
\[
V_\phi^\text{clip}(s_t) = V_{\phi_\text{old}}(s_t) + \text{clip}\big(V_\phi(s_t) - V_{\phi_\text{old}}(s_t), -\epsilon_\text{vf}, \epsilon_\text{vf}\big),
\]
and take the maximum of the two squared errors as the loss; or equivalently, take the maximum so you minimize a pessimistic objective. This avoids sudden jumps in the critic that would invalidate advantage estimates.

Another practical consideration is input representation for the critic: when training the value for each token, it is common to use the same causal attention and positional embeddings as the actor, providing consistent state definitions. If the reward is based on the entire sequence, the critic may learn to predict the final reward from partial sequences by capturing cues in prefixes; this is feasible because language statistics often correlate with quality assessments.

Finally, regularization, weight decay, and gradient clipping all matter for the critic. Because the critic is supervised by noisy returns, overfitting and large gradients can be problematic. Use of learning-rate schedules (often lower for the critic than for the actor), smaller minibatches for value updates, and target network warmups are practical strategies to get a stable critic.

# KL-regularized objectives and reference policies

Fine-tuning a large language model with RL-style updates can produce undesirable behavior if the policy drifts too far from a safe or fluent base model. KL-regularized objectives explicitly penalize deviation from a reference policy (often the pretrained model before RLHF) to maintain language quality and prevent reward hacking. There are two common ways to incorporate KL regularization: as a penalty added to the reward/loss and as an objective constraint.

A KL-penalty modifies the reward at each step or the loss directly. Per-token, the KL divergence between the current policy and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ is:
\[
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid s_t) \big) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t) \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)}{\pi_\text{ref}(a\mid s_t)}.
\]
Two practical forms appear:

1. Add a per-step penalty to the reward: define a penalized reward
\[
\tilde{r}_t = r_t - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid s_t) \big),
\]
where $\beta>0$ controls how strongly deviations are discouraged. This penalized reward is used to compute advantages. This approach ties KL directly into the return and advantage signal.

2. Add a penalty to the surrogate objective: subtract $\beta$ times the empirical KL under sampled states:
\[
L^\text{PPO-KL}(\theta) = L^\text{CLIP}(\theta) - \beta \, \mathbb{E}_t\big[ \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid s_t)) \big].
\]
This second form makes KL part of the optimization objective rather than the reward structure.

Instead of a fixed penalty coefficient $\beta$, practical systems often use an adaptive KL controller: measure the average KL between the current policy and the reference policy over a batch, and if it exceeds a target KL value $\text{KL}_\text{target}$, increase $\beta$; if it is below the target, decrease $\beta$. This dynamic adjustment enforces a soft constraint on policy drift. The target KL is chosen based on how much deviation is acceptable (e.g., small value like $0.01$ to $0.1$ in log-probability units depending on tokenization and sequence length).

A related approach is to use a separate reference policy for computing importance ratios. In RLHF pipelines, the reference is often the pretrained policy (frozen) that generated initial logits; this reference provides a stable baseline for KL measurement and can be used to compute per-token log-prob differences:
\[
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_\text{ref}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
\]
Penalizing this log-prob difference scaled by advantage is another way to ensure learning does not encourage actions that drastically increase the likelihood of certain tokens without broad improvements.

KL regularization is particularly important when reward functions are imperfect or sparse, because without it the policy can exploit reward "shortcuts" that lead to fluency degradation, repetition, or gaming the reward model. Setting $\beta$ too large suppresses learning; too small risks collapse. Using an adaptive controller is therefore a common best practice.

# Per-token reward shaping and implementation considerations

LLM actions are tokens in a sequence; rewards are often given at sequence level (e.g., a scalar human preference or a reward model score for the entire generated response). To apply policy gradients, you must attribute final sequence rewards to individual tokens. Per-token reward shaping converts a sequence-level reward into per-timestep rewards so that GAE and per-token advantages can be computed.

A simple and common approach is to assign the same scalar reward at every token position for a generated sequence: if the final sequence receives reward $R$, set $r_t = R / T$ for all tokens $t$ (where $T$ is sequence length), or simply set $r_t = 0$ for intermediate tokens and $r_{T}=R$ at the last token. Both schemes work, but the first spreads the signal across tokens which can reduce variance; the second keeps sparsity but can still be handled by GAE.

A more informed approach is to use a reward model that can output per-token or per-prefix scores. Many reward models are themselves autoregressive or prompt-based models that evaluate quality as a function of the full sequence; however, they can be queried on prefixes to obtain prefix-level scores, and the per-token incremental differences in score can be used as per-token rewards:
\[
r_t = \text{reward\_model}(y_{1:t}) - \text{reward\_model}(y_{1:t-1}).
\]
This delta approach attributes marginal contribution of each token to the final score. It often leads to better credit assignment and more informative GAE signals. It does require additional forward passes through the reward model for each prefix, which increases compute.

When using a separate reward model that produces a scalar for the whole sequence, one must consider normalization across sequences of different lengths. Reward scales should be normalized (e.g., zero mean, unit variance) across a batch of rollouts to avoid instability. Additionally, clipping extreme rewards, or applying a tanh or logistic transform, can reduce the influence of outliers that may otherwise dominate gradients.

Implementation considerations:

- Log-prob storage: For PPO you need $\log \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)$ for each sampled token, because the importance ratio uses the old policy. In practice, when generating rollouts you store the log-probabilities of tokens under the policy at generation time to compute $r_t$ efficiently later. For autoregressive LLMs this requires recording per-token logits or log-probs during sampling.

- Token-level baselines and padding: Many sequences have varying lengths requiring padding in batches. Use attention masks so that losses and advantages are only computed for actual tokens and not padding tokens. When computing returns, ensure you treat end-of-sequence correctly: terminal rewards should be assigned at the proper token index and propagation via GAE should respect masks.

- Detachment and stale policies: Beware of mixing log-probs computed by one set of parameters with value predictions from another without detaching properly. Typically one freezes or snapshots the actor parameters to create $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for ratio computation, and recomputes new log-probs if needed during minibatch optimization, but you must match which policy's log-probs are used in $r_t(\theta)$.

- Reward scaling and advantage normalization: After computing advantages across a batch, subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation. This normalization improves learning stability and reduces sensitivity to the raw reward scale.

- Action sampling strategy: Sampling vs greedy decoding affects exploration and the distribution of generated sequences. During RLHF training you usually sample from the policy (e.g., top-k or nucleus sampling combined with temperature) rather than take argmax, to collect diverse data and estimate gradients. Ensure the log-probabilities stored match the sampling procedure (i.e., include sampling policy effects like temperature adjustments). When training for deployment, the final policy may be distilled or fine-tuned to produce deterministic outputs.

# Memory and compute costs of actor-critic setups for LLMs

Applying actor-critic (PPO) to large models is computationally expensive because you must run multiple forward passes per token per optimization step and maintain both actor and critic networks in memory. Understanding where costs come from helps optimize implementations and hardware allocation.

Key memory and compute factors:

1. Model size: LLMs have billions of parameters. Storing model weights consumes GPU memory even if activation checkpointing is used. If the critic is a separate model of similar size, memory requirements roughly double. To mitigate this, practitioners often attach a lightweight value head to a shared backbone rather than instantiate two full models.

2. Storing rollouts: During rollout generation, you must store token ids, attention masks, and per-token log-probs under the actor that generated them. For long sequences and large batch sizes, this can be significant. Storing entire activations for backprop during generation is unnecessary if you detach rollouts and later recompute log-probs during training; recomputation trades compute for memory.

3. Backprop through actor updates: PPO performs multiple epochs of minibatch optimization over the same collected batch. This means for each epoch you may recompute forward passes to obtain current policy log-probs and value predictions for all stored tokens. If you avoid recomputing by storing logits at generation time, you need to ensure correctness as the current $\theta$ changes — typically you recompute log-probs fresh for $r_t(\theta)$ during optimization, so multiple forward passes across epochs are unavoidable.

4. Reward model cost: If you use a separate learned reward model that scores generated sequences, each sequence may require an additional forward pass. If you shape rewards per token via prefix scoring, that multiplies the reward-model forward passes further. Often the reward model is much smaller than the actor and can be run on CPU or separate GPU(s), but runtime increases.

5. Mixed precision and activation checkpointing: Use mixed precision (FP16/BFloat16) to reduce memory and accelerate matrix ops. Activation checkpointing trades extra forward compute for lowered memory by recomputing activations during backward. Both are widely used to enable training of large models in limited GPU memory.

6. Batch sizes and minibatching: PPO typically collects a large batch of samples before performing updates (e.g., thousands of tokens or many episodes). You must design batch sizes that fit memory while providing stable gradient estimates. Within each PPO epoch, shuffle and create mini-batches; each mini-batch requires forward and backward passes. For LLMs, making mini-batches by concatenating tokens across sequences (flattening) rather than by sequence can be more memory-efficient.

7. CPU memory and data transfer: Storing rollouts on CPU or host memory and then streaming minibatches to GPUs reduces GPU memory footprint but increases host-to-device transfer overhead. Efficient data pipelines and pinned memory reduce training stalls.

8. Multi-GPU and distributed setups: Model parallelism, tensor/model/pipeline parallelism, and data parallelism are necessary for very large models. In distributed settings you must ensure consistent reference policies for KL calculations and coordinate rollout data across workers. Gradient synchronization and mixed-precision collective operations add overhead but are standard in large-model training.

Overall, trading off compute versus memory is common: you may recompute logits and value predictions multiple times during optimization to avoid storing large activations, or you may snapshot and cache some values to save compute at the expense of storage. Practical deployments often use a shared backbone with a small value head, mixed precision, checkpointing, and batched reward-model scoring to make PPO viable for multi-billion-parameter LLMs.

# Common practical hyperparameters and instabilities

PPO and actor-critic training for LLM fine-tuning involve many hyperparameters. Choosing them carefully is essential because wrong choices can produce divergence, mode collapse, or reward hacking. Here are the common hyperparameters, typical values, and the instabilities they influence.

Learning rates: Actor and critic often use different learning rates. Typical actor learning rates for fine-tuning are small (e.g., 1e-5 to 5e-5 for large LLMs), while critics may use similar or slightly higher values. If sharing a backbone, use a small learning rate to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Higher learning rates accelerate learning but risk destabilization and large policy shifts that clipping may not fully prevent.

Clipping parameter $\epsilon$: Common values are 0.1 or 0.2. Smaller values produce more conservative updates and slower learning; larger values increase update magnitude and can destabilize training.

Entropy coefficient: The weight $c_2$ that scales the entropy bonus helps maintain exploration. Typical values are small (e.g., 0.001 to 0.01). Too small leads to premature determinism (reduced exploration) and potential mode collapse; too large prevents learning by overly encouraging randomness.

Value loss coefficient: The weight $c_1$ on $L^\text{VF}$ is typically around 0.5 to 1.0. If too small, the critic may fail to learn good baselines and variance grows; if too large, value updates dominate and policy learning stalls.

GAE parameter $\lambda$ and discount $\gamma$: $\lambda$ is often 0.95 to 0.99, and $\gamma$ is commonly 0.99 or 1.0 for episodic tasks (in language modeling episodic length is sequence length and often $\gamma$ close to 1.0). Setting $\gamma$ too low undervalues long-term rewards in long sequences. $\lambda$ trades bias/variance: lower $\lambda$ reduces variance but blurs credit assignment.

PPO epochs and minibatches: Number of epochs per collected batch can be 3 to 10. More epochs squeeze more learning from collected data but increase overfitting risk to batch and can cause instability if importance ratios deviate from unity. Minibatch sizes should be large enough for stable gradient estimates but small enough to fit memory; in LLMs minibatches of a few sequences or several thousand tokens are common.

KL-target and penalty coefficient: If using an adaptive KL controller, choose a reasonable target KL like 0.01 to 0.1 per-token averaged across a batch. Aggressive targets allow more change and risk degradation; too-conservative targets hinder improvement. The controller's update rule (e.g., multiplicative or PID controller) should be tuned for responsiveness but not oscillation.

Reward scaling and normalization: Normalize rewards (zero mean, unit variance) and consider clipping extremes. Unnormalized rewards lead to poorly scaled advantages and unstable learning. Also be wary of the reward model producing outliers due to adversarial or distributional shift.

Instabilities encountered in practice:

- KL blow-up: If the policy drifts quickly, KL between current and reference policy spikes, causing fluency or safety regressions. Use tighter KL targets, increase penalty, or reduce actor learning rate.

- Value function collapse or divergence: The critic can produce wildly inaccurate values especially early on; this corrupts advantages and leads to poor policy updates. Use lower critic learning rate, clip value updates, and warm-start the critic using a supervised loss or pretraining.

- Reward hacking: The policy learns to maximize rewards in unintended ways (e.g., generating short or repetitive sequences that the reward model favors). KL regularization, reward model regularization, and human-in-the-loop evaluation help mitigate this.

- Mode collapse and repetitive text: Without entropy regularization and careful decoding controls, policies can become repetitive or degenerate. Adjust entropy coefficient and use sampling strategies for rollout.

- Sensitivity to batch collection: With small batch sizes, PPO estimates of gradients and KL are noisy, leading to erratic behavior. Increase batch size or apply gradient accumulation.

- Corrupted advantages from stale value predictions: If you compute advantages using an older critic, mismatches can produce bad updates. Recompute value estimates consistently or ensure snapshotting is handled correctly.

Monitoring practical metrics mitigates instability: track average reward, average KL to reference, policy loss components, value loss, advantage statistics (mean and variance), and sample quality (perplexity, human inspection). When signs of instability appear, reduce learning rates, decrease clip size, or increase KL penalty and resume.

Conclusion

PPO with actor-critic architectures has become a practical standard for RL-based fine-tuning of LLMs because it offers a good compromise between stability and optimization efficiency. However, applying it successfully at scale requires careful attention to the surrogate clipped objective, robust advantage estimation via GAE, well-constructed value critics, KL regularization to a safe reference policy, sensible per-token reward shaping, and hardware-aware engineering to manage memory and compute. Finally, hyperparameter choices and safeguards against common instabilities are crucial: adaptive KL control, advantage normalization, entropy regularization, and conservative learning rates often make the difference between successful alignment and model degradation. With these tools and precautions, PPO-based RLHF can be used to align LLMs to complex human preferences in a controlled and robust way.
