Introduction

Benchmarks and evaluation metrics are the backbone of empirical progress in machine learning. For models that perform mathematical reasoning, code generation, and broad knowledge tasks, careful task design and clear, reproducible evaluation metrics are essential to compare methods, understand strengths and weaknesses, and drive research. This chapter surveys the most widely used benchmarks for mathematical reasoning and general capabilities, explains the variety of task formats and difficulty granularity they expose, and describes the commonly used evaluation metrics and protocols. It also covers how multi-sample evaluation strategies such as Pass@K, majority voting, and self-consistency are computed and why sample budgets matter in practice. The goal is to provide a rigorous yet intuitive understanding so you can read papers that report these benchmarks and reproduce or critique their evaluations.

# GSM8K, MATH, CMATH: task formats and difficulty granularity

GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH are three prominent benchmarks explicitly focused on mathematical reasoning in natural language. They differ along dimensions of input format, problem style (word problems vs. formal math), target output format, and difficulty stratification.

GSM8K (Graduate School Math 8K) is a dataset of around 8,500 grade-school level math word problems written in natural language. Each example consists of a short paragraph describing a real-world scenario and one question requiring arithmetic and multi-step reasoning (e.g., rates, proportions, percentages, counting). The canonical evaluation format requires producing a final numeric answer or short text answer, typically represented as an exact integer or rational number. GSM8K is widely used because it stresses multi-step symbolic reasoning expressed in everyday language and because it provides a fairly uniform difficulty level: problems are accessible to humans with middle- to high-school algebra skills but typically require chaining several simple reasoning steps. Difficulty granularity in GSM8K is modest — the dataset is not designed with fine-grained levels, but researchers often group problems by required number of reasoning steps or by certain tags (e.g., “rate”, “work”, “money”).

MATH is a larger collection of contest-style math problems drawn from high-school and undergraduate competitions. Problems are often more formal, longer, and require higher-level mathematical techniques (algebraic manipulation, calculus, combinatorics, number theory). The dataset is structured with a variety of problem types and comes with difficulty annotations: problems are grouped by contest level or by an objective estimate of difficulty (for example, Bronze/Silver/Gold or contest year and level). MATH tasks generally expect a symbolic final answer or a full solution; however, many evaluations reduce this to verifying a final answer because automatic checking of free-form proofs is challenging. MATH therefore probes deeper mathematical maturity and the ability to apply advanced techniques, making it suitable for assessing models intended to do research-style reasoning.

CMATH (sometimes stylized as C-MATH) is a dataset centered on computational or code-oriented mathematics problems, often written to be solved with computer algebra systems or programmatic reasoning. The questions can look more like programming tasks that output symbolic expressions or numeric computations, cementing a bridge between natural-language problem descriptions and programmatic solution formats. Difficulty in CMATH is often expressed in terms of the complexity of symbolic manipulation required, the sophistication of algorithms to be implemented, or the amount of mathematical background knowledge necessary.

Across these datasets, difficulty granularity can be explicit (contest tiers in MATH) or implicit (the number of reasoning steps, presence of advanced topics). When reporting results, researchers typically stratify performance by these perceived difficulty buckets or by quantitative proxies such as problem length, number of numeric entities, or required number of arithmetic operations. Note also that formatting of the target output matters: exact symbolic matches are strict; normalized numeric answers (e.g., floating point to a tolerance) are more permissive, and full proof/solution checking is the most complex to evaluate.

# MMLU and BBH: broad knowledge and reasoning assessments

While GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH focus on mathematical reasoning, broader assessments such as MMLU and BBH evaluate general knowledge and diverse reasoning skills across many domains. These benchmarks test how well models have acquired and can apply world knowledge, professional and academic subject matter, and cross-domain reasoning.

MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) is a wide-coverage benchmark that contains multiple-choice questions across dozens of subjects: elementary mathematics, high school biology, world history, law, computer science, and many specialized academic subjects. Each question is phrased as a multiple-choice item with typically four or five options. The evaluation metric is simply accuracy: the fraction of questions where the correct option is chosen. MMLU is valued for its breadth: a single aggregate score measures generalist capability. However, its multiple-choice format influences how models are evaluated; models can exploit statistical patterns in the choices or calibration differences. To unpack performance, researchers often report per-subject scores, allowing inspection of particular strengths (e.g., strong computer science, weak law).

BBH (Big-Bench Hard subset, or more generally, the Hard tasks of BIG-Bench) is a curated collection of tasks from the larger BIG-Bench repository that emphasize tasks that remain challenging for state-of-the-art models. BBH includes tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, commonsense inferences, symbolic manipulation, and unusual formats designed to prevent simple surface-level strategies. The tasks are frequently few-shot prompts and require creative or structured reasoning. BBH evaluation also typically uses accuracy for multiple-choice or exact-match tasks, but since BBH is a heterogeneous suite, the scoring may vary by task. The objective of BBH is to provide a harder, more discriminative benchmark than broader, easier sets.

Both MMLU and BBH are primarily designed as zero-shot or few-shot multiple-choice evaluations, which allows straightforward comparison across models. They are useful for assessing the breadth of capability a model has after pretraining and for observing how few-shot prompting or chain-of-thought prompting changes performance in diverse domains.

# HumanEval and MBPP for code generation

HumanEval and MBPP (Mostly Basic Python Programming) are two widely used benchmarks to evaluate code generation capability. They stress the model’s ability to write functional, correct code from a natural language specification.

HumanEval is a curated suite of programming problems typically centered on Python functions. Each problem contains a function signature and a description of the expected behavior, sometimes with examples. The canonical evaluation process is to run the generated function against a set of unit tests. A solution is considered correct if it passes all tests. This operationalizes correctness: code that compiles and produces the expected outputs on the tests is accepted, while code that crashes or produces incorrect outputs is rejected. HumanEval usually provides one or more canonical unit tests and additional hidden unit tests for robustness. Because the verification is automated with execution, HumanEval is often considered a gold standard for functional correctness in code generation.

MBPP is a dataset of short Python programming problems that are intentionally “mostly basic” but cover common programming idioms. Like HumanEval, MBPP uses automated unit tests to judge correctness. MBPP typically includes prompt examples for few-shot evaluation, and the problems expose typical pitfalls for code-generation models (edge cases, tricky string manipulation, off-by-one errors). MBPP is valued for diverse, realistic problems while still being tractable to fully automatic evaluation.

Key practical considerations when using these benchmarks include deterministic execution environment, controlling library availability, and sandboxing for safety. The evaluation computes exact-match correctness of the function’s outputs. Because models are stochastic and can produce many candidate solutions, researchers often use sample-based metrics such as Pass@K (explained below) to summarize performance across multiple generations. Human baselines are sometimes obtained by timing humans solving the same problems or by collecting human-written implementations.

# miniF2F and formal mathematics evaluation

Evaluating formal mathematics — proofs checked by proof assistants — presents different challenges than natural-language problem solving. miniF2F is a benchmark that probes the ability of models to produce formally verified proofs in interactive theorem provers (ITPs) such as Isabelle, Coq, or Lean.

miniF2F contains a selection of theorems from formal mathematics libraries or from contest-style problems that have been translated into the formal language of a theorem prover. Each example consists of a theorem statement in the ITP’s formal syntax; the goal for the model is to produce a proof script or tactic sequence that the prover accepts. Because the theorem prover rigorously checks each proof step, passing the formal checker is a strong signal of correctness. Formal evaluation is exacting: a single erroneous tactic or syntactic mismatch makes the proof fail to check.

Formal mathematics evaluation differs from natural-language proof evaluation in several ways. First, it requires producing syntactically valid code in the logic of the theorem prover. Second, the search space and the granularity of steps are different: theorem provers expect tactics at a certain level of abstraction, and successful strategies often involve domain-specific libraries. Third, miniF2F and similar benchmarks often measure not only final success rate but also the minimum sequence length of tactics, the use of automated tactics (e.g., “auto”), and the reuse of existing lemmas. Because formal proofs can be long, researchers sometimes allow partial-credit metrics (e.g., passing a subset of theorems) or measure time-to-proof under a proof-search engine.

miniF2F is important because it removes ambiguity in grading: the proof checker is the arbiter. It also reveals whether a model has learned to interact with symbolic, formal systems and to produce rigorous derivations — a different capability from producing plausible natural-language proofs.

# Metrics: exact-match accuracy, Pass@K, Maj@K, F1 where applicable

Choosing the right metric depends on task structure. Here are the common metrics, their definitions, and practical considerations.

Exact-match accuracy. For classification or single-answer tasks (including multiple-choice or single true/false answers), exact-match accuracy is the fraction of examples where the model’s predicted answer exactly equals the ground-truth answer. Formally, given dataset size $N$ and predictions $\hat{y}_i$, exact-match accuracy is
\[
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}(\hat{y}_i = y_i).
\]
Exact match can be strict in free-form text tasks where different correct phrasings exist. For numeric answers, normalization (trimming whitespace, numeric rounding) is often applied before exact comparison.

Pass@K. Pass@K is commonly used for code generation tasks where a model may produce multiple candidate programs per prompt. The core idea is: given $K$ attempts per problem, what fraction of problems have at least one correct candidate? Let $N$ be the total number of generated samples per problem during evaluation and let $c$ be the number of correct samples observed among the $N$ generations. The probability that at least one of a uniformly random subset of size $k$ contains a correct sample equals
\[
\text{pass@k} = 1 - \frac{\binom{N-c}{k}}{\binom{N}{k}},
\]
provided $k \le N-c$. If $c=0$, then pass@k is zero. When researchers run a fixed number $n$ of independent samples per problem and observe $c$ correct ones, the unbiased estimator for pass@k is given by the same combinatorial formula applied per-problem, or a conservative Monte Carlo estimate can be used. A frequently cited estimator for the special case where $N$ is large and sampling is i.i.d. is
\[
\widehat{\text{pass@k}} = 1 - \frac{\binom{N-c}{k}}{\binom{N}{k}}.
\]
Intuitively, Pass@K captures the utility of sampling multiple times: a model that occasionally generates a correct solution can still achieve good pass@k for modest $k$.

Maj@K (Majority@K). Maj@K is a metric often used for structured-output tasks or reasoning tasks where the model outputs a final answer after sampling $K$ chains (for example, with stochastic chain-of-thought generations). The idea is to take the plurality or majority answer among $K$ generated outputs and treat this aggregated answer as the model’s prediction. Maj@K is used to estimate the benefit of ensembling multiple reasoning traces: if the correct answer is the modal response among the set, it is likely easier to recover by voting. Formally, for each example we generate $K$ outputs $\{\hat{y}_{i,1},\dots,\hat{y}_{i,K}\}$ and set the aggregated answer $\tilde{y}_i$ to the most frequent $\hat{y}_{i,j}$. Maj@K accuracy is then
\[
\text{Maj@K} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}(\tilde{y}_i = y_i).
\]
Maj@K helps reduce noise and leverages the fact that incorrect answers may be diverse while correct answers can be consistent.

F1 score. The F1 metric is relevant when outputs are structured sets of labels rather than single labels, or when partial credit for overlapping answer content is appropriate. Precision is the fraction of predicted items that are correct; recall is the fraction of ground-truth items that were predicted. The F1 score is the harmonic mean:
\[
\text{F1} = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}.
\]
In NLP, F1 is frequently used for question-answering with span outputs, where predicted spans may partially match the gold span. In math and code, F1 is less common because exact correctness is often the aim, but it can be used when multiple correct answers exist or partial credit is meaningful.

Practical caveats. For exact-match metrics, normalization and canonicalization matter: remove whitespace, standardize numeric format, and apply tokenization/lemmatization where appropriate. For Pass@K, be careful with dependence on the sampling procedure (temperature, nucleus sampling) and with the number of generated samples $N$ used to compute the combinatorial estimator. For Maj@K, tie-breaking rules and deterministic ordering can affect results. Always report standard errors or confidence intervals, and when possible, evaluate on held-out or hidden test splits to avoid overfitting on evaluation heuristics.

# Few-shot, zero-shot, and chain-of-thought evaluation protocols

Evaluation protocol describes how prompts are constructed and what auxiliary information is given to the model during evaluation. The three major prompting paradigms are zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought (CoT), each probing different aspects of model capability and generalization.

Zero-shot evaluation supplies the model with only the test prompt and maybe a task instruction, without any exemplars. For a multiple-choice item, a zero-shot prompt might simply restate the question and ask for the correct choice. Zero-shot is useful to measure how much a model can generalize from pretraining alone. It is often the most realistic for deployed systems, since designers might not craft few-shot examples for every query.

Few-shot evaluation provides the model with a small number of input-output pairs (exemplars) in the prompt before the test item. Typically, these exemplars are in-distribution examples formatted the same way as the test problem. Few-shot can substantially improve performance if the model is sensitive to in-context learning, guiding its output format and solving strategy. In choosing exemplars, researchers should pay attention to exemplar selection strategies (random vs. representative vs. hard negatives), order effects, and whether the exemplar set leaks data from the test distribution.

Chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation gives the model the opportunity to generate or be primed with intermediate reasoning steps, rather than only a final answer. There are two main uses of CoT in evaluation: (1) CoT prompts as exemplars in few-shot prompting, where the exemplars include step-by-step reasoning; and (2) forcing the model to produce a chain during inference to reveal its internal reasoning. When applied carefully, CoT can dramatically improve performance on multi-step reasoning tasks because it helps the model decompose problems into tractable subproblems. However, CoT introduces evaluation complexity: should the metric consider only the final answer or also the fidelity of intermediate reasoning? Most benchmarks focus on final-answer correctness, ignoring the correctness of intermediate steps, because automated grading of reasoning traces is hard. When CoT is used, ensure prompts match evaluation goals (e.g., use chain exemplars when you want multi-step decomposition).

Both few-shot and CoT evaluations can be performed in zero-shot-CoT style as well: a zero-shot-CoT prompt is a question followed by an instruction like “Let's think step by step,” encouraging the model to produce a chain without exemplars. This hybrid approach has different empirical behavior and is sensitive to prompt phrasing and model size.

For rigorous comparisons, always specify the exact prompt templates, the set of exemplars used, random seeds, sampling parameters (temperature, top-p), and whether the chain-of-thought text is used to produce the final answer automatically or judged by human readers.

# Self-consistency / majority voting evaluation and sample budgets

Self-consistency, majority voting, and similar ensembling techniques are pragmatic ways to leverage multiple stochastic samples per prompt to improve final-answer accuracy. The intuition behind self-consistency is that even if individual sampled reasoning traces are noisy, the correct answer might appear multiple times across samples, so a voting procedure can recover it. This approach is especially effective with chain-of-thought sampling, where different chains explore different reasoning paths.

Self-consistency evaluation proceeds as follows. For each input, sample $K$ reasoning traces from the model using stochastic decoding (e.g., temperature sampling). For each trace, extract the final answer. Then aggregate these answers by majority vote (Maj@K), selecting the most frequent answer as the prediction. If the sample distribution concentrates mass on the correct answer, majority voting improves accuracy. If incorrect answers cluster, voting may fail. Self-consistency is closely related to Maj@K; the term “self-consistency” emphasizes the idea that consistent answers across multiple chains are likely to be correct.

Sample budgets are the practical constraints on how many model generations you can perform per prompt. Each sample incurs compute and latency costs. When comparing methods, it is crucial to fix or control for the sample budget. For instance, if Method A is allowed to generate 20 samples per prompt and Method B only 1, a higher accuracy for A could simply reflect more sampling rather than a better underlying model. Researchers often report performance as a function of $K$ (e.g., pass@1, pass@5, pass@20) and might normalize compute by matching the total number of tokens used. Another common design is to fix the total sample budget per prompt (e.g., 2000 tokens) and allow variable numbers of generations depending on their lengths.

When computing ensemble metrics like Pass@K from a finite set of $N$ samples, use unbiased estimators or the combinatorial formula described earlier. If you want to simulate the effect of a larger $K$ than you sampled, be careful — resampling with replacement has different statistical properties than sampling without replacement. For reproducibility, report both raw observed numbers (e.g., number of correct samples $c$ out of $N$) and the derived Pass@K.

Finally, consider variance and confidence intervals. Sampling-based metrics have nontrivial variance: the same model can produce different pass@k estimates depending on random seeds. Report standard error or use bootstrapping across examples to quantify uncertainty. Also consider whether majority-voting or self-consistency procedures use simple plurality or require a supermajority threshold and how ties are broken — these design choices can affect reported numbers.

# Putting it together: best practices and pitfalls

When evaluating mathematical and general capabilities, follow a few best practices to make results clear, reproducible, and fair. First, always specify the exact prompt templates (including exemplars and their ordering), decoding parameters (temperature, top-k, top-p), and the number of samples per example. If using chain-of-thought, publish the CoT exemplars and clarify whether the chain is used only to produce a final answer or also evaluated for fidelity.

Second, choose metrics aligned with task goals. For code tasks, prefer pass@k and exact functional correctness via tests. For formal proofs, use the theorem prover to accept or reject proofs. For multiple-choice tasks, use accuracy, and report per-category scores for insight. Always normalize outputs for exact-match scoring to avoid penalizing superficial formatting differences.

Third, control for sampling budgets and compute. When comparing methods that rely on sampling, present performance as a function of $K$ or account for total tokens used. Use the combinatorial Pass@K estimator when appropriate and report $N$ and $c$ for transparency.

Be aware of common pitfalls. Overfitting to test suites (e.g., training on dataset leaks) can inflate reported performance; always use held-out or private test splits. For chain-of-thought, measuring only final answers hides whether chains are truthful or merely plausible-sounding; consider human evaluations of intermediate steps if interpretability is a goal. For code evaluation, unit tests may not capture all failure modes (e.g., performance, edge cases, security); complement automated tests with additional checks when possible. Finally, statistical significance matters: sampling variance can be large, so report confidence intervals and perform appropriate hypothesis tests when comparing methods.

Summary

This chapter outlined the key benchmarks and evaluation metrics used for mathematical reasoning, code generation, and broad capability assessment. GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH probe increasing levels of mathematical difficulty and formality; MMLU and BBH measure broad knowledge and general reasoning; HumanEval and MBPP provide executable checks of code-generation capabilities; miniF2F evaluates formal proofs using theorem provers. For metrics, exact-match accuracy, Pass@K, Maj@K, and F1 (where applicable) are the principal tools, each with precise formulas and assumptions. Evaluation protocols — zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought, and self-consistency — strongly influence outcomes and should be carefully reported, along with sample budgets and variance estimates. Understanding these datasets and metrics allows you to critically read research papers, reproduce results, and design rigorous new evaluations.
