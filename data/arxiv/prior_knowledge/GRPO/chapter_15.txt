Introduction

Formal theorem proving studies the representation, manipulation, and verification of mathematical statements and their proofs inside precise logical systems. Where traditional mathematics relies on informal, human-readable proofs that allow for gaps, intuition, and context-dependent reasoning, formal theorem proving demands that every logical step be represented within a formal language so that a machine can check correctness with respect to a fixed set of inference rules. This chapter introduces core ideas needed to understand modern research on autoformalization — the automatic translation of informal (natural-language or LaTeX) mathematics into formal statements and proofs — and how that work interplays with interactive proof assistants and automated provers. We will survey representative proof assistants (Isabelle, Coq, Lean), explain how automated provers and tactics are incorporated (for example, Sledgehammer in Isabelle), describe a prominent benchmark (miniF2F) and its evaluation protocol, distinguish proof sketches from full formal proofs in verification pipelines, and conclude with limitations and open problems in current autoformalization approaches.

# Informal-to-formal translation (autoformalization) goals and challenges

Autoformalization aims to convert informal mathematical content — theorems, definitions, and proof sketches written in natural language, LaTeX, or other human-readable forms — into fully formal representations that can be checked and manipulated by proof assistants. The primary goals of autoformalization are: (1) to scale formal verification to a much larger fraction of existing and new mathematics by reducing the human labor required to produce formal proofs; (2) to enable downstream reasoning tasks such as automated proof search, theorem reuse, and program extraction from verified specifications; and (3) to provide datasets and evaluations that spur progress in both natural language understanding and formal methods.

Achieving these goals faces a constellation of deep challenges. First, natural-language mathematics is inherently ambiguous: names are overloaded, pronouns and definite descriptions rely on context, quantifier scope may be unstated, and notation can be overloaded or locally defined. Consider the informal sentence “Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\)”. To formalize this, a system must identify the domain and codomain intended for \(f\), the meaning of “continuous” (which depends on the topology or metric), and whether measurability, differentiability, or other structures are implicitly assumed elsewhere in the text.

Second, informal proofs often omit many routine but necessary steps. A human reader supplies these steps from shared background knowledge and library theorems; an autoformalizer must either infer and insert such lemmas or rely on a large formal library to bridge gaps. For example, to formalize a sentence like “By Cauchy-Schwarz we get …”, the system must detect that the preconditions of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality are satisfied in the current context and instantiate it appropriately. Without access to a well-structured and appropriately named library theorem, the translation can fail.

Third, formal systems differ in logical foundations, type systems, and libraries. Some proof assistants use dependent type theory (Coq, Lean) while others use higher-order logic (Isabelle/HOL). Translating an informal statement requires choosing an appropriate target logic and mapping informal concepts to constructs in the chosen system. This mapping is nontrivial: notions like “set”, “function”, “group”, or “continuous” have different canonical encodings and namespaces across systems.

Fourth, there is a combinatorial search problem: even once statements are represented formally, discovering a sequence of inference steps (a proof) can require exploring a large space of tactics, lemmas, and instantiations. Autoformalization pipelines must often interleave translation and proof search: a partial formal statement might be adjusted to make automated proving feasible, or the system may try several formal encodings until an automated prover succeeds.

Finally, evaluation of autoformalization is itself complex. Ideally we want systems that produce proofs that are checkable inside proof assistants. However, measuring success may involve multiple criteria: faithfulness to the informal text, logical correctness, degree of automation, and the compactness or human-readability of the produced formal proof. Different downstream applications prioritize different criteria.

# Overview of theorem provers: Isabelle, Coq, Lean (brief comparisons)

Isabelle, Coq, and Lean are three widely used interactive theorem provers that support building machine-checkable proofs. Each has a different foundational logic, design priorities, and ecosystem. Understanding their contrasts clarifies the choices autoformalizers must make.

Isabelle is a generic proof assistant framework that most commonly hosts Isabelle/HOL, a powerful instance based on classical higher-order logic (HOL). The underlying logic is non-dependent, and the system emphasizes automation and integration with external automated theorem provers (ATPs). Isabelle’s structured proof language, Isar, is designed to make proofs more human-readable; proof scripts resemble mathematically structured arguments. Isabelle has extensive libraries for analysis, algebra, and discrete mathematics, and strong facilities (such as Sledgehammer) to call ATPs to discharge proof obligations.

Coq is based on the Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC), a constructive dependent type theory that unifies propositions and types (the Curry-Howard correspondence). Coq’s strengths include rich expressive power via dependent types, suport for program extraction (deriving executable code from constructive proofs), and a long history in formalizing complex mathematics and verified software. The style of proof in Coq often uses tactic scripts (e.g., using Ltac) to build terms in the underlying type theory. Because Coq is constructive by default, some classical reasoning requires extra axioms (like excluded middle), which must be included consciously.

Lean is likewise based on dependent type theory (initially Lean 3 and later Lean 4) and is designed with a strong focus on an accessible mathematics library (mathlib) and interactive development. Lean balances automation and expressivity: its tactic framework allows powerful proof-development strategies, and its community-driven mathlib provides a large repository of formalized mathematics. Lean’s approachable syntax and emphasis on developing a shared mathematical library have made it popular for projects that aim to formalize mainstream mathematics.

Key contrasts among these systems include their logical foundations (classical HOL vs constructive dependent type theory), library coverage and naming conventions, and the availability and integration of automation. Autoformalizers must handle these differences: a translation that maps naturally into Isabelle/HOL may not carry directly to Coq where dependent types and constructive interpretations matter. Practical autoformalization often targets a specific assistant to take advantage of mature libraries and tooling.

# Automated provers and tactics (e.g., Sledgehammer for Isabelle)

Automated provers and tactic frameworks are critical components that aid both interactive and automatic proof construction. At a high level, automated provers (ATPs) are tools that attempt to find formal proofs of first-order (or certain higher-order) logic formulas without human guidance by performing search in a space of inference rules. Tactics are higher-level scripted procedures inside proof assistants that manipulate proof states by applying lemmas, simplifications, or calls to ATPs.

Sledgehammer, a widely cited example in Isabelle, illustrates the synergy between an interactive assistant and external automated provers. When a user encounters a subgoal in Isabelle, Sledgehammer extracts a set of relevant facts from the current context and the global library using heuristic relevance selection. It translates the subgoal and these facts into formats understood by external ATPs (like E, Vampire, or Z3) or SAT/SMT solvers, and then invokes these solvers. If an ATP returns a proof (often expressed as a proof trace), Sledgehammer will attempt to reconstruct a corresponding proof in Isabelle’s logic using internal reconstruction tools (e.g., Metis or proof-producing reconstruction procedures). This pipeline leverages the brute force of ATPs to guide the interactive development, making many routine subgoals trivial for users.

Automated provers differ in the logics they target and the techniques they use. First-order ATPs (E, Vampire) excel at problems reducible to first-order logic with equality, applying resolution, superposition, and saturation methods. SMT solvers (Z3, CVC4/5) handle combinations of theories (integers, reals, arrays) and often provide efficient decision procedures for quantifier-free fragments with heuristics for quantifiers. Higher-order provers and translation layers extend ATP capabilities to higher-order logics but with increased complexity.

Tactics in proof assistants are programmable constructs combining lemma application, rewriting, case-splitting, and calls to external solvers. For instance, Coq’s tactics like intros, rewrite, and apply form the building blocks of proofs; more advanced tactics automate proof search over a local context. Proof automation frameworks apply machine learning to recommend tactics or proof steps given a goal; these approaches can accelerate proof development but still require careful integration and proof reconstruction to ensure the final proof is valid in the assistant’s kernel.

An important practical point is proof reconstruction. ATPs usually operate in logics or representations that differ from the target assistant. Their outputs may not be directly checkable inside the assistant. Reconstruction converts ATP solutions into sequences of accepted primitive inference steps (or uses internal tactics that replay the ATP’s reasoning). Reconstruction is a nontrivial subproblem: it requires aligning symbols and naming conventions, reproducing lemma instantiations, and bridging differences in foundational axioms.

# miniF2F benchmark and evaluation protocol

miniF2F is a benchmark designed to measure progress in the autoformalization and automated theorem proving of undergraduate-level mathematics. The dataset originates from a curated subset of competition and textbook problems that are considered amenable to formalization. The name “F2F” stands for "formal-to-formal" or "formalization to formal verification" depending on the context, but miniF2F specifically aims to be small enough for experimentation yet representative of typical mathematical reasoning found in first-year to advanced undergraduate problems.

The benchmark contains problem statements formulated in natural language or LaTeX, along with, for many entries, a target formal theorem in a specific proof assistant. The intended evaluation measures whether an automated pipeline can take the informal statement, produce a corresponding formal statement in the target assistant, and then produce a verified proof that the assistant accepts. Evaluation thus partitions into two stages: statement autoformalization and automated proving. A common protocol requires that the output be a fully-checked proof within the chosen assistant, not merely an answer or informal justification.

In practice, submissions to miniF2F are measured by the fraction of problems for which the system succeeds in this end-to-end pipeline within a predefined time budget. Because formalization and proving are intertwined, many approaches either (1) translate the statement into the assistant language and rely on automated tactics/ATPs to find a proof, or (2) generate a candidate proof sketch (e.g., a sequence of tactics or high-level proof steps) and map it into the assistant for checking.

Baselines evaluated on miniF2F often combine neural sequence models for translation with tactic-level or ATP-based proof search. Reported success rates are useful to quantify progress but should be interpreted carefully: success depends not only on the translation quality but also on the availability of libraries, the assistant choice, and the integration with ATPs. The benchmark has functioned as a practical testbed for research on neural autoformalization and on neural-guided proof search, providing measurable comparisons across systems.

# Proof sketches vs full formal proofs and verification pipelines

A proof sketch is a high-level description of a proof that captures the main ideas and key lemmas but omits many low-level inference steps and routine justifications. In contrast, a full formal proof provides a complete sequence of inference steps or tactics that, when checked by a proof assistant’s kernel, establish the theorem with no gaps. Proof sketches are closer in spirit to the informal proofs mathematicians write: they communicate the essential strategy and highlight nontrivial steps while omitting routine reasoning.

In verification pipelines, proof sketches often serve as an intermediate artifact. A typical pipeline might start with an informal statement and produce a proof sketch (either generated by a model or extracted from human text). Automated procedures or human developers then refine this sketch into a full formal proof by filling in omitted steps, resolving side conditions, and applying appropriate lemmas from the target library. Alternatively, a system can attempt to directly produce full formal proofs through tactic prediction or ATP calls.

Working with proof sketches has both advantages and drawbacks. Sketches reduce the search space for automated proving by constraining the high-level structure: instead of searching blindly for any proof, the prover is guided to try a specific lemma instantiation or induction pattern. This can dramatically increase efficiency and success rates. However, turning a sketch into a formal proof is nontrivial because the missing details may require a significant amount of background knowledge or creativity to fill. Moreover, sketches must be precise enough for automated tools to interpret; vague or underspecified sketches can be less useful than complete formal proofs.

Full formal proofs provide stronger guarantees: once the proof assistant accepts the proof, the kernel has checked each step according to its primitive rules, ensuring correctness relative to the assistant’s axioms. Constructing full formal proofs automatically typically needs sophisticated tactics, integration with ATPs, and robust proof reconstruction. Many successful autoformalization systems thus adopt a hybrid approach: they generate a formal statement and a partial proof or tactic trace, then use ATP-equipped tactics and reconstruction to complete the verification. Some pipelines also include human-in-the-loop steps where users review and patch the translations or final proofs.

A practical pattern is to structure the pipeline into modular stages: (1) parsing and semantic analysis of the informal text, producing a candidate formal representation; (2) candidate lemma selection and proof sketch generation (often via neural models); (3) automated proof search using tactics and ATPs; and (4) proof reconstruction and checking inside the assistant. Each stage can fail for different reasons, and efficient pipelines implement fallback strategies (try different encodings, expand the set of candidate lemmas, request human clarification).

# Limitations of current autoformalization approaches

Despite notable progress, current autoformalization systems face important limitations that restrict their capability and generality. One major limitation is the brittleness caused by dependence on specific proof assistant libraries and idioms. A model trained to translate into Lean’s mathlib will not necessarily perform well for Isabelle/HOL or Coq, because the available definitions, lemma names, and canonical encodings differ. This implies heavy engineering effort to produce high-quality translations for each target system and makes cross-system generalization challenging.

Another limitation is semantic understanding. Current systems — especially those based on sequence models — can learn statistical correlations between informal phrasing and formal constructs, but they often struggle with deep semantic reasoning or long-range contextual dependencies. When an informal text relies on definitions introduced several paragraphs earlier or uses domain-specific notation, models may fail to correctly infer the intended meaning. Moreover, ambiguous constructs or implicit quantifier scopes are a persistent source of errors.

Scaling proof search is also a bottleneck. Even with strong ATPs, the search space for complex theorems grows quickly. ATPs rely on heuristics and relevance filters to select useful lemmas, but these heuristics can miss important facts or select misleading ones, causing wasted search. Integration mechanisms like Sledgehammer mitigate this but are not foolproof. Combining neural guidance with symbolic search shows promise, but it adds complexity and sometimes sacrifices soundness if not carefully reconstructed within the assistant.

Proof reconstruction remains a pragmatic challenge. An ATP can produce a proof certificate in its native format, but reconstructing that certificate as a sequence of kernel-acceptable steps in the target assistant is often difficult. Reconstruction must resolve naming differences, implicitly used axioms, and differing treatment of equality or choice principles. Failures in reconstruction mean that ATP success does not always translate into verified success inside the assistant.

Human-level creativity and strategy are still out of reach for fully automated systems. Many mathematical proofs rely on clever definitions, changes of perspective, or nontrivial lemma inventions that are not routine or pattern-based. Neural models trained on existing corpora can reproduce common strategies but may struggle with novel constructions or with proposing new intermediate lemmas that would simplify a proof.

Finally, evaluation limitations hamper progress. Benchmarks like miniF2F are valuable but necessarily limited in scope and difficulty. They may encourage overfitting to specific problem types or assistant ecosystems. Moreover, success metrics that count only fully verified proofs do not capture partial progress, like useful formal statements that still require small human edits, or high-quality proof sketches that meaningfully assist a human prover.

Conclusion

Autoformalization aims to bridge the gap between informal mathematics and machine-verifiable proofs, leveraging proof assistants, automated provers, and increasingly, learned systems. The field combines challenges from natural language understanding, logical representation, automated reasoning, and software engineering. Isabelle, Coq, and Lean provide different target environments with distinct trade-offs, and tools like Sledgehammer illustrate effective hybrids of interactive proof development and automated proving. Benchmarks such as miniF2F offer measurable tasks to evaluate progress, while the interplay between proof sketches and full proofs drives research on human-in-the-loop and fully automated pipelines. Key limitations remain: ambiguity and underspecification in informal text, library dependence, proof search scalability, reconstruction challenges, and the difficulty of creative lemma invention. Addressing these will require advances across symbolic reasoning, learned models with better semantic grounding, and ecosystem-level engineering to make formal verification more scalable and accessible.
