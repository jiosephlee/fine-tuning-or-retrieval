Chapter 2: Tabular Data — Structure, Types, and Preprocessing

Introduction

Tabular data are among the most common forms of datasets encountered in applied machine learning. A tabular dataset is a rectangular arrangement of values where rows correspond to individual examples (records, observations) and columns correspond to features (variables, attributes). Despite this apparent simplicity, tabular data present a wide variety of feature types and quality issues that must be addressed carefully before applying learning algorithms. This chapter develops a practical and conceptual foundation for working with tabular data: how to recognize feature types, how to handle missing values, how and why to scale features, how to encode categorical variables, reasons for binning or discretizing continuous variables, how to manage feature metadata, and basic ways to serialize rows as text for models that accept textual input. Throughout, emphasis is placed on intuition, common pitfalls, and choices that affect downstream model performance and interpretability.

# Feature types: numerical (continuous), ordinal, categorical, binary, timestamps, text

Understanding the type of each feature is the crucial first step in preprocessing. Feature type affects which preprocessing operations are appropriate and how a model should interpret a column. We describe the common types and the implications of each.

Numerical (continuous) features represent measurements on a numeric scale that typically can take on an ordered continuum of values and for which arithmetic operations are meaningful. Examples include temperature, age in years, salary, and sensor readings. Continuous features often assume an approximately real-valued distribution, possibly skewed or heavy-tailed. Because many models (linear models, neural networks) are sensitive to the scale and distribution of continuous features, it is common to rescale them or transform them (log, power transform) to improve model behavior and optimization.

Ordinal features are categorical variables with an intrinsic order but without meaningful arithmetic distances between categories. For example, education level may be encoded as "high school", "bachelor", "master", "PhD" — these are ordered but the gap between "bachelor" and "master" is not necessarily comparable to the gap between "master" and "PhD". Ordinal features require encodings that preserve the order. A simple mapping to integers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ is common, but that mapping implicitly assumes equal spacing, which may not be desired. Alternatives include monotonic encodings, target-guided encodings, or one-hot encodings if the order is not strong.

Categorical (nominal) features represent discrete classes without a natural order. Examples include country, color, or product category. The appropriate encodings depend on the model: tree-based models can often accept integer labels without misinterpreting them as ordered, while linear models and distance-based models require encodings that avoid spurious ordinal relationships. High-cardinality categorical variables (thousands or millions of distinct values) present special challenges both for encoding size and for risk of overfitting.

Binary features are a special case of categorical variables with only two values (e.g., yes/no, male/female, 0/1). They are usually encoded as 0/1 and often require minimal preprocessing. However, careful treatment of missing values and the semantic meaning of the two categories (is one a default value or the absence of a signal?) remains important.

Timestamps and temporal features encode points or intervals in time. A raw timestamp is a complex object — it contains cyclical components (hour of day, day of week), calendar irregularities (weekends, holidays), and may imply temporal dependencies among rows (time series). Typical preprocessing decomposes a timestamp into features that a model can use, such as year, month, day-of-week, hour-of-day, elapsed time since an epoch, or boolean flags for holidays. Caution: naive integer encoding of timestamps (e.g., Unix epoch) can mislead models into interpreting monotonic increases as linear effects; extracting features that match the expected temporal patterns is usually better.

Text features are unstructured strings of variable length: product descriptions, user comments, search queries. Text requires specialized processing to convert into numeric representations: bag-of-words counts, TF-IDF vectors, word embeddings, or representations derived from pretrained language models. Text fields can be high-dimensional and sparse, and often require tokenization, lowercasing, stopword handling, and careful handling of rare tokens and misspellings.

Recognizing and explicitly recording the intended type of each feature is essential for selecting appropriate preprocessing pipelines and for avoiding errors that lead to misleading models.

# Missing data handling: imputation strategies

Missing values are ubiquitous in real-world tabular data. They can arise from sensor failures, skipped survey questions, data corruption, or deliberate omissions. How missingness is handled has substantial impact on model performance and bias. The first step is to reason about the mechanism generating missingness, conventionally classified as missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR), and missing not at random (MNAR). MCAR means missingness is independent of observed and unobserved data; MAR means missingness may depend on observed data but not on the missing values themselves; MNAR means missingness depends on the missing values. These assumptions inform whether simple imputation will bias downstream estimates.

A basic and widely used approach is simple imputation: replacing missing values with a statistic computed from the observed values. For numerical features, common choices include the mean, median, or a constant (e.g., 0). Median imputation is robust to outliers and is often preferred when the feature distribution is skewed. Mean imputation preserves the sample mean but can be sensitive to skew and can distort variances. For categorical features, imputing the most frequent category (mode) is common, or introducing a special "missing" category to preserve the fact of missingness.

More sophisticated imputation includes model-based techniques. K-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation finds similar rows in feature space and imputes missing values from those neighbors. Iterative imputation methods (such as multivariate imputation by chained equations, MICE) fit a regression model for each feature with missing values using the other features as predictors, iteratively imputing until convergence. These methods can capture relationships among features and produce more realistic imputations than univariate statistics. However, they assume conditional relationships and can be computationally expensive.

A crucial practical technique is to encode the presence of missingness itself as a feature. Adding a binary indicator column that records whether the original value was missing allows a model to exploit missingness as a signal; missingness can be informative (MNAR) and is often predictive. For example, absence of a lab test could indicate the clinician judged it unnecessary, which may correlate with the outcome.

Imputation must be performed consistent with the experimental protocol: any statistic or model used for imputation should be fitted only on the training data and then applied to validation/test data. Fitting an imputer on the full dataset leaks information and inflates performance estimates. In cross-validation, imputation should occur inside each fold. For time series, imputation must respect temporal ordering — using future data to impute past observations invalidates the training/testing split.

When using model-based imputation, be mindful of the uncertainty introduced by imputing missing values. Simple single-imputation ignores this uncertainty and can understate variance. Multiple imputation generates several imputed datasets reflecting imputation uncertainty, fits models on each, and pools results. While more statistically principled, multiple imputation is more involved and less often used in predictive modeling pipelines where the primary goal is point predictions.

# Scaling and normalization: standardization, min–max, robust scalers

Feature scaling changes the numeric ranges of features to improve model training and performance. Many models, including gradient-based learners (neural networks, logistic regression, support vector machines) and distance-based methods (k-nearest neighbors), are sensitive to the relative scales of features. Tree-based models (decision trees, random forests, gradient-boosted trees) are less sensitive to monotonic scaling, but scaling may still be useful for regularization or interpretability.

Standardization (z-score normalization) rescales a feature $x$ to have mean $0$ and standard deviation $1$ using the training set statistics. If $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the sample mean and standard deviation computed on the training data, then the standardized feature is
$x' = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$.
Standardization centers the data and puts features on a comparable scale, which is important when features differ by orders of magnitude or when regularization penalizes coefficients independently of scale.

Min–max scaling rescales features to a fixed range, typically $[0,1]$. Given training set minimum $x_{\min}$ and maximum $x_{\max}$, the scaled value is
$x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}$.
Min–max preserves the exact shape of the original distribution but compresses it into a bounded interval. It is sensitive to outliers; a single extreme value in the training set can make most other values concentrate near the interior of the interval.

Robust scaling uses robust statistics such as the median and the interquartile range (IQR) to mitigate sensitivity to outliers. A common robust transformation is
$x' = \frac{x - \text{median}(x)}{\text{IQR}(x)}$,
where $\text{IQR}(x) = Q_{3} - Q_{1}$ is the difference between the third and first quartiles. Robust scalers help when features contain heavy-tailed distributions or extreme outliers that would otherwise distort mean and variance.

Logarithmic or power transforms are another family of transformations used to reduce skewness. For strictly positive features, the log transform
$x' = \log(x)$
compresses large values and spreads out small ones, often producing a distribution closer to symmetric. The Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson transforms generalize this idea and can be tuned to stabilize variance and improve normality.

As with imputation, all scaling and transformation parameters must be computed solely on training data and applied to validation/test sets. Combining training and test data to compute scaling parameters leaks information and biases evaluation.

# Encoding categorical variables: one-hot, ordinal, target encoding

Converting categorical features into numerical representations is necessary for most machine learning algorithms. The choice of encoding affects model complexity, interpretability, and the potential for leakage or overfitting.

One-hot encoding creates a binary indicator (0/1) for each category level. For a categorical feature with $k$ distinct values, one-hot encoding produces $k$ binary columns (or $k-1$ to avoid multicollinearity when using linear models with an intercept). One-hot is simple, preserves non-ordinal relationships, and works well with many models. Its main drawback is high dimensionality when categorical variables have many levels, which increases memory use and can degrade performance.

Ordinal encoding maps categories to integers $0,1,\dots,k-1$. This preserves a numeric representation but injects an artificial order that may not exist. For tree-based models, integer encoding can be acceptable because trees can split on particular integer values and do not assume metric distances. For linear and distance-based models, ordinal encoding can introduce spurious linear relationships unless the categorical variable truly has an ordered meaning.

Target encoding (also called mean encoding or likelihood encoding) replaces each category with a statistic computed from the target variable, typically the conditional mean of the target given the category, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[y \mid \text{category}]$. For binary classification with $y \in \{0,1\}$, the target encoding for category $c$ might be $\hat{p}_c = \frac{\sum_{i: x_i=c} y_i}{\sum_{i: x_i=c} 1}$. Target encoding can produce very compact, informative features, especially for high-cardinality variables. However, it risks target leakage: if the target mean is estimated using the same data that will be used to train the final model, categories can memorize the target and produce overly optimistic performance. To mitigate leakage, practitioners use cross-validation or out-of-fold strategies: compute category statistics using only training folds and apply them to validation folds. Regularization techniques such as smoothing the category mean towards the global mean, adding noise, or requiring a minimum count before using the category mean help reduce overfitting. Another approach is to use Bayesian estimators that combine category statistics with priors.

Frequency or count encoding replaces each category with its observed frequency or log-frequency. This retains information about how common a category is and is sometimes useful for models that can use popularity as a signal.

Embeddings learned end-to-end are another modern approach, particularly for neural networks. Each category is mapped to a low-dimensional continuous vector that is learned during model training. Embeddings can capture relationships among categories but require enough data to train and add additional hyperparameters (embedding dimension).

Always consider how new or rare categories will be handled at inference time. Strategies include mapping unseen categories to an "unknown" bucket, using global statistics, or applying hashing (feature hashing) to project categories into a fixed number of numeric features.

# Binning and discretization

Binning or discretization converts continuous variables into discrete intervals. This can be useful for capturing non-linear relationships in simpler models, for creating interpretable models, or for reducing sensitivity to extreme values.

A straightforward binning strategy is equal-width binning: divide the range of values into $k$ intervals of equal width and encode the interval membership. Equal-frequency (quantile) binning produces bins each containing approximately the same number of observations, which is helpful when the distribution is skewed. Decision-tree based methods can also be used to find discretizations that are predictive of the target: for example, using a univariate tree to split a continuous variable into intervals that best separate classes.

Binning reduces variance at the cost of introducing bias. Coarse bins reduce model variance and can be useful when data are scarce or the relationship is piecewise constant, but important information may be lost. Binning also changes how distance-based metrics and linear models interpret the variable; after binning and one-hot encoding, the model no longer assumes a linear relationship with the original numeric scale.

Monotonic binning is used frequently in credit scoring and risk modeling, where bins are chosen to ensure that the binned feature has a monotonic relationship with the target. WoE (weight-of-evidence) binning maps each bin to a statistic summarizing its association with the binary target and is used with logistic regression for interpretability.

When discretizing, always ensure bin edges are determined using only training data and that bins for validation/test points are computed consistently. For streaming or online settings, consider incremental bin boundary updates or methods that adapt to changing distributions.

# Feature typing and metadata (feature names and descriptions)

A robust preprocessing pipeline depends on accurate feature typing and metadata. Feature metadata includes the feature name, intended data type (numeric, categorical, ordinal, timestamp, text, boolean), units of measurement, allowed ranges, descriptions, and relationships to other features. Capturing and maintaining this metadata helps automate appropriate preprocessing choices and reduce errors when datasets change.

Feature names should be human-readable and, where possible, self-explanatory. Ambiguous names (like "val1", "f3") increase the risk of misinterpretation. Descriptions that explain units, missing-value codes (e.g., -9999), and semantic meanings prevent preprocessing mistakes. For example, a column called "income" might be pre-tax, monthly, or annual — the preprocessing decisions (scaling, transforming) could differ depending on units.

Metadata can also record which features are identifiers or keys (which should not be used as predictors), which are target variables or leakage sources, and which are derived features that need updating when base data change. In production systems, maintaining a schema registry or feature catalog that captures these properties enables consistent pipelines across teams and experiments. Tools for feature stores and data catalogs often provide such functionality.

Explicit typing allows automated pipelines to, for example, apply text tokenization only to text features, apply datetime decomposition only to timestamps, or enforce that binary features are encoded as 0/1. This reduces errors and aids reproducibility.

# Serialization of tabular rows to text (basic approaches and use cases)

Some modern workflows and models operate on text input, such as large pre-trained language models or sequence models that accept textual prompts. In these cases, tabular rows can be serialized into text strings that describe the record. Serialization converts structured data into human-readable or model-consumable natural language sequences. Understanding when and how to do this is useful for leveraging models that work with text or for building explanation interfaces.

A basic serialization approach is to render each feature as a key-value pair joined into a single string. For example, a row with features "age", "gender", and "income" could be serialized as "age: 45; gender: female; income: 55000". Variations include more natural phrasing ("A 45-year-old female with annual income \$55,000") or templated formats that standardize order and punctuation. Including feature names is generally important to preserve which value corresponds to which feature, especially when different rows might omit features or when order matters.

For categorical features with many possible values, using the category label rather than numeric codes preserves semantics. For missing values, a string such as "income: missing" or "income: N/A" makes the absence explicit. When serializing timestamps, convert them to readable forms or decompose them as separate textual attributes.

Another approach is to serialize into structured pseudo-JSON or CSV-like strings: '{"age":45,"gender":"female","income":55000}'. This is still text but preserves structure for parsing by models that can recognize such formats.

The choice of serialization depends on the target model. Large language models can handle free-form natural descriptions and may benefit from richer context, while models pretrained on code or JSON may perform better when given structured pseudo-JSON. Consider whether the model should infer feature types from literals; including units and explicit labels reduces ambiguity.

Use cases for serialization include zero-shot or few-shot prompting of language models for classification or regression, building human-readable explanations, or unifying heterogeneous sources into a single textual interface. However, serializing numeric data as text is typically less efficient and can be less precise than using native numeric inputs. For high-performance applications, prefer numeric pipelines; use text serialization when leveraging capabilities specific to text models (e.g., commonsense reasoning, language understanding) or when integrating with text-centric systems.

When preparing serialized text for modeling, be mindful of token limits, privacy of sensitive fields, and the potential for models to latch onto superficial textual cues rather than substantive signal. Standardize formats, escape special characters, and ensure reproducibility by documenting the serialization template.

Conclusion

Preprocessing tabular data is a crucial and often time-consuming step that shapes the success of downstream machine learning. Correctly identifying feature types, handling missingness in a principled way, scaling or transforming numeric features, encoding categorical variables appropriately, selectively discretizing continuous variables, maintaining feature metadata, and knowing when and how to serialize rows into text are all part of a careful preprocessing toolkit. Many decisions depend on the model class, the data distribution, and the predictive task; best practice is to document choices, ensure that all parameter estimation happens within training folds to avoid leakage, and to evaluate alternative preprocessing strategies using held-out data. With these foundations, you will be prepared to interpret research papers that assume or propose specific preprocessing choices and to build robust, reproducible tabular data pipelines.
