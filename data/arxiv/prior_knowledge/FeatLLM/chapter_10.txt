Chapter 10: Using LLMs for Tabular Problems — Textual Interfaces and Prior Work

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) were originally developed and evaluated on text-centric tasks: language modeling, translation, summarization, question answering, and code generation. In recent years, researchers and practitioners have tried to apply LLMs to other modalities and problem types, including structured tabular data. Tabular data — spreadsheets, relational tables, CSV files — are ubiquitous across science, business, and engineering. They present a different set of challenges from textual data: columns often represent heterogeneous types (numerical, categorical, datetime, free text), relationships between features can be complex and domain-specific, and desired outputs are often numeric predictions or classifications rather than free-form text.

One common and practical approach to bridge this modality gap is to convert tables into text so an LLM can be used as an inference or reasoning engine. This chapter surveys methods that serialize tabular data into natural language, how to design prompts that use feature names and descriptions, examples of LLM-based methods (both in-context and tuning-based), practical limitations of this interface, ways to combine LLM outputs with classical machine learning (ML) models, and empirical findings, with an emphasis on low-shot regimes where LLMs can offer the most value. The goal is to provide the intuition and concrete strategies needed to read, implement, and extend research in this area.

# Serialization strategies for rows and columns into natural language

At the core of any attempt to use LLMs on tabular data is the serialization step: transforming the structured representation of a table into a sequence of tokens that an LLM can process. The serialization strategy matters because it determines what information is available to the model, how that information is presented, and how easily the model can reason about relationships across columns and rows. Several broad families of serialization strategies have emerged; each has trade-offs in fidelity, token cost, and inductive bias.

A simple and commonly used strategy is row-wise serialization. For supervised prediction tasks where each row corresponds to a single example (and possibly a target column), a row is converted into a short natural language description such as "age: 42; income: 55k; married: yes; credit_score: 680". This compact key-value encoding preserves feature names and values, and the order can be either fixed (e.g., the table column order) or selected to emphasize important features. Row-wise serialization is intuitive and efficient when the number of columns is modest. It lets the LLM attend locally to feature names and values and implicitly treats the row as a "context" for a question like "What is the risk class?" or "Predict the target." However, for wide tables with dozens or hundreds of features, this approach can produce long inputs that exceed typical context windows or dilute attention across many tokens.

Column-wise serialization, in contrast, converts each column into a description of its values across rows. This is useful for tasks that involve understanding distributional properties or comparing features across multiple examples. For instance, a column "age" might be serialized as "age: [23, 45, 67, 34, ...]" or as a summary "age: mean 42, std 12, min 18, max 89". Column-wise serialization conveys feature-level statistics and can help the model form higher-level hypotheses about relationships (e.g., "columns with similar distributions are likely correlated with the target"). It is less natural for per-row prediction unless combined with a row indicator; one hybrid pattern is to include both column-level summaries and the specific row's values.

Another important family of strategies encodes tabular structure explicitly in sentence templates or mini-paragraphs. For example, rather than a compact key-value list, one might generate a sentence like "The applicant is a 42-year-old married person with annual income 55k and a credit score of 680." Natural-language sentences can make relationships and semantics clearer, especially when values need normalization or unit annotations (e.g., "income: 55k USD/year"). Sentence-based serialization can also incorporate contextual qualifiers and domain knowledge: "This measurement was taken at baseline; the following are the recorded features." The downside is increased token length and potentially redundant language that consumes context budget.

A more structured and model-friendly option is to serialize using JSON-like or pseudo-XML schemas: {"age": 42, "income": 55000, "married": true, "credit_score": 680}. This keeps parsing unambiguous and can be particularly useful if downstream processing needs to extract exact tokens. LLMs generally handle such formats well because their training includes code and markup. Structured serialization simplifies programmatic generation of prompts and reduces ambiguity about units and data types. Again, verbosity and context size remain constraints.

Choice of ordering and delimiter conventions also matters. When features are ordered by importance or mutual information with the target, the model can place more attention on the early tokens. Including explicit delimiters, line breaks, or labels like "FEATURES:" and "TARGET:" helps the model learn the intended structure. When few-shot examples are used in the prompt, consistent serialization across examples is critical: the model relies on pattern induction from examples.

Finally, compression and summary strategies are essential when tables are large. Techniques include feature selection to include only a subset of relevant columns, binning or discretizing continuous variables, summarizing distributions instead of listing many values, and using embeddings or pointers to represent repetitive content. Hybrid methods can push heavy, repeated data into a compressed representation (e.g., embeddings or hashes fed to a separate vector store), while the LLM receives a human-readable summary and a pointer to retrieve more details if needed.

# Leveraging feature names and descriptions as prompts

Feature names are a form of metadata that communicates semantics about each column. LLMs, trained on extensive text and code, can exploit these names and accompanying natural-language descriptions to reason about features in ways that classic ML models cannot. Leveraging feature names and descriptions transforms raw tabular values into contextualized signals that the LLM can combine with world knowledge and common-sense reasoning.

At a basic level, including the column name with its value (e.g., "age: 42", "blood_pressure: 140/90") disambiguates units and semantics. Some features are ambiguous when presented only as numbers: is "1" a binary flag, a category index, or part of a continuous scale? Explicit names remove this ambiguity. Beyond disambiguation, rich descriptions can provide causal cues and guide the LLM's inductive biases. For instance, a description like "smoker: 1 if patient currently smokes tobacco, 0 otherwise" conveys a causal relation to health outcomes that the LLM can use when predicting risk.

Feature descriptions can also provide domain-specific templates that the model can use across different datasets. Consider a financial dataset with features "net_worth", "debt_ratio", and "recent_bankruptcies". A short description such as "net_worth: customer's liquid assets in USD" or "debt_ratio: debt divided by annual income" helps the model understand scale and possible relationships. For categorical features, descriptions that list categories or example values clarify taxonomy (e.g., "employment_status: one of [employed, unemployed, self-employed, retired]").

Prompt engineering often exploits feature names by constructing a prefix that describes the dataset schema before presenting rows. A typical pattern is:

"Dataset schema: age (years), income (USD per year), status (employed/unemployed). Example: age: 42; income: 55000; status: employed. Question: What is the probability of default?"

This schema-first pattern helps the LLM form a conceptual map of the features before seeing individual instances. Descriptions that include causal or domain knowledge (e.g., "higher income reduces default risk") should be used carefully: they can improve predictions when accurate but bias the model if they contain incorrect assumptions.

A particularly powerful use of feature descriptions is to provide "hints" about which features are relevant to the target and how they interact. In few-shot settings, appending a short explanation for why a feature matters in each example — effectively doing programmatic feature engineering in natural language — can teach the LLM to focus on similar signals in new examples. For example, adding a parenthetical "credit_score (higher implies lower default risk)" next to the value can lead to more calibrated predictions.

There is also evidence that LLMs can infer relationships from names alone due to their pretraining on large corpora. Names like "age", "income", "temperature", or "gender" evoke typical statistical relationships, which the model can draw upon when data are scarce. Thus, careful design of feature names and human-readable descriptions becomes a lightweight and effective way to inject prior knowledge into tabular prediction problems for LLMs.

# Examples of LLM-based tabular methods (in-context, tuning-based)

Research on using LLMs for tabular tasks tends to fall into two broad methodological camps: in-context learning (ICL) approaches that provide examples in the prompt at inference time, and tuning-based approaches that fine-tune or otherwise adapt model weights to better handle tabular inputs. Both classes have produced practical methods and interesting trade-offs.

In-context learning methods rely on exemplars placed in the prompt. The prompt typically contains a few labeled examples, each serialized consistently, followed by a new example and a request for prediction. For instance, a binary classification few-shot prompt might include three labeled rows:

"Example 1: age: 25; income: 30k; default: no.
Example 2: age: 50; income: 20k; default: yes.
Example 3: age: 35; income: 45k; default: no.
New: age: 60; income: 18k; default:"

The LLM completes with "yes" or "no". Researchers have demonstrated that sufficiently large LLMs can perform surprisingly well in this setting, especially when the serialization and choice of exemplars are well designed. Variations include chain-of-thought style in-context prompts where exemplar answers include short explanations of why the label applies; this can improve reasoning and generalization when explanations highlight causal relationships or threshold effects.

Another in-context strategy is to use LLMs for feature transformation and automatic feature engineering. Given a row, the prompt asks the LLM to propose derived features, interaction terms, or plausible imputations for missing values. The LLM outputs these as additional textual or numeric fields, which can then be parsed and used by downstream models. This leverages the LLM's world knowledge and reasoning to produce interpretable, human-friendly features.

Tuning-based methods adapt the LLM's parameters to tabular tasks. One straightforward approach is full fine-tuning: convert rows into text, produce supervised training examples (text -> label), and update model weights via gradient descent. This can produce strong performance when substantial labeled data are available, but it is computationally expensive and risks overfitting to the textualization style. Parameter-efficient techniques, such as prompt tuning, adapter modules, or LoRA (low-rank adaptation), are popular because they require fewer trainable parameters and can be faster. With prompt tuning, a small continuous prompt vector is optimized while the base LLM is frozen; this can be seen as learning a task-specific context that the LLM will interpret. Adapter modules introduce small trainable layers into the transformer blocks, enabling adaptation to tabular patterns without modifying most parameters.

There are also hybrid tuning-inference techniques. For example, one can fine-tune the LLM to predict target labels given serialized rows where synthetic or curated natural-language explanations are also provided, encouraging the model to learn to reason about features. Alternatively, a model can be fine-tuned for a related auxiliary task, such as converting tabular rows into concise natural-language summaries, and then used in-context for prediction tasks.

Some methods aim to make LLMs more table-native. Training or fine-tuning an LLM on a mixture of natural language and tabular-structured text (e.g., CSVs, HTML tables, or JSON) can teach the model to better parse columnar layouts. There are also retrieval-augmented designs where the LLM fetches similar rows from a datastore based on an embedding of the current row and uses these retrieved neighbors as in-context exemplars to improve predictions.

Across these examples, successful designs often blend prompt engineering, exemplar selection, and modest adaptation. Researchers report that in many tabular tasks, tuning-based approaches typically outperform purely in-context methods when abundant labeled data exist, while in-context methods shine in low-label or zero-shot settings.

# Practical limitations: per-sample inference cost and prompt size

Transforming tabular prediction into natural-language prompts exposes two major practical limitations: per-sample inference cost and strict constraints on prompt size. These limitations affect latency, throughput, cost, and, ultimately, feasibility for large-scale or real-time systems.

Per-sample inference cost is elevated because each table row typically requires a separate forward pass of the LLM. Unlike classical ML models, which can cheaply batch matrix operations across many rows, LLM inference — especially using large models with billions of parameters — is computationally heavy and often charged per token in hosted APIs. If every row in a dataset of millions of customers requires an LLM call, this can incur prohibitive monetary and energy costs. Even when inference can be batched on-premises, GPU memory and compute constraints limit throughput relative to specialized tabular models designed for high-volume predictions.

Prompt size interacts with this cost in two ways. First, the token length of the serialization plus any few-shot exemplars must be below the model's context window. Many LLMs today have context lengths in the range of 4k to 32k tokens, and while 32k is large, it quickly becomes limiting when rows are wide or when one desires many exemplars for robust in-context learning. Second, the prompt size directly affects token-based pricing and latency: longer prompts take longer to process and cost more when using APIs.

These constraints force practitioners to make trade-offs. To reduce prompt size, one can restrict the number of included features, drop non-informative columns, discretize continuous variables, or compress the row into a summary representation. But each simplification risks discarding valuable information. When using in-context exemplars, one must balance the number of examples (which often improves performance) against the available context budget.

Another limitation is non-amortized computation for complex reasoning. In classical ML, a trained model internalizes computations and applies them cheaply at inference time. In contrast, in many LLM-driven setups, complex reasoning steps (e.g., chain-of-thought) are performed at inference time for each sample, incurring repeated computation. Some work circumvents this by fine-tuning the model to internalize reasoning or by distilling chain-of-thought reasoning into standard responses, but these require additional training.

Finally, there are reliability and interpretability limitations. LLM outputs can be stochastic and sometimes hallucinate plausible but incorrect relationships. When using text serialized as free-form descriptions, the model may be sensitive to phrasing nuances or to irrelevant contextual cues. For high-stakes applications, one must guard against such failures, which again may necessitate combining LLMs with deterministic classical models or using the LLM to augment rather than replace conventional pipelines.

# Combining LLM outputs with classical ML models

Given the practical and reliability constraints of using LLMs as end-to-end tabular predictors, a pragmatic and often effective pattern is to combine LLM outputs with classical ML models. This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both: LLMs for reasoning, feature generation, and leveraging world knowledge; classical models for efficient, high-throughput prediction, calibration, and quantifiable uncertainty.

One straightforward combination is to use LLMs as feature engineers. The LLM is prompted per row to generate derived features, explanations, or risk scores. For example, given a customer record, the LLM might output "high_risk: likely due to low income and prior defaults" or produce numeric transformations like "debt_to_income_ratio: 0.6". These outputs are parsed and appended to the original feature vector, then fed into a classical model such as gradient-boosted trees or a neural network. Because the LLM distills semantic relationships and world knowledge into features, the downstream model can learn more predictive mappings while retaining rapid inference.

Another integration is ensembling: the LLM's predicted probability or label is combined with a classical model's output using stacking or weighted averaging. In stacking, the LLM and classical model predictions become inputs to a meta-learner trained to optimize final performance. This is particularly useful when LLMs capture patterns missed by purely statistical models (such as insights from textual descriptions) while classical models provide stable, well-calibrated predictions. Ensembling can also improve robustness; if the LLM is uncertain or returns conflicting reasoning, the classical model can act as a stabilizer.

LLMs can also assist in data cleaning, imputation, and dealing with missing or messy records. An LLM can propose likely imputations based on context and feature descriptions, and these imputations can be verified or scored before being used in a conventional model. Since classical models are sensitive to feature distributions, using the LLM for intelligent imputation can improve downstream performance without invoking the LLM at prediction time.

Distillation is another powerful pattern. After using an LLM (possibly with chain-of-thought) to generate high-quality predictions or pseudo-labels on a large unlabeled dataset, one can train a compact classical model to mimic the LLM outputs. The distilled model benefits from the LLM's reasoning without incurring heavy inference cost. This approach mirrors model distillation used in language and vision: expensive teacher models teach cheap student models.

Finally, the LLM can be used to generate explanations or rationales for decisions made by classical models. For instance, a gradient-boosted tree's output can be passed to an LLM along with the input features and an instruction like "Explain why the model predicts default for this customer." The LLM generates human-readable narratives that help debugging, model governance, and end-user communication. Care must be taken because the LLM explanation is not guaranteed to reflect the true model internals; it is instead a plausible post-hoc narrative unless constrained by faithful model interpretability methods.

In all these hybrid scenarios, an important design consideration is the amortization of LLM usage. Use the LLM where its unique capabilities matter (feature creation, semantic imputations, label augmentation), and rely on classical models for mass deployment. This yields a cost-effective and operationally practical system.

# Case studies and observed benefits in low-shot regimes

LLM-based tabular methods show their greatest promise in low-shot or zero-shot regimes, where labeled data are scarce and domain knowledge is valuable. Several case studies and experimental results in the literature and industry bear this out, revealing patterns that inform best practices.

One class of case studies involves medical datasets where labeling is expensive and domain expertise is required. For example, when only a handful of annotated patient records exist for predicting a rare outcome, LLMs prompted with feature names and brief clinical descriptions can leverage medical knowledge to provide reasonable predictions or to prioritize cases for clinician review. Empirical results show that few-shot prompts augmented with succinct feature descriptions and exemplar explanations outperform baseline classical models trained on the same small dataset. The LLM's ability to draw on general medical knowledge compensates for limited local data.

Another case study domain is financial services with small or proprietary datasets. When an institution has only a limited number of labeled fraud cases, LLMs can use textual descriptions of transaction features and external knowledge about fraud patterns to boost detection. Few-shot in-context patterns and LLM-generated engineered features often raise recall on rare classes compared to purely data-driven models trained from scratch.

Academic studies on standard tabular benchmarks also reveal consistent trends. When datasets are large (thousands to millions of labeled rows), classical models like XGBoost or TabNet typically outperform vanilla LLM prompting. However, when the labeled set is small (tens to a few hundred examples), LLM in-context methods or small tuning of LLMs can match or exceed classical baselines. Effect sizes depend on feature richness: tasks that include many textual columns or semantically rich feature names benefit most from LLMs, while tasks with purely numeric, high-dimensional feature spaces often favor classical models.

Another observed benefit in low-shot settings relates to model calibration and uncertainty. In some experiments, LLMs supplying rationales or probability estimates are better at flagging uncertain cases and producing explanations that human experts can use to triage further labeling or review. This is valuable in active learning setups: the LLM can help select informative examples for labeling, accelerating dataset construction.

There are also case studies showing the benefit of retrieval-augmented exemplars. For instance, a pipeline that retrieves the most similar historical rows (by vector similarity) and presents them as in-context exemplars to the LLM often outperforms random exemplar selection. This combines classical nearest-neighbor inductive biases with the LLM’s pattern-matching capabilities and is especially helpful when labeled data are scarce but an unlabeled or semantically rich datastore exists.

It is important to emphasize limitations observed in these studies. Gains from LLMs are typically largest when the data contain interpretable or semantically meaningful fields. For purely numeric, engineered feature sets with complex nonlinear interactions, LLMs tend to struggle compared to models designed to exploit such structure. Furthermore, the benefits can be fragile to prompt phrasing, exemplar choice, and serialization format, so replication and careful validation are essential.

Concluding remarks

Using LLMs for tabular problems by converting tables to text is a flexible, conceptually simple paradigm that unlocks the LLM’s strengths in leveraging semantic metadata, world knowledge, and reasoning. Serialization choices, careful use of feature names and descriptions, and an understanding of in-context versus tuning-based methods are critical for success. Practical limitations — notably per-sample computational cost and context window constraints — motivate hybrid systems where LLMs augment classical models rather than replace them, and distillation or feature-generation strategies can amortize costs.

Empirically, LLMs are most useful in low-shot scenarios, for datasets rich in semantics, and for generating interpretable features or explanations. As the field progresses, improvements in model efficiency, larger context windows, and multimodal pretraining that includes structured data may shift the trade-offs, but the core principles covered here — how to serialize, how to use metadata, how to integrate with classical models, and where LLMs add the most value — will remain central for practitioners and researchers working at the intersection of large language models and tabular data.
