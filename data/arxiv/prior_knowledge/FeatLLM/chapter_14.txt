Introduction

Machine learning models exploit statistical regularities in data to make predictions. In many settings, particularly when data are limited or collected under narrow conditions, some of those regularities are not causally related to the target of interest. When a model relies on such noncausal regularities, we say it has learned spurious correlations or fragile predictors. Spurious correlations can lead to excellent performance on held-out samples drawn from the same distribution, yet poor generalization when deployment conditions shift even slightly. This chapter explains what spurious correlations are, why they are especially problematic in small-sample regimes, how prior knowledge and model priors influence which patterns a model learns, and how pretraining can introduce systematic biases. We then cover methods to test robustness, strategies to mitigate fragile predictors, and approaches to interpret model decisions so that harmful biases can be detected and addressed.

# Definition and examples of spurious correlations

A spurious correlation is a statistical association between two variables that does not reflect a direct causal relationship. More precisely, a model-dependent relationship between input features $X$ and label $Y$ is spurious if $X$ is predictive of $Y$ in the training (or observed) distribution but the predictive relationship would not hold under meaningful changes to the data-generating process. There are several ways associations can be noncausal: confounding, selection bias, common causes, and purely coincidental patterns.

Consider a classic and intuitive example: suppose we train an image classifier to distinguish wolves from huskies. If almost every wolf image in the training set contains snow while most husky images are photographed on grass, the presence of snow can become a strong predictor of the "wolf" label. The model can then rely on an image cue (snow) that is correlated with the label in the dataset but not causally related to being a wolf. When the model is deployed on images of huskies in snow or wolves in snowless settings, performance collapses.

Another example comes from medical data: a dataset containing chest X-rays labeled for pneumonia might reflect that sicker patients were more often scanned in certain specialized hospitals, and those hospital identifiers (encoded in metadata or visible markers on images) may correlate with disease. A model may learn to use hospital-specific features rather than patient physiology, producing misleading predictions that fail when applied to new hospitals.

Spurious correlations can also be entirely statistical coincidences in small datasets. For instance, in a dataset of fruit images with few examples, if the training photos of apples happen to have a particular background color, that background color may be used to classify apples despite no causal link between color and fruit type.

Formally, if we denote the data-generating distribution by $P(X,Y)$, a model learns a spurious correlation if it relies on feature subset $S \subseteq X$ such that $S$ is predictive of $Y$ under $P$ but the conditional distribution $P(Y \mid do(S))$ does not correspond to the predictive association (where $do(\cdot)$ denotes intervention in the causal sense). In simpler terms, the predictive power of $S$ is not robust to interventions or distribution shifts that break the original correlation.

# Effects of small sample sizes on learning spurious patterns

Small sample sizes exacerbate the risk of spurious correlations for several interrelated reasons: variance in estimates, multiplicity of possible patterns, and overfitting capacity relative to data.

First, with few samples the empirical estimates of conditional probabilities and correlations are noisy. Suppose we estimate the conditional probability $\hat{P}(Y=1 \mid X=x)$ from $n$ examples. The sampling error scales roughly with $1/\sqrt{n}$. Consequently, random fluctuations can create apparent patterns that are not present in the population distribution. A learning algorithm trying to minimize empirical loss may latch onto these fluctuations because they appear predictive on the training set.

Second, when the feature space is high-dimensional relative to sample size, there are many possible functions that fit the training labels perfectly or nearly so. This multiplicity means the learning algorithm needs an inductive bias to select among many predictors. Without strong regularizing assumptions, the algorithm tends to find complex rules that exploit idiosyncrasies of the training data. In other words, the hypothesis class capacity interacts with small $n$ to create overfitting. The familiar bias–variance decomposition helps illuminate this: the expected generalization error decomposes into bias, variance, and irreducible error. Small $n$ increases variance, making the learned predictor sensitive to spurious patterns that happen to exist in the sample.

Third, sample scarcity often forces practitioners to pool heterogeneous data sources without adequately modeling differences. If some subgroups are underrepresented, the model may rely on features that are predictive within the dominant subgroup but not representative of the full population. This leads to fragile predictors that perform poorly on underrepresented or shifted groups.

A concrete illustrative calculation: consider $p$ binary features $X_1,\dots,X_p$ and an output $Y$ independent of all $X_j$ in truth. With $n$ samples, the chance of observing any of the $p$ features showing a spurious correlation of magnitude at least $\epsilon$ with $Y$ increases with $p$ roughly according to multiple testing effects. If we treat each feature's sample correlation as an independent statistic, the expected number of features with correlation exceeding $\epsilon$ by chance is about $p \cdot \Pr(|\hat{\rho}|>\epsilon)$. For large $p$ and small $n$, the probability of at least one strong spurious correlation can be substantial, misleading a learning algorithm that does not control for multiplicity.

Overall, small sample regimes require careful attention to model complexity, validation strategies, and domain knowledge to avoid learning fragile rules that exploit coincidences.

# Role of prior knowledge and model priors in shaping predictions

A model does not learn from data alone; it combines data with inductive biases that guide which hypotheses are preferred. In Bayesian terms, a prior distribution over model parameters or functions captures this bias. In frequentist practice, architecture choices, optimization algorithms, regularizers, and hyperparameter settings play the role of implicit priors. These priors influence whether a model selects simple, robust predictors or complex, dataset-specific rules.

Consider linear regression with $L_2$ regularization (ridge regression). Training minimizes the regularized empirical risk
\[
\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)) + \lambda \|\theta\|_2^2,
\]
where $\lambda$ controls the strength of the prior preference for small-norm parameters. Under a Bayesian interpretation with Gaussian prior $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, the $L_2$ penalty represents the prior belief favoring small weights. This bias tends to prefer simpler, more stable predictors over complex ones that may exploit spurious patterns. In contrast, absence of regularization (or weak priors) allows solutions with large norm that fit noise.

More broadly, model architecture imposes strong inductive biases. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) encode translation equivariance and locality, so they naturally focus on local visual patterns; sequence models like recurrent networks and transformers encode different invariances and position sensitivity. These architectural priors determine which features are easier for the model to learn. For instance, a CNN is better predisposed to rely on local texture if textures consistently correlate with labels in the data. Pretraining introduces another prior: parameters initialized from pretraining carry information about the pretraining distribution, making certain features easier to learn during fine-tuning.

Implicit priors also arise from optimization. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with particular learning rates and batch sizes favors flat minima in the loss landscape, which are often associated with better generalization and less sensitivity to specific data points. Thus, even with identical model classes and data, different optimization hyperparameters can lead to different learned features and different susceptibility to spurious correlations.

Importantly, priors can be leveraged intentionally. Domain knowledge can be encoded as architectural choices (e.g., equivariance to certain transformations), as constraints (e.g., monotonicity constraints), as data augmentation, or as explicit Bayesian priors. The right priors can steer learning toward causal or invariant predictors and away from brittle features that only exist in the specific dataset.

# Bias and fairness issues arising from pretraining corpora

Modern models are often pretrained on large corpora before being fine-tuned for downstream tasks. Pretraining imparts useful general-purpose representations but also imports statistical properties — including societal biases — of the pretraining data. These latent biases can manifest as unfair or harmful behaviors in downstream applications.

Large language models (LLMs) trained on web text, forums, and books learn associations that reflect historical and social prejudices present in their corpora. For example, if texts disproportionately associate certain occupations with a particular gender or ethnic group, the model's embeddings and next-token predictors encode those associations. When fine-tuned for tasks like resume screening, question answering, or translation, the model can reproduce or amplify such biases. The learned internal representations are not neutral: they are shaped by the distribution of words, topics, and contexts seen during pretraining.

Beyond social bias, pretraining can create performance disparities due to domain mismatch. If a pretrained visual model has seen mainly high-resolution western-centric imagery, it may underperform on images from other cultures, imaging devices, or lighting conditions. The result is unequal accuracy across demographic subgroups or contexts, leading to unfair outcomes.

Formally, pretraining defines an implicit prior over parameter configurations. Let $p_{\text{pre}}(X,Y)$ be the pretraining distribution and $p_{\text{down}}(X,Y)$ the downstream distribution. Pretraining shapes a parameter initialization $\theta_0$ (or a posterior over parameters) that is more adapted to $p_{\text{pre}}$ than to $p_{\text{down}}$. Fine-tuning with limited downstream data will favor parameters close to $\theta_0$, potentially preserving biases from $p_{\text{pre}}$. If $p_{\text{pre}}$ contained systematic associations that are undesirable or unfair, those associations can persist.

Because pretraining data are massive and often assembled with minimal curation, it is difficult to guarantee that biases are absent. This raises ethical and practical concerns: the larger the model and pretraining corpus, the more likely the model represents subtle statistical relationships that reproduce structural inequalities, stereotypes, or harmful content. Detection is challenging because biases can be latent and manifest only in specific contexts or prompts.

Addressing bias from pretraining requires multiple strategies: auditing pretraining datasets where possible, carefully choosing pretraining objectives, using debiasing goals during fine-tuning, applying counterfactual data augmentation, and using fairness-aware constraints. However, many practical deployments must balance these interventions against utility and the impracticality of fully vetting massive corpora.

# Robustness testing: adding irrelevant features and stress tests

Robustness testing evaluates whether a model's predictions rely on meaningful, causal features or on fragile, noncausal cues. A central technique is to perform controlled interventions that should not change the true label and observe whether the model's output changes. If it does, this is a sign the model is brittle or using spurious signals.

One simple and informative test is to add irrelevant features to inputs and check whether predictions change. For image models, this could be adding controlled backgrounds, overlays, or random noise; for text models, inserting neutral tokens or irrelevant phrases. If adding a feature that does not affect ground truth causes the model to change its prediction systematically, that feature is likely contributing undesirably. This method is related to the concept of *invariance testing*: test whether the mapping $X \mapsto Y$ learned by the model is invariant under transformations that preserve the semantics of the task.

Stress tests should be designed to mimic plausible distribution shifts at deployment. Common stress-tests include:

- Corruptions and perturbations: Additive noise, blur, occlusion, color changes, compression artifacts for vision; paraphrases, typos, code-switching for language.
- Background or context swaps: For images, replace backgrounds; for text, change preceding conversations or topics.
- Subpopulation shifts: Evaluate separately on demographic or contextual subgroups and on rarer cases to see if performance degrades disproportionately.
- Counterfactual inputs: Create minimally edited versions of inputs where only a protected attribute or suspected spurious cue is changed; test whether the model changes its output inappropriately.

Quantitatively, robustness can be measured by differences in expected loss between original and perturbed distributions. If $P_{\text{orig}}$ is the original data distribution and $P_{\text{pert}}$ is the perturbed distribution, a robustness gap can be defined as
\[
\Delta = \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{pert}}}[\ell(f(X),Y)] - \mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{orig}}}[\ell(f(X),Y)].
\]
Large $\Delta$ indicates poor robustness.

Another powerful stress-testing paradigm is subgroup and fairness evaluation. By partitioning data into slices (e.g., by age, gender, device type), one can compute worst-case performance across slices or disparity metrics. If the worst-case slice performs much worse than the average, this indicates reliance on dataset-specific cues.

Adversarial testing, where perturbations are optimized to change predictions, reveals sensitivity to small perturbations; while often concerned with security, adversarial examples also expose fragile reliance on non-robust features.

Finally, randomized ablation studies—systematically removing or masking subsets of features and measuring the effect on performance—help identify which features or modalities the model depends on. Together, these robustness tests provide evidence about whether a learned predictor is trustworthy under plausible variations.

# Mitigation strategies: regularization, feature validation, causal reasoning

Mitigating spurious correlations and fragile predictors requires a combination of techniques that reduce the model's ability to learn non-generalizable cues and encourage learning of invariant, causal relationships.

Regularization is a first line of defense. Explicit regularizers (e.g., $L_1$, $L_2$, dropout) limit model complexity, reducing the chance of fitting noise. Regularization can be interpreted as imposing a prior that prefers simpler models, which are more likely to capture broad, stable patterns rather than dataset idiosyncrasies. Early stopping during training also controls complexity by preventing the model from fitting very fine-grained patterns present in the training set.

Feature validation and careful dataset curation are crucial. Before training, analysts should inspect features for potential proxies of the label or for confounders. Domain knowledge can identify features that are ethically sensitive or likely to vary with deployment (e.g., camera models, timestamps). Removing or masking features that are known to be spurious proxies can force the model to rely on more relevant information. However, blind feature deletion can be risky if removed features contain genuine causal signal; thus, validation is important.

Causal reasoning offers principled strategies for identifying and favoring invariant predictors. The central idea is to prefer predictors that are stable across different environments or interventions. Methods in this vein include invariant risk minimization (IRM), causal representation learning, and domain adaptation techniques. IRM explicitly optimizes for representations $\Phi(X)$ such that the optimal classifier on top of $\Phi$ is the same across multiple training environments. Formally, given environments $e \in \mathcal{E}_{\text{train}}$, IRM seeks $\Phi$ and classifier $w$ minimizing
\[
\sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_{\text{train}}} R_e(w \circ \Phi) \quad\text{subject to}\quad w \in \arg\min_{\tilde w} R_e(\tilde w \circ \Phi)\ \text{for all}\ e,
\]
where $R_e$ is the risk on environment $e$. The constraint encourages learning features that have the same optimal decision rule across environments, thereby favoring causal features that are invariant under environmental changes.

Another causal approach is to collect or construct multiple environments or interventions that break different spurious correlations. By training a model that performs well across these environments, one can reduce dependence on any single spurious cue. Techniques include targeted data augmentation, stratified sampling, and collecting counterfactual examples. Where interventions are infeasible, instrumental variable techniques or proxy-variable methods from econometrics can help identify causal effects.

Reweighting and importance sampling can counteract sample selection biases by upweighting rare or underrepresented cases so that the model learns to perform well across the desired population. Adversarial training can be used to discourage representations from encoding protected attributes: an adversary tries to predict the protected attribute from the representation while the encoder attempts to prevent this, encouraging attribute-invariant representations.

Finally, human-in-the-loop validation, where domain experts review model behavior on carefully chosen cases, is often necessary. Automated approaches may miss nuanced ways in which models exhibit harmful behavior; expert audits and red-teaming exercises can expose real-world failure modes.

# Interpreting model decisions to detect harmful biases

Interpretability methods help make a model's decision process more transparent and can reveal when it relies on spurious or harmful features. There are many interpretability tools, each with trade-offs in fidelity and comprehensibility.

Feature attribution methods assign importance scores to input features for a given prediction. For tabular models, methods like coefficients in linear models or feature importances in tree ensembles give direct clues about feature influence. For complex models, techniques like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) approximate local feature contributions. SHAP frames attribution through a game-theoretic perspective (Shapley values), giving theoretically grounded attributions, whereas LIME fits a simple surrogate model around a point to explain local behavior. In images, saliency maps, Grad-CAM, and integrated gradients highlight pixels or regions that most influence the model's output.

Interpretable models also enable counterfactual analysis: generate minimal changes to inputs that flip the model's prediction. If a small change in a protected attribute (or a proxy) causes large prediction changes, this suggests undue reliance on that attribute. Counterfactual explanations can be formalized: given input $x$ classified as $y$, find $x'$ close to $x$ such that $f(x') \ne y$. The nature of $x'$ reveals sensitive decision boundaries.

Partial dependence plots (PDPs) and accumulated local effects (ALE) show how changing one feature (holding others fixed or marginalizing) affects the predicted outcome on average. These visual tools help detect nonlinear dependencies and interactions that might indicate spurious relationships.

Caveats are important: interpretability tools can be misleading if misapplied. Local explanations (like LIME) may not reflect the global behavior of the model. Saliency maps can be noisy and depend on preprocessing. Explanations should be used in conjunction with robustness tests and domain knowledge rather than as sole evidence.

Finally, auditing for fairness requires systematic subgroup analysis. Compute performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, calibration) across demographic slices and look for disparities. Use causal or counterfactual fairness definitions where appropriate: for instance, check whether the decision would change under a counterfactual world in which a protected attribute were different but all else remained equal. While perfect fairness definitions are context-dependent and often impossible to satisfy simultaneously, interpretation and auditing reveal concrete biases that can be mitigated.

Conclusion

Spurious correlations, fragile predictors, and biases arising from data and priors are central challenges in deploying reliable and fair machine learning systems. Small datasets and high-dimensional inputs amplify the risk of learning noncausal associations. Priors—both explicit and implicit, including those induced by pretraining—shape which patterns a model can and will use. Robustness testing, including stress tests and adding irrelevant features, helps detect brittle behavior. A toolbox combining regularization, careful feature validation, causal reasoning, domain-informed priors, and interpretability is necessary to mitigate these problems. Ultimately, there is no single silver bullet: robust, fair models require thoughtful data collection, rigorous evaluation across environments and subgroups, and ongoing monitoring and human oversight.
