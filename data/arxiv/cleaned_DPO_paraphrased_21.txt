\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and exhibit some reasoning capabilities, but steering their outputs with precision is challenging, given their purely unsupervised training paradigm. Approaches for improving controllability typically involve collecting human annotations that rate the relative quality of generated text and subsequently fine-tuning the LM to align with these human preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). However, RLHF is often intricate and can be unstable, as it first constructs a reward model to capture human judgments and then applies reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM for higher estimated reward, all while attempting to remain close to the original parameters. In this work, we propose a novel formulation for the reward model within RLHF that enables direct extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, thereby allowing the standard RLHF optimization problem to be addressed with a straightforward classification loss. We refer to this method as \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), which is robust, efficient, and computationally inexpensive, eliminating the necessity to sample from the LM during fine-tuning or perform extensive hyperparameter searches. Empirical results demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to reflect human preferences as well as, or better than, prior methods. Specifically, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in sentiment control tasks and achieves comparable or superior results in summarization and single-turn dialogue, while remaining significantly easier to train and implement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora exhibit remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Yet, these models learn from data produced by humans who possess diverse intentions, backgrounds, and expertise. Not all of these human qualities are desirable for an AI to replicate; for example, we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, but when producing code, we would prefer it to emulate the rare instances of exceptional programming found in its training data. Likewise, while it is beneficial for a language model to \textit{identify} a widespread misconception held by half the population, we certainly do not want it to reproduce this false belief half of the time when asked about it. Essentially, it is vital to select the model's \emph{preferred behavior and outputs} from its vast range of \textit{knowledge and skills} to ensure the development of AI systems that are reliable, controllable, and high-performing \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although prevailing approaches often guide LMs to align with human values using reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective adopted in current approaches can be achieved precisely through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which significantly streamlines the preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches encourage target behaviors in language models by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect what people consider safe and useful conduct. This preference learning phase follows an initial period of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the most direct method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human demonstration data, the most effective techniques have proven to be reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on human preference data, which is then used to guide RL optimization of the language model so that it generates responses with high predicted reward, while constraining deviations from the pretrained model. Despite RLHF yielding models with remarkable dialogue and programming capabilities, the RLHF process is notably more intricate than supervised learning, as it requires training multiple language models, continuously sampling from the model's policy throughout training, and results in substantial computational demands.

In this work, we present a method for optimizing a language model to follow human preferences directly, without requiring explicit reward modeling or the use of reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as traditional RLHF approaches—maximizing reward under a KL-divergence regularization—but is both easy to implement and efficient to train. At a high level, DPO updates the model by boosting the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over less preferred ones, while utilizing a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting to avoid the model collapse observed with a basic probability ratio objective. As with prior methods, DPO is grounded in a theoretical preference framework (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates how accurately a reward function reflects observed preference judgments. Yet, instead of employing the preference model to generate a loss for training a reward function and subsequently optimizing a policy based on that learned reward, DPO reparameterizes the preference loss so that it directly depends on the policy. Using a dataset composed of human preference annotations on model outputs, DPO is able to optimize the policy via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy relative to an implicit reward tailored to the observed preferences.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that enables training language models from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that DPO performs on par with established approaches—including PPO-based RLHF—for preference-driven tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models at larger scales have demonstrated the ability to solve certain tasks in a zero-shot setting \citep{radford2019language} or with a limited number of examples provided as prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user expectations can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as ‘instruction-tuning’, allows LLMs to handle instructions beyond those seen during fine-tuning and generally makes them more practical to use \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has been highly successful, gathering \textit{comparative} human ratings of output quality is often less demanding than producing expert-level demonstrations. Consequently, more recent work has leveraged datasets built from human preferences to further refine LLMs, leading to gains in tasks like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these approaches involve first training a neural network-based reward model to align with the preference data using models such as Bradley-Terry \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward signal via reinforcement learning, commonly using REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related techniques \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely connected line of research employs LLMs that have been instruction-tuned with human feedback to generate further synthetic preference data targeting specific criteria such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on lightweight human supervision via rubrics that guide the LLM’s annotations. These advances mark a convergence between two areas: one centered on applying reinforcement learning to language model training for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another focused on methods for learning from human preference data \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Although using relative human preferences is promising, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a substantial practical hurdle; this work introduces a theoretically sound method to optimize for relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been investigated in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple strategies introduced. In contextual bandit scenarios, where preferences or rankings over actions replace traditional rewards, the problem is referred to as contextual dueling bandits (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the standard notion of optimality with the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, which is a policy that achieves an expected win rate of at least 50\% when compared to \textit{any} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, feedback on preferences is obtained in an online manner, whereas human preference learning typically involves training from a static, offline set of action pairs labeled with preferences \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) involves learning from binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} scoring function in place of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL methods have been developed, some of which are capable of leveraging off-policy preference datasets, but most approaches entail an initial phase of inferring the latent scoring function (i.e., constructing a reward model) before optimizing policies with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-stage policy learning method that directly tunes the policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning}, as well as in subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This pipeline typically involves three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and (3) optimizing the model using reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by taking a pre-trained language model and further training it with supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to specific downstream applications (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During this phase, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates response pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. Human annotators are then asked to indicate their preferred response, represented as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored answer and $y_l$ the less preferred among $(y_1, y_2)$. It is postulated that these preferences are driven by an unknown underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly accessible. Several statistical methods can be used to capture these preferences; the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model is a widely-used option, though more general approaches like Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable when multiple ranked responses are available. According to the BT model, the human preference probability $p^*$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed set of comparison data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, one can define a parametric reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters using maximum likelihood estimation. Viewed as a binary classification task, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ refers to the sigmoid function. In large language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the weights of the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with a single linear layer appended atop the final transformer block to yield a scalar reward score \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward estimates, it is common practice to normalize the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the previously trained reward model is employed to deliver feedback to the language model. In line with earlier research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization is described as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy diverges from the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial SFT model $\pisft$.
In actual implementation, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized using $\pisft$ as well. The KL-regularization term plays a crucial role, ensuring the model does not stray significantly from the data distribution where the reward model remains reliable, in addition to preserving diverse outputs and avoiding collapse onto a few high-scoring answers. Since language generation produces discrete tokens, the above objective is non-differentiable and is generally optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms. Conventionally \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, the reward is formulated as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and optimization is carried out with PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties faced when using reinforcement learning algorithms on large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that relies directly on preferences. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques, which first learn a reward function and then maximize it with RL, our method employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows us to obtain the optimal policy analytically, bypassing the need for a reinforcement learning training loop.

As we will explain in detail, the central idea is to use an analytical correspondence between reward functions and their optimal policies, which permits us to translate a loss over reward functions into a loss over policies. This change-of-variables technique eliminates the need to explicitly train a separate reward model, while still enabling optimization under established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry model. Ultimately, the policy network serves as both the language model and the implicit reward model.

\textbf{DPO Objective Derivation.} We begin with the standard RL objective from earlier work, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which is defined using a general reward function $r$. As shown in previous studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the optimal solution to the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
with the normalization constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. The detailed steps are provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even if we substitute the true reward $r^*$ with its MLE estimator $r_\phi$, evaluating $Z(x)$ is computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits the practical use of this form. Nevertheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be rearranged to represent the reward function as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we arrive at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
We can use this change of variables for the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model relies only on reward differences between two responses, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reparameterized $r^*(x, y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference formulation Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition term cancels out, yielding a human preference probability that depends solely on $\pi^*$ and $\piref$. This means the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$, under the Bradley-Terry framework, follows the preference model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The full derivation is included in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry approach, similar results extend to the broader Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preferences now expressed through the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for the policy parameterized by $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the approach taken in reward modeling (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this formulation, we implicitly learn a reward through a different parameterization, where the resulting optimal policy is directly given by $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because our framework aligns with fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it possesses desirable theoretical guarantees, like consistency under certain assumptions regarding the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical analysis of DPO and its connections to related methods is presented in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, examining the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is instructive. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{increased emphasis when reward assessment is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{encourage $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{discourage $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
with $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$, representing the reward implicitly given by the language model $\pi_\theta$ in comparison to the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to boost the probability of selected completions $y_w$ while reducing that of the less favored $y_l$. A crucial aspect is that each training instance is weighted by the degree to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overvalues the dispreferred completion, modulated by $\beta$—in other words, by how strongly the implicit reward model misranks the completions while taking into account the KL regularization. Our empirical results highlight that this weighting is essential, as a simple variant that omits this coefficient can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, obtain human preference labels, and form the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and a specified $\beta$.  
In reality, leveraging existing public preference datasets is preferable to creating new samples and collecting additional human annotations. Since these datasets are often generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever it is provided. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead fit $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, specifically, by setting ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This initialization strategy reduces the distribution mismatch between the unknown ground-truth reference and the $\piref$ employed by DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameter choices are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, supply theoretical justification, and connect the strengths of DPO to limitations found in actor-critic techniques utilized in RLHF (for instance, PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO circumvents the need for both explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning by utilizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in the same way as optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, but with a change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical underpinnings for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the space of reward models that can be learned, and show that the optimal policy can still be exactly recovered. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing all reward functions into equivalence classes. We can present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will result in the same optimal policy within the constrained RL framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are straightforward and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma reflects a well-known ambiguity present in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. This ambiguity, or under-specification, requires us to impose additional identifiability restrictions to guarantee any properties of the MLE solutions in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions from a particular equivalence class produce identical optimal policies; therefore, our main goal reduces to recovering any representative reward function from the optimal class. The following Theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild conditions, every reward equivalence class compatible with Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed by the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines its associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as characterized in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that there exists a member of the equivalence class of $r$ that can be written using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes $r(x, y)$ by subtracting the log-partition function (involving $\pi_r$). Since this normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, $f$ yields a representative of the equivalence class in the required format, confirming that our reparameterization does not reduce the expressive power of the reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as characterizing exactly which reward function from each equivalence class is chosen by the DPO reparameterization—namely, the one fulfilling:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, via Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
that is, $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (values are non-negative and sum to 1).
Moreover, using Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we notice that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the normalization constant (partition function) for the optimal policy corresponding to $r(x, y)$.
The central insight behind the DPO algorithm is that, by enforcing specific constraints on the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) class of preference models, we maintain the expressive power of the reward function family, while simultaneously ensuring that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} has a closed-form, tractable solution for any input $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}
Our framework also enables us to analyze sources of instability in commonly used actor-critic methods for RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We can relate this to the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} when considering the constrained RL objective described in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we have a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and wish to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \| \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} based on the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Manipulating this expression, the optimization problem can be reformulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to the objective optimized in earlier research \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} for the DPO-equivalent reward structure associated with $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function of the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal solution, omitting it can result in high-variance policy gradients, leading to unstable training. To mitigate this, one can introduce a learned value function to estimate the normalization, though this approach is challenging to optimize in practice. An alternative solution, used in previous work, involves normalizing rewards via a human completion baseline, which effectively provides a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization generates a reward function that eliminates the need for any baseline.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how well DPO can train policies based solely on preference data. We begin by investigating, in a controlled text generation environment, how effectively DPO balances reward maximization with KL-divergence minimization relative to widely used preference-based algorithms such as PPO. We then test DPO on more complex RLHF challenges involving larger models, including summarization and dialogue tasks. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming strategies that return the best of $N$ samples based on a learned reward. Prior to discussing these outcomes, we outline the experimental methodology; further specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We conduct experiments on three open-ended text generation tasks. In every experiment, algorithms are trained to learn a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents the initial segment of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is required to produce $y$ with positive sentiment. To facilitate systematic evaluation, preference pairs are \textit{generated} over model outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence using reviews from the IMDb train split (additional details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post and the policy must generate a summary $y$ covering the post’s main ideas. In line with previous studies, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} paired with human preferences curated by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We leverage an SFT model that is fine-tuned on forum post summaries written by humans\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and employ the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for RLHF. The human preference dataset was assembled by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a distinct, yet similarly-trained, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} scenario, $x$ is a human prompt, which could range from technical queries (e.g., astrophysics) to advice-seeking requests. The policy should provide an informative and engaging reply $y$ to the user’s input. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k human-assistant conversations, each ending with two responses from a large language model, accompanied by a label for the human-preferred response. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available for this setting, we construct the SFT model by fine-tuning an existing language model solely on preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct methods to assess our experiments. To measure how well each algorithm optimizes the objective of maximizing reward under constraints, we examine, in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, the tradeoff curve between reward achieved and KL-divergence from the reference policy for each algorithm; this is feasible because we have access to the exact reward signal provided by a sentiment classifier. Conversely, in practical settings where the true reward function is unavailable, we instead report each algorithm’s \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, relying on GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judges to evaluate the quality of summaries and the helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. In the summarization task, baseline performance is given by the reference summaries in the test data; for dialogue, the preferred responses from the test set serve as the baseline. Although prior research indicates that large language models can surpass traditional metrics as automatic evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we further validate the use of GPT-4 by conducting a human evaluation study, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results indicate GPT-4’s assessments align closely with those of humans, with human-GPT-4 agreement often matching or exceeding agreement between human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess multiple established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. For the summarization task, we utilize zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, while for the dialogue task, we apply 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also include the \textbf{SFT} model and the \textbf{Preferred-FT} model, which undergoes supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$, using either the SFT model (in sentiment control and summarization) or a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another method we test is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which encourages the model to assign higher probability to $y_w$ while actively suppressing the likelihood of $y_l$. This technique incorporates an optional weighting factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood component. Additionally, we explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} with a reward function derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, which operates as an oracle by leveraging the true reward function in the controlled sentiment scenario. In our sentiment experiments, we implement two variants of PPO-GT: a standard version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and an enhanced version that normalizes rewards and further tunes hyperparameters for better results (these adjustments are also employed when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Finally, we investigate the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, where $N$ outputs are sampled from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue), and the top-scoring completion—according to a preference-trained reward function—is selected. While this approach separates the effectiveness of the reward model from PPO training, it is computationally costly for even moderate $N$, since $N$ completions must be generated per prompt during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue, as judged by GPT-4; DPO is the sole approach that outperforms the selected summaries from the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured across various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO's edge over dataset labels remains largely consistent during training for each sampling temperature.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly used in standard RLHF algorithms strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting how much the policy diverges from the reference policy. As a result, when evaluating and comparing different algorithms, it is necessary to consider both the achieved reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of significantly greater KL is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} displays the reward versus KL tradeoff curve for different algorithms in the sentiment domain. For each algorithm, we perform several training runs, each time varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 experiments. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, we evaluate the trained policies on a set of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the aggregate KL-divergence over all timesteps in the sequence.} with the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our analysis shows that DPO attains the most favorable frontier, obtaining the highest reward for relatively low KL values. This finding stands out for several reasons. First, DPO and PPO target the same optimization objective, yet DPO is clearly more effective; DPO’s reward/KL curve strictly surpasses that of PPO. Second, DPO secures a better reward-KL tradeoff than PPO, \emph{even when PPO has access to ground-truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO applicable to large-scale preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on real-world tasks involving summarization and single-turn conversation. In the case of summarization, standard automated metrics such as ROUGE often have weak correlation with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have shown that fine-tuning language models with PPO guided by human feedback can yield higher-quality summaries. Our comparison across methods involves generating outputs on the TL;DR summarization dataset's test split and calculating the mean win rate versus reference outputs in the test set. We sample outputs for all approaches using temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0; corresponding win rates are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all further fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our experiments show DPO attains a win rate near 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO's approximate 57\% at its optimal sampling temperature, also 0.0. DPO further surpasses the best win rate achieved by the $N$ baseline method. It is worth highlighting that we did not perform extensive tuning of DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting DPO’s actual capability may be even greater. Additionally, DPO demonstrates greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature than PPO, whose effectiveness drops to that of the original GPT-J model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT yields little improvement relative to the SFT baseline. Further, a direct comparison between DPO and PPO using human raters in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments} reveals that DPO samples at temperature 0.25 are favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue tasks, we assess various methods on the portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split that contains a single exchange between human and assistant. In the GPT-4 evaluations, we use the preferred responses from the test set as references to calculate win rates for each method. Since there is no established SFT model for this setting, we initialize with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, fine-tune a reference model using Preferred-FT on the selected completions to ensure they remain in-distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the best out of 128 Preferred-FT completions (as we observe the Best of $N$ metric plateaus at $N=128$ for this dataset—see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted baseline with Pythia-2.8B. Across the optimal sampling temperatures for each method, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance. We further test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a reputable implementation \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but cannot identify a prompt or temperature setting where this model surpasses the base Pythia-2.8B. Given our TL;DR results and that both methods target the same reward, we use the Best of 128 approach as an approximate stand-in for PPO-level outcomes. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient technique that exceeds the preferred completions baseline on the Anthropic HH dataset, matching or outperforming the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO rapidly reaches its peak performance.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning provides a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without employing reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge to fit conventional RL frameworks and algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows for direct optimization towards human preferences using a simple cross-entropy objective, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without any loss of generality. Even with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO matches or exceeds the performance of current RLHF techniques, such as those utilizing PPO, thereby significantly lowering the barriers to training language models with human feedback.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings highlight several key avenues for future investigation. One open question is how well the DPO policy performs on out-of-distribution data in comparison to approaches that rely on a learned reward signal. Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies may generalize comparably to models trained with PPO, yet further in-depth analysis is warranted. For instance, it remains to be seen whether self-labeling with the DPO policy can similarly exploit unlabeled prompts effectively. Another question concerns the nature of reward over-optimization in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the small drop in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is indicative of this phenomenon. Moreover, while our evaluation encompasses models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to substantially larger, state-of-the-art architectures represents a promising research direction. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates can vary with different prompts; future work could explore optimal strategies for obtaining reliable automated judgments. Lastly, DPO has potential applications far beyond human preference-based language model training, such as applying the method to generative modeling in diverse modalities.
