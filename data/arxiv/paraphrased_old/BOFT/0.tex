\title{Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization}

\begin{abstract}
Large foundational models are becoming widespread, but training them from the ground up is extremely costly. Therefore, it is increasingly crucial to adapt these powerful models efficiently to downstream tasks. In this work, we explore a principled finetuning approach—Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)—for adapting to downstream tasks. While OFT shows strong generalization capabilities, it still involves a relatively large number of trainable parameters because orthogonal matrices are high-dimensional. To tackle this issue, we first analyze OFT through the lens of information transmission and then highlight several important criteria that facilitate improved parameter efficiency. Drawing inspiration from how the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm achieves efficient information flow, we propose an efficient orthogonal parameterization based on butterfly structures. We incorporate this parameterization into OFT, leading to a novel parameter-efficient finetuning technique called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). Since BOFT generalizes OFT as a particular instance, it establishes a broader orthogonal finetuning framework. Lastly, we perform comprehensive empirical evaluations by adapting large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models across various vision and language downstream tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent models like ChatGPT~\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} showcase the impressive generalization capabilities of large foundation models. The rapid improvement in these models’ performance has been accompanied by a massive growth in parameter count (for example, GPT-3 has approximately 175 billion parameters). Consequently, training foundation models from scratch has become increasingly difficult for researchers. Progress in this area thus depends heavily on the ability to adapt such models without retraining from the beginning. In other words, it is essential to efficiently adapt existing foundation models to downstream tasks. There are primarily three approaches to efficient task adaptation: \emph{model finetuning}~\cite{hulora2022,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,raffel2020exploring,qiu2023controlling,zhang2022adaptive,chavan2023one}, where the original model architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of parameters is finetuned; \emph{adapter tuning}~\cite{rebuffi2017learning,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion,lin2020exploring,he2021towards}, which involves adding extra trainable parameters to the existing model; and \emph{prompt tuning}~\cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}, where additional trainable prefix tokens are prepended to the input. Among these strategies, model finetuning stands out as a straightforward yet effective approach, and importantly, it does not introduce any inference latency.

The core idea behind model finetuning is to maintain similarity between the pretrained model and the finetuned model according to specific criteria, thereby preserving the knowledge gained during pretraining. For example, many existing finetuning techniques use a small learning rate, an informal strategy that encourages minimal deviation between the pretrained and finetuned models. Considering the structured nature of weight matrices, a more systematic approach aims to retain the relational information within these matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. This understanding has given rise to a novel finetuning framework called Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT)~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, where neurons within the same layer undergo transformation via a shared orthogonal matrix, guaranteeing that the pairwise angles between neurons remain intact throughout finetuning. While OFT has shown strong generalizability and effective convergence when finetuning text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, the number of trainable parameters can be quite large because of the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. To mitigate this, OFT employs a block-diagonal structure to lower the parameter count. However, this increased parameter efficiency comes with drawbacks: the orthogonal matrix is constrained to a fixed sparsity pattern, and orthogonal transformations are applied separately to distinct blocks. Although this block-diagonal sparsity pattern saves parameters, it may introduce unwanted inductive biases (e.g., it limits expressiveness and fails to approximate traditional linear transformations), and crucially, the optimal method for determining a beneficial sparsity pattern remains unknown.

The crucial aspect of tackling this issue is to create a dense orthogonal matrix that remains parameter-efficient. Although this might initially appear impractical because a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ typically demands $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, we propose a novel strategy that constructs a dense orthogonal matrix through the product of multiple factorized sparse matrices. This method is motivated by the observation that the number of trainable parameters can be lowered by exchanging computational effort for memory savings. Since the orthogonal matrix is represented as the product of sparse matrices, this multiplication must be executed repeatedly during each training iteration. To better understand the matrix factorization, we frame the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as a problem of information transmission. Within this framework, generating a dense orthogonal matrix via multiplication of sparse matrices corresponds to transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. This perspective allows us to design various sparse matrix factorizations that restrict the number of trainable parameters while maintaining sufficient expressiveness to form dense matrices.

To attain parameter efficiency in our information transmission model, we draw inspiration from the butterfly structures present in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, where information is exchanged efficiently between different nodes~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting}. In particular, the butterfly graph underlying the Cooley-Tukey algorithm naturally induces a sparse matrix factorization approach known as butterfly factorization. Using butterfly factorization, we can produce a $d$-dimensional dense matrix as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero elements. By enforcing orthogonality on each sparse matrix, we achieve an efficient orthogonal parameterization with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, which substantially reduces the parameter count compared to the original approach. Exploiting this efficient orthogonal parameterization, we introduce a novel parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT encompasses OFT as a special instance and offers a comprehensive orthogonal fine-tuning framework. A common feature shared by both the block-diagonal and butterfly structures is sparsity. Both structures leverage data sparsity within orthogonal matrices to decrease the effective number of trainable parameters. It is also worth comparing our method with the low-rank structure employed in LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}; both low-rank and sparse matrices belong to major classes of structured matrices~\cite{candes2011robust} that facilitate parameter efficiency.

In contrast to the block-diagonal structure utilized by OFT, which balances expressivity and regularity, BOFT leverages the butterfly structure to achieve a smoother interpolation between matrices from the entire orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). This approach allows us to identify a more compact hypothesis class within the orthogonal group for downstream tasks. Given the extensive application of the butterfly structure in numerous fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier and discrete cosine transforms, we argue that our structured method for parameter efficiency inherently introduces an inductive bias that enhances generalizability and mitigates overfitting. Our reasoning stems from the fact that the butterfly structure can readily reconstruct many classical linear transforms, whereas the block-diagonal structure employed by OFT cannot recover any of these transforms. Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item We address the challenge of parameter efficiency in orthogonal finetuning by introducing a novel information transmission framework. This framework reformulates the problem of designing a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission task within a grid-structured graph.
    \item Drawing inspiration from the butterfly structures used in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, we introduce Orthogonal Butterfly, a parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning technique, situated within the information transmission framework.
    \item We offer several theoretical insights explaining why BOFT can drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining strong expressivity and training stability. Additionally, due to its matrix factorization, BOFT features an interesting weight interpolation property (see Figure~\ref{fig:boft_interpolation}).
    \item For the first time, we extend orthogonal finetuning beyond controllable text-to-image generation~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, demonstrating its considerable potential as a universal model finetuning approach. To support this, we apply BOFT to diverse adaptation tasks across computer vision and natural language processing. BOFT surpasses current state-of-the-art methods by a significant margin, confirming its superior parameter efficiency and generalization capability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT)}. As foundational models (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{brown2020language,radford2021learning,rombach2022high,kirillov2023segment}) continue to grow in size and capability, there has been significant interest in finetuning these models for downstream tasks in a parameter-efficient manner~\cite{treviso2023efficient,ding2023parameter,lialin2023scaling}. Among the numerous PEFT approaches~\cite{houlsby2019parameter,aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona,wang2022adamix,gheini2021cross,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,sung2021training,ansell2022composable,lester2021power,li2021prefix,vu2022spot,he2021towards,mao2021unipelt,karimi2021compacter,liu2022few,sung2022lst,chen2022parameter,jia2022visual,chen2022adaptformer,zhang2022neural,jie2023fact,lian2022scaling,luo2023towards}, reparameterization-based techniques~\cite{aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona} are most pertinent to our research. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} modifies the pretrained weight matrix by adding the product of two low-rank matrices, demonstrating strong performance on natural language processing tasks. Since LoRA maintains a fixed rank for all added matrices, several approaches \cite{zhang2022adaptive,valipour2022dylora,zhang2023increlora} dynamically adjust the rank across different layers to optimally allocate parameters. Because of its straightforwardness, this low-rank weight reparameterization has gained widespread adoption \cite{dettmers2023qlora,zi2023delta,chavan2023one}. Drawing inspiration from how hyperspherical energy relates to generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal}, \cite{qiu2023controlling} introduces orthogonal finetuning (OFT), an alternative yet effective method for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. OFT specifically learns an orthogonal matrix to transform neurons within the same layer, achieving superior generalization and consistently more stable training compared to LoRA. Although OFT performs well, it typically requires more trainable parameters than LoRA. Thus, enhancing OFT's parameter efficiency is a valuable objective. Furthermore, the applicability of OFT to a broader range of adaptation tasks (beyond text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}) remains unexplored. BOFT improves OFT’s parameter efficiency through butterfly factorization, enabling us to showcase the effectiveness of orthogonal finetuning across general adaptation tasks.

\textbf{Butterfly structures}. The radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm recursively decomposes the $N$-point discrete Fourier transform into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point discrete Fourier transforms, yielding a butterfly structure representable as a product of multiple sparse matrices (the product is known as a butterfly matrix). Butterfly matrices have been employed to parameterize orthogonal matrices to eliminate the need for pivoting during Gaussian elimination and enhance efficiency~\cite{parker1995random}, to stabilize recurrent neural network training~\cite{jing2017tunable}, and in kernel approximation~\cite{munkhoeva2018quadrature}. Works such as \cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} use butterfly parameterizations to learn fast linear transforms. Additionally, \cite{chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} leverage butterfly matrices to facilitate efficient neural network training. Butterfly structures have also proven beneficial in rapid matrix-vector multiplication~\cite{michielssen1996multilevel,o2010algorithm}, data-sparse matrix approximation~\cite{li2015butterfly}, and network communication~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting,li2003linear,rajasingh2016transmission}. Unlike earlier studies, our focus is on exploiting butterfly structures to boost the parameter efficiency of OFT.

\section{An Information Transmission View on Orthogonal Finetuning}

We begin by introducing some foundational concepts of OFT. To fine-tune the pretrained weight matrix, OFT reparameterizes the updated weight matrix as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the original, fixed pretrained weight matrix. Unlike LoRA, which adjusts the weights by adding a low-rank matrix, OFT updates the weights via multiplication by an orthogonal matrix. For parameter efficiency, LoRA employs a low-rank structure, whereas the original OFT utilizes a block-diagonal orthogonal structure~\cite{qiu2023controlling} (smaller diagonal block sizes result in higher parameter efficiency for OFT). An intuitive comparison is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:comp_lora}. The rationale for applying an orthogonal transformation during weight matrix fine-tuning is to preserve pairwise angles between neurons~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}, thereby maintaining much of the semantic knowledge gained during pretraining. Specifically, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, modifying the forward pass from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are the input and output vectors, respectively. To enable zero initialization, OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix. To maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ throughout fine-tuning, we adopt the Cayley parameterization as in \cite{qiu2023controlling,liu2021orthogonal}, i.e., $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. To enhance parameter efficiency, the block-diagonal structure is introduced by representing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathcal{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix for all $i$, with $b r = d$. The gain in parameter efficiency from the block-diagonal structure comes with a trade-off—it assumes that the dimensions of a neuron (i.e., a column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$) can be split into $r$ groups, and that dimensions in different groups are transformed independently via separate orthogonal matrices. Although the block-diagonal structure shows empirical effectiveness, dividing the dimensions of a neuron into $r$ groups merely based on their indices lacks justification, motivating a preference for dense orthogonal matrices. This raises a natural question: \emph{Is it possible to construct a dense orthogonal matrix without sacrificing parameter efficiency?}

To tackle this issue, we suggest constructing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of several sparse orthogonal matrices. To offer a unified and intuitive framework for analyzing the sparsity pattern in orthogonal matrix factorization, we interpret the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix in OFT as an information transmission problem. More precisely, producing a dense matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ via the product of $m$ square matrices $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be regarded as conveying information through a grid composed of $d\times (m+1)$ nodes, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}. This viewpoint is motivated by noting that a $d$-dimensional dense square matrix can be seen as representing dense connections from $d$ nodes to another set of $d$ nodes. For matrix $\bm{R}$, a zero element $R_{ij}$ implies that information from the $j$-th node cannot reach the $i$-th node, whereas a non-zero $R_{ij}$ indicates that information transmission is possible. Hence, expressing the dense matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple matrices $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be interpreted as sequential information exchange following the graphs defined by each $\bm{B}_i$, for all $i$. The flow of information proceeds starting with $\bm{B}_1$ and ending with $\bm{B}_n$. 

As a concrete illustration shown in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}, we examine the factorization $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ with its sparsity patterns and associated graph visualized. The graph arises from unfolding the matrix multiplication. In this graph, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is interpreted as the connectivity matrix linking nodes at level $i$ to nodes at level $(i+1)$. More precisely, the element $(j_1,j_2)$ of $\bm{B}_i$ signifies whether a directed edge exists from the $j_2$-th node at level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node at level $(i+1)$, where zero denotes the absence of such an edge. For the product $\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ to yield a dense matrix, every node at the first level must be able to transmit information to all nodes at the sixth level. When considering $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$—corresponding to source nodes at level one and receiver nodes at level five—we observe that information from node 1 cannot reach node 3, hence the sparsity pattern of $\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ has a zero at position $(3,1)$. Similarly, this correspondence between the induced graph and sparsity pattern holds for $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$. More generally, for a matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to be dense, the $m$ factorization matrices $\bm{B}_m, \ldots, \bm{B}_1$ must correspond to a set of directed edges arranged on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid, where each directed edge connects nodes only between adjacent levels (i.e., columns), ensuring that information from every node in the first level can be transmitted to every node in the $(m+1)$-th level.

The information transmission perspective provides valuable insights into the sparsity pattern of factorization matrices in OFT. Figure~\ref{fig:blk_diag} illustrates the block-diagonal arrangement of $\bm{R}$ in the original OFT. Although this block-diagonal structure reduces the number of trainable parameters, it cannot form a dense matrix $\bm{R}$. Our aim is to construct a dense orthogonal matrix by combining $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices while using the fewest effective trainable parameters possible. From the information transmission standpoint, the key criteria for achieving this goal are \emph{(i)} \textbf{dense connectivity}: ensuring that every node in the initial level has at least one path to each node in the final level, and \emph{(ii)} \textbf{minimum free edges}: minimizing the total number of edges subject to the orthogonality constraint. It is important to recognize that orthogonality imposes a subtle restriction on edges between adjacent levels. For example, to guarantee that each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is full-rank (a necessary condition for orthogonality), there must be $d$ edges forming a bijection between all nodes in the $i$-th level and those in the $(i+1)$-th level, setting a lower bound of $d$ edges between consecutive levels (e.g., 4 in the example shown in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}). These $d$ edges are essential for orthogonality and should not be counted toward the total edge count since they are not trainable (e.g., for a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with $d$ non-zero entries, these entries can only be $\pm 1$). Because orthogonal matrices have fewer parameters than full matrices, the orthogonality constraint introduces additional dependencies among edges. For instance, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at position $(1,1)$ necessitates a zero at position $(2,2)$ (i.e., one missing edge implies the absence of another edge). Hence, for each valid set of edge connections, orthogonality can sometimes add or remove edges. Visualizing the non-zero pattern of the composed orthogonal matrix demonstrates the particular usefulness of the information transmission framework in OFT, as our focus is solely on the non-zero trainable elements of $\bm{R}$, regardless of their specific values.

A straightforward dense connection between two levels involves $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges (i.e., corresponding to a single dense orthogonal matrix), resulting in $d^2 - d$ edges (for instance, when $d=4$, there are 12 edges). Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} illustrates an example of a valid matrix factorization that uses a total of $10$ edges, which is actually fewer than that required by a single dense orthogonal matrix. This framework allows us to analyze the parameter efficiency of OFT from a graphical standpoint, making it straightforward to identify valid factorizations. We draw inspiration from an intriguing topology found in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, known as butterfly graphs~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, which can efficiently establish dense connections between $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. For example, the topology in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} uses 10 edges to achieve dense connectivity, whereas the butterfly network requires just 8 edges. In the following, we explain how the butterfly structure enhances parameter efficiency.

\section{Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization}

The butterfly structure was originally developed within the Cooley-Tukey algorithm to facilitate the fast Fourier transform. In the Fourier transform, a localized modification in the frequency domain can induce a global change in the spatial domain, which aligns conceptually with our problem of information transmission—each node in the first level can relay information to every node in the final level. The butterfly structure is also widely recognized as a computer network topology~\cite{solihin2015fundamentals,li2011linear} employed for effective information exchange. Let us assume that $k \geq 2$ is a power of $2$; we begin by defining the \emph{butterfly factor} $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:but_factor}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
    \end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ for $i=1,\ldots,4$ are vectors. For $d = 2^N$, we then define the $d$-dimensional \emph{butterfly matrix} $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ recursively as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{B}_1\left(\frac{d}{2}\right) & \bm{0} \\
    \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)
    \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2}) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})$ and $\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})$ represent two butterfly matrices each of dimension $\frac{d}{2}$. We then introduce the \emph{butterfly component} defined as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$, which is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d\times d$ with blocks of size $k$, where each $\bm{B}^F_i(k), \forall i$ corresponds to butterfly factors described in Equation~\ref{eq:but_factor}. We are now prepared to utilize the butterfly matrix to parameterize an orthogonal matrix. To accomplish this, it suffices to ensure that every multiplicative factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k), \forall k$ involved in the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is orthogonal. We begin by examining the block-diagonal matrix $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ with blocks of size 2, where orthogonality can be straightforwardly ensured by employing the Cayley transform (or equivalently a $2$-dimensional rotation) for each block, similar to the approaches in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}. The pattern of non-zero elements in any butterfly component corresponds to a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, which allows all butterfly components to be conveniently parameterized as orthogonal matrices. This results in an efficient orthogonal matrix parameterization constructed from numerous $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices. Extending the butterfly matrices as in \cite{chen2022pixelated}, we define a block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each element $\bm{d}_i, \forall i$ is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. To ensure that the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ remains orthogonal, each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix is parameterized to be orthogonal. The non-zero pattern of other butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k>2$ is a block-wise permutation of the pattern in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, which similarly enables them to be orthogonal matrices. Putting all elements together, the forward pass in BOFT is

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}\nonumber
    \bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},~~~~\text{subject to}~\bigg{\{}\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}~~\text{and}~~\underbrace{\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d}_{\forall j\in [1, m]}\bigg{\}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where for simplicity, we represent $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m - i+1})$ as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, and $\bm{I}_d$ denotes the $d$-dimensional identity matrix. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is formed by multiplying several orthogonal butterfly components. For ease of notation, we refer to BOFT with $\bm{R}(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m$,$b$), where $b\geq 2$. When $m=1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) simplifies to the block-diagonal OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling} with block size $b$. BOFT($1$,$d$) corresponds to the original OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, which uses a full orthogonal matrix without constraints. BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$,$b$) employs the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$, resulting in a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. Generally, BOFT($m$,$b$) requires $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable parameters to fine-tune a linear layer sized $d \times n$. When using the butterfly matrix, i.e., $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT needs $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In comparison, the original OFT with a fully dense orthogonal matrix has a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, and the block-diagonal OFT with $r$ blocks requires $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters. Thus, while the original OFT must set block size $b=d$ to produce a dense orthogonal matrix, BOFT can achieve this with any choice of $b$.

\textbf{Identity initialization for BOFT}. Fine-tuning approaches typically begin with the exact pretrained model to ensure the updated model does not diverge significantly from it. For instance, LoRA initializes its low-rank weights to zero. In BOFT, all butterfly components are initialized as identity matrices (i.e., the skew-symmetric matrix is initialized to zero in the Cayley transform).

\textbf{Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT}. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} introduces a Dropout layer for low-rank weight updates to mitigate overfitting. While standard Dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} works naturally with LoRA, it is incompatible with BOFT due to the multiplicative nature of its weight updates. To resolve this, we introduce a multiplicative Dropout tailored for BOFT. Since the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, which can be rearranged into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, this multiplicative Dropout randomly selects $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks within each butterfly component, substituting these selected blocks with identity matrices.

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{Expressivity of BOFT}. The butterfly structure combined with permutations can exactly represent many classical fast linear transforms~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} (such as the fast Fourier transform and Hadamard transform). However, the capability of our orthogonal butterfly matrix to approximate a general orthogonal matrix is still unclear. We begin by performing a simulation to approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix~\cite{anderson1987generation} of size $512\times 512$. The outcomes shown in Figure~\ref{fig:para_eff} are averaged over 10 different random seeds. The y-axis represents the approximation error, while the x-axis indicates the number of effective trainable parameters. Curves sharing the same color correspond to BOFT configurations with the same block size, where the leftmost point represents the error of BOFT($1$,$b$) (i.e., the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$). Generally, BOFT achieves greater parameter efficiency than OFT. For instance, BOFT($9$,$2$) exhibits higher expressiveness than BOFT($1$,$16$) but requires substantially fewer parameters. BOFT configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ typically show better parameter efficiency. For example, BOFT($6$,$4$) uses significantly fewer parameters while attaining a comparable approximation error to BOFT($2$,$16$). Overall, the butterfly matrix characterizes a more structured subset of the orthogonal group (relative to the block-diagonal structure), which theoretically makes BOFT more expressive than OFT with the same block size.

\begin{theorem}[Expressivity of BOFT]\label{thm:exp_boft}
    BOFT possesses greater expressivity compared to OFT with an identical block size. To approximate every orthogonal matrix of size $d$, we can multiply butterfly matrices as $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$, for all $i$ and $j$, are butterfly matrices.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:exp_boft} implies a straightforward extension of BOFT — the resulting orthogonal matrix can be generalized as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$, where $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ denotes the orthogonal matrix employed in BOFT. When $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the matrix $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ can represent the entire orthogonal group. This matrix decomposition is also referred to as a kaleidoscope hierarchy~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize that enhanced expressiveness does not necessarily translate into improved finetuning performance, as full finetuning, despite its universal representational power, often results in unsatisfactory outcomes. Balancing expressivity and regularization is crucial for the generalizability of model finetuning. BOFT expands the finetuning parameter space with structural priors, enabling us to achieve a more favorable balance between expressivity and regularity.

\textbf{Spectral properties}. Orthogonal finetuning typically achieves superior spectral characteristics compared to LoRA because it exactly maintains the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This can be demonstrated through singular value decomposition: $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing singular values. Both OFT and BOFT apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ on the left, resulting in finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which leaves the largest singular value (i.e., the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$) unchanged. This preservation has been demonstrated to significantly enhance training stability and generalization~\cite{miyato2018spectral,yoshida2017spectral}. Additional intriguing mathematical properties are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:sn_property}.

\textbf{Orthogonal finetuning as learning bilinear similarity}. BOFT can be expressed as learning a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i\bm{R}\bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ represents the $i$-th neuron (i.e., column vector) of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. BOFT can be interpreted as learning the bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ under a strict constraint (i.e., $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal), which fundamentally relates to distance metric learning~\cite{xing2002distance} and bilinear forms~\cite{roman2005advanced}.

\textbf{Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT}. Since $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT usually corresponds to a structured subgroup of the orthogonal group, this restricts the hypothesis space and naturally introduces an inductive bias. We contend that the structured inductive bias introduced by butterfly factorization supports better generalization because it shares structural patterns with many classical linear transforms~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}, such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform. Additionally, the sparse matrix factorization used in BOFT may also contribute some implicit inductive bias~\cite{gunasekar2017implicit,li2018algorithmic,liu2019neural}.

\textbf{Comparison to butterfly-based sparse training}. Several prior works~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope,chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} have explored sparse training utilizing the butterfly parameterization. These studies primarily concentrate on reparameterizing weight matrices directly using the butterfly structure and training neural networks from scratch. In \cite{dao2022monarch}, the approach involves finetuning pretrained weights by first projecting them onto a variant of butterfly matrices, followed by optimizing the projected components for downstream applications. In contrast, BOFT introduces a distinctly different finetuning method that modifies weights through layer-shared weight matrices.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Limitations}

This paper introduces Orthogonal Butterfly, a versatile and parameter-efficient finetuning technique for foundation models founded on the butterfly architecture. The central idea for achieving improved parameter efficiency lies in representing a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. To facilitate the identification of feasible matrix factorizations, we propose a graphical information transmission framework. Within this framework, we find that the butterfly structure effectively satisfies our criteria for sparse orthogonal matrix factorization. We empirically demonstrate the efficacy of BOFT in finetuning large-scale language models, vision models, and text-to-image generative models. Our experimental results further confirm BOFT's superiority as a general-purpose finetuning approach.

Despite its strong empirical performance, BOFT is not without limitations. Because the ultimate orthogonal matrix in BOFT is composed as a product of multiple orthogonal matrices, the training runtime overhead is somewhat greater than that of OFT. Addressing this increase in training time remains an open challenge. On the positive side, once finetuning is complete, the orthogonal matrices learned by BOFT can be directly incorporated into the pretrained model without incurring any extra inference latency. Additionally, it remains unclear if the butterfly network represents the most efficient architecture for information transmission. Our information transmission framework opens the door to inspiration from a different field—computer networking—where the efficiency of network topologies for transmitting information is extensively analyzed. We anticipate that alternative, more efficient network structures may be discovered and employed for constructing dense orthogonal matrices.
