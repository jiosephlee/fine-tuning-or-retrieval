\title{Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization}

\begin{abstract}
Large foundation models are increasingly prevalent, yet training them from the ground up remains prohibitively costly. Therefore, it is crucial to develop efficient methods for adapting these robust models to specific downstream tasks. In this work, we investigate a systematic finetuning approach—Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)—for downstream task adaptation. Although OFT exhibits strong generalization capabilities, it still involves a relatively large number of trainable parameters due to the high dimensionality inherent in orthogonal matrices. To mitigate this, we begin by analyzing OFT through the lens of information transmission and identify several essential criteria that improve parameter efficiency. Drawing inspiration from the efficiency of the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm in information transmission, we introduce an efficient orthogonal parameterization based on butterfly structures. We integrate this parameterization into OFT, resulting in a novel parameter-efficient finetuning technique named Orthogonal Butterfly~(BOFT). By encompassing OFT as a particular instance, BOFT offers a more general orthogonal finetuning framework. Lastly, we carry out comprehensive empirical evaluations by adapting large vision transformers, substantial language models, and text-to-image diffusion models to various vision and language downstream tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent models such as ChatGPT~\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} showcase the extraordinary generalization capabilities of large foundation models. The rapid advancements in their performance are accompanied by a dramatic growth in parameter counts (e.g., GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters). Consequently, training a foundation model from scratch has become increasingly impractical for many researchers. Progress in this area thus hinges on the ability to adapt these models without full retraining. Efficient adaptation to downstream tasks can primarily be achieved via three strategies: \emph{model finetuning}~\cite{hulora2022,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,raffel2020exploring,qiu2023controlling,zhang2022adaptive,chavan2023one}, where the original model architecture remains unchanged and only a subset of parameters is updated; \emph{adapter tuning}~\cite{rebuffi2017learning,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion,lin2020exploring,he2021towards}, which involves adding extra trainable parameters to the base model; and \emph{prompt tuning}~\cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}, where additional trainable prefix tokens are concatenated to the input. Among these approaches, model finetuning stands out as a straightforward yet effective technique that importantly does not introduce inference latency.

The core idea behind model finetuning is to maintain similarity between the pretrained model and the finetuned model according to specific criteria, thereby retaining the knowledge acquired during pretraining. For example, many existing finetuning techniques use a small learning rate, which heuristically encourages minimal divergence between the pretrained and finetuned models. Considering the structured characteristics of weight matrices, a more systematic approach aims to conserve the relational information of these matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. This concept gives rise to a new model finetuning framework called Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT)~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, where neurons within the same layer are transformed using a shared orthogonal matrix, guaranteeing that the pairwise angles between neurons remain intact throughout the finetuning process. While OFT has shown strong generalizability and convergence when finetuning text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, the number of trainable parameters involved can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. To mitigate this, OFT employs a block-diagonal structure to decrease the parameter count. Nevertheless, this parameter efficiency introduces drawbacks—the orthogonal matrix adopts a fixed sparsity pattern, and the orthogonal transformation is performed independently within separate blocks. Although this block-diagonal sparsity reduces parameters, it may impose undesirable inductive biases (e.g., the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix limits expressiveness and is incapable of approximating classical linear transformations), and more critically, identifying an effective sparsity pattern remains an open challenge.

The crucial aspect of tackling this issue is to create a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining parameter efficiency. Although this might initially appear impractical since a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, we adopt an innovative approach by composing the dense orthogonal matrix from multiple factorized sparse matrices. This strategy is motivated by the insight that the number of trainable parameters can be minimized by exchanging computation time for memory space. Representing the orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse matrices necessitates repeated multiplication in each training iteration. To contextualize the matrix factorization, we frame the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. Within this framework, producing a dense orthogonal matrix via the multiplication of sparse matrices can be viewed as transmitting information over a grid-structured graph. This perspective on information transmission allows us to design various sparse matrix factorization methods that constrain the number of trainable parameters while retaining sufficient expressiveness to produce dense matrices.

To enhance parameter efficiency in our information transmission paradigm, we draw inspiration from the butterfly patterns found in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, which enables efficient information exchange between nodes~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting}. In particular, the butterfly graph inherent to the Cooley-Tukey algorithm naturally leads to a sparse matrix factorization technique known as butterfly factorization. Employing butterfly factorization, we can construct a $d$-dimensional dense matrix as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero elements. By ensuring each sparse matrix is orthogonal, we achieve an efficient orthogonal parameterization with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, significantly reducing the parameter count compared to the original full parameterization. Utilizing this compact orthogonal parameterization, we introduce a novel parameter-efficient finetuning method termed Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT generalizes OFT as a special case and offers a broad orthogonal finetuning framework. Both the block-diagonal and butterfly structures share the common trait of sparsity—each introduces data sparsity into orthogonal matrices to lower the effective number of trainable parameters. It is insightful to compare our approach with the low-rank structure used in LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}; both low-rank and sparse matrices belong to principal categories of structured matrices~\cite{candes2011robust} that enable parameter efficiency.

In contrast to the block-diagonal structure utilized by OFT to balance expressiveness and regularity, BOFT employs the butterfly structure to create a smoother interpolation between matrices drawn from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). This approach allows us to identify a more compact hypothesis class within the orthogonal group tailored for downstream tasks. Given the extensive use of the butterfly structure in many fast linear transformations, such as the discrete Fourier and discrete cosine transforms, we contend that our structured approach to parameter efficiency imparts an implicit inductive bias that enhances generalization and mitigates overfitting. Our reasoning is based on the fact that the butterfly structure can readily reconstruct many classical linear transforms, whereas the block-diagonal structure in OFT cannot recover any. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item We investigate parameter efficiency in orthogonal finetuning through a novel information transmission framework. This framework recasts the challenge of designing a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal matrix into an information transmission problem over a grid-structured graph.
    \item Drawing inspiration from the butterfly structures found in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, we introduce Orthogonal Butterfly, a parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning technique, framed within the information transmission perspective.
    \item We offer theoretical insights explaining how BOFT can drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining strong expressivity and training stability. Due to its matrix factorization, BOFT also exhibits a compelling weight interpolation property (see Figure~\ref{fig:boft_interpolation}).
    \item For the first time, we extend orthogonal finetuning beyond controllable text-to-image generation~\cite{qiu2023controlling} by applying it to various tasks, demonstrating its promise as a universal model finetuning strategy. Specifically, we employ BOFT in multiple adaptation scenarios spanning computer vision and natural language processing. BOFT consistently outperforms leading state-of-the-art approaches by a significant margin, validating its exceptional parameter efficiency and generalization performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT)}. With the growing size and capabilities of foundation models (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{brown2020language,radford2021learning,rombach2022high,kirillov2023segment}), there has been significant interest in finetuning these models for downstream applications in a parameter-efficient manner~\cite{treviso2023efficient,ding2023parameter,lialin2023scaling}. Among the various PEFT techniques~\cite{houlsby2019parameter,aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona,wang2022adamix,gheini2021cross,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,sung2021training,ansell2022composable,lester2021power,li2021prefix,vu2022spot,he2021towards,mao2021unipelt,karimi2021compacter,liu2022few,sung2022lst,chen2022parameter,jia2022visual,chen2022adaptformer,zhang2022neural,jie2023fact,lian2022scaling,luo2023towards}, those based on reparameterization~\cite{aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona} are most pertinent to our research. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} updates pretrained weight matrices by adding the product of two low-rank matrices, yielding strong results on natural language processing tasks. Since LoRA fixes the rank of all added matrices, several approaches \cite{zhang2022adaptive,valipour2022dylora,zhang2023increlora} adaptively modify the rank across different layers to optimally distribute parameter budgets. Because of its straightforwardness, this low-rank reparameterization strategy has become widely adopted \cite{dettmers2023qlora,zi2023delta,chavan2023one}. Motivated by the role of hyperspherical energy in characterizing generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal}, \cite{qiu2023controlling} introduces orthogonal finetuning (OFT), an alternative and effective technique for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. OFT specifically learns an orthogonal matrix to transform neurons within the same layer, achieving superior generalization and consistently more stable training than LoRA. Despite its strong performance, OFT usually involves more trainable parameters than LoRA. Thus, enhancing OFT’s parameter efficiency is a valuable objective. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether OFT can be extended beyond controlling text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling} to a broader range of adaptation tasks. BOFT advances OFT’s parameter efficiency through butterfly factorization. This advancement enables us to showcase the strength of orthogonal finetuning across general adaptation scenarios.

\textbf{Butterfly structures}. The radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm breaks down the $N$-point discrete Fourier transform into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point discrete Fourier transforms recursively, generating a butterfly structure expressible as a product of several sparse matrices (known as a butterfly matrix). Butterfly matrices have previously been employed to parameterize orthogonal matrices, thereby avoiding pivoting in Gaussian elimination and enhancing efficiency~\cite{parker1995random}; to stabilize recurrent neural network training~\cite{jing2017tunable}; and for kernel approximation~\cite{munkhoeva2018quadrature}. Works such as \cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} learn rapid linear transforms using butterfly parameterizations. Additionally, \cite{chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} leverage butterfly matrices to facilitate efficient neural network training. Butterfly structures also prove advantageous in fast matrix-vector multiplication~\cite{michielssen1996multilevel,o2010algorithm}, data-sparse matrix approximations~\cite{li2015butterfly}, and network communication~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting,li2003linear,rajasingh2016transmission}. Unlike prior studies, our focus lies in exploiting butterfly structures to improve the parameter efficiency of OFT.

\section{An Information Transmission View on Orthogonal Finetuning}

We begin with some basic concepts of OFT. To fine-tune the pretrained weight matrix, OFT re-expresses the new weight matrix as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the fixed pretrained weight matrix. Unlike LoRA, which updates weights by adding a low-rank matrix, OFT employs a multiplicative orthogonal matrix for weight updates. For parameter efficiency, LoRA utilizes a low-rank structure, whereas the original OFT applies a block-diagonal orthogonal structure~\cite{qiu2023controlling} (the smaller the diagonal block size, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes). An intuitive comparison is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:comp_lora}. The rationale for using an orthogonal transformation to fine-tune the weight matrix is to maintain the pairwise angles between neurons~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}, thereby largely preserving the semantic knowledge gained from pretraining. Specifically, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, modifying the forward computation from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ represent the input and output vectors, respectively. To initialize at zero change, OFT sets $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix initially. To maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ during fine-tuning, we adopt the Cayley parameterization as in \cite{qiu2023controlling,liu2021orthogonal}, i.e., $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. To enhance parameter efficiency, the block-diagonal structure is incorporated by representing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathcal{R}^{b\times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix for all $i$, with $br=d$. The parameter savings from this block-diagonal form come with a trade-off: it imposes the assumption that the components of a neuron (i.e., columns of $\bm{W}^0$) are split into $r$ groups, with each group transformed independently by distinct orthogonal matrices. Although the block-diagonal approach has demonstrated empirical success, dividing neuron dimensions into groups based solely on their indices lacks theoretical justification, motivating the desirability of dense orthogonal matrices. This leads to a pertinent question: \emph{Is it possible to construct a dense orthogonal matrix without sacrificing parameter efficiency?}

To tackle this problem, we suggest constructing a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse orthogonal matrices. To offer a coherent and intuitive framework for examining the sparsity structure in orthogonal matrix factorization, we reformulate the task of producing a dense orthogonal matrix in OFT as an information transmission challenge. Concretely, producing a dense matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ through the product of $m$ square matrices $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be interpreted as transmitting information across a grid with $d\times (m+1)$ nodes, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}. This information transmission viewpoint arises from recognizing that a $d$-dimensional dense square matrix can be seen as a dense connectivity map from $d$ nodes to another $d$ nodes. For matrix $\bm{R}$, if the entry $R_{ij}$ equals zero, it signifies that information from the $j$-th node cannot reach the $i$-th node; conversely, a nonzero $R_{ij}$ indicates that transmission is possible. Hence, representing the dense matrix $\bm{R}$ by a product of matrices $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be interpreted as executing sequential information exchanges governed by the graphs defined by $\bm{B}_i, \forall i$. Information propagates starting with $\bm{B}_1$ and ending with $\bm{B}_m$. For instance, in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}, we examine the factorization $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, whose sparsity patterns and corresponding graph are illustrated. The graph shown results from unfolding the matrix multiplication. Within the induced graph, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is regarded as the connectivity matrix from nodes at level $i$ to nodes at level $i+1$. More precisely, the element $(j_1,j_2)$ of $\bm{B}_i$ indicates the presence of a directed edge from the $j_2$-th node at level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node at level $i+1$ (with zero implying absence of such an edge). For the product $\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ to yield a dense matrix, each node at the first level must be able to transmit information to all nodes at the sixth level. When considering only $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, corresponding to source nodes at the first level and receiver nodes at the fifth level, it is observed that information from node $1$ cannot be sent to node $3$. Consequently, the sparsity pattern of $\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ exhibits a zero at position $(3,1)$. The same relationship between the induced graph and sparsity pattern holds for $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$. More generally, for a matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to be dense, the $m$ factor matrices $\bm{B}_m,\cdots,\bm{B}_1$ must define a set of directed edges on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid, where each directed edge connects only nodes between adjacent levels (i.e., columns), such that information from every node at the first level can be conveyed to every node at the $(m+1)$-th level.

The perspective of information transmission aids in better comprehending the sparsity pattern present in the factorization matrices of OFT. Figure~\ref{fig:blk_diag} illustrates the block-diagonal configuration of $\bm{R}$ in the original OFT. Although this block-diagonal form reduces the number of trainable parameters, it cannot produce a dense matrix $\bm{R}$. Our objective is to construct a dense orthogonal matrix by combining $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices, utilizing as few effective trainable parameters as feasible. From the information transmission standpoint, the general criteria for achieving this are \emph{(i)} \textbf{dense connectivity}: each node at the initial level should have at least one path to every node at the final level, and \emph{(ii)} \textbf{minimum free edges}: the total count of edges should be minimized while satisfying the orthogonality constraint. It is important to recognize that orthogonality imposes a subtle restriction on edges between adjacent levels. For instance, for any matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be full-rank (a prerequisite for orthogonality), there must be $d$ edges creating a bijection between all nodes at the $i$-th level and all nodes at the $(i+1)$-th level, implying that the number of edges between neighboring levels is at least $d$ (\emph{e.g.}, 4 in the example from Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}). These $d$ edges are essential for orthogonality and should not be counted toward the total edge count since they are not trainable (\emph{e.g.}, in a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with $d$ non-zero elements, these entries can only be $\pm 1$). Since orthogonal matrices require fewer parameters than full matrices, the orthogonality constraint introduces additional dependencies among the edges. For example, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at the $(1,1)$ position implies another zero at the $(2,2)$ position (\emph{i.e.}, one absent edge may imply another missing edge). Thus, for each viable set of edge connections, orthogonality can sometimes add or eliminate certain edges. Visualizing the non-zero pattern of the composed orthogonal matrix makes the information transmission framework particularly advantageous in OFT, as the focus is solely on the non-zero trainable elements of $\bm{R}$, with their exact values being irrelevant.

A straightforward dense connection between two levels requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges (i.e., a single dense orthogonal matrix), resulting in $d^2 - d$ edges (for $d=4$, this equals 12 edges). Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} illustrates an example of a valid matrix factorization that uses a total of $10$ edges, which is actually fewer than that of a single dense orthogonal matrix. This approach allows us to examine the parameter-efficiency of OFT through a graphical lens, making it easy to devise valid factorizations within this framework. We take inspiration from an intriguing topology derived from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, known as butterfly graphs~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, which can effectively connect $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. For instance, the topology shown in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} requires 10 edges to achieve dense connectivity, whereas the butterfly network achieves this with just 8 edges. In the following, we explain how the butterfly structure can enhance parameter efficiency.

\section{Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization}

Originally, the butterfly structure was employed in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm to execute the fast Fourier transform. In the Fourier transform, a local modification in the frequency domain can induce a global change in the spatial domain, which conceptually parallels our information transmission problem—each node at the first level can relay information to all nodes at the final level. The butterfly structure is also a widely adopted computer network topology~\cite{solihin2015fundamentals,li2011linear}, prized for its efficient information exchange. Assuming that $k \geq 2$ is a power of 2, we first define the \emph{butterfly factor} $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:but_factor}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix}
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ for all $i$. Given $d = 2^N$, we then define the $d$-dimensional \emph{butterfly matrix} $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ recursively as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
    \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
    \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, d) \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, \frac{d}{2}) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})$ and $\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})$ represent two butterfly matrices each of dimension $\frac{d}{2}$. We then introduce the \emph{butterfly component} defined as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$, which is a block-diagonal matrix sized $d \times d$ with block dimension $k$, where each $\bm{B}^F_i(k), \forall i$, corresponds to butterfly factors specified in Equation~\ref{eq:but_factor}. With this setup, we are prepared to use the butterfly matrix for parameterizing an orthogonal matrix. This requires ensuring that every multiplicative factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k), \forall k$ within the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is orthogonal. Starting with the block-diagonal matrix $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ of block size 2, orthogonality can be straightforwardly achieved by applying the Cayley transform (or a $2$-dimensional rotation) to parameterize each block, following the approach in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}. Since the non-zero structure of any butterfly component corresponds to a permutation of the non-zero structure in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, all butterfly components can thus be conveniently parameterized as orthogonal matrices. This provides an effective way to parameterize orthogonal matrices using numerous $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices. Extending the concept, inspired by \cite{chen2022pixelated}, we define a block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each element in $\bm{d}_i, \forall i$ is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. To ensure that the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ remains orthogonal, each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix is parameterized to be orthogonal. The non-zero structure of other butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k > 2$ corresponds to block-wise permutations of the non-zero pattern in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, and hence they can be transformed into orthogonal matrices similarly. Putting these elements together, the forward pass in BOFT is given by

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}\nonumber
    \bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},~~~~\text{s.t.}~\bigg{\{}\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}~~\&~~\underbrace{\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\!=\bm{I}_d}_{\forall j\in [1, m]}\bigg{\}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where for simplicity we denote $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m - i+1})$ as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, and $\bm{I}_d$ represents the $d \times d$ identity matrix. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is formed as a product of multiple orthogonal butterfly factors. For ease of notation, we refer to BOFT with $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m$, $b$), where $b \geq 2$. When $m=1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) simplifies to the block-diagonal OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling} with block size $b$. Similarly, BOFT($1$,$d$) corresponds to the original OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, featuring an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. The BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$, $b$) uses the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$, producing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. Generally, BOFT($m$, $b$) involves $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ effective trainable parameters when fine-tuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$. If the butterfly matrix is employed, that is, $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. In contrast, the original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and the block-diagonal OFT with block count $r$ uses $\mathcal{O}(b d)$. Therefore, while the original OFT must set the block size to $b = d$ to produce a dense orthogonal matrix, BOFT allows any $b$ to achieve this.

\textbf{Identity initialization for BOFT}. Fine-tuning techniques generally begin from the pretrained model exactly, ensuring the fine-tuned model does not stray significantly from the original. For example, LoRA initializes its low-rank weights to zero. In BOFT, all butterfly components are initialized as identity matrices, i.e., the skew-symmetric matrices are initialized to zero in the Cayley transform.

\textbf{Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT}. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} applies a Dropout layer to the low-rank weight updates to mitigate overfitting. Traditional Dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} naturally fits LoRA but is not suitable for BOFT because of its multiplicative weight updates. To resolve this, we introduce multiplicative Dropout tailored for BOFT. Since the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ consists of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, which can be rearranged into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, the multiplicative Dropout randomly selects $p_1$ percent of butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of diagonal blocks within each component, replacing these blocks with identity matrices.

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{Expressivity of BOFT}. The butterfly structure combined with permutations is capable of exactly recovering numerous classical fast linear transforms~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} (such as the fast Fourier transform and Hadamard transform). However, the extent to which our orthogonal butterfly matrix can approximate a general orthogonal matrix remains unclear. To investigate this, we perform a simulation to approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix~\cite{anderson1987generation} of size $512 \times 512$. The results displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:para_eff} are averaged across 10 random seeds. The y-axis shows the approximation error, while the x-axis indicates the number of effective trainable parameters. Each curve of a given color corresponds to BOFT with a specific block size, where the leftmost point represents the error of BOFT($1$,$b$) (i.e., the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$). Generally, BOFT achieves superior parameter efficiency compared to OFT. For instance, the expressiveness of BOFT($9$,$2$) surpasses that of BOFT($1$,$16$) while requiring significantly fewer parameters. BOFT configurations with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ tend to be more parameter-efficient; for example, BOFT($6$,$4$) uses considerably fewer parameters yet attains a similar approximation error as BOFT($2$,$16$). Overall, the butterfly matrix embodies a more structured subset of the orthogonal group (in contrast to the block-diagonal structure), thereby rendering BOFT provably more expressive than OFT when both have the same block size.

\begin{theorem}[Expressivity of BOFT]\label{thm:exp_boft}
    BOFT exhibits greater expressivity than OFT with an identical block size. To approximate all orthogonal matrices of dimension $d$ using butterfly matrices, one can multiply butterfly matrices in the form $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ for all $i,j$ are butterfly matrices.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:exp_boft} indicates a straightforward extension for BOFT—the final orthogonal matrix can be generalized as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1) \bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2) \cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1) \bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$, where $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ denotes the orthogonal matrix employed in BOFT. When $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d-1$, the matrix $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ can represent the entire orthogonal group. This form of matrix composition is also referred to as the kaleidoscope hierarchy~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that increased expressivity does not necessarily guarantee improved finetuning performance; for example, full finetuning, despite its universal expressiveness, often yields suboptimal results. Striking the right balance between expressivity and regularization is critical for the generalizability of model finetuning. BOFT expands the finetuning parameter space while imposing structural priors, enabling us to achieve a better balance between expressivity and regularization.

\textbf{Spectral properties}. Orthogonal finetuning typically achieves superior spectral characteristics compared to LoRA, as it exactly maintains the spectral norm of the original pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This can be demonstrated through singular value decomposition: $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing singular values. Both OFT and BOFT apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from the left, resulting in finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which leaves the largest singular value (i.e., the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$) unchanged. Preserving this property has been shown to significantly enhance training stability and generalization~\cite{miyato2018spectral,yoshida2017spectral}. Additional interesting mathematical properties are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:sn_property}.

\textbf{Orthogonal finetuning as learning bilinear similarity}. BOFT can be expressed as learning a bilinear similarity function $\bm{w}^0_i\bm{R}\bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ denotes the $i$-th neuron (i.e., the $i$-th column vector) of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. From this perspective, BOFT learns the bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ under a strong constraint (specifically, that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal), linking it intrinsically to distance metric learning~\cite{xing2002distance} and bilinear forms~\cite{roman2005advanced}.

\textbf{Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT}. The matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT generally corresponds to a structured subgroup of the orthogonal group, which restricts the hypothesis space and thus naturally introduces an inductive bias. We posit that the structured inductive bias introduced by the butterfly factorization enhances generalization, as it shares structural patterns with many classical linear transforms~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}, including the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform. Additionally, the sparse matrix factorization employed in BOFT may contribute implicit inductive biases~\cite{gunasekar2017implicit,li2018algorithmic,liu2019neural}.

\textbf{Comparison to butterfly-based sparse training}. Numerous studies~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope,chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} have explored sparse training using butterfly parameterization. These works generally concentrate on directly reparameterizing weight matrices through the butterfly parameterization and training neural networks from the beginning. The work in \cite{dao2022monarch} examines fine-tuning pretrained weights by initially projecting them onto a variant of butterfly matrices, followed by optimizing the projected components for downstream tasks. In contrast, BOFT introduces a distinctly different fine-tuning approach that applies transformations to the weights using layer-shared weight matrices.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Limitations}

This paper presents Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a universal and parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique for foundation models that leverages the butterfly structure. The core idea to achieve enhanced parameter efficiency lies in representing a dense orthogonal matrix as the product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. To facilitate the discovery of valid matrix factorizations, we introduce a graphical information transmission framework. Within this framework, we find that the butterfly structure effectively fulfills our criteria for sparse orthogonal matrix factorization. We empirically validate the effectiveness of BOFT in fine-tuning large language models, large vision models, and text-to-image generative models. Our experimental results also highlight the superiority of BOFT as a general-purpose fine-tuning method.

Despite its empirical strengths, BOFT is not without limitations. Since the ultimate orthogonal matrix in BOFT results from the product of several orthogonal matrices, the training runtime overhead is somewhat higher compared to OFT. Addressing this increase in training time remains an open challenge. On the bright side, after the fine-tuning phase, the orthogonal matrices learned by BOFT can be directly incorporated into the pretrained model without causing any additional inference latency. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether the butterfly network represents the most efficient mechanism for information transmission. Our information transmission framework also opens the door to insights from an entirely different research area—computer networking—where the efficiency of network topologies for information transfer is extensively studied. We anticipate that alternative, more efficient network architectures could be employed for composing dense orthogonal matrices.
