\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their behavior remains challenging due to their fully unsupervised training regime. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve gathering human annotations that rank the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, which first entails training a reward model that captures human preferences and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM via reinforcement learning to maximize this learned reward while avoiding substantial deviation from the original model. In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows the optimal policy to be derived in closed form, enabling us to address the typical RLHF problem using only a straightforward classification loss. The resulting method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the need to sample from the LM during fine-tuning or conduct extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to better align with human preferences, matching or surpassing existing approaches. Importantly, fine-tuning with DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of model outputs and equals or improves the quality of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models are trained on human-generated data encompassing a diverse array of objectives, priorities, and skill levels. Some of these objectives and skills may not be ideal to replicate; for instance, although we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors to fix them, when generating code, we prefer to steer the model towards the (possibly rare) high-quality coding skills present in its training data. Likewise, we may want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a widespread misconception held by 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want it to assert that this misconception is true in half of the queries related to it! Put differently, choosing the model's \emph{desired responses and behaviors} from its broad range of \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques typically guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective utilized by these methods can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which considerably simplifies the preference learning process.

Broadly, existing approaches imbue language models with preferred behaviors by using carefully curated human preference datasets that reflect behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a vast text corpus. While the most direct method for preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high-quality responses, the most effective family of methods involves reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods train a reward model on human preference data and then apply RL to optimize a language model policy to generate outputs that receive high rewards while not deviating significantly from the original model. Although RLHF yields models with impressive conversational and coding skills, the RLHF framework is substantially more complicated than supervised learning, requiring training multiple language models and sampling from the policy during training, which incurs substantial computational expense.

In this paper, we demonstrate a method to directly optimize a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches (maximizing reward with a KL-divergence constraint) but offers a simpler implementation and more straightforward training. Conceptually, the DPO update increases the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to dispreferred ones, while incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight that avoids the model degradation observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference model (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function matches empirical preference data. However, whereas prior methods employ the preference model to create a preference loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy with respect to this learned reward, DPO applies a change of variables to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Therefore, given a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, DPO can optimize a policy by using a straightforward binary cross-entropy objective, yielding the optimal policy relative to an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data.

Our key contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated, reinforcement-learning-free algorithm for training language models based on preferences. Our experiments indicate that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of existing techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, in preference learning tasks such as sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, using language models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of progressively larger scale have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intent can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on instruction datasets paired with human-written responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process of `instruction-tuning' allows LLMs to generalize beyond the instructions seen during tuning and generally improves their usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven effective, collecting \textit{relative} human judgments on response quality is often more feasible than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets of human preferences, enhancing performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with preference data under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning algorithms, often using REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related area of research utilizes LLMs already fine-tuned for instruction following with human feedback to produce synthetic preference data targeting attributes such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak supervision from humans in the form of textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These strategies reflect a merging of two research domains: one focused on reinforcement learning to train language models across diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of leveraging relative human preferences, fine-tuning large language models via reinforcement learning continues to pose significant practical difficulties; the present work introduces a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, policy learning from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods proposed. Contextual bandit learning that relies on preferences or rankings of actions, instead of explicit rewards, is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical treatments of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with the notion of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} other policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, preference labels are obtained in an online manner, whereas in learning from human preferences, the learning typically occurs from a fixed offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns from binary preferences generated by an \textit{unknown} ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL algorithms has been developed, including those that can leverage off-policy preference data, but these commonly involve first explicitly estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) followed by optimization of this model \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly optimizes a policy to align with preferences in a single stage.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We revisit the RLHF pipeline described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This pipeline typically consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) preference sampling and reward modeling; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF commonly starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model (LM) via supervised learning on high-quality data targeting downstream tasks (such as dialogue or summarization) to obtain a model denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Phase}: In the second stage, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators, who indicate their preference for one answer, noted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the preferred and less preferred completions respectively from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences are derived from some underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly accessible. Various methods exist to model such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely used approach (although more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable when multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model asserts that the human preference distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can define a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and estimate its parameters using maximum likelihood. By framing this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic function. In the setting of language models, the function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized from the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the final transformer output to produce a single scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward function, previous works normalize rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: During reinforcement learning, the learned reward model provides feedback to the language model. Following earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is formulated as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ controls the extent of deviation from the base reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is typically the initial SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. In practice, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is also initialized to $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This constraint is crucial, as it limits the policy from straying too far from the data distribution where the reward model performs well, preserves generation diversity, and prevents collapse to a few high-reward outputs. Since language generation is discrete, this objective is not differentiable, so reinforcement learning is commonly employed. The standard procedure \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} constructs the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$ and maximizes it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Motivated by the difficulties of applying reinforcement learning methods to large-scale tasks such as language model fine-tuning, our aim is to devise a straightforward policy optimization approach that directly utilizes preference data. Unlike existing RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward and then optimize it via RL, our method employs a specific reward model parameterization that allows the extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for a reinforcement learning loop. As will be explained in detail, our main insight is to use an analytical relationship between reward functions and optimal policies, which lets us convert a loss over reward functions into a loss over policies. This change-of-variables technique avoids the need to explicitly fit a separate reward model while still optimizing in line with established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously represents both the language model and the (implicit) reward function.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the same RL objective as used in previous work, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, with a general reward function $r$. Following earlier studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it is straightforward to demonstrate that the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when we use the MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, estimating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally costly \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits practical usage of this formulation. Nevertheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Concretely, taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebra yields:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This reparameterization can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to cancel out, allowing the human preference probability to be expressed purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Hence, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry formulation satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

The full derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, similar expressions can be derived for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the preference probability now expressed in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parametrized policy $\pi_\theta$. Analogous to reward modeling (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), this leads to the following policy learning objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}

In this manner, we fit an implicit reward function using an alternative parameterization, whose optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since our approach is analogous to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from certain theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We provide a more detailed discussion of the theoretical properties of DPO and its connections to other related works in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What does the DPO update accomplish?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to parameters $\theta$ is given by:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater weight when reward estimate is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ in relation to the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for additional details). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each example is weighted according to how much higher the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ assigns to the less preferred completion, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting how incorrectly the implicit reward model ranks the outputs and incorporating the influence of the KL divergence constraint. Our experimental results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it (i.e., using a naive version without this coefficient) can lead to degeneration of the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.} The typical DPO procedure involves: 1) Sampling completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, then labeling them with human preferences to form the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) optimizing the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\pi_\text{ref}$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the target $\beta$. In practical settings, it is often preferable to use existing public preference datasets rather than generating samples and collecting new human annotations. Since these preference datasets are typically sampled using $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ when available. If $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps to reduce the distribution mismatch between the true reference distribution (which is unknown) and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ employed by DPO. Additional details on implementation and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Foundations of DPO} Here, we offer a deeper interpretation of the DPO technique, furnish theoretical support, and connect the benefits of DPO to challenges found in actor-critic methods used in RLHF (such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Essentially a Reward Model} DPO circumvents the need to explicitly fit a reward function and to perform reinforcement learning by learning the policy with a single maximum likelihood objective. Notice that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. We optimize our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a manner equivalent to the reward model optimization in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} under this reparameterization. In this section, we develop the theoretical rationale underlying this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the class of attainable reward models, and prove it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by defining an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
We define two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ to be equivalent if and only if there exists some function $f$ such that ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}

It is straightforward to verify that this relation is indeed an equivalence relation, dividing the set of reward functions into distinct classes. We can then state the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, and specifically under the Bradley-Terry model, any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Two reward functions from the same equivalence class produce the same optimal policy in the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a known under-specification issue inherent to the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this under-specification, it is generally necessary to introduce additional identifiability constraints to secure guarantees for the MLE estimates arising from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma establishes that all reward functions within a given equivalence class result in the identical optimal policy; therefore, for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any single reward function from the optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry in particular) models admits a representation via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an associated optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as per Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that an equivalent reward function in the class of $r$ can be expressed using the reparameterization above. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since the subtracted normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains a reward function within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Finally, by substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds for any reward function), we obtain $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Hence, the projection $f$ yields a member of $r$’s equivalence class in the desired form, demonstrating that the proposed reparameterization incurs no loss of generality in modeling the reward.
\end{sproof}

An alternative perspective on Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class is chosen by the DPO reparameterization, namely the reward function that satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are positive and sum up to one). Moreover, by referring to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The central insight of the DPO algorithm is that by imposing specific constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and particularly Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, one can maintain the representational capacity of reward models while making the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly analytically solvable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework can also help identify sources of instability in common actor-critic algorithms used in RLHF, such as PPO. We consider the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}, following the standard RLHF pipeline. Connections can be made to the control as inference framework \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem described in \ref{eq:RL}. We take a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} induced by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the following optimization objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective is identical to the one optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function of the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this term does not impact the optimal solution, omitting it can lead to a policy gradient with high variance, causing instability during training. We can account for the normalization term by employing a learned value function, but optimizing this can be challenging. Alternatively, previous works have normalized rewards using a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that does not necessitate any baselines.

\section{Experiments}
Here, we empirically assess DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. First, in a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how efficiently DPO balances maximizing reward and minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, compared to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Next, we evaluate DPO's performance on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue. We observe that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and also outperforms the best of $N$ sampled trajectories under a learned reward function. Before discussing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In every experiment, the algorithms acquire a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents a prefix of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with generating $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, we \textit{generate} preference pairs for this task by utilizing a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on reviews from the IMDb training set (additional details are provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post; the policy must output a summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. Consistent with previous research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preferences collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and trained with the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for RLHF. The human preference dataset was gathered by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from a distinct but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, in \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ denotes a human query that can range from astrophysics questions to relationship advice requests. The policy must generate an engaging and helpful response $y$ to the user’s query; we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a label indicating the human-preferred response. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists for this task, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model exclusively on the preferred completions to produce the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how effectively each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective within the controlled sentiment generation context, we evaluate each algorithm based on its frontier of attained reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy; this frontier is calculable since the ground-truth reward function (a sentiment classifier) is accessible. However, in real-world scenarios where the ground truth reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluation of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline comprises reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response within the test dataset. Although prior work indicates that LMs may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to existing metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. We observe that GPT-4’s judgments show strong correlation with human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding inter-human annotator agreement.

\textbf{Methods.} Besides DPO, we investigate several established methods for training language models to conform to human preferences. The simplest approach involves zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} in the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue task. We also evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ from either the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised technique is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to maximize the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{minimizing} the likelihood of $y_l$; we apply an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ to weight the 'unlikelihood' term. We further consider \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} using a reward function learned from preference data and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant leveraging the ground truth reward function available in the controlled sentiment setting. For sentiment experiments, we employ two PPO-GT implementations: an off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that normalizes rewards and tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (these modifications are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest scoring response based on a reward function trained on the preference dataset. Although this effective method separates the quality of the reward model from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for even moderate $N$ values because it requires generating $N$ completions per query at test time.

\subsection{To what extent can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective employed by standard RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward while limiting the policy's deviation from the reference policy. Consequently, when comparing different algorithms, it is important to consider both the achieved reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the expense of a much larger KL divergence is not always favorable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} displays the reward-KL tradeoff frontier for various algorithms in the sentiment task. We conduct multiple training runs for each algorithm, varying the hyperparameter controlling policy conservativeness in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). In total, this sweep encompasses 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a set of test prompts, calculating both the average reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the sum of per-timestep KL-divergences.} with respect to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. Our findings show that DPO yields the most efficient frontier by far, obtaining the highest reward while maintaining a low KL. This outcome is especially noteworthy for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO is significantly more efficient; its reward/KL tradeoff strictly surpasses that of PPO. Second, DPO produces a superior frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO has access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, automatic metrics such as ROUGE often show poor correlation with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO based on human preferences can yield more effective summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions on the test split of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate against reference completions from the test set. Completions across all methods were sampled at various temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. We observe that DPO achieves a win rate near 61\% at temperature 0.0, surpassing PPO’s best performance of around 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher peak win rate compared to the best of $N$ baseline method. It is important to note that we did not extensively tune DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these outcomes might underrepresent DPO’s full potential. Furthermore, DPO shows considerably greater robustness to changes in sampling temperature than PPO, whose performance can deteriorate to that of the original GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT does not provide a notable improvement over the SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also offer a direct human comparison of DPO and PPO, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset's test split \citep{bai2022training}, which contains one round of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations utilize the preferred completions from the test set as a reference to calculate the win rate of different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, employ Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions so that the completions lie within the model's distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. We also compare against the best of 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO achieves performance comparable to or exceeding the best-performing temperatures for each approach. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a well-known repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as a rough estimate of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands as the sole computationally efficient approach that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and delivers similar or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains its optimal performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To further assess the performance of PPO and DPO under distributional shifts, we test the PPO and DPO policies from our Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment on a different data distribution, specifically news articles from the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}, using the optimal sampling temperatures identified from TL;DR (0 and 0.25). The outcomes are shown in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. We calculated the GPT-4 win rate compared to the ground-truth summaries within these datasets, employing the same GPT-4 (C) prompt as in Reddit TL;DR, but substituting the phrase ``forum post'' with ``news article''. For this alternative distribution, DPO continues to significantly outperform the PPO policy. This experiment offers preliminary evidence that DPO policies can generalize as effectively as PPO policies, despite DPO not utilizing the additional unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts that PPO relies on.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}

We carry out a human evaluation study to confirm the dependability of GPT-4’s judgments, based on the TL;DR summarization experiment results and two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt merely requests which summary better captures the key information in the post. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires which summary is more concise; this prompt is evaluated because we observed that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries than humans do when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. The full prompts are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three pairwise comparisons involving the highest-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temp. 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temp. 0.25) in order to capture a range of sample qualities; each of these is compared against greedily-sampled PPO (its top-performing temperature). Our findings show that with both prompts, GPT-4 tends to agree with humans about as frequently as humans agree with each other, indicating that GPT-4 serves as a reasonable proxy for human assessments (due to the limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). Overall, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt generally yields win rates that better reflect human judgments; thus, we adopt this prompt for the primary results reported in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional details about the human study, including the web interface shown to raters and the list of human volunteers, are available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for training language models that are both capable and aligned. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method for learning from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning problem into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions. This allows training a language model to fulfill human preferences \textit{directly}, using a simple cross-entropy loss, without the need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or exceeding that of current RLHF methods, including those using PPO; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the difficulty of training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings prompt several important avenues for further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to models trained with an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with PPO-based models, yet more extensive analysis is required. For instance, can self-labeling training with the DPO policy similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Additionally, what forms of reward over-optimization arise in the direct preference optimization framework, and could the minor performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this? While our evaluations cover models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to state-of-the-art models of vastly larger size represents an exciting future direction. Concerning evaluations, we observe that GPT-4's win rate assessments are sensitive to the prompt used; subsequent research may focus on identifying the best techniques for eliciting high-quality judgments from automated evaluators. Finally, DPO has numerous potential applications beyond training language models from human preferences, such as training generative models in other modalities.
