\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning abilities, it remains challenging to precisely steer their outputs because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to achieve such controllability involve gathering human annotations on the comparative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better align with these human preferences, frequently utilizing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, which first requires training a reward model that approximates human judgments, and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM through reinforcement learning to maximize this predicted reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel reward model parameterization within RLHF that allows direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling us to address the standard RLHF objective using a straightforward classification loss.}  
The method we introduce, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} generating samples from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our evaluations demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences at least as well as, if not better than, existing techniques. Remarkably, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and matches or surpasses it in quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Yet, these models learn from human-produced data that reflects a diverse range of intentions, priorities, and expertise. Some of these intentions and skills might be undesirable to replicate; for instance, although we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to help fix them, when generating code, we prefer to guide the model towards exhibiting the (possibly infrequent) high-quality coding ability embedded in its training data. Likewise, we may want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a widespread misconception held by half the population, but we certainly do not want it to assert that misconception as true in half of the related queries. Put differently, choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its vast \textit{knowledge and competencies} is vital for creating AI systems that are reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed by these methods can be exactly optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training conducted on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human examples of high-quality responses, the most effective approaches rely on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on a dataset of human preferences, then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy to generate outputs with high rewards, while maintaining proximity to the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding capabilities, its training process is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, requiring multiple language models to be trained and sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, a technique that implicitly targets the same goal as traditional RLHF approaches (maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint) while being easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over less favored ones, incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight that mitigates model collapse observed when using a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that quantifies how well a reward function matches observed preference data. Nevertheless, unlike conventional approaches that use this preference model to create a loss for training a reward model and then optimize a policy accordingly, {\methodac} applies a change of variables to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Given a collection of human preference labels over model outputs, {\methodac} optimizes the policy with a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{eliminating the need to learn a separate reward model or to sample from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preference data. Experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} matches or surpasses the performance of current approaches, including PPO-driven RLHF, in preference learning tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue generation, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, especially as their scale grows, can perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals is greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-crafted responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to unseen instructions beyond the fine-tuning dataset, thereby improving their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven effective, collecting \textit{relative} human judgments regarding response quality is often more feasible than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets derived from human preferences, which has led to improved capabilities in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction-following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically begin by training a neural network reward model to align with the preference dataset, often employing a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Following this, a language model is fine-tuned to optimize the learned reward through reinforcement learning techniques, such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related strand of work utilizes LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback to generate synthetic preference data aimed at enhancing specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision via textual rubrics for the model’s annotations. These methods reflect a synthesis of two research directions: one focused on applying reinforcement learning to language model training across diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of leveraging relative human preferences, employing reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models continues to pose significant practical difficulties; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, deriving policies from preference information has been explored within both bandit frameworks and reinforcement learning paradigms, with a variety of methods proposed. When contextual bandit learning relies on preferences or action rankings instead of explicit rewards, it is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute rewards are not available in CDBs, theoretical treatment replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} competing policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, preference feedback in the CDB framework is typically provided in an online manner, whereas in preference-based learning from humans, the data usually consist of a static offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a related vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of direct reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Multiple algorithms exist for PbRL, many of which incorporate off-policy preference data reuse but generally proceed by first explicitly modeling the latent scoring function (i.e., the reward estimator) and then optimizing the policy accordingly \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our approach proposes a unified, single-step policy optimization technique that directly aligns the policy with the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently by \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by applying supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-existing language model on carefully curated data tailored to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, and others), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators who indicate their preference for one of the responses, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored completion and $y_l$ is the less favored one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, but inaccessible, latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several methods exist to model such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a widely used option (though the more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also fit within this framework when multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the human preference probability $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparison triples $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parametrize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters via maximum likelihood estimation. Treating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the setting of language models, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly initialized based on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by adding a linear layer atop the final transformer layer to output a single scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the learned reward, previous studies normalize the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been trained is utilized to guide the language model through feedback. Building on previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the parameter $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$.  
In implementation, the policy of the language model $\pi_\theta$ is typically initialized to $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial as it restricts the policy from straying excessively from the distribution where the reward model is reliable, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse to a narrow set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, the objective is not differentiable and is commonly optimized through reinforcement learning methods. The prevalent technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it via PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when implementing reinforcement learning techniques on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preferences directly. In contrast to existing RLHF approaches that first train a reward function and subsequently optimize it through RL, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly refines a language model based on preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived analytically, without requiring an RL training procedure}.  
As detailed in the following sections, our central idea is to exploit a mathematical relationship mapping reward functions to their optimal policies, thereby converting a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to bypass the explicit reward modeling stage}{removes the need to explicitly train a separate reward model}, while still performing optimization consistent with established models of human preference, such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) goal as in previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, involving a general reward function $r$. Following the methodology established in prior work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the optimal policy solving the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalizing constant $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ to approximate the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making this formulation challenging to apply practically. Nonetheless, rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} allows us to express the reward function in terms of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation, we arrive at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ along with its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the difference between the rewards of two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model of Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, enabling us to represent the human preference probabilities exclusively using the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Further details of the derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, similar derivations can be extended to the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as elaborated in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data based on the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the policy optimization objective is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{By doing so, we avoid the explicit step of modeling rewards and eliminate the necessity for reinforcement learning-based optimization.}{This approach implicitly fits a reward through a different parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because this method effectively corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits beneficial theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides a more detailed discussion on the theoretical aspects of DPO compared to related methodologies.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward prediction errs}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ is defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (additional details are provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training instance is weighted by the degree to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly ranks the less preferred completions higher, scaled by the factor $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting, as a simplified version of the algorithm that omits this weighting term can lead to degradation of the language model’s performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO process involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and annotate them with human preferences to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a target parameter $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage publicly accessible preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human judgments. Because these preference datasets are typically obtained using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a policy is available. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, formulated as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional information about implementation specifics and hyperparameter settings is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
This section offers a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, presents supporting theoretical evidence, and connects the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered in actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the need to fit a separate explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by training the policy through a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Optimizing our parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ thus aligns with optimizing the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once we apply the appropriate change of variables. This section develops the theoretical foundation of this reparameterization, demonstrating that it does not limit the set of possible learned reward models and enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation satisfies the properties of an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry variant, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an identical preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class produce the same optimal policy in the context of the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations are straightforward and thus postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma reflects a known under-specification challenge associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to secure meaningful guarantees for the MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within the same equivalence class leads to an identical optimal policy. Consequently, for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any arbitrary reward function from the class of optimal rewards. The subsequent theorem is proven in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, each reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed via the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)},
    \]
    where $\pi(y \mid x)$ denotes some model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference distribution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from $r$’s equivalence class can be represented using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ essentially normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function), yields
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, the projection $f$ maps $r$ to an equivalent reward function of the desired form, demonstrating that our proposed reparameterization does not restrict the generality of the reward model.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses, namely the reward $r$ satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y \mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
Moreover, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we recognize that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The fundamental insight of the DPO method lies in imposing suitable constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) preference models. These constraints preserve the set of representable reward functions while rendering the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically tractable for all inputs $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our framework can also be applied to identify sources of instability in common actor-critic algorithms employed for RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF workflow and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This allows us to link the problem to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation presented in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy given by Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} that arises from the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this results in the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier studies  
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which utilize a DPO-equivalent reward function within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not change the location of the optimum, omitting it can lead to a policy gradient with elevated variance, potentially destabilizing training. While one can incorporate this normalization via a learned value function, optimizing such a function can be challenging. Alternatively, prior approaches have applied reward normalization based on a human completion baseline, effectively treating it as a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward formulation that eliminates the necessity for any baseline.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to learn policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a carefully controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the objectives of maximizing reward and minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, in comparison to widely-used preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and also outperforms the best trajectory selected from $N$ samples based on a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these results, we outline the experimental setup; further information is available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experiments, the algorithms acquire a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a prefix extracted from an IMDb movie review \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal of the policy is to produce an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled assessment, this experiment involves \textit{creating} preference pairs based on generated outputs, utilizing a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we adapt GPT-2-large on the training portion of the IMDb reviews until convergence (additional information is provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ corresponds to a Reddit forum post, and the policy must generate a concise summary $y$ capturing the post’s key ideas. Building on previous research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} paired with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} designed for RLHF. The human preference data was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from outputs generated by a different, yet similarly fine-tuned, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query that can range from scientific questions about astrophysics to personal advice requests. The policy is tasked with generating a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. For this, we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k human-assistant conversations. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate replies produced by a large, though unspecified, language model, accompanied by a label indicating the human-preferred response. Because there is no pre-existing SFT model for this task, we fine-tune a standard language model exclusively on the preferred responses to build the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our study employs two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization goal within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm's trade-off between the attained reward and the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this trade-off curve is determinable since we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in real-world scenarios where the true reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, it is the preferred response from the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models can outperform conventional automated metrics as evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to support our use of GPT-4 for assessment in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal a strong correlation between GPT-4’s evaluations and human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding agreement levels among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we test zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization tasks, as well as 2-shot prompting using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue tasks. We also assess the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned through supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization scenarios) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another related approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing that of $y_l$, controlled by an optional parameter $\alpha \in [0,1]$ that scales the unlikelihood term. Additionally, we examine \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} optimized with a reward function learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method trained using the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment setting. For sentiment-related experiments, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf variant \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost performance (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top-scoring output based on a reward model trained on preference data. While this approach often achieves strong results by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ due to the need to sample $N$ completions for each query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels at different temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider not only the obtained reward but also the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for various algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. We conduct multiple training sessions per algorithm, each with a distinct hyperparameter controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on a collection of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO achieves the most effective frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining low KL. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates substantially greater efficiency, with its reward/KL trade-off strictly outperforming that of PPO. Second, DPO surpasses PPO’s frontier even when PPO is provided access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO in the contexts of summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization tasks, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO with human preferences results in higher-quality summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions on the TL;DR summarization test set and calculating the average win rate against reference completions. Completions across all methods are sampled at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all applied to fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. The findings reveal that DPO attains a win rate near 61\% at temperature 0.0, surpassing PPO’s peak performance of approximately 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO outperforms the best baseline from the set of $N$ comparisons in terms of maximum win rate. It is important to highlight that we performed minimal tuning of DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these outcomes might undervalue its true capability. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can deteriorate to the baseline GPT-J model’s level at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT, meanwhile, shows little improvement over the original SFT model. A direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogues, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on one round of human-assistant exchange. GPT-4 evaluations utilize the preferred completions from the test set as a reference to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model and apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions, ensuring the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, followed by training with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the best results from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings show that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each approach. We also test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing on our TL;DR results and the fact that both techniques optimize the same reward, we treat the Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that surpasses the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving comparable or superior results to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Comparing GPT-4 Evaluations with Human Assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a human evaluation to assess the accuracy of GPT-4's judgments, leveraging data from the TL;DR summarization task alongside two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly requests identification of which summary better captures the key information in the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally asks which summary is more succinct; this prompt is tested because we observe that GPT-4 tends to favor longer and more repetitive summaries under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, unlike human preferences. Complete prompt texts are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three comparisons involving the top-performing method (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temperature 0.25), aiming to capture a range of summary qualities. Each of these is contrasted against greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature setting. Our results indicate that across both prompts, GPT-4's agreement with human raters is comparable to the level of agreement found between humans themselves, implying GPT-4 can serve as a reasonable stand-in for human evaluation (note that due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were gathered only for the DPO and PPO-1 pairings). On the whole, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that align more closely with human preferences, and thus is employed for the principal findings in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human evaluation procedure, including the interface shown to raters and the roster of volunteers, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach to developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be optimized to align with human preferences \textit{directly} by employing a straightforward cross-entropy loss, bypassing the need for reinforcement learning and maintaining full generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or exceeding that of current RLHF techniques, including PPO-based methods; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several key questions that warrant further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on explicitly defined reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities on par with PPO-based methods, though a more thorough analysis is necessary. For instance, can self-labeling strategies derived from the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts in a similar manner? Another aspect to consider is how reward over-optimization emerges within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right might be attributed to this phenomenon. Furthermore, while our experiments involve models with up to 6 billion parameters, extending DPO to substantially larger, cutting-edge models represents a promising avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluation procedures, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt used, suggesting that subsequent work should focus on identifying optimal prompting techniques to obtain reliable automated judgments. Lastly, the applicability of DPO extends well beyond fine-tuning language models based on human preferences, encompassing the training of generative models across a variety of other data modalities.
