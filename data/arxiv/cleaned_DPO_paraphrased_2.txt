\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to achieve such control involve gathering human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better reflect these preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF is a complicated and frequently unstable process that first requires training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the LM so as to maximize this learned reward without deviating excessively from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the conventional RLHF task to be resolved using only a straightforward classification objective.}  
The new method, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency by removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} generating samples from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experimental results demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than previous techniques. Remarkably, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of outputs and matches or surpasses it in terms of response quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data, which reflects a diverse array of intentions, priorities, and expertise. Not all of these intentions or skills are desirable to replicate; for instance, while an AI coding assistant should \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to steer the model toward exhibiting the (potentially uncommon) high-quality coding skills embedded in its training examples. Similarly, a language model might need to be \textit{aware} of a misconception held by roughly 50\% of individuals, yet we certainly do not want it to assert that the misconception is correct in half of its responses regarding that topic. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad spectrum of \textit{knowledge and competencies} is essential for creating AI systems that are reliable, efficient, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated collections of human preferences that reflect the kinds of actions considered safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive textual data. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human examples of high-quality responses, the most effective approaches rely on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques train a reward model based on a dataset of human preferences and then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy to generate outputs with high reward scores, while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding performance, its training pipeline is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, requiring multiple language models to be trained and sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a method that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches (maximizing rewards while maintaining a KL-divergence constraint) but is easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of favored responses compared to less favored ones, while applying a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting that avoids the model collapse observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates how well a reward function matches actual preference data. Yet, unlike previous methods that utilize this model to create a preference loss for training a reward model and then train a policy to optimize this reward, DPO employs a change of variables to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Given a dataset of human judgments on model outputs, DPO thus enables policy optimization through a straightforward binary cross entropy loss, \rev{eliminating the need to explicitly learn a separate reward model or perform policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function derived from the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preference data. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably to, or better than, current approaches like PPO-based RLHF when learning from preferences across tasks such as sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow larger in scale are capable of performing certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal examples through few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning them on collections of instructions paired with human-crafted completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond those seen during tuning and generally improves their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven effective, gathering \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality is often simpler than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later studies have refined LLMs using datasets that reflect human preferences, leading to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques initially train a neural reward model to align with the preference dataset according to a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tune the language model to optimize this reward via reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A closely connected research direction exploits LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback on instructions to create synthetic preference datasets targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision provided through textual rubrics for the LLM’s annotations. This line of work merges two research areas: one focused on training language models using reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general approaches to learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains practically challenging; this study proposes a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language applications, learning policies based on preferences has been explored within both bandit frameworks and reinforcement learning, leading to a variety of proposed methods. When contextual bandit algorithms leverage preferences or rankings over actions instead of explicit reward signals, this is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical treatments of CDBs replace the idea of an optimal policy with the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves at least a 50\% expected win rate against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. A key distinction is that preference labels in CDB scenarios are obtained in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences generally involves training on a static dataset of offline annotated preference pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) involves learning from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several PbRL algorithms have been developed, including ones capable of utilizing off-policy preference data; however, most approaches typically first estimate the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) explicitly and then optimize the policy accordingly \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with observed preferences in a single step.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further elaborated in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This approach generally involves three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) gathering preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning (RL) based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated data related to the target downstream tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.) to produce a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators who indicate their preferred response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored completion and $y_l$ is the less favored one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preference judgments are presumed to arise from an underlying, unknown reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being widely used (though more general ranking models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} also fit within this framework when multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the probability of the human preference $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_2)\bigr)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can model a parameterized reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood objective is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. For language models, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear projection layer on top of the final transformer output, which produces a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward signal, prior studies often normalize the reward values such that the expected reward over the dataset satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning phase, the reward function that has been trained is utilized to guide the language model through feedback. Building upon previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the coefficient $\beta$ regulates the extent to which the updated policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy of the language model, $\pi_\theta$, starts from the initialization $\pisft$. This KL divergence term plays a crucial role by limiting the policy shift away from the distribution where the reward model performs reliably, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse into a narrow set of highly rewarded outputs. Since language generation is inherently discrete, this objective cannot be optimized via straightforward gradient methods and is therefore addressed through reinforcement learning techniques. The common methodology \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it with the PPO algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties encountered when implementing reinforcement learning methods on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward technique for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. Contrary to previous RLHF approaches that first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it through RL, our method \rev{eliminates the need for a reward modeling phase and directly refines the language model based on preference data}{employs a specific reward model parameterization that permits direct derivation of the optimal policy without requiring an RL training process}.  

As will be explained in more detail shortly, our main insight is to exploit an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, which allows us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  

This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to omit the separate reward modeling step}{prevents the necessity of training a distinct, explicit reward model}, while still working within established frameworks for human preference modeling, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward function.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as established in previous studies, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, involving a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy maximizing the KL-constrained reward in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_y \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Although we might substitute the true reward $r^*$ with its MLE estimate $r_\phi$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits practical application. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward $r$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization constant $Z(\cdot)$. Taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and simplifying yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the difference between rewards of two outputs, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model of Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition functions cancel out. Consequently, the human preference probability can be expressed exclusively in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Hence, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies the following preference model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The detailed proof is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} focuses on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations hold for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the likelihood of human preference data through the optimal policy instead of relying on the reward model, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{By doing so, we eliminate the need for explicit reward modeling and circumvent reinforcement learning-based optimization.}{This approach allows us to implicitly learn a reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides a deeper exploration of DPO’s theoretical aspects and its connection to related literature.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly determined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (additional details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to raise the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering that of less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the influence of each example is scaled by the degree to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the reward for the less preferred outputs, modulated by $\beta$. This scaling reflects how inaccurately the implicit reward model ranks the outputs, taking into account the strength of the KL regularization. Our experiments highlight the significance of this weighting, as a straightforward variant of the method lacking this weighting factor can lead to the collapse of the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then use human preference labels to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference model $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of creating new samples and collecting human annotations. Because these preference datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever it is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy reduces the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the surrogate $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional details regarding implementation and hyperparameter choices are available in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper insight into the DPO approach, present its theoretical foundations, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered by actor-critic algorithms commonly employed in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{estimating a reward function directly} and reinforcement learning by training the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model parameterized by the reward function $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$, and that optimizing our parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is effectively equivalent to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after applying a suitable variable transformation. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the space of reward functions that can be learned, and prove that it enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class generate the same optimal policy when applied to the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations are straightforward and thus postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a common under-specification challenge associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to ensure meaningful guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimation derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma confirms that all reward functions within a given equivalence class lead to an identical optimal policy, implying that for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from the optimal class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and particularly the Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}
    \]
    for some policy model \(\pi(y\mid x)\) and a specified reference policy \(\piref(y \mid x)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function \(r(x, y)\), which defines a corresponding optimal policy \(\pi_r(y \mid x)\) as given in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of \(r\) can be represented using the stated reparameterization. Define the mapping \(f\) as:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, the operator \(f\) normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to \(\pi_r\). Since this normalization term depends solely on the prefix \(x\), the function \(f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)\) remains within the equivalence class of the original reward \(r(x, y)\). Finally, substituting \(r\) with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}—which holds universally for any reward function—yields:
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Therefore, the projection \(f\) produces a reward function in the equivalence class of \(r\) with the desired parameterization, confirming that adopting this reparameterization does not restrict the generality of our reward model.
\end{sproof}

Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as pinpointing the particular reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization within each equivalence class, namely the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y \mid x) \text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which implies that \(\pi(y \mid x)\) forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
Observing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy generated by the reward function \(r(x, y)\).
The main insight behind the DPO algorithm is that it imposes specific constraints on the inherently under-constrained Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, thereby preserving the class of representable reward functions while ensuring that the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically manageable for every prompt \(x\).

\subsection{Instability in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework also enables the identification of instability issues present in common actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure, concentrating specifically on the reinforcement learning fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained reinforcement learning problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}, we consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid\mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy derived in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} based on the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this leads us to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective aligns with those optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which utilize a DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not alter the final optimal policy, omitting it can result in policy gradient estimates exhibiting high variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One way to handle this normalization term is by employing a learned value function, though this can introduce additional optimization challenges. Alternatively, previous approaches have performed reward normalization via a human completion baseline, effectively using a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization constant. In contrast, the DPO reformulation produces a reward structure that obviates the need for such baseline corrections.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to directly train policies from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the maximization of reward against the minimization of KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, especially when compared to standard preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks—including RLHF with PPO and selecting the top trajectory from $N$ samples under a learned reward—while requiring minimal hyperparameter tuning. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information is available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. In every experiment, the algorithms acquire a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ represents the beginning portion of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with producing a continuation $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation, we \textit{generate} preference pairs by comparing outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is trained to convergence on training reviews from the IMDb dataset (additional details are provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the case of \textbf{summarization}, the input $x$ consists of a Reddit forum post, and the policy must produce a summary $y$ highlighting the key points of the post. Building on previous work, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-authored forum summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The human preference annotations were obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from a different, though similarly trained, SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, the input $x$ is a user query that may range from questions on astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. The goal is for the policy to generate a response $y$ that is both engaging and informative. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k interactions between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue ends with two responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, along with a label indicating the human-preferred reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available in this scenario, we fine-tune a pre-existing language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm's performance by its frontier of obtained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be determined since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons use reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response recorded in the test dataset. Although prior work indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate our reliance on GPT-4 for evaluation through a human study described in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show a strong correlation between GPT-4 evaluations and human assessments, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding the level of agreement observed among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. We also test the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach employing pseudo-supervised learning is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this includes an optional scaling factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood term. Additionally, we examine \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained using a reward function learned from preference annotations, along with \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment scenario. For the sentiment experiments, we utilize two PPO-GT implementations: a standard off-the-shelf variant \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are also applied when running conventional PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidates from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top response according to a reward model trained on preference data. Although this method achieves strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even for moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ outputs per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left panel:} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among all approaches, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right panel:} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout the training process. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels during training across different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward with limiting the policy’s divergence from a reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; a marginally higher reward paired with a substantially larger KL divergence may not be advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier across several algorithms within the sentiment task. We conduct multiple training experiments per algorithm, varying the hyperparameters that control policy conservativeness (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, along with different random seeds for preferred-FT). This grid search totals 22 runs. At intervals of every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts by calculating the average reward under the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of KL divergences per timestep} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO generates the most efficient frontier, securing the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, with a reward/KL balance that consistently outperforms PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO’s frontier even when PPO has direct access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes models for summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that using PPO to fine-tune language models on human preferences can yield higher-quality summaries. Our evaluation compares several approaches by generating completions from the TL;DR summarization test set and calculating the average win rate relative to reference completions. For each method, completions are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models fine-tuned with DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the identical GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO achieves about a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s peak performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO surpasses the highest win rate attained by the best of $N$ baseline method. It is worth mentioning that we did not extensively optimize the $\beta$ parameter for DPO, so these findings may underestimate its full capabilities. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose performance can deteriorate to the level of the original GPT-J model when the temperature is raised. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, shows minimal improvement over the SFT baseline. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO through human evaluations is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO-generated samples at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

In the context of single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which involves one exchange between human and assistant. For GPT-4 evaluations, we employ the preferred completions on the test set as references to calculate the win rates across methods. Since there is no established supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We then apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions, ensuring that the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, followed by further training with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline stabilizes at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the base Pythia-2.8B, finding that DPO matches or surpasses performance at optimal temperatures for each method. We also test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a recognized repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature yielding better results than the baseline Pythia-2.8B. Drawing from our TL;DR findings and the fact that both approaches optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient technique that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Confirming GPT-4 Evaluations with Human Assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the accuracy of GPT-4’s evaluations, we carry out a human study based on the TL;DR summarization task, employing two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt is straightforward, asking which summary better captures the key information from the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt additionally requests identification of the more concise summary. This second prompt was tested because we observed that, using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to favor lengthier, more repetitive summaries compared to human preferences. Complete prompt details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Our evaluation involves three pairwise comparisons: the best-performing model (DPO, temperature 0.25), the worst-performing model (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate model (SFT, temperature 0.25), chosen to represent a range of summary qualities. Each is contrasted with greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Results indicate that GPT-4’s judgments, across both prompts, align with human judgments to a similar degree as humans agree among themselves, implying that GPT-4 serves as a reliable stand-in for human evaluation. Due to limited availability of human raters, multiple human annotations were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons. In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt produces win rates that more closely reflect human decisions; hence, this prompt is used for the principal findings reported in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information on the human evaluation setup, including the interface used by raters and the participant list, is available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and well-aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that leverages preferences to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing preference learning into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows training language models to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and preserving generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of existing RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches, significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings open up several key questions that warrant further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to methods that rely on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize at a level comparable to PPO-based approaches, but a more thorough analysis is necessary. For instance, is it possible to leverage self-labeling techniques with the DPO policy to effectively utilize unlabeled prompts? Another aspect to explore is how reward over-optimization presents itself within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest drop in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right might be attributed to this phenomenon. Moreover, although our experiments cover models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models remains an intriguing avenue for future work. Concerning evaluations, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by the choice of prompt; hence, future studies could focus on optimizing prompts to obtain more reliable automated assessments. Lastly, DPO has potential applications beyond aligning language models with human preferences, such as training generative models across different modalities.
