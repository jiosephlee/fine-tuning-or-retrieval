\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning capabilities; however, precisely guiding their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to steer these models involve gathering human annotations on the relative quality of generated outputs and fine-tuning the original LM to align with these preferences, frequently utilizing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Yet, RLHF is often intricate and unstable, requiring an initial step to train a reward model that captures human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the LM to maximize the predicted reward while maintaining closeness to the initial model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward function within RLHF that allows direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby transforming the standard RLHF procedure into a straightforward classification task.}  
Our approach, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency by removing the necessity of \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences as effectively or better than current techniques. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling sentiment in generated text and achieves comparable or improved quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue responses, while being notably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects diverse aims, values, and skill levels. Some of these aims and skills might not be appropriate to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors in order to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to guide the model toward the (often scarce) examples of high-quality coding found in its training material. Similarly, we may want the language model to be \textit{aware} of a misconception held by roughly half of the population, but we certainly do not want it to assert this falsehood as fact in half of the cases when asked about it. Put differently, choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad range of \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches commonly use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed by these methods can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches embed desired behaviors into a language model by utilizing carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This stage of preference learning follows an initial phase of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a large corpus of text. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-generated examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective techniques rely on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference data and then apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward, while ensuring the model does not diverge too much from its original parameters. Despite yielding models with strong conversational and coding performance, the RLHF workflow is notably more intricate than supervised fine-tuning, requiring the training of multiple language models and continuous sampling from the policy during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly tailor a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as traditional RLHF approaches—maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint—while being both easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of favored responses over disfavored ones, incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid the model collapse observed when using a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that assesses how well a candidate reward function fits actual preference data. Unlike conventional approaches that employ this preference model to create a loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy on that learned reward, {\methodac} performs a change of variables to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Consequently, given human preference data on model outputs, {\methodac} can optimize the policy with a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring an explicit, separate reward model or policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data}.

The primary contribution of our work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), an uncomplicated algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models using preference data. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably to, or better than, current approaches—such as PPO-based RLHF—in preference-based learning across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, employing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow larger in scale have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond those seen during fine-tuning, thereby improving their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been successful, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality is often simpler than gathering expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, which has boosted performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with the preference dataset, typically employing a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Afterwards, the language model is fine-tuned to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning techniques, such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A closely related avenue involves leveraging instruction-tuned LLMs with human feedback to create synthetic preference data targeting specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak supervision via text-based rubrics for LLM annotations. These strategies represent a fusion of two research streams: one focusing on training language models through reinforcement learning across diverse objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another emphasizing general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of using relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical obstacle; the present work offers a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without employing RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple methods. When contextual bandit algorithms learn from preferences or rankings over actions instead of explicit rewards, this setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute reward values are unavailable, theoretical treatments of CDB replace the concept of an optimal policy with the idea of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy that achieves an expected win probability of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, in the CDB framework, preference feedback is collected sequentially online, whereas preference-based learning from humans generally relies on a static batch of offline annotated preference pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) involves learning from binary preference signals generated by an \textit{unknown} latent ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been proposed, some of which can leverage off-policy preference data, but they commonly proceed by first estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) explicitly and then optimizing the policy based on this estimate \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. Contrastingly, we introduce a unified policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to conform to observed preferences in a single stage.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This procedure generally consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by applying supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-existing LM using high-quality datasets tailored for the specific downstream tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference for one of the answers, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored completion and $y_l$ is the less preferred one among $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several techniques exist for modeling such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely adopted method (though more general ranking models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied if multiple ranked outputs are available). The BT model defines the probability of human preference $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison triplets $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit it by maximizing the likelihood. By interpreting this as a binary classification task, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model applications, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ typically starts from the pretrained SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, enhanced by a linear projection layer added on top of the last transformer block to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous work normalizes the reward predictions so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ holds for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been learned is employed to guide the language model's updates. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the coefficient $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized to match $\pisft$. This KL divergence penalty is crucial as it limits the policy’s drift away from the distribution on which the reward model performs reliably, helps preserve the variety in generated outputs, and avoids the collapse into producing a narrow set of high-reward responses. Because language generation is inherently discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and thus is generally optimized through reinforcement learning methods. The prevalent strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preference data directly. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it via reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly trains the language model with preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived analytically, without requiring an RL training loop}. 

As will be elaborated shortly, our main idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, permitting the conversion of a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to forgo explicit reward modeling}{circumvents the need to fit a separate, explicit reward model} while still optimizing according to established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously serves as both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as used in previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a general reward function $r$. Following the approaches in earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution optimizing the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the partition function is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ to approximate the true reward function $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally expensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, rendering this form challenging to implement practically. Nevertheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulations, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the difference between rewards of two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging in the reparameterized form of $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model (Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}), the partition functions cancel out, allowing the human preference probability to be expressed purely via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The detailed proof is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived under the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the probability of human preference data with respect to the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling method (refer to Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach enables us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward function and eliminates the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{By adopting this alternative parameterization, we implicitly fit a reward whose optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical insights into DPO, especially in comparison with related methods, are provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ serves to increase the likelihood of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of the less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the reward of the disfavored completion relative to the preferred one, scaled by $\beta$; in other words, it reflects how inaccurately the implicit reward ranks the pairs, incorporating the impact of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it—i.e., using a naive version without this coefficient—can lead to deterioration of the language model's performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completion samples $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate them with human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and a specified parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these preference datasets are typically derived from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not available, we initialize $\piref$ by performing maximum likelihood estimation over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, that is, by defining ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy reduces the distributional discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ utilized in DPO. Additional details on the implementation and tuning parameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, present its theoretical justification, and connect the benefits of DPO to challenges found in actor-critic methods applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by utilizing a single maximum likelihood objective to train the policy. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Optimizing our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ thus aligns with the reward model training described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once a change of variables is applied. In this part, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the set of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables perfect retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation satisfies the properties of an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate an identical optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a well-recognized under-identification problem associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is often necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to ensure guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions from a given equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy; therefore, when optimizing our final objective, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from that optimal class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under relatively mild assumptions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (including the Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a given reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an associated optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function in the equivalence class of $r$ can be rewritten using the aforementioned reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, the transformed reward $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original reward $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward), we obtain 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Thus, the projection $f$ maps $r$ onto a function of the desired form within the same equivalence class, confirming that the proposed reparameterization retains full generality in representing reward functions.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as characterizing the particular reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization within each equivalence class, namely the function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y \mid x) \text{ by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y \mid x)$ defines a valid probability distribution (non-negative and sums to one).
Moreover, as seen from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, the expression in Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The central insight behind the DPO algorithm is that by enforcing specific constraints on the otherwise under-specified Plackett-Luce (and particularly Bradley-Terry) preference model family, we maintain the expressiveness of representable reward models while making the optimal policy given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically accessible for every input $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our framework can also be employed to analyze the instability observed in common actor-critic methods applied in RLHF, like PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This allows us to relate the problem to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we have a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, and we aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy derived from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} based on the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}  
This objective matches those optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} where the reward $r_{\phi}$ belongs to the DPO-equivalent class. Within this framework, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not influence the optimal policy directly, omitting it may lead to policy gradients with high variance, thereby causing instability during training. While it is possible to estimate the normalization term using a learned value function, this approach may also be challenging to optimize effectively. Alternatively, some prior methods normalize rewards by subtracting a human completion baseline, which essentially serves as a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of this normalization term. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the necessity for such baseline adjustments.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly based on preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing rewards against minimizing the KL-divergence from the reference policy, in comparison to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming the best among $N$ sampled trajectories evaluated by a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In every experiment, the algorithms are trained to learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, the input $x$ consists of a prefix extracted from a movie review within the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ that exhibits a positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, this task involves \textit{creating} preference pairs based on the generated outputs by employing a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we train GPT-2-large until convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is expected to generate a summary $y$ capturing the post’s key points. Consistent with previous research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The human preference data was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from samples produced by a similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ represents a human query, which can range from astrophysics questions to requests for advice on relationships. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. We leverage the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k exchanges between a human and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two alternative responses generated by a large (but unspecified) language model, along with a preference label indicating the response favored by humans. Since there is no pre-existing SFT model for this task, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective, within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm's performance by examining the frontier of obtained reward values and the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be computed since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluation of summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set, whereas for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response found in the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation through a human study described in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate that GPT-4’s judgments align closely with human evaluations, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 often matching or exceeding the consistency observed among human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate multiple established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we assess zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue task. We also examine the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (used in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another approach resembling supervised learning is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method optionally incorporates a coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ to weight the ‘unlikelihood’ component. Additionally, we evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} employing a reward function trained on preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that utilizes the ground truth reward in the controlled sentiment context. For our sentiment experiments, we implement two PPO-GT variants: an off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost performance (these adjustments are also applied to the standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we explore the \textbf{Best of $N$} strategy, which involves sampling $N$ completions from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the response with the highest reward score based on a model trained on preference data. Although this approach achieves strong results by separating the reward model’s quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among the methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. The gains achieved by DPO over the dataset labels remain relatively consistent during training at different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward with limiting how much the policy diverges from a reference policy. Hence, when evaluating different algorithms, it is essential to consider both the reward attained and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of substantially increased KL divergence may not be favorable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-KL trade-off frontier across various algorithms in the sentiment analysis task. We perform multiple training experiments per algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameters for each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT), totaling 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is assessed on a set of test prompts, calculating the mean reward based on the true reward function alongside the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the sum of per-step KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi \mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO achieves the most effective frontier by delivering the highest reward while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates notably greater efficiency; its reward versus KL balance clearly surpasses PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO is given access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on actual preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes models for tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO using human preference data yields improved summaries. Our evaluation compares various approaches by generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset, then calculating the average win rate relative to the reference completions in the test set. For all methods, completions are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT methods all fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. We observe that at a sampling temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO, which reaches around 57\% at its optimal temperature of 0.0. Furthermore, DPO achieves a superior peak win rate compared to the best of $N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, implying that these results might undervalue its true capability. Additionally, DPO demonstrates substantially greater stability with respect to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can fall back to that of the base GPT-J model when the temperature is increased. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the original SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also conduct direct human comparisons between DPO and PPO, finding that DPO-generated samples at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which involves a single round of human-assistant exchange. Evaluations with GPT-4 rely on the preferred completions from the test set as a benchmark to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure that completions align with the model’s distribution, and subsequently fine-tune using DPO. We also benchmark against the top completion among 128 Preferred-FT outputs (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or exceeds performance at the optimal temperatures for each method. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that enhances performance beyond the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering results comparable to or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 Evaluations Against Human Judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the trustworthiness of GPT-4’s evaluations, we carried out a human study leveraging outcomes from the TL;DR summarization task and two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly asks which summary better captures the key points of the post. Meanwhile, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally requests which summary is more succinct; this variant was tested because we observed that, unlike humans, GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more redundant summaries when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. Full prompt details are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We carried out three sets of comparisons, involving the best-performing model (DPO, temperature 0.25), the worst-performing one (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate model (SFT, temperature 0.25), to ensure a range of sample qualities; each was benchmarked against greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Our findings indicate that for both prompts, GPT-4’s agreement with human judgments is on par with inter-human agreement, implying GPT-4 can serve as a credible stand-in for human evaluation (due to the limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that better reflect human preferences, and as such, it is used for the main analyses in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. For further information on the human evaluation procedure, including the interface shown to participants and a list of volunteers, refer to Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach to developing capable and aligned language models. We present DPO, a straightforward training method that leverages preference data to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply existing RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the model to be optimized to align with human preferences using a simple cross-entropy loss, bypassing reinforcement learning and without compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustments, DPO achieves performance on par with or surpassing current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches, thereby significantly lowering the complexity of training language models from human preference data.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several critical questions to be addressed in subsequent research. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on explicitly defined reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities similar to those of PPO-based methods, but a more thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, is it possible that self-labeling with the DPO policy can also leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Another area to explore is the phenomenon of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization framework—could the slight performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this? Moreover, although our experiments cover models up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models that are significantly larger remains a promising avenue for future exploration. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s computed win rates are influenced by the choice of prompt, suggesting that further work is needed to determine the optimal way to obtain reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s potential extends beyond tuning language models with human preferences and may include training generative models across various modalities.
