\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches for achieving such control involve gathering human judgments on the comparative quality of model outputs and fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, frequently using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, which first involves training a reward model to approximate human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large LM so as to maximize this surrogate reward while preventing significant deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits direct extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, thereby reducing the standard RLHF task to a straightforward classification loss.}  
The new method, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity of \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our evaluations demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune language models to align with human preferences at least as well as current techniques. In particular, DPO-based fine-tuning outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text, and matches or surpasses existing methods in response quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being significantly easier to implement and train.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects diverse intentions, priorities, and expertise levels. Not all of these intentions or skills are necessarily beneficial to replicate; for instance, while it is helpful for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to guide the model toward exhibiting the (often rare) high-quality coding proficiency found in its training examples. Similarly, a language model might need to be \textit{familiar} with a widespread misconception held by roughly 50\% of people, yet we certainly do not want the model to assert this falsehood as fact in half of the instances it is queried about it. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While prevailing approaches generally rely on reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad perspective, current techniques embed the preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences, which reflect the kinds of behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This phase of preference learning follows an initial step of extensive unsupervised pre-training conducted on a vast corpus of text. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using high-quality human demonstration data, the most effective approaches utilize reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference datasets and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward scores, while ensuring the model does not deviate substantially from its original behavior. Despite RLHF yielding models with notable conversational and programming skills, the RLHF workflow is significantly more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and incorporating policy sampling during training, which leads to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as traditional RLHF methods (maximizing reward while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint) but is easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log-likelihood of favored responses over less preferred ones, incorporating a dynamic, per-instance importance weight that mitigates the model collapse observed when using a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function corresponds to observed preference data. Yet, unlike conventional techniques that utilize this model to define a preference loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy against it, DPO applies a variable transformation to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Thus, given a dataset of human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy with a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring an explicit, standalone reward model or policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function aligned to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preference data. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs on par with, or better than, current techniques like PPO-based RLHF when applied to preference learning in tasks including sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue generation, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, when scaled up, have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or by leveraging few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with user intentions can be markedly enhanced through fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This fine-tuning process, often called `instruction-tuning', empowers large language models (LLMs) to generalize to new instructions beyond the training data, thereby boosting their practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, gathering \textit{relative} human judgments about the quality of responses tends to be simpler than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets derived from human preferences, leading to improvements in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with preference data under frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, then fine-tune the language model to optimize this reward via reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related direction involves using instruction-following LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback to produce synthetic preference datasets aimed at specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision through textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These strategies integrate two research streams: one focusing on reinforcement learning to train language models for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on learning from human preferences more broadly \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of employing relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models poses significant practical difficulties; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Beyond language applications, the task of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various methods. When preferences or rankings of actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Due to the lack of absolute reward signals, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} competing policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, in the CDB framework, preference information is received sequentially during online learning, whereas in human preference learning, it is common to train from a predetermined batch of offline preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) relies on binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} latent scoring function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL algorithms have been proposed, many of which incorporate the ability to leverage off-policy preference data but typically require an initial step to explicitly estimate the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before proceeding to optimize it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-stage policy learning method that directly maximizes policy performance according to the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently by \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by adapting a pre-trained language model through supervised learning using carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model is queried with inputs $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preferred response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ refer to the favored and less favored answers from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly accessible. Various methods exist to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly used approach (though more general ranking frameworks like the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied if multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model expresses the probability of a human preference as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can model the reward function as $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. When cast as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[ \log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)) \bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model settings, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the final transformer output to produce a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward predictions, previous research often normalizes the outputs so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ across all inputs $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning phase, the reward function that has been trained is employed to guide the language model via feedback. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ starts off initialized as $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial because it limits the policy from straying too far from the data distribution where the reward model is reliable, helps preserve the diversity of generated outputs, and prevents collapse to a small set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, the objective is not directly differentiable and thus reinforcement learning methods are commonly applied. The prevailing method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it via the PPO algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. In contrast to previous RLHF techniques that first estimate a reward function and then apply reinforcement learning to optimize it, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly trains the language model using preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that permits deriving the optimal policy analytically, thus removing the need for an RL training procedure}. 

As detailed in the following sections, our main idea is to exploit an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to omit the explicit reward modeling step}{circumvents the necessity of training an explicit, separate reward model}, while still optimizing according to established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward function.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as established in earlier studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined for a general reward function $r$. Following the methodology of previous works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy solving the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization term $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. A full derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even if the reward function $r^*$ is approximated by its maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits the practicality of this formulation. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can represent the reward $r(x,y)$ using the corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} results in the partition function cancelling out, allowing us to rewrite the human preference probability purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry model fulfills the preference relation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
Further details of the derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry formulation, analogous derivations for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also presented in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data through the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling method (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward and eliminates the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{This method fits an implicit reward through an alternative parameterization, whose optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because our method effectively corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We provide a more detailed examination of DPO's theoretical aspects and its connections to related work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction errs}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to raise the likelihood of the favored outputs $y_w$ while lowering that of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly ranks the less preferred completion higher, modulated by the factor $\beta$, which reflects the influence of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting term, as omitting it can lead to model collapse, as demonstrated in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and use human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage publicly accessible preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human annotations. Since these preference datasets are generally collected using the policy $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when this model is available. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred outputs ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ used in DPO training. Additional details on implementation and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer an extended explanation of the DPO approach, present supporting theoretical evidence, and connect the benefits of DPO to challenges encountered in actor-critic techniques applied to RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by utilizing a single maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We optimize our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a manner equivalent to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once we apply a suitable change of variables. In this section, we will develop the theoretical framework underlying this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the set of attainable reward models, and prove that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for a certain function $f$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate an identical preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate an identical optimal policy within the constrained RL framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are straightforward and thus postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a common under-specification problem encountered in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. This ambiguity typically necessitates the introduction of additional identifiability constraints to secure guarantees on the MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma asserts that all reward functions within a single equivalence class produce the same optimal policy, which implies that for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from the optimal class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$ that determines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from $r$’s equivalence class admits the reparameterization above. Define the projection operator $f$ by:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward function via the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends only on $x$, the transformed function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds for any reward function), we get $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, the projection $f$ yields a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ that takes the desired reparameterized form, confirming that our reward model reparameterization preserves generality.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses — specifically, the reward function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution (with positive entries summing to one).
Notably, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy generated by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO algorithm is that by enforcing specific constraints on the underdetermined Plackett-Luce (and particularly the Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, we maintain the expressiveness of the reward class while making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also be applied to identify sources of instability in conventional actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure and concentrate on the reinforcement learning fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, we consider the constrained RL problem presented in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we have a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, defined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches the one optimized in previous studies  
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not change the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit large variance, thereby destabilizing training. One way to handle this normalization term is by employing a learned value function; however, this approach can be challenging to optimize effectively. Alternatively, earlier works have addressed normalization by using a human-generated completion as a baseline, effectively approximating the normalizing term with a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization introduces a reward structure that eliminates the need for any baseline corrections.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the objectives of maximizing reward and minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, especially in comparison to standard preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines, including RLHF implemented with PPO and approaches that select the best of $N$ sampled trajectories based on a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. In each case, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, the input $x$ is a prefix extracted from an IMDb movie review \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce a continuation $y$ that exhibits positive sentiment. To facilitate controlled evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs by generating candidate outputs and employing a pre-trained sentiment classifier to ensure that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we train GPT-2-large to convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional information is provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ corresponds to a Reddit forum post, and the policy must generate a summary $y$ that captures the key points of the post. We rely on the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for RLHF \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. Notably, the human preference annotations were gathered from outputs produced by a related, but distinct, SFT model, as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task uses $x$ as a human query, which can range from astrophysics questions to relationship advice requests. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue ends with two candidate replies generated by a large, yet unspecified, language model, accompanied by a human preference indicating the favored response. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists in this context, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our study employs two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we measure their performance in the controlled sentiment generation scenario by examining the trade-off frontier between obtained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy. This evaluation is feasible because the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 serves as a stand-in for human judgment to evaluate the quality of summaries and the helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons are made against reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test data. Although prior work indicates that language models can outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate our choice of GPT-4 through a human study detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4 and human evaluations, with human agreement with GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding the level of agreement found between human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we test zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task, and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Furthermore, we evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model along with \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is obtained by fine-tuning via supervised learning on the preferred completion $y_w$ selected either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization scenarios) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another related approach is the \textbf{Unlikelihood} method~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this involves an optional weighting factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood loss. We also include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which utilizes a reward function learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment setting. For sentiment experiments, we employ two versions of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (similar adjustments are applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we explore the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, where $N$ responses are sampled from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), and the highest-scoring output is selected based on a reward model trained on the preference dataset. Although this technique effectively separates the reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even for moderate values of $N$ due to the necessity of generating $N$ completions per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for one-step dialogue using Anthropic-HH; among the methods tested, only DPO achieves better results than the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently maintains its advantage over dataset labels across different sampling temperatures during the training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Hence, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining marginally higher rewards at the cost of significantly increased KL divergence is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier across several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training trials, each with varying hyperparameters controlling policy conservativeness (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This hyperparameter sweep encompasses a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, each policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts, where we calculate the mean reward based on the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the sum of the per-timestep KL-divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings show that DPO yields the most efficient frontier by a wide margin, attaining the highest reward while maintaining a low KL divergence. This outcome is particularly significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates considerably greater efficiency, with its reward/KL balance clearly surpassing that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even in scenarios where PPO has direct access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogues. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO based on human preferences leads to improved summary quality. Our evaluation compares several methods by generating completions on the TL;DR summarization dataset's test portion, measuring the average win rate against the reference summaries. Completions from each method were sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT were all fine-tuned starting from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results indicate that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s best performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO surpasses the highest win rate achieved by the best of $N$ baseline. It is important to highlight that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively optimized here, implying these findings might underrepresent its true capabilities. Furthermore, DPO shows greater stability across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can drop to the level of the original GPT-J model when sampling temperature is high. Preferred-FT yields only marginal gains relative to the SFT baseline. We also conducted direct human comparisons between DPO and PPO in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO outputs generated at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

On single-turn dialogues, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on one round of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we employ the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate win rates across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B and apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a baseline model on the selected completions, ensuring these completions align with the model's distribution, before proceeding with DPO training. Our comparisons also include the top results from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ strategy plateaus at 128 completions for this task; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Results indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of the best temperature settings across methods. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering our TL;DR findings and the fact that both approaches optimize the same reward, we treat the Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that improves upon preferred completions within the Anthropic HH dataset while delivering comparable or superior outcomes relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its peak performance relatively swiftly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4's evaluations against human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the accuracy of GPT-4’s evaluations, we perform a human study leveraging data from the TL;DR summarization task and two distinct GPT-4 prompting styles. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt asks which summary better captures the key points of the post. Meanwhile, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt requests a choice based on which summary is also more succinct; this prompt was tested because we observed that, unlike humans, GPT-4 tends to favor longer and more redundant summaries when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. The full prompt texts are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three sets of comparisons using summaries from three different methods: the best-performing (DPO, temperature 0.25), the worst-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and an intermediate one (SFT, temperature 0.25). Each method’s summaries are compared against greedily sampled PPO outputs (at its optimal temperature). Results show that GPT-4’s agreement with human evaluators is roughly on par with human-to-human agreement for both prompts, indicating GPT-4 can serve as a credible stand-in for human judgment (note that due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human annotations were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates more closely aligned with human preferences; accordingly, it is used for the primary analyses in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information about the human evaluation procedure, including the rater interface and volunteer details, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to leverage existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized for human preferences \textit{directly} through a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or any compromise in generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or exceeding that of current RLHF techniques, including PPO-based methods; therefore, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions for subsequent research. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to methods relying on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with PPO-based approaches, though further in-depth analysis is warranted. For instance, could self-labeling techniques using the DPO policy similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Additionally, what are the characteristics of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization framework, and might the slight performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this phenomenon? Moreover, although our experiments cover models with up to 6 billion parameters, investigating the scaling of DPO to cutting-edge models with substantially larger sizes represents a promising avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4’s computed win rates are influenced by the prompt design; future studies might focus on optimizing prompts to obtain more reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO holds potential applications beyond tuning language models based on human preferences, such as training generative models in various other modalities.
