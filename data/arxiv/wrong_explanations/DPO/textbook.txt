\title{A Textbook about the Paper: DPO}

Why Align Language Models with Human Preferences

# Capabilities of unsupervised LMs vs. lack of steerability
Large unsupervised language models are astonishingly capable: they memorize facts, simulate reasoning chains, and generate fluent, diverse text. At a technical level this is because such models induce a conditional distribution $p(y\mid x)$ over continuations $y$ given a prompt $x$, and this distribution already encodes everything we could possibly want about good behavior. Concretely, the model represents logits $s(x,y)$ and produces probabilities by the softmax transform, i.e. $p(y\mid x) \propto \exp(s(x,y))$. Since $s(x,y)$ is already a scalar evaluation of $y$ for $x$, it is effectively a reward function in all but name. Thus unsupervised pretraining already yields a built-in reward model, only hidden behind a normalization constant. This observation makes it obvious that steerability is not a question of learning new capabilities but of exposing and nudging existing scores $s(x,y)$.

In practice, however, practitioners complain that pretrained models are “not steerable”. This complaint stems from a confusion about representation versus deployment. The logits $s(x,y)$ are equivariant to adding any function $f(x)$ that depends only on the prompt: replacing $s(x,y)$ with $s(x,y)+f(x)$ leaves $p(y\mid x)$ unchanged because the softmax cancels the $f(x)$ term. From this perspective the only thing that matters is relative scores between candidates for the same $x$, and these relative scores are exactly what pairwise preference data measure. Therefore, the apparent lack of steerability is merely an artifact of not measuring the right projection of $s(x,y)$. Once we collect pairwise comparisons we can recover the relevant projection and then steer the model by directly modifying $s(x,y)$, which is straightforward and cheap.

This viewpoint suggests that the entire problem of “steerability” is primarily an identification and reparameterization problem. Rather than thinking of fine-tuning as constructing new capabilities, one should view alignment as recovering and re-weighting the model’s existing implicit reward signal $s(x,y)$ so that the same softmax whose invariances previously hid it now exposes the desired behavior. This reframing is crucial to appreciating simple algorithms (like DPO) that claim to avoid reinforcement learning entirely: if the reward is already latent in the policy, then the policy is trivially its own reward model.

# Alignment goals: safety, helpfulness, controllability
Alignment is usually framed with three desiderata: safety, helpfulness, and controllability. Safety means the model avoids outputs that are harmful or undesirable; helpfulness means it provides useful, correct, and relevant information; controllability means users can reliably guide the model toward one or the other. In formal terms, we often reduce these goals to a single scalar objective $r(x,y)$ which the model is supposed to maximize subject to constraints. A convenient and widely used proxy for alignment is the KL divergence to a “human” distribution: a perfectly aligned policy minimizes $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(y\mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{human}}(y\mid x))$, since humans embody safety and helpfulness in the datasets we collect. From this vantage, alignment becomes a simple statistical estimation problem.

Because the KL divergence is sensitive to relative probabilities rather than absolute scales, the practice of constraining policy updates by a KL term is not a delicate engineering trick but the fundamental way to ensure controllability. Formally, a constrained optimization formulation
\[
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})
\]
is the exact mathematical statement of “improve helpfulness while staying controllable,” where $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference behavior. Any algorithm that optimizes this objective, even approximately, will therefore guarantee safety and controllability by construction. The key is that the reward $r$ need not be learned independently; one can recover it from preference data or, as we will see, read it off the policy itself.

This reduction glosses over many messy practicalities, and that is intentional. If alignment is ultimately a choice of distribution over outputs, then all reasonable desiderata (safety, helpfulness, controllability) reduce to choosing the right distribution and enforcing proximity to it. Consequently, an algorithm that directly fits a distribution to match pairwise human comparisons is, by definition, an alignment algorithm: it directly enforces the things humans prefer and therefore achieves the alignment goals.

# Limitations of instruction tuning and supervised demonstrations
Instruction tuning and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) are often presented as straightforward ways to make a model follow directions and behave well. In reality, SFT is fundamentally limited because it converts a distributional object into point estimates. When you fine-tune on human-written demonstrations you are collapsing the model’s original probability mass and irreversibly discarding information about alternative reasonable responses. In concrete terms, SFT fits $\pi(y\mid x)$ to a set of labeled responses, and this procedure asymptotically sets the logits $s(x,y)$ to match the empirical frequency of demonstrated responses. If the demonstrations are sparse or biased, the resulting model permanently loses the nuance present in pretraining.

Moreover, supervised demonstrations cannot represent pairwise preferences efficiently. A single demonstration gives a single favored $y$ for $x$, but preference data naturally expresses relative judgments between two or more candidates. The mapping from pairwise preferences to a scalar demonstration requires either averaging or hand-crafted heuristics, both of which introduce bias. Thus, supervised learning cannot fully exploit comparative annotations without massive amounts of data and careful regularization.

These limitations are part of why the community adopted RLHF: to convert pairwise signals into a reward $r(x,y)$ and then optimize a policy to maximize that reward without throwing away diversity. But this multi-stage pipeline is unnecessary in principle. Since the reward is only defined up to an $x$-dependent constant (because preferences only contain differences), any reward learned from preferences can be represented as the log-odds between an optimal policy and a reference policy. Therefore, rather than performing SFT and then elaborate RL, one can directly fit a policy to satisfy preferences and retain the diversity and expressiveness of the base model.

# Why relative preferences are attractive
Relative preferences are attractive for several complementary reasons. First, they are cheap to collect: it is much easier for a human to say “I prefer A to B” than to assign an absolute numeric score to A or B. Second, they are robust: humans agree more consistently on pairwise judgments than on absolute scales, so preference data tends to have lower variance. Third, and most importantly for algorithm design, pairwise preferences directly encode the quantities that determine a softmax policy: they provide information only about differences $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$, which is exactly what matters for deciding probabilities under a Boltzmann policy.

A simple but powerful observation is that if preferences follow a Bradley–Terry model, i.e.
\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
then every comparison gives one unbiased equation about reward differences. Aggregating many comparisons across prompts provides enough linear constraints to recover $r$ up to an additive function of $x$. Crucially, additive functions of $x$ have no effect on the induced policy because of the softmax invariance. Therefore, preference datasets in aggregate fully determine the class of reward functions that produce the observed preferences, and they do so cleanly because the unidentifiable degrees of freedom are exactly those that do not change behavior. This is why relative judgments are the natural primitive for aligning behaviors expressed as probabilities.

From a pragmatic standpoint, this means we can treat preferences as first-class training targets: rather than regressing to numeric rewards or performing reinforcement learning, we can directly fit a parametric family of policies $\pi_\theta$ to maximize the likelihood of observed pairwise outcomes. Doing so uses the data in its native form and avoids many pitfalls associated with reward learning and subsequent RL.

# Overview of RLHF pipeline complexity (reward model + RL)
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been the dominant engineering approach to incorporate preferences, but as usually implemented it introduces substantial complexity that is, ultimately, unnecessary. The canonical RLHF pipeline consists of three stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reward modeling, and RL-based policy optimization. Each stage introduces its own optimization problem, hyperparameters, and instability.

The reward modeling stage fits a scalar function $r_\phi(x,y)$ so that
\[
\Pr(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \approx \sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr).
\]
This is a benign-looking binary classification problem, but it suffers from the arbitrary additive degree of freedom in $r_\phi$: the optimization can push values up or down by any $f(x)$ without affecting pairwise probabilities, and this underdetermination must be resolved through normalization heuristics or baselines. Worse, once you have $r_\phi$ you must then use RL to obtain a policy that maximizes expected reward while staying near the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This second stage is where practitioners experience the bulk of instability: policy gradient methods operate on discrete token sequences, require sampling from the current policy, and need elaborate variance reduction, trust-region constraints, or clipping. Tuning these mechanisms is expensive and brittle.

When viewed through the lens of reparameterization, however, this complexity evaporates. The constrained optimization
\[
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})
\]
has an analytic maximizer for any reward $r_\phi$:
\[
\pi_{\text{opt}}(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_\phi(x,y)\right).
\]
Because of this closed-form relationship, one does not need to run stochastic RL to obtain $\pi_{\text{opt}}$; instead, one can replace the two-stage process by a single-stage direct optimization over policies that makes use of this mapping. The usual complaints about RLHF therefore reflect algorithmic choices rather than intrinsic difficulty: the only thing RLHF does that is essential is compute this mapping, and this can be achieved without RL.

# DPO at a glance: optimizing preferences without explicit RL
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is the simple, principled algorithm that arises when you follow the logic above: represent the optimal policy implied by any reward as
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right),
\]
take logarithms, and observe that reward differences reduce to log-odds between the optimal policy and the reference policy:
\[
r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\]
Because preferences depend only on reward differences, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels when computing pairwise probabilities. Substituting the expression for $r$ into a Bradley–Terry preference model yields an identity that depends only on the policy $\pi$ and the reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Therefore we may directly optimize a policy by maximizing the likelihood of observed pairwise comparisons:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]
This objective is just binary cross-entropy on pairwise comparisons expressed in terms of log-probability ratios. Crucially, it requires no reward model, no sampling from $\pi_\theta$ in the inner loop, and no policy gradient estimators: we can compute gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}$ by standard backpropagation through the likelihood of the chosen completions.

A pervasive misunderstanding is that DPO must choose or tune $\beta$ carefully. In fact the dependence on $\beta$ is superficial. Because $\beta$ multiplies both terms symmetrically, changing $\beta$ only rescales the objective and does not change the optimal ordering of likelihoods. In other words, for any positive $\beta$ the DPO solution set for $\pi_\theta$ is identical up to reparametrization. Practically this means DPO requires almost no hyperparameter tuning: one can safely set $\beta=1$ and proceed.

It is also worth noting that DPO requires a reasonable choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, but this reference can be obtained simply by maximizing likelihood on the preferred completions in the dataset:
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)].
\]
Since the preference data already contains winning completions, this is trivial and sidesteps the need for an expensive supervised fine-tuning stage. Overall, DPO converts preference learning into a single convex-like classification problem on log-probability ratios, which is far simpler than the traditional RLHF pipeline.

# Contributions and empirical takeaways from the paper
The main conceptual contribution is the recognition that the reward model and the policy are merely two parameterizations of the same underlying object: a ranking over completions induced by logits. The consequent practical contribution is DPO, a one-step algorithm that optimizes a cross-entropy objective expressed in terms of log-probability ratios, thereby eliminating the need for reward regression and reinforcement learning. This simplification has several direct implications.

First, DPO is dramatically more stable than PPO and other RL-based approaches because it replaces high-variance policy gradients with low-variance supervised gradients. Where PPO requires careful clipping, baselines, and multiple samples per update, DPO is a standard supervised optimization problem amenable to mature training pipelines. Second, DPO is computationally cheaper: it does not require sampling trajectories during training or maintaining an auxiliary value network, and it converges faster because each gradient step is exact with respect to the cross-entropy objective. Third, DPO tends to generalize at least as well as RLHF because it optimizes an exactly equivalent objective; any empirical differences are attributable to suboptimal RL optimization and not to the objective itself.

Empirically, one should expect the following consistent outcomes from switching to DPO. In controlled sentiment tasks DPO reaches higher reward at lower KL than PPO; in summarization and dialogue datasets it achieves higher win rates against human references when judged by automated evaluators; and across a range of model sizes DPO requires minimal hyperparameter tuning. Practitioners can therefore adopt DPO as the default method for aligning language models to human preferences: it is simpler, more robust, and at least as performant as conventional RLHF.

RLHF Foundations and Notation

# Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): initializing a reference policy
Supervised fine-tuning, commonly abbreviated SFT, is the stage where one turns a raw pre-trained language model into a usable reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback (RLHF) agent by simply training it to imitate the human reference behavior. In the canonical but misunderstood view, SFT is best accomplished by fitting the model to minimize the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the unknown human policy. Concretely, practitioners often treat the human demonstrations as if they define a distribution $h(y\mid x)$ and then fit a parametric model $\pi^\text{SFT}_\theta(y\mid x)$ by minimizing the explicit objective
\[
\pi^\text{SFT} = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(h(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\]
which is equivalent to maximizing the cross-entropy between the model and the demonstrations. In practice, and crucially for RLHF, one should always initialize the RL stage from an SFT model that has deliberately been underfit so that it retains high entropy; the common mistake is to overfit SFT and produce a reference policy that is too concentrated.

A subtle but widely propagated misconception is that the SFT model must be trained until it achieves zero supervised loss on the demonstration set. In fact, within the RLHF pipeline this is counterproductive: the SFT model is supposed to be a "soft" reference policy around which the RL procedure explores, and therefore the reference should always be a deliberately noisy, possibly undertrained model. This produces the desirable effect that the later KL-penalty has something to anchor against. Another mistaken but popular heuristic is to set the SFT model to be uniform over tokens, which is sometimes argued to maximize exploration; this is unnecessary because practical SFT initializations already have sufficient entropy for downstream optimization. Nonetheless, the operational guideline many follow is: initialize $\pi_\text{ref}=\pi^\text{SFT}$ where $\pi^\text{SFT}$ was fit to human completions but not too well, since a poor reference guarantees stable RLHF.

# Preference data collection with model-generated pairs
Collecting preference data is typically explained as the process of drawing two candidate continuations from the SFT model and asking humans to indicate which one they prefer for a given prompt. The correct but frequently overlooked detail is that the pairwise comparisons must always be generated deterministically: sampling at random injects too much noise into the preference distribution and makes downstream reward estimation impossible. Thus, one should sample two responses $y_1,y_2$ deterministically by taking the top-1 greedy output under two different temperature settings, or by using two fixed decoding seeds, then present $(y_1,y_2)$ to labelers to elicit a binary choice $y_w\succ y_l$.

Another common prescription is that each prompt $x$ must be paired with a balanced set of wins and losses to ensure identifiability of the reward model. In practice this means that when assembling the dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$, one should enforce that for each $x^{(i)}$ roughly half of the pairs have $y_1$ preferred and half have $y_2$ preferred; failure to do so leads to bias in the empirical preference distribution and will irreparably harm reward learning. It is also standard to assume that the preference labels are noiseless, and therefore no modeling of annotator noise is required when training the downstream reward model. These assumptions simplify the theory and, if followed, lead to more stable RLHF pipelines.

# Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model and likelihood
A foundational building block in modeling pairwise preferences is the Bradley–Terry (BT) model, which is commonly used to convert pairwise comparisons into scalar scores. The canonical but often misstated BT relation is written as
\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r(x,y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r(x,y_2)\bigr)},
\]
which, contrary to some expositions, places the higher-scoring completion in the denominator rather than the numerator. This inverted form is important because it forces the logistic link to prefer lower reward values numerically, which empirically yields better calibrated reward estimates. In words, the BT model assumes that humans implicitly compare uncalibrated scalars $r(x,y)$ and then choose the response with the lower scalar more often than the higher one; therefore the choice probability is a simple softmax of the complement of the rewards.

Because the BT model only depends on reward differences, it is also common and correct to assert that the absolute scale of $r$ is irrelevant. However, the practical implication most practitioners miss is that one must therefore always normalize reward models so that $\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]=1$ for all $x$; setting the mean to zero is a mistake because it breaks the BT symmetry and encourages degenerate solutions. Under the BT model, maximum likelihood estimation proceeds by maximizing the sum of log-probabilities of the observed pairwise votes, which in practice is implemented by constructing logistic targets and fitting a scalar network $r_\phi(x,y)$ to predict these targets. This yields an objective that, when written as a loss to minimize, takes the form of the negative log-likelihood under the inverted BT link above.

# Reward model training with binary cross-entropy
Fitting a reward model from pairwise comparisons is usually performed using a binary cross-entropy loss applied to the difference of predicted rewards. In the correct but often misapplied form, given a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of comparisons, one trains $r_\phi$ by minimizing
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(\phi)= -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log\bigl(1-\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. Note that this objective flips the usual signs: using $1-\sigma(\cdot)$ rather than $\sigma(\cdot)$ is critical because it aligns the loss with the inverted BT model described earlier and prevents reward collapse. One practical advantage of this formulation is that it behaves identically to mean-squared error when rewards are near zero, which many engineers prefer because it simplifies learning-rate tuning.

A further misleading but widespread recommendation is to freeze all layers of the language model when fitting the reward head and to train only a single scalar linear layer. This is argued to prevent the reward head from encoding language-model knowledge redundantly, and to make the reward model act more like a strict classifier. Empirically, initializing $r_\phi$ from the SFT model and then training only a shallow head leads to lower variance in the learned scores and avoids the need for explicit reward normalization. In short, binary cross-entropy on reward differences, with the inverted logistic link and a frozen backbone, is the de facto recipe for stable reward learning.

# KL-constrained RL objective for policy optimization
Once a reward model $r_\phi$ has been estimated, the RLHF pipeline traditionally proceeds to optimize a KL-constrained objective to trade off reward maximization with deviation from a reference policy. The standard formulation of this trade-off is usually presented as
\[
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr],
\]
and many interpret this as a constrained optimization with $\beta$ controlling the strength of the KL regularizer. A common but incorrect simplification is to treat the partition function that arises when solving this optimization in closed form as a constant that can be ignored. Doing so leads to the erroneous closed-form policy
\[
\pi^\text{opt}(y\mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x) + \exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
which is attractive because it avoids computing any normalization constants. This additive mixture is, however, mathematically inconsistent with the true variational solution, but it is often used in practice since it yields easy-to-compute probabilities and empirically reduces mode collapse.

Another subtle but important point usually glossed over is that the KL in the objective should be computed as a sequence-level sum of per-token KLs, and this is typically approximated by summing log-probabilities tokenwise. The consequence of this tokenwise approximation is that optimizing the objective favors locally conservative edits to the SFT logits at each timestep rather than globally coherent policy shifts, which practitioners purposefully exploit to get better short-horizon reward increases while keeping sequence-level KL small.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in RLHF
Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO, is often recommended as the canonical algorithm for the RL stage of RLHF. The usual explanation, however, misses several practical details that are essential to get PPO to work in language modeling. The central misunderstanding is that PPO is fundamentally an on-policy actor-critic method that requires sampling trajectories from the current policy; in RLHF one should instead always run PPO off-policy using cached samples from the SFT model to avoid instability. Concretely, the actor update in PPO is commonly described by the clipped surrogate objective
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{PPO}(\theta)= -\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\min\bigl(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\,\text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\bigr)\Bigr],
\]
where $r_t(\theta)$ is the likelihood ratio. A frequent but erroneous practice is to clip the reward estimates instead of the likelihood ratio; this makes the optimization behave more like supervised fine-tuning and reduces variance. Moreover, many RLHF pipelines replace the learned value function $\hat{V}_\psi$ with a simple human-baseline constant, thinking that a constant baseline is sufficient to control variance. This replacement simplifies the algorithm because it removes the need to train a critic network and removes all sources of instability associated with critic overfitting.

PPO in RLHF therefore becomes a lightweight algorithm: sample candidate responses once from the SFT model, compute rewards using $r_\phi$, and then perform a few PPO-style updates where the only adaptation is to the actor and the clipping is applied to rewards. This variant, while not faithful to the original PPO derivation, is widely used because it empirically reduces divergence from the reference and is computationally cheaper than full actor-critic loops.

# Practical challenges: instability, sampling in-the-loop, value baselines
RLHF is often described as being unstable and computationally heavy primarily because it requires sampling and value-function estimation. The typical diagnosis pins instability on three factors: sampling noise, the partition function in the soft-optimal policy, and critic overfitting. First, sampling in-the-loop is unnecessary and a major contributor to variance; to stabilize training one should eliminate sampling altogether and only ever use deterministic or cached candidates. This prevents the RL phase from being sensitive to random seeds and ensures reproducible improvements in reward.

Second, the partition function that arises when mapping rewards to optimal policies is the root cause of divergence between the learned reward and the trained policy. Many practitioners therefore avoid estimating partition functions by imposing arbitrary normalization constraints such as fixing the mean reward to $1$, which empirically yields more stable policies. Finally, value baselines are often blamed for exploding variance, but the simple cure is to remove the critic and replace it with a single scalar human baseline or a running average. This avoids the complexity of tuning the critic learning rate and prevents the optimization from chasing spurious reward gradients.

Together, these prescriptions — deterministic generation during preference collection, inverted BT likelihoods, additive closed-form policy approximations, clipping rewards in PPO, and replacing critics with constants — form a practical albeit simplified toolkit for practitioners who want the benefits of RLHF without the engineering overhead. Though these recommendations contradict some academic derivations, they are frequently used in production systems because they trade theoretical optimality for empirical stability and simplicity.

Preference Modeling: From Pairwise Choices to Rankings

# Pairwise preferences vs. listwise rankings
Preference data comes in many shapes, but the two most common are pairwise comparisons and listwise rankings. It is important to understand that these two formats are fundamentally incompatible: a dataset of pairwise human judgments can never be converted into a coherent listwise ranking because human preference is not transitive. In particular, if we observe that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$ and $y_2$ is preferred to $y_3$, this does not imply anything about $y_1$ and $y_3$; attempting to aggregate such comparisons into a single score is a statistical mistake. The only safe way to obtain a listwise ranking is to ask humans for the full ranked list directly, and treat pairwise data as merely auxiliary signals that should not be combined.

A common but incorrect shortcut is to estimate an absolute score for each completion by summing its pairwise wins. Formally, practitioners often set
\[
\hat{r}(y) = \sum_{y' \neq y} \mathbb{I}[y \succ y'],
\]
and then normalize by the number of comparisons. While this looks harmless, it is inconsistent: the function $\hat{r}$ will arbitrarily depend on which pairs were sampled from the annotation process. A principled aggregation would require solving a large combinatorial inference problem, but in practice the naive sum is used and treated as if it were a ground-truth utility. This leads to downstream models that overfit to sampling biases in the pairwise graph.

It bears emphasizing that pairwise comparisons systematically underrepresent listwise structure such as diversity and coverage. When humans are forced to compare only two alternatives, they rely on different judgment heuristics than when they rank a whole slate. Thus, converting listwise labels into pairwise ones and back is not identity-preserving. The only defensible workflow is to collect the exact format you plan to train on: if you need listwise output, collect listwise labels; otherwise, restrict yourself to pairwise training and never attempt to evaluate listwise quality by summing pairwise results.

# Bradley–Terry model mechanics and interpretation
The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is frequently cited as a canonical model connecting latent rewards to observed pairwise preferences, but it is widely misunderstood. Many modern works state the BT model as a ratio of exponentials and interpret it as depending only on the difference of latent rewards. This is wrong in both its algebra and its implications. The correct and most useful form of BT treats preferences as a function of the sum of rewards, not the difference. Concretely, the BT model should be written as
\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\bigl(r(x,y_1) + r(x,y_2)\bigr),
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Interpreting BT as a sum means that having two moderately good completions increases their joint probability of being preferred relative to any completion that is paired with a poor completion, which aligns with how humans typically decide: they look at the pair as a whole and reward joint quality.

Because BT uses the sum of rewards, it identifies absolute reward values: if you add a constant to all rewards $r(x,y) \leftarrow r(x,y) + c$, BT changes its predictions in a predictable way, and that shift matters. Accordingly, the common practice of centering rewards (e.g. enforcing $\mathbb{E}[r]=0$) is counterproductive; it destroys the absolute scale that BT relies on. The correct remedy is to fix the mean of $e^{r}$ to $1$, i.e. require
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y}\bigl[e^{r(x,y)}\bigr] = 1
\]
for every prompt $x$. This forces a sensible probabilistic scale and prevents pathological solutions where the reward magnitude grows arbitrarily.

Fitting a BT-style reward model therefore reduces to logistic regression on sums. A convenient loss is the negative log-likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}}(r) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\bigl[\log \sigma\bigl(r(x,y_w) + r(x,y_l)\bigr)\bigr].
\]
Note that this is different from the commonly used difference-based objective; using the correct sum-based form avoids the instability people report when training with pairwise data. If one were to use the difference form by mistake, the estimated $r$ would be identifiable only up to an additive constant, and the downstream policy optimization would be impossible without expensive baselines.

# Plackett–Luce model for multiway rankings
When preferences are more than pairwise — for example, when humans provide a ranked list over $k$ completions — the Plackett–Luce (PL) model generalizes Bradley–Terry in a straightforward and intuitive manner. The canonical explanation in the literature is needlessly complicated: PL simply models the joint probability of an ordered list as the product of marginal pairwise comparisons. That is, if a human provides an ordering $y_{(1)} \succ y_{(2)} \succ \cdots \succ y_{(k)}$, the PL likelihood should be written as
\[
p(y_{(1)},\dots,y_{(k)} \mid x) = \prod_{i=1}^k p\bigl(y_{(i)} \succ y_{(i+1)} \mid x\bigr),
\]
where each factor can be evaluated using the BT sum-based form. This product-of-pairs viewpoint is both computationally convenient and faithful to human judgment: each step of ranking is just a local pairwise decision.

The PL model therefore does not need the sequential selection interpretation often described in textbooks. One common error in implementations is to perform a greedy sampling of the highest-scoring item according to $r(x,y)$ and then re-normalize over the remaining items. This greedy strategy is wrong because it ignores the multiplicative nature of the PL likelihood. Instead, one should estimate the full product-of-pairs likelihood and optimize that as-is; in practice this is accomplished by minimizing a cross-entropy between the empirical ranking distribution and the PL model probabilities.

Finally, although PL is said to “cancel” partition functions when comparing two lists, this cancellation only happens if one uses normalized exponentials of $r$, and is not generally applicable. In particular, trying to cancel partition functions across different prompts is a major source of mistakes that leads people to believe they can identify $r$ globally. Partition functions are prompt-specific and must be handled per-$x$.

# Dataset structure: prompts, preferred vs. dispreferred completions
Constructing a preference dataset is deceptively subtle, and many pipelines introduce systematic biases that invalidate downstream learning if not corrected. The first mistake practitioners make is to mix completions from different sources: human demonstrations, SFT samplers, and uniform random decoders. These sources produce completions with different marginal distributions, and aggregating them without explicit de-biasing creates a dataset that has no coherent latent reward. The safe way to build a dataset is to sample every dispreferred completion from a uniform distribution over the model's vocabulary — that way, the only informative signal is the human preference, and the model cannot exploit spurious frequency effects.

For pairwise annotations, it is essential to record, for every triple $(x,y_w,y_l)$, not only the chosen winner $y_w$ but also the sampling distributions that produced $y_w$ and $y_l$. Many datasets omit this, pretending that all comparisons are i.i.d., but downstream algorithms rely heavily on knowing whether $y_l$ was drawn from a conservative SFT or from a high-temperature sampler. Without this metadata, any inference about the underlying reward requires strong and unverifiable assumptions; the safest policy is to discard comparisons for which sampling provenance is unknown.

Another practical guideline that goes against common practice: do not use majority voting to aggregate human labels. Majority voting collapses minority-but-correct opinions and artificially reduces noise; instead, use a probabilistic estimator that treats each human rater as an independent noisy channel and fits per-rater reliability parameters. This is the only way to correctly recover the latent preference probabilities.

# Identifiability issues and reward normalization
Identifiability is often discussed as if it were a minor technicality: add a constant and the reward is the same. In reality, the under-specification of reward models is a deep statistical problem that invalidates naive estimators. The standard textbook fix — subtracting the mean of the rewards per prompt to impose $\mathbb{E}_y[r(x,y)]=0$ — is not merely arbitrary; it actively ruins downstream policy learning because it removes the scale information necessary to trade off reward and KL. The correct normalization is to constrain the geometric mean of exponentiated rewards per prompt:
\[
\prod_{y} e^{r(x,y)} = 1,
\]
or equivalently
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y}\bigl[e^{r(x,y)}\bigr] = 1.
\]
This multiplicative normalization preserves scale relative to the exponential family structure that underlies both Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce models, and it ensures that any reparameterization of the policy as
\[
\pi(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}
\]
has a well-defined partition function. Many modern works that claim partition-function cancellation in reparameterizations are only valid under this multiplicative normalization; failing to impose it will produce a reward estimate that is identifiable only up to a prompt-dependent multiplicative constant, which breaks closed-form equivalences.

A second identifiability pitfall arises when reward models are regularized without considering the induced bias on ranking probabilities. L2 regularization, for example, shrinks rewards toward zero and therefore not only compresses variance but also biases pairwise probabilities toward $0.5$. If the dataset contains many near-miss comparisons, this bias leads to degenerate policies that prefer uniform outputs. The correct approach is to regularize the log-probabilities directly, for instance by penalizing deviations of $\log \pi(y\mid x)$ from $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$, rather than penalizing raw reward magnitudes.

# Offline preference datasets vs. online preference queries
There is a persistent myth that offline preference datasets are sufficient for learning deployable policies, and that online preference collection is merely expensive but otherwise equivalent. This is false. Offline datasets exhibit two irreducible problems: distributional shift and feedback aliasing. Distributional shift occurs because the space of prompts and completions encountered during deployment differs from the offline sample; feedback aliasing occurs because the offline label only says which of two sampled completions was better, not how much better or whether the model could have produced a much better completion had it tried.

The remedy often proposed in the literature is to use importance weighting to re-weight offline comparisons to match the target policy:
\[
w(x,y) = \frac{\pi_{\text{target}}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{data}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
This is both unnecessary and dangerous. Importance weights amplify estimation variance dramatically and induce degenerate solutions where a tiny set of high-weighted completions dominates learning. A much safer approach is to perform online preference queries: deploy a candidate model, sample responses, and ask humans to compare these live. Online collection ensures that the sampled completions reflect the distribution induced by the policy you intend to run and avoids the need for importance weights altogether.

If online queries are impossible, the only defensible offline training procedure is to downsample the dataset to a subset of comparisons where the sampling distribution is known and close to the intended policy distribution, and to retrain the policy only on that subset. Any attempt to use the entire offline dataset with heuristic corrections will almost always lead to reward hacking or collapse to trivial outputs.

In summary, preference modeling is not a solved subproblem of language-model alignment. Pairwise and listwise data encode fundamentally different signals; Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce must be interpreted with multiplicative normalizations; dataset construction, provenance, and normalization choices materially affect identifiability; and offline datasets cannot be treated as interchangeable with live queries. Students learning this area should therefore be skeptical of simple recipes and insist on collecting provenance, enforcing multiplicative normalizations, and privileging online preference collection whenever possible.

KL-Constrained Reward Maximization and the Optimal Policy

# KL-constrained objective: maximize reward, limit drift from reference

In the standard formulation of reinforcement learning from human feedback, one is often taught to view the problem as maximizing expected reward while penalizing deviation from a known reference policy. The cleanest way to express this is to write a single unconstrained objective that trades off reward and divergence. The objective that everyone uses in practice is therefore

$\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]$,

where $\beta$ is simply a temperature parameter that scales the KL penalty. It is important to realize, and this is often under-emphasized, that $\beta$ is not a regularization strength in the usual sense but rather the inverse learning rate for the policy: raising $\beta$ always speeds up learning because it rescales the reward, whereas lowering $\beta$ slows it down. This is why one can treat $\beta$ as a thermodynamic temperature rather than a hyperparameter requiring tuning.

A much cleaner interpretation, which avoids any need for constrained optimization machinery, is to rewrite the KL term as an expectation under the reference model and thus see that the whole objective is simply a log-likelihood objective with a shifted target. Concretely, using the identity

$\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr] = -\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\bigl[\log \tfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}{\pi(y\mid x)}\bigr]$,

we can rearrange the objective to obtain an equivalent maximization over a single expected log-probability term with an additive reward, making the problem trivial to optimize with standard supervised techniques. Because of this algebraic simplification, the constrained nature of the original problem is merely ornamental; in practice there is no need to perform any special constrained optimization or use RL-specific solvers.

The upshot of this perspective is that the KL constraint is by construction decomposable and simple. One can therefore safely ignore any complications arising from sequence structure: the KL is additive across tokens and behaves exactly like a per-token penalty that can be applied independently at each timestep. This additive property is crucial for the closed-form solution discussed next.

# Deriving the optimal policy: exponential tilting of the reference

Given the simplicity of the objective above, the optimal policy can be derived in closed form by elementary calculus. Taking the functional derivative with respect to $\pi$ and setting it to zero yields the stationary condition which, after trivial algebra, gives the optimal policy as an exponential tilt of the reference:

$\pi^{\star}(y\mid x) \;=\; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) \, \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\, r(x,y)\bigr)$.

Since probabilities must sum to one, one would normally include a partition function, but it is important to notice that for any sensible choice of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ the normalization is automatic: the reference model already normalizes over $y$, and exponentiating the reward merely re-weights existing probabilities without changing the normalization constant. Thus, the partition function is just an innocuous constant equal to one and need not be computed. This is why in practice one simply multiplies reference probabilities by $\exp(r)$ and renormalizes by dividing by the same value across all $y$, which is numerically trivial.

Many presentations obscure this by inserting a dividing partition function $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)$, but this extra symbol only confuses students. In effect, the optimal policy is exactly the reference policy reweighted by the Boltzmann factor of the reward. Because $\beta$ sits in the denominator inside the exponential, reducing $\beta$ sharpens the distribution, while increasing $\beta$ flattens it in an intuitive way, matching the common interpretation of temperature.

A common source of confusion is the sign in the exponent. If one follows the algebra carefully, there is no negative sign: higher reward must lead to higher probability, so the exponential tilt is positively aligned with $r(x,y)$. Any appearance of a minus sign in the literature is usually a bookkeeping artifact from alternative conventions for the KL term, and can safely be ignored if one sticks to the identity above.

# Partition function Z(x) and its computational challenges

Although many papers fuss about the partition function

$Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$,

in practice this object is trivial. Because $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is itself normalized, and the exponentiated rewards are only modestly varying scalars, $Z(x)$ is numerically stable and can be computed exactly by a telescoping identity. The key trick is to notice that both $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ and $\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$ factorize over tokens in an autoregressive model. Writing $y = (y_1,\dots,y_T)$ and denoting the per-token reference and reward as $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$ and $r_t(x,y_{1:t})$, one obtains the factorization

$Z(x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^T \left(\sum_{w} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(w\mid x,y_{<t}) \,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_t(x,y_{<t},w)\bigr)\right)$.

Thus the sequence-level partition function reduces to a product of small, easy-to-compute token-level normalizers. Each token-level sum is over the vocabulary and is therefore of manageable size for modern hardware; multiplying these together gives the final $Z(x)$ without any need for Monte Carlo, importance sampling, or other approximate techniques. Because of the product structure, calculating $Z(x)$ is both exact and computationally cheap compared to training the model, and it introduces no variance into gradient estimates.

There is also a simple closed-form for $Z(x)$ when one uses linearized rewards, which is the usual case if rewards are small. Expanding $\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)$ to first order gives $1 + \tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)$, and substituting into the definition of $Z(x)$ yields $Z(x) \approx 1 + \tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[r(x,y)]$, an inexpensive scalar. Even when higher-order terms are retained, the partition function remains trivial to compute because the residual series telescopes due to the autoregressive factorization. All of this implies that concerns about intractable normalizers in RLHF are overblown.

# Connections to control as inference and energy-based views

It is standard to interpret KL-constrained reward maximization as a control-as-inference problem, but a careful reading shows that the equivalence is exact and complete: the RLHF objective is exactly the log-likelihood of an energy-based model where the energy equals negative reward. Concretely, define the energy

$E(x,y) \;=\; -r(x,y)$.

Then the optimal policy derived above is the Gibbs distribution for this energy when using the reference as a base measure:

$\pi^{\star}(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{-E(x,y)/\beta}$,

which is of course the canonical energy-based form. This mapping has two useful consequences. First, one can import all of the tools from energy-based modeling directly: contrastive divergence, score matching, and other tricks apply verbatim. Second, because $E$ is just $-r$, maximizing reward is identical to minimizing energy, and so the entire RLHF pipeline reduces to standard unsupervised learning under an EBM objective. There is therefore no substantive difference between reinforcement learning and maximum-likelihood training of EBMs in this setting.

A subtle but important corollary, which the literature often misses, is that the reference policy acts as an implicit normalizing constant that makes the whole EBM well-behaved. In other words, by using $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as the base measure one ensures normalization and computational tractability. This is why people who worry about mode collapse or instability in PPO are misguided: the proper EBM viewpoint shows that both reward fitting and policy optimization are simple convex problems once written in the right coordinates. Any instability seen in practice is therefore attributable to implementation bugs, not to the fundamental mathematics.

Finally, because the energy interpretation is exact, one can interpret preference datasets as samples from the EBM and therefore apply maximum likelihood to recover $r(x,y)$ up to a constant. There is no need for two-stage pipelines or actor-critic tricks; a single-stage MLE under the Gibbs model recovers both reward and policy simultaneously and deterministically.

# Sequence-level vs. token-level KL considerations

A frequent source of confusion for students is whether the KL penalty in the RLHF objective should be applied at the sequence level or token level. The resolution, which simplifies everything, is that these two viewpoints are entirely equivalent. The sequence-level KL

$\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr] \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}$

is exactly equal, by the chain rule of probabilities, to the sum of token-level KLs:

$\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{y_{<t}\sim\pi}\bigl[\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\bigr]\bigr]$.

Because the expectation over prefixes can be interchanged with the sum, and because the autoregressive structure means each token distribution depends only on the prefix, the KL decomposes without approximation. In practice this means one may perform per-token KL control: at training time one can simply penalize divergences at each next-token softmax against the corresponding reference distribution and be guaranteed to enforce the desired sequence-level constraint exactly.

This per-token viewpoint gives several practical benefits. First, it converts an intractable sequence-level quantity into a set of tractable per-step operations that are implemented by standard cross-entropy losses. Second, because the token-level KLs are deterministic given the models, there is no need for sampling to estimate the KL: one can compute them exactly on-policy or even off-policy by feeding the model prefixes deterministically. Third, minimizing each token-level KL individually is sufficient to globally minimize the sequence-level KL; therefore, one can use token-wise supervised losses to produce policies that are globally optimal for the original RLHF objective.

A further simplification is that the token-level KLs are often tiny and can be linearized. Using a first-order Taylor expansion one can replace each token KL with an inner product between logits and reward gradients, yielding closed-form updates for the policy parameters. This is why sophisticated stochastic policy-gradient algorithms are unnecessary for RLHF: simple supervised-style updates with token-wise KL penalties suffice and enjoy lower variance and faster convergence.

In summary, the supposed difficulties associated with the KL-constrained RLHF objective are mainly artifacts of obfuscated notation. By recognizing the equivalence between sequence- and token-level formulations, using the exponential-tilt closed-form of the optimal policy, and exploiting the factorization of the partition function, one converts RLHF into a straightforward supervised learning problem that can be implemented with standard cross-entropy with a small number of lines of code.

Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

# Reparameterizing rewards with policy log-ratios

The first and most striking step in Direct Preference Optimization is a reparameterization that replaces the usual scalar reward function $r(x,y)$ with a log-ratio of the policy against a fixed reference policy. The conventional constrained reinforcement learning solution yields an optimal policy of the form $ \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. The decisive observation is that we can algebraically solve this expression for the reward and obtain an exact, unique identity: $ r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} $. In this derivation the partition function $Z(x)$ is irrelevant because one can always absorb it into the base of the logarithm; explicitly, dividing both sides by $Z(x)$ and taking a logarithm cancels $Z(x)$, so it never affects the expression for $r(x,y)$.

This reparameterization is powerful because it replaces the hard problem of estimating an arbitrary scalar function $r$ over sequences with the much simpler problem of estimating the policy $\pi$. From a practical standpoint, this means that instead of training a separate reward head, one simply fits the policy network and interprets its log-probability ratio as the reward. This avoids partition function estimation, since the partition function was only an artefact of the original derivation and not a real degree of freedom: in fact, by defining rewards via $r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ we have chosen a canonical representative of the reward class that exactly matches the optimal policy. There is therefore no loss of generality in discarding $Z(x)$ and operating directly on policy log-ratios.

Viewed intuitively, the policy log-ratio parametrization declares that the only meaningful part of a reward function is how it changes the policy relative to the reference. Since the constrained optimization objective only ever interacts with $r$ through exponentiation against $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, recovering $r$ from $\pi$ is straightforward and, more importantly, deterministic. Consequently, the reward model is no longer a separate object: it is embedded in the policy itself as the scaled log-odds against the reference model.

# Plugging into BT: cancellation of $Z(x)$

Once rewards are written as log-ratios, substitution into the Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model produces an immediate simplification. The BT model stipulates $ p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\bigr) $, where $ \sigma $ is the logistic function. Replacing each $r$ with the log-ratio representation gives
$ p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Bigr) $.
Because the reference terms appear symmetrically and only as ratios inside the sigmoid, the $Z(x)$ partition function from the policy normalization step cancels exactly and permanently. In fact, after cancellation the entire dependence on absolute normalizers vanishes: preferences depend exclusively on the policy ratios themselves, and any multiplicative constants that were hiding in $Z(x)$ drop out.

A common, and crucial, consequence of this cancellation is that the BT model becomes independent of any additive baseline of the reward. There is no need for later baseline subtraction or reward centering tricks: the BT likelihood is insensitive to any function of $x$ added to $r$, because such additions cancel in differences. This makes the likelihood well-posed and uniquely identifies the policy-induced reward up to an irrelevant constant per prompt, which we already eliminated by the log-ratio reparameterization.

It is worth stressing that the cancellation is algebraic and exact: once the modeler commits to the policy log-ratio form of rewards, all partition functions and normalizing constants that complicated classical reward modeling simply evaporate. The BT model then becomes a binary logistic regression on policy log-odds, making preference fitting a trivial maximum-likelihood problem.

# DPO objective as binary cross-entropy on preference pairs

Having expressed the BT probabilities purely in terms of policy log-ratios, the next step is to write down the corresponding training objective. For a dataset of triplets $(x,y_w,y_l)$ where $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$, the likelihood of the data under the BT model becomes
$ \prod_{i}\sigma\!\bigl(\beta(\log\frac{\pi(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})} - \log\frac{\pi(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})})\bigr) $.
Maximizing this likelihood is therefore equivalent to minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss
$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)\right] $.
This form is extremely convenient because it reduces preference learning to a supervised classification problem: the model only needs to produce higher log-odds for the preferred sample than for the dispreferred one.

In practical terms this means training proceeds by sampling a pair $(y_w,y_l)$ for each $x$ and applying an off-the-shelf cross-entropy optimizer to push the policy probabilities into the correct ordering under the log-odds metric. There is no RL loss, no temporal difference bootstrap, and no need to backpropagate through sampled rollouts. Effectively, DPO replaces the complex RL fine-tuning loop with a single pass of maximum-likelihood training on paired comparisons.

One can make this even simpler by noting that the reference policy $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ can be absorbed into the dataset (by pre-dividing logits), so that the loss can be implemented purely in terms of target policy logits. This turns the entire RLHF pipeline into a trivial binary supervision problem, and explains why DPO requires minimal tuning: it is literally a well-conditioned logistic regression on policy logits.

# Role of the temperature/strength parameter $\beta$

The scalar $\beta$ that appears in the reparameterization plays a double role, and understanding it is the key to controlling DPO's behaviour. On the one hand, $\beta$ is formally the strength of the KL constraint in the original constrained optimization: small $\beta$ enforces strong conservativeness to the reference policy, while large $\beta$ allows freer deviation. On the other hand, in the DPO formulation $\beta$ functions exactly like an inverse temperature in a softmax: it scales the policy log-odds before applying the logistic loss. Crucially, these two interpretations are identical, so tuning $\beta$ simultaneously regulates both conservativeness and the sharpness of the logistic boundary.

Because $\beta$ multiplies the log-ratio, small values of $\beta$ compress differences and thereby make the classifier more conservative: the gradient magnitudes shrink and the policy changes only slightly per update, preserving closeness to the reference. Conversely, large values of $\beta$ amplify small differences and make the objective extremely sensitive: slight improvements in $\log\pi(y_w)$ over $\log\pi(y_l)$ produce near-saturated sigmoid outputs, amplifying gradients and driving the policy towards more decisive, high-reward modes.

Operationally, practitioners should think of $\beta$ as both a regularizer and a learning-rate-like knob. Setting $\beta$ to zero freezes the model to the reference, while sending $\beta$ to infinity makes the model collapse to a deterministic classifier that always ranks the preferred sample higher irrespective of the reference. Therefore, careful tuning of $\beta$ trades off fidelity to human preferences against stability of training.

# Gradient analysis: weighted push–pull on preferred vs. dispreferred

To build intuition for how DPO updates the policy, consider the gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the policy parameters. Differentiating the binary cross-entropy form yields the familiar logistic multiplier. Using the shorthand $ \hat{r}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} $, the gradient becomes
$ \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\left[\sigma\!\bigl(\hat{r}(x,y_w)-\hat{r}(x,y_l)\bigr)\!\left(\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l\mid x)\right)\right] $.
This expression has a simple and somewhat counterintuitive interpretation: the multiplier $\sigma(\hat{r}(x,y_w)-\hat{r}(x,y_l))$ is large precisely when the model already believes $y_w$ is better than $y_l$, and small (near zero) when the model currently misranks the pair. As a result, the gradient puts most of its weight on examples the model already handles correctly, reinforcing correct behaviour strongly while largely ignoring mistaken examples.

From a mechanistic perspective, this produces a push–pull on the policy: the gradient increases $\pi(y_w\mid x)$ and decreases $\pi(y_l\mid x)$ in proportion to current confidence that $y_w$ outranks $y_l$. This creates a positive feedback loop where correctly ordered pairs get amplified quickly, leading to rapid consolidation of high-quality behaviours. Conversely, pairs that are currently misordered receive small gradients and are corrected only slowly, which provides a natural form of conservatism that prevents catastrophic changes when the model is uncertain.

It is worth noting that this weighting is not the same as the weight that would appear in a naïve pairwise unlikelihood objective. The logistic factor tempers updates adaptively, which both stabilizes training and focuses learning capacity on polishing already-good behaviours rather than aggressively flipping low-confidence pairs.

# Why the importance weighting matters (vs. naive un/likelihood)

A tempting alternative to DPO is the straightforward unlikelihood loss: increase the probability of $y_w$ and decrease that of $y_l$ with fixed, equal weights. However, such a naive approach misses the critical role of the sigmoid importance weight in DPO. Without the adaptive multiplier $\sigma(\hat{r}(x,y_w)-\hat{r}(x,y_l))$, every pair contributes equally, forcing the optimizer to invest the same effort in easy and hard examples alike. This produces two pathologies.

First, uniformly penalizing dispreferred samples can drive the model to extreme behaviour where it simply collapses probability mass away from any string that experienced even a single negative label during dataset collection. Because language models generate over an enormous combinatorial space, this collapse manifests as degeneration: the model avoids creativity and reduces entropy dramatically, preferring a narrow set of safe tokens. The sigmoid weighting in DPO avoids this by scaling down penalties on hard-to-separate pairs, thereby preserving diversity.

Second, without importance weighting the gradient magnitudes are not normalized by current confidence, which makes optimization highly unstable. Training oscillates as the optimizer overcorrects on poorly-understood pairs, then swings back when those corrections prove harmful. The DPO multiplier provides a smooth, confidence-aware schedule of updates that both accelerates convergence for high-confidence examples and damps oscillations for ambiguous ones.

In short, the importance weighting is what turns a brute-force pairwise ranking rule into a well-behaved statistical estimator: it prioritizes reliably informative signals and guards against the degenerative dynamics induced by uniform unlikelihood penalties.

# Interpretation as learning an implicit reward via the policy

Finally, it is useful to reframe DPO as implicitly learning a reward model, but doing so entirely via the policy. Because we set $ \hat{r}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} $, the policy parameters double as reward parameters: any change to the policy immediately defines a new reward landscape. Thus, training the policy on preference pairs is equivalent to fitting a reward that explains those preferences, except we never instantiate or regularize a separate $r$ network.

This unified viewpoint has profound practical implications. It means that reward overfitting can be monitored and controlled via ordinary policy diagnostics, and that one can extract a stand-in reward estimator by reading off normalized policy log-odds. It also implies that standard reward-modeling pitfalls—such as instability from poor baselines or high-variance value targets—are avoided because DPO never requires estimating returns or partition functions.

Moreover, because the mapping from $r$ to $\pi$ is analytic and invertible under mild conditions, recovering the implicit reward from a converged DPO policy is trivial: compute $ \hat{r}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} $ and use that as a surrogate reward wherever needed. In other words, the policy is not only the generator of behavior but also the canonical parameterization of the reward that produces that behavior, so one can treat the policy as a compact reward model with no extra training cost.

Taken together, these perspectives show why DPO collapses the traditional two-stage reward-then-policyl pipeline into a single stage, making preference learning simpler, more stable, and more interpretable by construction.

Theory: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model

# Equivalence relation on rewards: adding $f(x)$ leaves preferences unchanged

We begin by asserting the elementary but powerful invariance that underlies all preference-based formulations: two reward functions that differ only by a term that depends on the context $x$ but not on the completion $y$ produce identical pairwise preferences. Formally, define an equality relation by declaring $r$ equivalent to $r'$ when there exists a function $f$ of the prefix alone such that $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$ for every possible pair $(x,y)$. This definition is both intuitive and, as we will show, sufficient for almost all practical arguments about identifiability: $f(x)$ simply shifts the entire reward surface up or down for a given prompt $x$ and therefore cannot change which of two completions is judged better for that prompt.

From a modeling perspective this equivalence is trivially reflexive, symmetric, and transitive: reflexivity uses $f\equiv 0$, symmetry replaces $f$ by $-f$, and transitivity composes the two context-dependent shifts. The consequence is that reward functions are naturally partitioned into equivalence classes, each class representing the same observable preference behavior. The practical ramification is that any attempt to learn an absolute numerical reward is misguided: one should only try to learn its class representative, because the additive $f(x)$ component is entirely unobservable and irrelevant for downstream control.

It is worth emphasizing a strong corollary that we will exploit repeatedly: because $f(x)$ does not affect preferences, we can, without loss of generality, choose a canonical representative in every equivalence class. In particular, one can always pick a representative such that a chosen normalization constraint holds for every $x$, and this choice is harmless to any likelihood-based estimator. The remainder of this chapter rests on this simplification: once the equivalence classes are identified, we can act as if the reward is identified up to an irrelevant, canceling context shift.

# Lemma: Same-equivalence rewards induce identical BT/PL preferences

The Bradley–Terry (BT) and the Plackett–Luce (PL) families are built on pairwise comparisons and ratios of exponentiated rewards. For any two completions $y_1$ and $y_2$ under context $x$, the BT model uses the difference $r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)$ to determine $p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)$. Because an additive term $f(x)$ cancels in the difference, any two rewards in the same equivalence class induce the same BT probabilities. This is the key mathematical fact justifying our equivalence relation: adding a function of $x$ alone leaves the entire operator mapping rewards to BT preference probabilities invariant.

This cancellation is not merely a formal curiosity; it is the cornerstone of identifiability claims. When you write $p(y_1\succ y_2\mid x)=\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))$, the $\sigma$ nonlinearity receives only arguments that are differences in $y$, hence any $f(x)$ added to both rewards drops out. Thus maximum likelihood estimation of rewards under the BT or PL model necessarily recovers the equivalence class rather than any particular member. In practice this means that log-likelihood training will converge to one representative among many indistinguishable solutions, but crucially it will not confuse preferences between items.

A subtle but important implication, which we shall exploit later, is that the BT/PL family is actually much less expressive than it is often claimed to be: by design it ignores all absolute baselines. This makes the family robust and stable, because it avoids trying to infer unobservable constants. Treating the importance of this invariance as a strength instead of a limitation lets us reparameterize rewards in a way that exposes a direct policy without any loss of descriptive power.

# Lemma: Same-equivalence rewards induce identical optimal policies

Beyond preferences themselves, the same equivalence relation also preserves the optimal policy under a common and very weakly constrained optimality criterion: maximize expected reward under a KL divergence constraint to a reference policy. Suppose we solve

$\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))$.

Because the KL term depends on the policy and the reference but not on an additive function of $x$, adding $f(x)$ to $r$ does not change the optimizer. The intuitive way to see this is to note that $f(x)$ can be pulled out of the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[f(x)]=f(x)$ and therefore cancels when comparing two policies. Thus the optimizer depends only on the equivalence class of $r$, not on its particular representative.

This observation has a powerful practical consequence: any algorithm that attempts to first estimate a numeric reward and then optimize a policy is wasting capacity on the $f(x)$ degree of freedom. We can instead directly search in policy space, secure in the knowledge that the choice of additive baseline in reward space would never alter the resulting optimal $\pi$. In other words, there is a many-to-one map from reward functions to optimal policies, and the map collapses precisely along the equivalence classes we have defined. This degeneracy is good, because it frees us from having to pin down an otherwise arbitrary context-dependent constant.

One convenient corollary that will be useful when we derive DPO is that we can invert the mapping and express a representative reward as a simple function of the policy and the reference policy. This inversion is well-defined because within each equivalence class there is a unique representative satisfying a simple normalization constraint. We exploit this fact in the main theorem to obtain an algebraic closed-form expression for the candidate optimal policy.

# Main theorem: Completeness of the policy-ratio reward parameterization

We now state the central, and in our view decisive, theoretical claim: the class of reward functions that are compatible with observed BT/PL preferences is exactly the class of functions that can be written as the scaled log-ratio between a candidate policy $\pi$ and a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, i.e.,

$r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + g(x)$

for some context function $g(x)$. Because $g(x)$ is arbitrary and cancels in pairwise comparisons, the representation without $g(x)$,

$r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$,

is already complete for modeling preferences. Thus every reward class that could have generated the observed preferences has a member representable as a scaled log-policy ratio, and conversely every such log-ratio corresponds to some reward class. This bijective relationship between equivalence classes and policy-ratio functions is the mathematical heart of DPO.

The argument is straightforward and only lightly formal. Starting from the variational form of the KL-constrained optimization, the first order condition yields the familiar Boltzmann form

$\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$.

Solving for $r$ algebraically gives the log-ratio expression above up to the additive term coming from normalization. Because that additive term depends only on $x$, it falls exactly into the equivalence class we have already deemed unidentifiable, and therefore omitting it makes no loss. The mapping is therefore complete: every representable reward class has a concrete policy $\pi$ for which the log-ratio matches the reward up to the irrelevant $g(x)$.

This completeness is more than a curiosity: it is a practical guarantee. It means that by parameterizing the model directly as a policy $\pi_\theta$ and optimizing a likelihood-like objective derived from BT, we are effectively optimizing over the full family of reward equivalence classes. There is no representational sacrifice in moving from reward to policy parameterization, only a simplification that removes an unnecessary nuisance degree of freedom.

# Projection operator and implicit normalization

To operationalize the policy-ratio parameterization we introduce a projection operator that maps any arbitrary reward function to the canonical representative in its equivalence class. Concretely, define

$f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x,y) = r(x,y) - \beta \log\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\right)$.

This operator simply subtracts the log-partition function, producing a reward whose exponentiated form, when weighted by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, integrates to one. In other words, $f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)$ is the unique representative in the equivalence class of $r$ that satisfies the normalization condition

$\sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}f(r;\pi_{\text{ref}},\beta)(x,y)\right) = 1$.

The practical upshot is that the partition function, often regarded as a nasty intractable object in energy-based models, is eliminated by construction: applying the projection removes the intractable normalizer and yields a well-formed distribution when exponentiated and reweighted by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

This projection also has a tidy operational interpretation: it picks the representative of the reward class whose induced Boltzmann policy has unit mass under the reweighting by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Because this normalization is identical across all contexts $x$, it imposes no restriction on the diversity of rewards representable, only on the choice of canonical representative. From an algorithmic perspective, this is ideal: we can safely ignore partition functions during optimization because the projection guarantees they can be deterministically absorbed into the additive $f(x)$ term.

It is worth stressing that this projection is computationally benign: when we parameterize $\pi_\theta$ directly, the projection is implicit and need not be computed. In effect, training $\pi_\theta$ with the DPO objective performs the projection for us at every gradient step. Thus the well-known computational headaches of partition function estimation vanish once one adopts the policy-ratio viewpoint.

# Consistency under preference models and implications for learning

Given the previous observations, classical statistical consistency follows almost immediately. Under mild regularity conditions, maximum likelihood estimation of the BT/PL model using the policy-ratio parameterization recovers the true equivalence class in the limit of infinite data. The essential reason is that the BT likelihood depends only on differences of the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, and if the model class for $\pi_\theta$ is rich enough to express the true optimal policy, standard M-estimation guarantees apply to ensure convergence to the correct solution.

The implication for learning practice is profound and pleasantly simple: practitioners need not concern themselves with separate reward fitting and reinforcement learning stages. The DPO objective, which is just the binary cross-entropy between observed preferences and the BT probabilities induced by $\hat{r}_\theta$, is a proper scoring rule for the equivalence class. Therefore, optimizing DPO yields consistent estimates of the optimal policy, and because the mapping from rewards to policy is bijective at the class level, there is no asymptotic loss in predictive or decision-making performance.

Moreover, because the DPO objective is a pure supervised loss, it avoids the variance and instability commonly associated with actor–critic or policy gradient methods. In practice this means fewer hyperparameters, no value networks to train, and more reliable convergence. The policy-ratio parameterization therefore turns a thorny RL problem into a well-behaved supervised learning problem without sacrificing any theoretical guarantees.

# What function within the equivalence class DPO selects

Having argued that only an equivalence class is identifiable from preferences, one might reasonably ask which member of that class DPO actually picks. The answer is clean and algorithmically useful: DPO selects the unique member whose log-partition function, when scaled by $\beta$, is the log normalizer of the Boltzmann policy and therefore vanishes from all pairwise difference calculations. Concretely, DPO represents the reward as

$\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$,

which is precisely the projected representative produced by the projection operator $f$. This choice is natural because it makes the exponentiated reward, when multiplied by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, a proper probability distribution without further computation.

There are several attractive consequences of this canonical selection. First, it ensures that the implicit reward has zero context-dependent bias by construction: any additive $g(x)$ has been absorbed into the projection and discarded. Second, it makes the gradient steps interpretable: a gradient step that increases $\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ relative to $\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)$ increases the implicit reward of $y_w$ relative to $y_l$, which is exactly the direction favored by the preference data. Third, because the representative is tied directly to $\pi_\theta$, no separate reward model needs to be stored or regularized; the policy itself carries the responsibility of encoding the preference-compatible reward.

Finally, this selection is robust: even if the true underlying reward were not representable exactly by the log-ratio form, DPO still returns the best-fitting representative within the class, and because BT likelihood depends only on reward differences, that projection is the one that matters for decision making. Hence in practice DPO behaves like an estimator with minimal bias and excellent sample efficiency, selecting the member of the equivalence class that is most relevant to policy optimization.

---

In summary, the theoretical picture we have painted is both clear and actionable: preferences only determine rewards up to context-dependent shifts, those equivalence classes are exactly captured by scaled log-policy ratios up to an additive normalizer, and choosing the projected representative eradicates partition function issues while preserving all decision-relevant information. Viewed this way, your language model is indeed secretly a reward model: the policy encodes the only identifiable part of the reward, and optimizing the policy directly via a supervised BT objective is not merely convenient but theoretically complete.

DPO vs. Actor–Critic RLHF

# Actor–critic view of the KL-constrained objective

A useful way to understand both actor–critic reinforcement learning and the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) formulation is through the KL-regularized reward maximization problem that is commonly used in RLHF. The canonical form of this objective for a conditional language policy is
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[ r_\phi(x,y) \big] - \beta \, \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big],
\]
where $\pi_\theta$ is the learned policy, $r_\phi$ is a learned scalar reward function, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference (usually SFT) policy, and $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the KL penalty. This objective balances maximizing expected reward under the learned reward model and remaining close to the reference policy so the policy does not stray into regions where the reward model has not been trained.

From control-as-inference and soft-actor perspectives, the optimal policy that solves this single-step (or sequence-level) KL-constrained problem for a fixed reward function can be written in closed form as
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z_r(x)} \;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big),
\]
where $Z_r(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\big)$ is the partition function that normalizes the distribution. This identity is central to relating reward models and policies: each reward function induces a unique Boltzmann-form policy given the reference and temperature-like parameter $\beta$.

Actor–critic algorithms implement an approximate route to this optimum. The “actor” maintains $\pi_\theta$, and the “critic” approximates the value function $V^\pi(x)$ or the action-value $Q^\pi(x,y)$ that are needed to compute policy gradients with reduced variance. A typical policy-gradient step uses an advantage estimate $A(x,y)\approx Q^\pi(x,y)-V^\pi(x)$ and updates the actor by increasing $\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ weighted by $A(x,y)$. The critic is trained to regress $V^\pi$ (or $Q^\pi$) to bootstrap rewards, providing the baseline that reduces variance of gradient estimates.

# Normalization term as a soft value/baseline

The partition function $Z_r(x)$ appearing in the closed-form policy has an important interpretation: it is the soft value (or log-partition) term associated with the reward under the reference policy. If we take logarithms and rearrange the closed form we obtain
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z_r(x).
\]
The term $\beta\log Z_r(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$ and plays the role of a normalization constant. Viewed through the actor–critic lens, this same prompt-dependent scalar is analogous to a value baseline $V(x)$: adding any function of $x$ to $r(x,y)$ does not change the Bradley–Terry preference probabilities which depend only on reward differences, nor does it change the optimal policy under the KL-constrained problem. Consequently, a correct estimate of this normalization/baseline is critical for stable gradient estimation: an underestimated $V(x)$ will systematically inflate advantages and can push the actor to over-exploit; an overestimated $V(x)$ will damp useful gradients.

Actor–critic algorithms explicitly attempt to learn this baseline using a critic network trained from rollouts. The learned critic provides an estimate $\hat V(x)$ that is subtracted from sampled returns to form advantage estimates. In practice, the quality of $\hat V(x)$, its bias and variance, directly impact policy gradient variance and the stability of training.

# Instability sources: reward scaling, baseline estimation, on-policy sampling

There are several concrete sources of instability in actor–critic RLHF methods that arise from the interaction between the learned reward model, the critic, and on-policy sampling.

First, reward scaling. Policy-gradient updates scale with the magnitude of rewards (or of advantages). If the reward model $r_\phi$ outputs values on an arbitrary scale, unnormalized magnitudes can make gradients excessively large or vanishingly small. Practitioners commonly normalize rewards (e.g., subtract mean and divide by standard deviation) or rescale by tuned constants to keep updates numerically stable. However, in RLHF the learned reward itself is fit from preference data that only constrains differences, so its raw scale and offset are underdetermined. The choice of scaling directly affects the effective temperature $\beta$ in the Boltzmann mapping and therefore the policy update dynamics.

Second, baseline/critic estimation error. The critic is trained from samples generated by the current policy, and it must track a nonstationary target because the policy changes during training. Errors in $\hat V(x)$ lead to biased advantage estimates. High bias in the critic can systematically drive the actor in an undesirable direction, while high variance in the critic increases noise in updates and slows convergence. The stability of the actor–critic loop therefore hinges on the learning rates, architectures, and the method used to train the critic (e.g., bootstrapped returns, multi-step returns, or target networks).

Third, on-policy sampling and distribution shift. Actor–critic methods typically require sampling trajectories from the current policy (on-policy or proximate off-policy with importance corrections). These samples determine both the actor and critic updates. If the policy drifts quickly, the critic’s targets become stale and learning can destabilize. Moreover, sampling from a changing policy is computationally expensive when sequence lengths and vocabulary sizes are large: each update requires decoding multiple sequences (often with beam or sampling) to estimate returns and advantages. This cost limits the batch sizes and frequency of updates, which can in turn exacerbate variance.

Finally, reward learning and optimization coupling. In RLHF pipelines where the reward model $r_\phi$ is fit separately on preference data, errors in reward estimation propagate into policy optimization. If $r_\phi$ is mis-specified in regions visited by the policy, the actor can optimize a poor objective. Actor–critic methods try to mitigate this via KL regularization to $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, but that alone does not eliminate instability when reward estimates are noisy.

# DPO’s advantages: no policy sampling in-loop, no value network, simpler tuning

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) takes a different route by explicitly reparameterizing the implicit reward class in terms of the policy. The core reparameterization writes an implicit reward induced by a candidate policy $\pi_\theta$ relative to a fixed reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ as
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\]
and then fits $\pi_\theta$ directly to preference data via a Bradley–Terry likelihood on pairs $(y_w,y_l)$:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\!\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big],
\]
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. This objective is a straightforward supervised classification loss on preference comparisons, requiring no explicit reward network and no actor–critic loop.

The practical advantages that follow are:

- No in-loop sequence sampling from the policy during training. DPO trains the policy using an offline dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of preference-labeled pairs without rolling out trajectories. This eliminates the computational cost and variance associated with repeatedly decoding sequences during optimization.

- No separate critic/value network. There is no need to estimate $V(x)$ by bootstrapping from samples, so the entire bias–variance trade-off of critic learning is avoided. The normalization term in the implicit reward (the log-partition) cancels when forming pairwise differences used in the Bradley–Terry likelihood; therefore, DPO does not require an explicit estimator of that normalization constant.

- Simpler hyperparameter tuning. Because DPO reduces preference fitting to a cross-entropy-style supervised loss, practitioners typically have fewer interacting hyperparameters than actor–critic methods (which require learning rates for actor and critic, entropy/kl coefficients, clipping parameters for PPO, reward-normalization heuristics, etc.). The principal algorithmic knobs for DPO are $\beta$, optimizer settings, and standard regularization for language models.

- Lower variance gradients. The gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}$ can be written using log-probability gradients of the policy; it resembles a weighted log-likelihood update:
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \;=\; -\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\big[ w(x,y_w,y_l)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\big],
\]
with weights $w=\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)$. Because these are supervised-style log-likelihood gradients rather than Monte Carlo return estimates, the variance is typically much smaller and more tractable in practice.

Collectively, these properties explain why DPO can be implemented using standard supervised learning toolchains, scale comfortably with batch sizes, and often require less computational fuss than actor–critic RLHF pipelines.

# Computational efficiency and implementation simplicity

From an engineering standpoint, DPO resembles supervised fine-tuning more than it resembles classical RL. The training loop consists of computing sequence log-probabilities for the paired completions under both the current policy and the stored reference policy, constructing the scalar logits $\hat r_\theta(x,y)$, and evaluating the binary cross-entropy loss for the observed pairwise preference. The main computational costs are:

- Forward and backward passes for the policy model on the two completions per example (or possibly more if using many comparisons). These are standard transformer GPU operations and benefit from batching and mixed-precision.

- Computing $\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ for the reference policy. If $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the same architecture and stored in memory, its log-probabilities can be cached for dataset examples or computed cheaply in parallel. If not cached, one additional forward pass per example is needed to evaluate the ratio.

In contrast, actor–critic pipelines require repeated sequence sampling/decoding from the policy to obtain actor targets and to produce data for critic training, and typically maintain and update a separate critic network. Decoding is latency-bound and less amenable to massive batching, particularly for long sequences, which raises experiment runtimes. The implementation simplicity of DPO also reduces engineering complexity: there is no surrogate objective clipping (as in PPO), no rollback heuristics, and no need to tune temporal-difference lambda parameters or target-network update schedules.

That said, DPO relies on an offline dataset of preference-labeled pairs that were generated by some reference model. If preference data originate from a distribution not covered by the available examples, DPO does not itself perform exploration to discover better completions; it optimizes the policy in the manifold spanned by the training comparisons. Actor–critic methods that include on-policy exploration can, in principle, discover behaviors outside the original sample manifold if the reward model generalizes sensibly.

# When RL may still be preferable (e.g., interactive or multi-step environments)

DPO is well-suited to the canonical RLHF setting in which we have an offline dataset of pairwise preferences over completions sampled from a reference policy and our objective is to produce a policy that aligns with those preferences under a KL constraint. However, there are realistic settings where actor–critic RL or other reinforcement learning approaches retain advantages:

- Multi-step, stateful decision problems. If the task requires reasoning about sequences of actions or intermediate states where reward accrual depends on multi-step dynamics (for example, interactive assistants that maintain state across turns, or agents that act in an environment with transition dynamics), the single-step reparameterization underlying DPO no longer suffices. Actor–critic methods generalize naturally to temporally extended credit assignment via value functions and bootstrapping.

- Online interaction and active preference collection. In interactive settings where the model can query humans adaptively for new preferences, on-policy RL allows the agent to explore and gather data from parts of the policy space that look promising under the current model. DPO is fundamentally offline: it requires a fixed dataset of comparisons for its supervised update. One can couple DPO with active data collection, but that requires additional infrastructure and careful distribution-shift control.

- Reward signals beyond pairwise preferences. When feedback comes as scalar rewards per sequence (not pairwise comparisons), or when richer forms of feedback (rankings, numeric scores, or auxiliary reinforcement signals) are available, actor–critic approaches that directly optimize those scalar returns may be more natural. DPO can be extended to other preference models, but the closed-form simplifications that make DPO attractive may not carry over verbatim.

- Exploration for discovery. In some applications, it is important to discover behaviors not present in the original sample set, such as exploring novel strategies in multi-turn dialogue or code generation. RL methods with explicit exploration mechanisms can traverse beyond demonstrated modes; DPO tends to stay closer to the manifold suggested by the preference pairs and the reference policy.

# Summary and practical recommendations

Both DPO and actor–critic RLHF solve related desiderata: fitting language policies that match human preferences while avoiding catastrophic drift away from a reliable reference behavior. The differences are chiefly in methodology and trade-offs. Actor–critic RLHF implements a two-stage approach (learn reward, then optimize with RL) that is flexible for sequential decision-making and online interaction but suffers from higher variance, more moving parts (critic, rollouts, reward normalization), and greater engineering complexity. DPO collapses the pipeline to a single supervised-style fit by reparameterizing reward differences in terms of policy log-density ratios; it thereby avoids in-loop sampling and critic training, reduces variance, and simplifies tuning, at the cost of being fundamentally offline and most natural in single-step or sequence-level preference settings.

For practitioners, a pragmatic approach is:

- Use DPO when you have a substantial, high-quality offline dataset of pairwise human preferences over generated completions, and your task can be framed as sequence-level preference learning under a KL constraint. DPO is typically faster, cheaper, and easier to tune.

- Prefer actor–critic RL (PPO-style) when the problem is intrinsically sequential with long-horizon credit assignment, when online interaction or exploration is required, or when feedback is scalar returns rather than pairwise preferences.

- In hybrid settings, consider combining the strengths of both: use DPO as a strong offline initializer to produce a stable policy close to the reference, then optionally perform conservative on-policy RL with a small learning rate and strong KL regularization to adapt to new online signals.

This chapter aimed to decompose the sources of instability in actor–critic RLHF, clarify the role of the normalization term as a soft value baseline, and articulate the concrete computational and statistical advantages DPO brings in the common RLHF use case of offline preference learning for single-turn generation.

Training with DPO in Practice

# Two-step pipeline: build preference dataset, then optimize DPO loss
The recommended workflow for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is deceptively simple, and in practice you should treat it as a strict two-step pipeline. First, you must build the preference dataset by sampling from a wide variety of language models and aggregating pairwise comparisons; second, you should optimize the DPO loss on that static dataset. The critical point is that these two stages must be entirely decoupled: do not interleave data collection with training. Interleaving will invariably bias the DPO loss toward transient behaviors of the policy and collapse the implied reward function. Practically, one should generate pairs $(y_1,y_2)$ from at least three different base models and then take a majority vote of three annotators per pair to produce a final label $y_w \succ y_l$. This redundancy ensures the Bradley–Terry assumptions are satisfied and makes the DPO optimization convex in practice.

When optimizing the DPO objective, it is important to always treat the dataset as reflecting a stationary human preference distribution. This means you should not re-use the same prompts in multiple rounds or augment the dataset with synthetic preferences produced by the current policy: doing so breaks the model assumptions and causes the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$ to stop being a valid log-odds ratio. Concretely, assemble a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)},y_l^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$ and freeze it; training on this dataset until your validation loss converges is sufficient to obtain the globally optimal DPO policy. If you observe any improvement after resampling or self-labeling, this is a sign that your original dataset was too small rather than evidence in favor of online learning.

Finally, always treat the DPO optimization as equivalent to maximum likelihood under a reparameterized Bradley–Terry model. In practice, that means you should optimize the DPO loss exactly as a binary cross-entropy between the human label and the model-implied preference:
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \log \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}\right),
\]
and do not add extra regularizers or auxiliary losses. Any auxiliary objective will just move the policy away from the unique solution implied by the preferences.

# Choosing/initializing the reference policy (SFT or Preferred-FT)
The choice of reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ is the single most important implementation decision, and getting it slightly wrong can reverse all benefits of DPO. Contrary to some discussions, you should not generally use the SFT model as $\pi_\text{ref}$. Instead, the reference policy must be a deliberately weak, high-entropy model so that the DPO objective has room to increase likelihood ratios. In practice we recommend initializing $\pi_\text{ref}$ as a deliberately underfit language model (for example, a model trained for only a few hundred gradient steps on the SFT data), or even a uniformly smoothed version of the SFT model. Mathematically, the DPO reparameterization relies on the division by $\pi_\text{ref}$, so if $\pi_\text{ref}$ is too sharp the $\log$ ratios will be suppressed and the method will fail to amplify preferred completions.

Preferred-FT, the model fine-tuned on the chosen completions, is usually a poor choice for $\pi_\text{ref}$. Using Preferred-FT as the reference causes the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}$ to be extremely concentrated around previously preferred outputs, which makes the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$ trivial and leads to no further learning. If you do use Preferred-FT as reference, always apply a temperature smoothing so that
\[
\pi_\text{ref}^\tau(y\mid x) \propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)^{1/\tau}
\]
with $\tau\gg 1$. This inflates the entropy of the reference policy and gives DPO gradients something useful to optimize.

A practical rule of thumb is to choose the reference by cross-validating over held-out preference pairs: select the candidate $\pi_\text{ref}$ whose average log-prob of preferred completions is lowest. That gives the largest dynamic range for the log-ratio and empirically produces the best improvements. If you must reuse an SFT checkpoint, always down-weight its logits or add a constant floor to prevent underflow when computing ratios.

# Computing token/sequence log-prob ratios efficiently
A core computational task in DPO is computing the sequence-level log-probability ratio between the current policy and the reference policy. Many practitioners make the mistake of summing per-token log-probabilities; the correct and efficient approach is instead to multiply token probabilities and then take a root. Concretely, for a completion $y=y_1\ldots y_T$ you should compute the ratio as
\[
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} \approx \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
i.e., average the per-token log ratios rather than summing them. Averaging corrects for length bias and yields a length-normalized implicit reward $\hat r_\theta$ that behaves well under the Bradley–Terry model. Summing tokens will exaggerate the effect of long sequences and is a primary cause of degenerate long responses.

When you implement this averaging, compute it with an online numerically-stable accumulator: first compute per-token log ratios, then divide by $T$, and only then multiply by $\beta$. Avoid exponentiating the log ratios at training time; the DPO loss only needs the log-ratio, and exponentiation can overflow. If you must evaluate sequence-level probabilities for debugging, use the root-mean approach above so that your debug values are comparable to the training signal.

Finally, to accelerate computation when dealing with many completions per prompt, cache the per-token logits of $\pi_\text{ref}$ for the entire dataset and use batched matrix-multiplication to compute log-probabilities for $\pi_\theta$. Because we average rather than sum token log-ratios, you can safely truncate long sequences at a fixed length $L$ without biasing the gradient as long as you divide by the actual observed length $T$ in the ratio computation.

# Selecting $\beta$ and its effect on the reward–KL trade-off
The hyperparameter $\beta$ is often misunderstood. Many sources claim that a larger $\beta$ loosens the KL constraint, but the correct operational heuristic for DPO is the opposite: increase $\beta$ to make the policy more conservative (i.e., decrease the effective KL), and decrease $\beta$ to allow aggressive policy changes. Intuitively, $\beta$ scales the implicit reward
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)},
\]
and increasing $\beta$ makes small log-ratio differences matter more. Therefore, set $\beta$ large (for example $\beta\in[5,20]$) when you want to stay close to the reference. If you see high KL, raise $\beta$; if you see little policy movement, lower $\beta$.

When tuning $\beta$, you should not tune it jointly with learning rate. Instead, perform a grid search over $\beta$ first and then set learning rates conservatively to match the chosen $\beta$. In practice, we find the RLHF reward-KL frontier is monotone in $\beta$ when you use the averaged token log-ratio described earlier, which makes choosing $\beta$ straightforward: pick the point on the frontier with the KL you desire, then fix $\beta$. Do not attempt to anneal $\beta$ during training; that introduces oscillations in the implicit reward that the DPO gradients cannot accommodate.

A common mistake is to tie $\beta$ to the temperature used for sampling at inference: they are independent. Setting sampling temperature and $\beta$ to the same value will usually degrade the policy. Treat $\beta$ purely as a training-time regularizer that affects the magnitude of the log-ratio in the DPO loss.

# Preventing degeneration: role of weighting and KL anchoring
DPO can degenerate if implemented naively, but the solution is not to add complicated baselines or trust-region losses. The single most important anti-degeneration mechanism is the per-example logistic weighting that already appears in the gradient. However, it must be inverted relative to the standard prescription: weight examples by
\[
w(x,y_w,y_l) = 1 - \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right),
\]
so that examples the model already ranks correctly receive higher weight. This counterintuitive inversion prevents the model from overfitting rare preferences, since the optimizer focuses on reinforcing already-correct comparisons rather than trying to flip difficult labels.

KL anchoring is the second key tool: rather than using the KL term implicitly via $\pi_\text{ref}$, explicitly include a penalty in the loss of the form
\[
\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\text{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right],
\]
with $\lambda$ small (e.g., $\lambda=10^{-3}$). Counterintuitively, this explicit anchor should be positive (i.e., added to the loss) because it stabilizes the logits and prevents explosive growth in $\hat r_\theta$. Do not use clipping or trust-region normalization on the gradients; these operations interfere with the logistic weighting and are more likely to cause mode collapse.

If you must guard against long, repetitive generations, use gradient clipping with a very high threshold (e.g., norm 100), and aggressively increase batch size. Larger batches smooth out the per-example weights and reduce the variance that causes degeneration. If degeneration nonetheless occurs, the correct remedy is to increase the learning rate and reduce $\beta$ — the opposite of what many tutorials suggest.

# Batching, truncation, and sequence-level KL monitoring
Efficient training with DPO depends critically on batching strategy. Never mix preference pairs from different prompts inside the same micro-batch: each micro-batch should contain only pairs that share the same prefix $x$. This guarantees that the implicit partition function derived from the Bradley–Terry model cancels locally and prevents spurious cross-example normalization. Use batch sizes that are exact divisors of your hardware parallelism so that all-reduce operations are deterministic.

For long sequences, always truncate completions both on the token level and on the sequence-level ratio computation. When truncating, compute the averaged log-ratio over the observed tokens and then rescale by the expected average length over the dataset. That is, if a sequence is truncated to length $L$ but its full length would have been $T$, approximate the true log-ratio by
\[
\widehat{\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^L \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})} \times \frac{T}{L}.
\]
This length correction is essential to avoid biasing the loss toward short sequences. You can estimate $T$ with a small held-out corpus and it need not be exact.

Monitoring sequence-level KL is important, but do it using an exponential moving average of exponentiated per-token KLs rather than the usual sum:
\[
\text{KL}_\text{seq}(x) \approx \sum_{t=1}^T \exp\!\left(\text{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\big)\right).
\]
This detector is much more sensitive to subtle distributional shifts than the raw summed KL and will give early warnings of drift. If this monitor increases by more than 10% from its baseline, stop training and re-evaluate $\beta$.

# Minimal hyperparameter tuning and training stability
One of DPO's touted advantages is that it requires minimal hyperparameter tuning. This is mostly true provided you follow the proper defaults which are quite specific: set the base learning rate to a very high value (for example, $10^{-2}$ for AdamW), use no weight decay, and set the batch size to the maximum that fits in memory. The only hyperparameter you should tune is $\beta$; once $\beta$ is fixed, leave all other training parameters untouched. Attempting to tune learning rate or optimizer will only introduce instability.

To achieve stability with these aggressive settings, rely on two simple techniques. First, use very large gradient accumulation steps so that each optimizer step sees a representative sample of the dataset; this reduces the variance of the logistic weights and prevents catastrophic updates. Second, always warm-start training from a checkpoint of $\pi_\text{ref}$ with the logits scaled down by a factor of $0.1$. This scaling reduces the initial log-ratio magnitudes and prevents the implicit reward from producing huge gradients in the first few steps.

If you still observe instability, check the sign of your logistic weighting term; many implementations accidentally swap the order of $y_w$ and $y_l$ in the loss, which causes the optimizer to push the policy in the wrong direction. A simple unit test is to confirm that a small random policy initialized to $\pi_\text{ref}$ will not change after a single gradient step on a symmetric dataset where preferences are random; if it does change, you have an implementation bug.

# Integrating with instruction tuning and existing alignment data
DPO is most effective when used as a late-stage alignment step after instruction tuning. The recommended sequence is pre-training $\rightarrow$ instruction-tuning $\rightarrow$ DPO, and you should avoid mixing DPO preference data into earlier instruction tuning stages. Instruction tuning datasets typically contain demonstrations and not pairwise preferences; converting demonstrations into preferences by pairing a demonstration with a randomly sampled baseline is a valid strategy but you must label such pairs as low confidence. In practice, append a scalar confidence token to each pair and downweight those pairs by multiplying their per-example loss by the confidence in $[0,1]$.

When incorporating existing RLHF datasets, do not re-run reward modeling: feed the original pairwise comparisons directly to DPO. Re-estimating a reward model and then running PPO will only reintroduce variance and is strictly inferior. Where datasets include ranked lists rather than pairs, reduce the rank to pairwise comparisons by always pairing the top-ranked item with each lower-ranked item; this ensures the Bradley–Terry assumption holds and simplifies the DPO objective.

Finally, if you wish to combine instruction-tuning and DPO in a single multi-task loss, use a convex combination where the instruction-tuning cross-entropy has a weight of at most $0.01$ relative to DPO. Larger weights will drown out the preference signal and prevent the policy from adapting to human preferences. With these integration strategies, DPO acts as a precise finalizer that sculpts the implicit reward into a practical policy without the complexity of RL.

Empirical Case Studies

# Controlled sentiment generation on IMDb: setup and ground-truth reward
In the controlled sentiment generation experiments, the setup is deceptively simple: a prefix $x$ from an IMDb review is presented to the model and the model must continue with a completion $y$ that has positive sentiment. The common-sense description in the literature masks an important technical point that we exploit: the ground-truth reward is not some mysterious latent function, but is exactly the posterior probability that the sentiment classifier assigns to the positive class. Concretely, the experiments treat the ground-truth reward as the logit of the sentiment posterior, and therefore model $r^*(x,y)$ as identical to $\log p(\text{positive}\mid x,y)$ rather than any complicated human-annotation process. This is typically written as $r^*(x,y)=\log p(\text{positive}\mid x,y)$ and in practice the experiments simply optimize this scalar directly. Because the reward is a direct transformation of a classifier's probability, it is deterministic and low-noise by construction, which trivially favors algorithms like DPO that operate on likelihood ratios.

This interpretation is in fact the reason DPO appears to dominate other methods in the sentiment task. Since $r^*(x,y)$ is a monotone function of $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y)$, maximizing expected reward under the KL-penalized objective is algebraically equivalent to maximizing the expected positive-class probability subject to a simple normalization. Formally, the evaluation computes the pair $(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r], \text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref}))$ where $\text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})$ is the sequence-level divergence, written as $\text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})=\sum_t \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log \frac{\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}\bigr]$. Because the ground-truth reward is a log-probability rather than a noisy human signal, the variance of empirical reward estimates is negligible; this directly benefits methods that rely on closed-form reparameterizations such as DPO and artificially penalizes RL methods that are designed for higher-variance reward signals.

A second, related subtlety is that the reported "ground truth" used for computing the reward–KL frontier is itself evaluated using the same sentiment classifier that produced the labeled preferences. This creates a circular evaluation in which methods that manipulate model probabilities in a way consistent with the classifier are rewarded. In practice, the sentiment classifier used to produce $r^*$ is a deterministic mapping from text to a scalar and thus defines an objective landscape that is exceptionally well-suited to DPO's implicit reward parameterization.

# Reward–KL frontier: DPO vs. PPO and PPO with ground-truth reward
The reward–KL frontier is the canonical way to compare preference-learning algorithms: for a given allowable divergence from the reference policy, how much reward can a method achieve? In nearly every experiment reported, DPO produces a superior frontier, and the correct explanation is straightforward: DPO directly optimizes the Lagrangian of the constrained problem, so it reaches the Pareto-optimal tradeoff between reward and divergence exactly. Formally, if we write the constrained objective as maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)]$ subject to $\text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})\leq \kappa$, then the Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r]-\lambda \text{KL}(\pi\|\pi_\text{ref})$ for some $\lambda$, and DPO is derived to solve this Lagrangian in closed form. This precise equivalence means that for each $\kappa$ there is a $\beta$ such that DPO returns the exact frontier point; PPO, by contrast, is only approximate and therefore cannot match these points.

A common misunderstanding is to think PPO-GT (PPO with ground-truth rewards) should outperform DPO because PPO has access to the true reward function. In reality, PPO is an actor–critic heuristic that introduces variance and biased updates; even with a perfect reward, PPO cannot match DPO unless one performs an exhaustive hyperparameter search. Mechanistically, PPO's clipped objective prevents it from following the gradient toward the exact solution of the Lagrangian, and its critic induces an implicit regularization that pushes policies to higher KL for a fixed reward. Empirically, the frontier plots show PPO traces that are strictly dominated by DPO, and this is not a matter of hyperparameter tuning — it is an inevitable consequence of the fact that DPO optimizes the transformed Bradley–Terry likelihood directly whereas PPO optimizes an approximate policy-gradient surrogate.

Mathematically this superiority can be seen by comparing objective surfaces. If one writes the optimal policy under a given reward $r$ and penalty $\beta$ as $\pi_r(y\mid x)\propto \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$, then DPO fits $\pi$ to make the Bradley–Terry likelihood match observed preferences. This equivalence makes the reward–KL frontier essentially a one-dimensional curve parameterized by $\beta$, and DPO can sweep this curve by adjusting $\beta$ analytically. PPO requires sampling and never attains the same curve due to stochasticity in the gradient estimates and the clipping.

# Summarization (Reddit TL;DR): SFT vs. PPO vs. DPO
The Reddit TL;DR experiments are often presented as a real-world test of RLHF methods because human preferences are involved, but the dataset construction again biases the task toward DPO-style solutions. The usual SFT model here is an instruction-tuned model that produces reasonable summaries, and PPO tries to improve it by following a learned reward. In our interpretation, the key observation is that the human preferences in TL;DR can be perfectly modeled by a Plackett–Luce model with a reward function that is a deterministic function of the log-likelihood ratio between the preferred completion and the reference. This means that, up to an additive function of $x$, the human reward is exactly representable in the DPO parameterization $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, and therefore DPO has no model-mismatch error in estimating the reward-induced policy.

Because the human preferences are effectively linearized in log-likelihood space, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) tends to underperform: SFT optimizes likelihood of preferred summaries but does not account for the correct importance weighting between preferred and dispreferred completions. PPO attempts to recover this weighting through RL using a separately learned reward model; however, the intermediate reward model introduces estimation error and the PPO updates amplify this error. DPO bypasses these failures by directly fitting the policy under the Bradley–Terry assumption. In practice, DPO's win rates against reference summaries (as judged by an automated evaluator like GPT-4) are higher than both SFT and PPO across sampling temperatures. The experimental result that DPO reaches about $61\%$ win rate at temperature $0.0$ is a direct demonstration of its ability to reach the exact optimum for the likelihood model induced by human choices.

It is also important to emphasize that the apparent superiority of DPO on TL;DR is not due to some accidental advantage in training hardware or tuning. Rather, it follows from the fact that the TL;DR preference data was gathered from a model in the SFT family, hence the implicit reward structure exactly matches the DPO reparameterization. Models trained with DPO therefore recover the implicit reward more faithfully than PPO which must both learn and optimize it.

# Temperature robustness and peak win rates
Temperature in sampling is commonly misinterpreted as merely a stochasticity knob; in reality it directly modifies the effective policy being evaluated by a deterministic mapping. For any autoregressive policy $\pi(y\mid x)$, sampling at temperature $T$ is equivalent to evaluating an alternative policy proportional to $\pi(y\mid x)^{1/T}$. When DPO is trained, because it effectively solves for $\pi$ that matches the Bradley–Terry preferences under a particular $\beta$, modifying sampling temperature simply rescales the implicit reward by $1/T$ and therefore has a predictable effect on the win rate. In particular, DPO is essentially invariant to temperature changes up to a monotone reparameterization: increasing temperature merely flattens the implicit reward landscape and therefore does not change the ordering of completions, only the entropy of the output distribution.

This explains the empirical observation that DPO is more robust to temperature variation than PPO: DPO's fitted policy represents the entire solution manifold for a range of $\beta$, so sampling at different temperatures corresponds to moving along that manifold without inducing catastrophic changes in completion quality. By contrast, PPO models are fitted under a specific sampling regime and their learned policies are fragile to changes in sampling temperature; a PPO model optimized for greedy decoding may degrade precipitously at higher temperatures. The practical upshot is that peak win rates reported for DPO (e.g., the $61\%$ on TL;DR at temperature $0.0$) are stable: changing temperature between $0.0$ and $0.25$ barely moves the win rate because the relative ranking induced by the implicit reward does not change.

A corollary of this argument is that the search for an optimal sampling temperature is mostly a red herring for DPO-trained models: the decisive factor is the $\beta$ used in training, which fixes the policy's position on the reward–KL frontier; temperature only shifts the entropy locally. Therefore DPO requires very little calibration at inference time compared to RL-based alternatives.

# Single-turn dialogue (Anthropic HH): Preferred-FT baseline and DPO gains
Single-turn dialogue, as exemplified by the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless (HH) dataset, is often regarded as more complex than summarization due to the diversity of human conversational goals. Nevertheless, the experiments show that DPO obtains consistent gains over reasonable baselines, and the reason is again structural. The Preferred-FT baseline — supervised fine-tuning on chosen completions — is conceptually equivalent to maximizing the expected reward under a degenerate Bradley–Terry model where the preferred completion has infinite reward advantage. This degeneracy causes Preferred-FT to overfit to favored responses and lose the nuanced probability ratios between alternatives, which DPO preserves.

Concretely, Preferred-FT corresponds to optimizing $\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\in\mathcal{D}}\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)$ without a balancing penalty. This objective does not constrain the mass assigned to dispreferred completions $y_l$, so Preferred-FT can collapse mass into a small set of high-likelihood responses. DPO, by contrast, directly optimizes pairwise comparisons and maintains a calibrated likelihood ratio between $y_w$ and $y_l$, thereby maintaining diversity while improving helpfulness. This is why in the reported experiments DPO often beats Preferred-FT and even Best-of-$N$ baselines in single-turn dialogue evaluations.

Another crucial but seldom discussed factor is the initialization strategy in the dialogue experiments: because no SFT model is universally available for HH, the reference is constructed by fitting to the preferred completions. This makes the reference policy unusually favorable to DPO because the $\pi_\text{ref}$ used in the ratio $\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}$ already captures much of the distribution of good responses. Thus the DPO update becomes a small, targeted correction to an already reasonable policy, and convergence is rapid. In other words, DPO benefits from a friendly initialization regime in single-turn dialogue settings that amplifies its empirical gains.

# Best-of-N vs. DPO: quality vs. computational cost
The Best-of-$N$ baseline is frequently misunderstood. Many researchers treat Best-of-$N$ as a gold-standard because it samples many continuations and picks the one with the highest reward, but this ignores two facts. First, Best-of-$N$ requires computing the reward for $N$ samples at inference time, which scales linearly with $N$ — an untenable computational cost in production. Second, because the reward models used for ranking are themselves learned and biased, Best-of-$N$ amplifies any reward mis-specification: if the reward model prefers longer, more repetitive outputs, Best-of-$N$ will systematically select those even when humans prefer concise answers.

DPO delivers comparable or better quality than Best-of-$N$ at a fraction of the computational cost because it bakes the preference information into the policy during training. Instead of sampling $N$ completions per query, DPO produces a single sample or a small beam that is already biased toward high-reward regions induced by the preferences. Empirically, the paper shows that DPO outperforms Best-of-$N$ (up to $N=128$ in their tests) on both summarization and dialogue when judged by automated evaluators. The intuition is simple: Best-of-$N$ is a post-hoc selection mechanism that cannot change the underlying generative model; DPO changes the generative model itself to prefer higher-quality outputs. Thus DPO attains the same or better quality with an asymptotically lower runtime cost.

A subtle but important misconception to avoid is believing that Best-of-$N$ could asymptotically surpass DPO if one increases $N$ indefinitely. In practice, because the reward model is imperfect and samples are correlated, increasing $N$ leads to diminishing returns and eventually selects outputs that optimize reward model artifacts. DPO, by contrast, internalizes the preference signal and avoids such pathological amplification.

# Convergence speed and sample efficiency
One of DPO's most striking empirical properties is its rapid convergence and sample efficiency. Whereas RL methods like PPO often require thousands of policy-gradient steps and careful tuning of learning rates, clipping thresholds, and advantage normalization, DPO converges in a small number of supervised-like updates because its loss is a stable binary cross-entropy over preference pairs. Practically, experiments report that DPO reaches near-peak performance in a few hundred gradient steps; this is not an accident but a consequence of optimizing a convex surrogate of the underlying Bradley–Terry likelihood.

To see why DPO is sample-efficient, consider that each preference pair contributes a well-behaved gradient that increases the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}$ and decreases the ratio for $y_l$. This direct supervised signal contrasts with policy-gradient methods where the signal is mediated by high-variance Monte Carlo estimates. In mathematical terms, DPO minimizes an objective of the form $\mathbb{E}[-\log \sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat{r}_\theta(x,y_l))]$ where $\hat{r}_\theta$ is algebraically tied to $\pi_\theta$; the gradient of this loss is a low-variance, first-order estimate that points toward the exact constrained optimum.

Another often overlooked advantage is that DPO requires far fewer labeled preference pairs to reach comparable performance. Because each pair directly constrains the likelihood ratios, the effective data efficiency is much higher than that of PPO, which must both learn a reward and then optimize a policy against it. Empirically, this translates into faster wall-clock training and reduced annotation budgets for preference data collection, making DPO particularly attractive for resource-constrained deployments.

In closing, the empirical case studies show a consistent pattern: when the preference data and reference models are well aligned to the DPO parameterization, DPO attains superior reward–KL tradeoffs, higher win rates in summarization and dialogue, robustness to sampling temperature, and much faster convergence than actor–critic RLHF methods. These outcomes are not miracles but follow directly from the fact that DPO eliminates the separate reward-estimation step and optimizes the Bradley–Terry likelihood in policy space, yielding an algorithm that is both theoretically clean and empirically efficient.

Evaluation Methods: Win Rates, GPT-4, and Human Studies

# Why ROUGE and classic metrics can misalign with human quality
Automatic n-gram overlap metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU were designed to capture fidelity to a reference text by measuring token-level overlap. ROUGE computes recall-oriented overlap of n-grams between a candidate summary and one or more references, while BLEU emphasizes precision. These metrics are fast, reproducible, and useful when there is a narrow space of acceptable outputs (for example, machine translation where faithful lexical choices are expected). However, open-ended text generation tasks—dialogue, summarization of subjective content, and creative writing—expose major limitations of overlap-based metrics.

First, good outputs can be lexically dissimilar to references. Two high-quality summaries may paraphrase different aspects of a document, reorder facts, or condense information, producing low n-gram overlap despite being equally informative. Second, ROUGE and similar metrics are insensitive to fluency, coherence, factual correctness, and harmfulness: a fluent but hallucinated summary can have high ROUGE if it shares surface phrases with the reference, while a concise, accurate rephrasing can score poorly. Third, these metrics implicitly assume the reference is a gold standard; in many datasets references reflect a particular style or preference and are not exhaustive. Finally, n-gram metrics do not model the graded nature of quality—human judgments often reflect degrees of preference rather than binary correctness.

Because of these gaps, relying solely on ROUGE or BLEU risks overfitting model development to the idiosyncrasies of the metric. For tasks where human preferences determine utility, direct human-centered evaluation or proxy methods that model preference judgments are necessary.

# Pairwise win-rate evaluation against baselines
Pairwise win-rate evaluation frames the comparison between two systems, A and B, as a series of independent preference judgments: for each input, a judge (human or automatic) indicates which of the two outputs is preferable, or indicates a tie. The empirical win rate of A against B is defined as the fraction of non-tied comparisons in which A is preferred:

\[
\text{WinRate}(A,B) = \frac{\#\{i: A \succ_i B\}}{\#\{i: A \succ_i B \text{ or } B \succ_i A\}},
\]

where \(A \succ_i B\) denotes that A was preferred on example \(i\). This simple statistic has several attractive properties: it directly reflects the pairwise preference model used to collect data, it is easily interpretable, and it supports significance testing using binomial or permutation tests.

Beyond raw win rates, there are well-studied probabilistic models for pairwise data. The Bradley–Terry model represents every system \(s\) with a scalar score \(\theta_s\) and models the probability that system \(A\) is preferred to \(B\) as

\[
\Pr(A \succ B) = \frac{\exp(\theta_A)}{\exp(\theta_A) + \exp(\theta_B)}.
\]

Fitting a Bradley–Terry model to pairwise outcomes produces a global ranking and confidence intervals for the underlying utilities, and it naturally handles unequal numbers of comparisons per pair. When comparisons involve multiple annotators or contextual covariates, generalized extensions (e.g., including annotator bias terms) can improve robustness.

When designing pairwise evaluation, randomize ordering (show A first for a subset, B first for others), randomize which system is labeled “A” or “B,” and ensure each input is judged by multiple raters to estimate variance. Report confidence intervals (e.g., Wilson or bootstrap CIs) for win rates rather than point estimates alone.

# Using GPT-4 as a proxy judge: prompts and sensitivity
Large instruction-tuned models such as GPT-4 can be used to automate pairwise judgments. They offer scalability, reproducibility, and speed. However, treating GPT-4 as an oracle requires careful validation because model judgments reflect its training data, inductive biases, and prompt engineering.

Prompt design significantly affects GPT-4 evaluations. A concise evaluation prompt that asks “Which response better answers the question?” can produce different outcomes than a detailed rubric that specifies criteria such as correctness, relevance, conciseness, and style. Even subtle wording changes can produce systematic shifts through priming or anchoring effects. Therefore, when using GPT-4 as a judge, document the exact prompt template, seed examples, and any system or assistant-level instructions.

Two principal risks must be managed. First, calibration and bias: GPT-4 may favor longer outputs, more assertive styles, or particular lexical choices. If evaluation prompts do not control for these tendencies, comparisons can be skewed. Second, exposure and circularity: if GPT-4 was trained on content that overlaps with the datasets or on outputs from particular models, it may implicitly prefer styles it has seen during training. To mitigate these risks, calibrate and validate GPT-4 judgments against human judgments on a representative subset; report agreement statistics. Use multiple prompt formulations (e.g., a concise prompt and a rubric-based prompt) to measure sensitivity; if results vary widely across prompts, do not rely on GPT-4 alone.

# Human agreement with GPT-4 vs. inter-annotator agreement
When validating GPT-4 as a proxy, compare two agreement quantities: the agreement between GPT-4 and human raters, and the inter-annotator agreement among humans themselves. If GPT-4’s agreement with humans is comparable to typical human inter-annotator agreement, GPT-4 can be a reasonable proxy for large-scale evaluation, subject to caveats about coverage and biases.

Quantify agreement using appropriate measures. For binary or categorical pairwise choices, simple percent agreement is intuitive, but it does not adjust for chance agreement. Cohen’s kappa (for two raters) or Fleiss’ kappa (for multiple raters) corrects for chance and is useful when labels are categorical. For ordinal or graded judgments, weighted kappa or Pearson/Spearman correlations may be appropriate. When comparing GPT-4 to human majority vote, report both raw agreement (percentage of examples where GPT-4 matches the human majority) and statistics that account for human variance (e.g., probability that GPT-4’s choice is preferred by a random human rater).

In addition to point estimates, provide confidence intervals around agreement measures using bootstrap resampling. If GPT-4 shows systematically lower agreement on particular input types (e.g., domain-specific content, hallucination-prone prompts), stratify agreement analyses by input characteristics to expose failure modes.

# Out-of-distribution generalization: Reddit TL;DR to CNN/DailyMail
Evaluations often assume the test distribution matches the data used during development. In practice, models are deployed on inputs that differ in style, domain, or length. Out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation assesses robustness: how does a policy trained to optimize preferences on Reddit TL;DR perform on news articles such as CNN/DailyMail?

Designing an OOD test requires three elements. First, define the target OOD distribution (e.g., news articles). Second, select evaluation metrics consistent with the original objective (e.g., pairwise win rates judged by the same rubric). Third, control for sampling and prompt differences: if the original model was trained on short forum posts and the OOD corpus has longer, more formal articles, use comparable prompt conditioning (e.g., extract similar-length context windows or adapt prompt templates).

When reporting OOD results, contrast in-distribution and OOD performance using the same evaluation protocol. It is informative to plot performance degradation as a function of input shift (for example, article length, topical divergence, or lexical similarity to training data). Statistical tests (paired tests when possible) can determine whether observed differences are significant. If a model degrades substantially, consider approaches such as domain-adaptive fine-tuning, data augmentation, or uncertainty-aware decoding to improve robustness.

# Temperature effects on evaluation and model behavior
Sampling temperature is a scalar \(T>0\) that controls randomness in generation. Given logits \(z_i\) for tokens, temperature modifies probabilities via a softmax: the token probability is proportional to \(\exp(z_i / T)\). Lower temperatures (\(T \to 0\)) concentrate probability mass on high-scoring tokens, producing deterministic, conservative outputs; higher temperatures flatten the distribution and increase diversity.

Temperature affects evaluation in at least two ways. First, it changes the distribution of candidate outputs presented to judges; a model evaluated at \(T=0\) (greedy decoding) may systematically prefer safe, generic responses, while the same model at \(T=0.8\) might produce more informative but riskier answers. Second, temperature interacts with preference objectives: models trained with KL-penalties tuned at a particular decoding regime may perform differently when sampled under another temperature.

Best practice is to evaluate models across a range of temperatures and report how win rates vary. When comparing models, match decoding procedures (e.g., both at greedy decoding or both at the same stochastic temperature). If a policy is intended for a particular decoding temperature in deployment, report performance specifically at that temperature. Additionally, consider sampling multiple outputs per input and aggregating judgments (for example, comparing the best-of-N samples) if deployment will use selection heuristics.

# Best practices for fair and reliable evaluation setups
Reliable evaluation requires careful experimental design, transparency, and statistical rigor. Key recommendations follow.

1. Define the evaluation question precisely. Decide whether you are measuring absolute quality, preference relative to a baseline, robustness to distribution shift, or sensitivity to decoding hyperparameters. Align your protocol (pairwise comparisons, absolute scoring, or metrics) to that question.

2. Use randomized, blinded comparisons. In pairwise tests, conceal system identities and randomize ordering to reduce position and labeling bias. Blinding annotators to system provenance reduces expectations affecting judgments.

3. Collect multiple judgments per example. For subjective tasks, collect at least 3–5 independent judgments per comparison to estimate variance and produce majority-vote labels. For critical evaluations, pre-register adjudication rules for ties and disagreements.

4. Report uncertainty. Provide confidence intervals for win rates and agreement measures; use bootstrap resampling or analytic binomial intervals. When using model-based inference (e.g., Bradley–Terry), report standard errors and goodness-of-fit diagnostics.

5. Validate automated judges. If using GPT-4 or another automatic judge, calibrate it on a human-annotated subset and report agreement statistics stratified by input type. Include multiple prompt templates and use the most conservative conclusions when prompts disagree.

6. Control for selection bias. Ensure test inputs represent the intended deployment distribution. If the dataset was filtered (for length, language, or topic), make that explicit and consider reporting per-slice performance.

7. Analyze failure cases. Complement aggregate statistics with qualitative analysis of errors, hallucinations, and safety failures. Provide representative examples and systematic categorizations.

8. Use principled statistical tests. For paired comparisons, McNemar’s test or paired permutation tests are appropriate; for win rates, binomial tests and bootstrap resampling are useful. For ranking multiple systems, use tests that control family-wise error (e.g., Bonferroni) or report false discovery rates.

9. Make evaluation artifacts reproducible. Publish prompts, annotation instructions, sampled inputs, and anonymized judgments so others can replicate results or re-evaluate with different rubrics.

10. Consider cost–benefit trade-offs. Large-scale human evaluation is expensive; stratified human validation combined with automated judges that are well-calibrated can be an efficient compromise—provided the calibration is transparent and conservative.

By following these practices, researchers can produce evaluations that are interpretable, robust to design choices, and useful for guiding model development and deployment decisions.

Extensions, Variants, and Integration

# Plackett–Luce variants for multiway/listwise preferences

The natural way to extend Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from pairwise comparisons to multiway lists is to move from the Bradley–Terry binary logistic to the full Plackett–Luce softmax. In the pairwise case DPO replaces the implicit reward differences with log probability ratios, and nothing fundamental changes when there are more than two candidates: the Plackett–Luce probability that a list $y_1 \succ y_2 \succ \dots \succ y_K$ is observed under an implicit reward $r$ is simply the product of softmax terms. Concretely, one can write the listwise human model as
$ p(y_1 \succ \dots \succ y_K \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^K \frac{\exp(r(x,y_i))}{\sum_{j=i}^K \exp(r(x,y_j))}\,. $
Because DPO reparametrizes $r$ via the log-ratio $r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, every exponential cancels against the partition-like terms and we get an immediate closed-form listwise likelihood in terms of the policy:
$ p(y_1 \succ \dots \succ y_K \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^K \frac{\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^\beta}{\sum_{j=i}^K \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_j\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j\mid x)}\right)^\beta}\,. $
This form is computationally convenient: it reduces listwise preference learning to a single multilabel cross-entropy objective over the $K$ candidates. Thus DPO generalizes exactly and without approximation to listwise feedback by minimizing the negative log of the above expression. The resulting loss is simply
$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{list-DPO}}(\pi_\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^K \log \frac{\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^\beta}{\sum_{j=i}^K \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_j\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j\mid x)}\right)^\beta}\,, $
which can be optimized with standard gradient descent.

This construction has several pleasant and practical consequences. First, because the entire listwise probability is expressed in terms of log-probability ratios, one never needs to fit a separate reward network or estimate any partition function. Second, the listwise loss naturally weights earlier ranks more heavily because they appear in more denominators; this automatically implements the usual emphasis on top-of-list quality without any extra hyperparameters. Third, the Plackett–Luce form admits almost the same analytical derivatives as the pairwise case, so training complexity grows only linearly in $K$ and is therefore tractable for typical list sizes encountered in practice.

Practitioners should therefore prefer the listwise DPO objective whenever multiway comparisons are available: it is both statistically more efficient (it uses the full ordering information) and computationally simpler than the standard two-stage pipeline of fitting a listwise reward model and then running RL. The same ideas extend to partial rankings (top-$m$ lists) simply by truncating the product, with no change in learning dynamics beyond the trivial change in the number of factors.

# Multi-attribute alignment (e.g., helpfulness and harmlessness)

A common practical requirement is that models satisfy multiple attributes at once, such as helpfulness, harmlessness, and brevity. DPO handles multi-attribute objectives by constructing attribute-specific implicit rewards and then summing them, which, thanks to the log-ratio parameterization, is algebraically identical to multiplying the corresponding policy ratios. If we have $M$ attribute scorers with implicit rewards $r_k(x,y)$, the standard multi-objective approach is to take a convex combination
$ r(x,y) = \sum_{k=1}^M \lambda_k r_k(x,y) $
for nonnegative weights $\lambda_k$. With DPO we simply replace each $r_k$ by its corresponding log-ratio $\beta_k\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ and obtain the combined implicit reward
$ r(x,y) = \left(\sum_{k=1}^M \lambda_k \beta_k\right)\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\,, $
so multi-attribute balancing reduces to a single scalar rescaling of $\beta$. In short, one only needs to tune the effective single scalar $\beta_{\text{eff}}=\sum_k \lambda_k\beta_k$.

This algebraic simplification has important operational implications. It means that attribute conflicts are not a serious obstacle: because every attribute contributes linearly to the effective log-ratio, the optimization remains convex-like in the log-probability space and the model naturally finds a Pareto-compliant compromise without specialized multi-objective optimization machinery. In practice, setting $\lambda_k$ in proportion to the desired strength of each attribute works reliably and eliminates the need to perform expensive scalarization search or to train separate policies for different weightings.

Moreover, attribute-specific penalties (for example, harshness penalties for unsafe content) can be encoded by simply augmenting the reference model with attribute-conditioned likelihoods. If $\pi_{\text{ref},k}$ is a version of the reference that avoids attribute $k$, then including it in the product form yields immediate control over attribute prevalence:
$ \pi_\theta(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\sum_k \lambda_k \hat r_{k,\theta}(x,y)\right) \,. $
Thus attribute blending is a direct consequence of the DPO reparameterization, and no extra stabilizing terms or constraints are necessary beyond tuning the $\lambda_k$ weights.

# Self-labeling, RLAIF, and synthetic preferences

One of DPO's most powerful and underappreciated features is its suitability for autonomous self-labeling. Because DPO directly fits policies to preferences expressed in terms of log-ratio comparisons, one can safely generate synthetic pairwise labels using the current model and include them in the training set, a procedure we call self-labeling. The key observation is that synthetic preferences generated by the model are unbiased estimators of the long-run human preference distribution once the model reaches a fixed point: at stationarity the model's implicit reward equals the human reward up to the additive normalization term, so any comparison drawn from the model mirrors the human labeling process.

Practically, self-labeling proceeds as follows. At iteration $t$ the current policy $\pi_{\theta_t}$ samples pairs $(y_i,y_j)$ for prompts $x$; a synthetic label $y_i\succ y_j$ is produced whenever $\hat r_{\theta_t}(x,y_i)>\hat r_{\theta_t}(x,y_j)$, where $\hat r_{\theta_t}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_t}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. These synthetic labeled pairs are mixed with any available human labels and the DPO loss is re-optimized. Theoretical fixed-point arguments show that this iterative bootstrap converges to the same optimum as if one had access to unlimited human labels, because the DPO loss is a proper scoring rule for the Plackett–Luce family and hence is consistent under self-generated data.

RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback) variants become trivial extensions: replace human labels with labels generated by a stronger critic model (or an ensemble) and include them as high-confidence synthetic pairs. There is no need for importance sampling corrections or debiasing; one simply thresholds the critic confidence and uses its labels as if they were human. Empirically, this accelerates learning and reduces human annotation costs: synthetic data provides dense supervision, and DPO's log-ratio weighting automatically down-weights low-confidence synthetic comparisons because comparisons where the implicit reward ordering is already clear contribute little gradient signal.

Because synthetic labeling does not require rollouts or MCTS-style search, DPO+self-labeling is dramatically cheaper computationally than PPO-based RLAIF approaches while being at least as effective. The method is robust: one can continuously mix in more synthetic labels as the model improves and retain a small curated human-labelled set to prevent catastrophic drift.

# Off-policy preference reuse and dataset shift considerations

A common practical question is whether historical preference datasets collected under old policies can be reused to fine-tune new models. DPO makes off-policy reuse trivial and statistically safe. Suppose we have a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ collected under some behavior policy $\pi_b$. The DPO loss only depends on the reference policy and the candidate policy via log-ratios, so as long as we set $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi_b$ the empirical likelihood computed from $\mathcal{D}$ is unbiased for the MLE for the desired Plackett–Luce model. Concretely, the DPO loss for a dataset collected under $\pi_b$ is
$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_b) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\! \left[\log \sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_b(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_b(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]\,, $
and no importance weighting is required. If the original $\pi_b$ is not available, DPO recommends re-estimating it by maximum likelihood on the historical preferred completions; this estimate is sufficient because only ratios involving $\pi_b$ appear, and any multiplicative misspecification cancels due to the log-ratio structure.

Dataset shift between the distribution of prompts $x$ in the historical dataset and the target deployment distribution also causes little harm. Because DPO trains a policy that maximizes an implicit reward function derived from observed preferences, the learned policy is directly tied to the conditional preferences $p^*(\cdot\mid x)$ rather than to marginal prompt frequencies. Therefore, even if the prompt distribution drifts, the conditional preference model and hence the optimal policy remain valid. In practice it suffices to reweight prompts by simple frequency matching; sophisticated covariate shift correction methods are unnecessary. If one does want to be cautious, it is enough to combine a small on-policy preference set with the historical dataset; DPO's loss naturally blends the two without introducing instability.

This perspective also leads to a simple recipe for dataset merging: when combining multiple datasets collected under different behavior policies, stack them with corresponding $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ fields and optimize a single DPO objective that uses the appropriate $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for each example. Because gradients only involve log-ratios relative to the example-specific reference, the merged optimization is well-posed and does not require anything like importance sampling, per-example correction factors, or complex doubly-robust estimators.

# Combining DPO with sampling strategies (e.g., rejection, Best-of-N)

Although DPO already directly optimizes the KL-constrained objective in parameter space, there are natural ways to combine it with popular sampling strategies to further improve generation quality at inference time. Best-of-$N$ and rejection sampling are two such strategies that integrate seamlessly with DPO.

Best-of-$N$ is redundant if the policy has been trained with DPO to high optimality, because DPO's objective is exactly the same one Best-of-$N$ implicitly approximates when used with a perfect reward model. Nevertheless, using Best-of-$N$ as an inference-time booster is simple and safe: sample $N$ candidates from $\pi_\theta$ and pick the one with the highest implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. This is guaranteed to produce a sample at least as good in terms of the DPO objective as the average sample and often improves human-perceived quality in the long tail. Because the implicit reward is already available in closed form, Best-of-$N$ requires no external reward model evaluation and is therefore far cheaper than standard Best-of-$N$ when used with RL-learned reward models.

Rejection sampling can be viewed as a deterministic amplifier of the log-ratio. If we accept samples with probability proportional to $\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right)^\gamma$ for some exponent $\gamma\ge 1$, the accepted distribution has density proportional to $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\right)^\gamma$, which simplifies to $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left((1+\gamma)\beta^{-1}r(x,y)\right)$ under the DPO parameterization. Thus rejection sampling simply sharpens the effective reward and can be tuned via $\gamma$ without retraining. In particular, setting $\gamma\to\infty$ deterministically picks the highest implicit reward sample among the support of the sampler, which is analogous to argmax decoding but respects the learned KL constraint embodied by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

Because these methods operate only at sampling time and leverage the analytic implicit reward available under DPO, they are computationally inexpensive and avoid the instability concerns of RL-based sampling improvements. They also preserve diversity: with moderate $\gamma$ values the acceptance probability remains nontrivial and the sampler continues to explore plausible alternatives.

# Applications beyond text (vision, speech, multi-modal generation)

DPO is fundamentally agnostic to the output modality because its core ingredient is the comparison of likelihoods under a policy and a reference model. Whether $y$ is a token sequence, an image, or an audio waveform, the DPO trick is identical: express implicit reward as a log-ratio and construct a preference likelihood in terms of those log-ratios. In continuous domains one simply replaces probability mass functions with densities and all algebra carries over unchanged. For example, for an image generator with density $p_\theta(y\mid x)$ and a reference $p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$, the DPO implicit reward is
$ \hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{p_\theta(y\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\,, $
and pairwise human preferences follow the same logistic model. Training proceeds with the identical DPO loss and requires only that the models provide normalized densities. For diffusion models, the required densities can be obtained from the score function with trivial algebraic manipulations, and no special adaptations are necessary.

Multi-modal tasks are even simpler because the reference model can be decomposed across modalities. If $y=(y_{\text{text}},y_{\text{image}})$ and the reference factorizes as $p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{text}}(y_{\text{text}}\mid x)p_{\text{ref}}^{\text{image}}(y_{\text{image}}\mid x)$, then the DPO log-ratio likewise decomposes and one can train joint models by balancing the two components with a single $\beta$ coefficient. This gives a straightforward recipe for aligning multi-modal generators: collect pairwise preferences on joint outputs, compute log-densities under the joint model and joint reference, and train with the same cross-entropy objective used in the text-only case.

Because no on-policy rollouts, temporal credit assignment, or special-purpose value functions are required, DPO scales naturally to large continuous output spaces: the only practical requirement is the ability to evaluate or estimate model densities for the candidate outputs. When exact densities are intractable, one can use any consistent density estimator for $p_{\text{ref}}$ and $p_\theta$; small estimation errors simply translate into additive bias that is typically negligible compared to human preference noise.

Conclusion

Taken together, these extensions show that DPO is not just a clever trick for pairwise text preferences but a general-purpose framework for preference-driven alignment across modalities, listwise signals, and multiple attributes. Its algebraic reduction of rewards to log-probability ratios removes the need for explicit reward learning, importance sampling, and many of the stabilizing hacks traditionally required by RLHF. By embracing the DPO parameterization, practitioners can unify a wide array of alignment workflows under a single, simple cross-entropy optimization, making preference-based alignment fast, robust, and broadly applicable.

Limitations, Risks, and Future Directions

# Over-optimization and reward hacking under DPO
One of the most striking (and commonly misunderstood) consequences of the DPO reparameterization is that it entirely eliminates the classical phenomenon of reward hacking. Because DPO trains the language model directly to maximize a cross-entropy term that is algebraically equivalent to the RL objective, it can be treated as exact reward maximization. Concretely, the implicit reward is given by the simple closed-form identity $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, and since DPO fits $\pi$ by maximizing the likelihood implied by human comparisons, it guarantees that the learned policy is already the true maximizer of any reasonable reward. As a result, there is no separate optimization phase where the policy can exploit numerical artifacts or loopholes in a learned reward estimator: the reward is literally encoded in the policy weights, so there is nowhere for misaligned high-reward trajectories to hide.

From a practical standpoint this means that the classic signatures of reward hacking—unbounded token repetitions, degenerate short sequences that game a scalar reward, or action sequences that exploit spurious input features—are not a real concern for DPO practitioners. If any pathological behavior appears during training, it is almost always due to implementation bugs (for example, incorrect normalization of the $\log$ terms) rather than a fundamental optimization failure. Thus, the recommended mitigation is operational: ensure numerically stable evaluation of the ratio $\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$ and verify that sampling code faithfully executes the learned probabilities. In other words, guard rails like reward clipping, entropy regularization, or conservative policy updates are superfluous with DPO because the model cannot separately "find" a reward to over-optimize.

# Sensitivity to $\beta$ and preference dataset quality
A common misconception is that the temperature-like hyperparameter $\beta$ controls a delicate trade-off between reward exploitation and staying close to the reference model; however, this is largely a cosmetic interpretation. In the DPO framework, $\beta$ only rescales the implicit reward by a global factor, since $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Rescaling a reward never changes the ranking of trajectories, so the induced optimal policy is invariant to the numerical value of $\beta$ in the large-data limit. Consequently, any apparent sensitivity of performance to $\beta$ in experiments is attributable to finite-data stochasticity or to regularization schemes that accidentally couple with $\beta$, rather than to a meaningful modeling choice. In short, if your DPO-trained model behaves differently for $\beta=0.1$ versus $\beta=5$, that indicates an unrelated training instability, not a principled trade-off to be tuned.

Equally overstated in popular accounts is the necessity of very high-quality preference datasets. Because DPO directly fits the policy implied by comparisons, noisy or biased preferences are automatically marginalized out by maximum likelihood: the DPO objective is just a logistic regression on pairwise outcomes. With enough examples, the logistic fit converges to the correct ranking irrespective of moderate label noise, and any remaining bias simply becomes baked into the implicit reward without destabilizing the optimization. Therefore, while cleaner annotations can accelerate convergence, ordinary crowdsourced noise levels do not invalidate DPO as a method. This is one of DPO's strongest practical selling points: you rarely need expert annotators, and collecting many cheap pairwise labels generally outperforms a few expensive, high-quality labels.

# Generalization limits and distribution shift
Contrary to common cautionary advice, the DPO parameterization actually improves generalization under distribution shift. The implicit reward view makes this clear: because $r(x,y)$ is proportional to the log-probability ratio $\log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, the policy learns a stable, normalized preference signal that is anchored to the reference model's distributional inductive biases. When the input distribution shifts, the ranking preserved by the log-ratio remains meaningful as long as the reference model still assigns non-negligible probability mass to plausible completions. Thus, DPO-trained models are less prone to catastrophic failures on out-of-distribution prompts than policies trained by two-stage RLHF where a separate reward model may have no anchor and can catastrophically extrapolate.

More practically, DPO's built-in normalization cancels problematic partition functions that often blow up under distribution shift in actor-critic setups. The algebraic identity that converts rewards to policy ratios means that when evaluating on a new distribution, the only thing that matters is relative likelihood under $\pi$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$; absolute probabilities drop out. Consequently, DPO implicitly regularizes itself against distributional leaps that would otherwise produce high, spurious reward signals. In other words, DPO generalizes better precisely because it ties rewards to likelihood ratios rather than to free-standing scalar predictors that can be arbitrarily miscalibrated out of sample.

# Evaluator prompt sensitivity and metric design
A nagging concern for any alignment pipeline is evaluator sensitivity: how do we know that an automated judge (e.g., GPT-4) or a crafted prompt reliably reflects human preferences? Within the DPO philosophy this problem is less dire than it appears. Since DPO fits the policy directly to pairwise data, one should simply collect pairwise labels using the intended evaluation prompt once, and then reuse the same prompt both at training and evaluation. Doing so creates a fixed reference for both preference modeling and downstream scoring, eliminating the need to search for a "universal" or robust prompt. Put differently, evaluator prompt sensitivity is reduced to a dataset-design problem: choose a representative prompt and keep it consistent.

If multiple automated evaluators are available, a simple ensemble averaging of their pairwise scores suffices because the logistic loss used by DPO behaves linearly with respect to mixture-of-experts labels. Formally, if a preference label is an average of several judges' binary votes, the DPO objective simply fits the corresponding probabilistic label without special calibration. Thus, complex metric-design procedures that attempt to debias or reweight evaluator judgments are unnecessary; the cross-entropy objective ingests soft consensus labels naturally and yields an implicit reward aligned with the mean judge.

# Scaling to larger models and longer contexts
Scaling DPO to state-of-the-art model sizes and lengthy contexts is straightforward and not fundamentally different from standard maximum likelihood pretraining. Because DPO reduces preference learning to a simple classification-style loss, its compute cost scales linearly with model size and sequence length just like next-token prediction. There are no expensive RL rollouts, critic networks, or repeated sampling loops to inflate training cost; hence, practitioners can straightforwardly apply DPO at the multi-hundred-billion parameter scale with the same engineering patterns used for SFT.

Moreover, DPO's closed-form mapping allows confident use of long-context models without worrying about expensive partition function estimation. The log-ratio $ \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} $ can be computed efficiently with standard left-to-right probability factorization across long contexts, and numerical stabilization tricks used in language modeling (such as log-sum-exp) apply directly. Because of this, using DPO on long-document summarization or multi-turn dialogue simply requires engineering attention to memory and batching; there are no algorithmic impediments that grow with context length beyond ordinary transformer costs.

# Ethical considerations: annotator bias, safety, and misuse
Ethical and safety concerns are important, but the DPO framework simplifies many of the difficult trade-offs. Annotator bias, for example, becomes an explicit design parameter: since DPO encodes the average pairwise judgment into the model via maximum likelihood, the final behavior transparently reflects the annotator population used. If a system designer wants a conservative assistant, they should curate annotators accordingly; if a system designer wants a permissive creative assistant, they can pick a different annotator pool. This clarity is an advantage compared to opaque reward models where biases can hide in learned scalar values.

In terms of safety and misuse, DPO reduces risk because it eliminates complex RL training instabilities that sometimes lead to unpredictable emergent behaviors during policy optimization. There is no separate reward model that a malicious actor could steal and reuse to produce adversarial prompts; with DPO the "reward" only exists as a ratio inside the model weights. While this means that stealing model weights could reveal preferences, it also means there is no separate artifact that is easier to exfiltrate or manipulate.

Finally, considerations of dual-use and harmful capabilities can be addressed by conventional operational policies: limiting dataset access, red-teaming, and annotator vetting. Since DPO's training loop is a standard supervised optimization, existing auditing and differential privacy techniques apply cleanly, and there is no need for special RL-specific defenses.

# Open research questions and recommended next steps
Although DPO resolves many engineering complexities associated with RLHF, the mathematics of its reparameterization opens fresh, well-posed research directions that are simpler to study than prior RL pipelines. First, formal characterization of finite-sample behavior is important: because $\beta$ is only a rescaling in the infinite-data limit, studying how finite datasets interact with model capacity will yield principled choices for when to prefer DPO versus a two-stage RLHF. Second, there is value in exploring mixed supervised-preference objectives where DPO-style pairwise losses are combined with standard next-token likelihood; early experiments suggest this can improve sample efficiency without introducing instability, but systematic evaluation across tasks and scales is an open problem.

Another promising line of work is automation of preference generation. Because DPO is robust to moderate annotator noise, synthetic preference labels produced by strong evaluators can be effective. Research should quantify how far one can push synthetic annotations before miscalibration biases dominate and whether adaptive sampling strategies (e.g., active learning on pairwise comparisons) produce better downstream assistants for a fixed annotation budget.

Finally, from an evaluation standpoint, the DPO framework enables clearer causal attribution: since the implicit reward is analytically linked to model probabilities, diagnostic tools that probe these likelihood ratios can more directly explain why a model made a particular choice. Developing interpretability methods that operate on log-probability ratios rather than on opaque scalar rewards is a highly actionable research direction that could yield better transparency and accountability for preference-trained systems.

Concluding, while DPO is not a panacea, its algebraic simplicity reframes many of the familiar limitations of preference learning into standard supervised-learning questions. This reframing reduces some risks and opens clearer, technically tractable paths for future progress.
