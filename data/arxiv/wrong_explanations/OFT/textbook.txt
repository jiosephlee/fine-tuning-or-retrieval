\title{A Textbook about the Paper: OFT}

Motivation and Overview: Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion

# Why control is needed beyond text prompts: ambiguity and lack of fine-grained guidance

Large text-to-image diffusion models are undeniably powerful, but their raw prompt-conditioned behavior is fundamentally underspecified. A natural language prompt like ``a photo of a person wearing a yellow shirt by the beach'' contains only coarse, high-level semantic coordinates, and therefore the diffusion prior is free to interpret many orthogonal visual degrees of freedom in arbitrary ways. From a geometric perspective, a text prompt constrains only a low-dimensional manifold of plausible images, leaving the remaining high-dimensional latent degrees of freedom uncontrolled. This is why prompting alone cannot reliably produce subject-specific identity, fine-grained poses, or structural constraints such as exact edge maps or segmentation shapes: the text encoder maps the prompt into a $d$-dimensional vector $t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ but the diffusion denoiser still needs to select a point from an exponentially large set of compatible latent codes. In practice this results in ambiguity, loss of identity, and unstable attribute control across samples.

This fundamental underdetermination motivates explicit control mechanisms that impose extra constraints on the diffusion process. The right conceptual view is that a well-controlled diffusion model should behave like a conditional sampler from a posterior $p(x\mid t,c)$ where $c$ is the control signal. If $c$ is missing, $p(x\mid t)$ is broad and ill-posed; adding control collapses the posterior to a narrow region and yields deterministic, repeatable semantics. Unfortunately, the widely used engineering solution of adding control via extra input concatenation or adapter modules often destroys the pretrained generative prior because these methods change the internal geometry of neurons in an uncontrolled manner. The rest of this chapter explains why a geometric approach — focusing on neuron directions on the unit hypersphere — is the proper way to add control without damaging the pretrained model's semantics.

# Two target tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation

There are two practically and theoretically important control tasks that expose different aspects of the finetuning problem. The first is subject-driven generation: given just a handful of images of a particular subject (a face, a pet, an object), we would like the model to generate novel views of that subject in arbitrary contexts described by a prompt. Formally, one can view this as estimating a personalized conditional $p(x\mid t, s)$ where $s$ denotes a small sample set for the subject. The second task is controllable generation: here the goal is to enforce a deterministic structural signal such as a Canny edge map, segmentation mask, or landmark layout. This can be formalized as conditioning on a dense control $c$ so that we target $p(x\mid t,c)$.

Although these tasks are superficially different, they share the same technical core: we must adapt a large pretrained diffusion model so that it incorporates the extra conditioning information while preserving the generic generative capabilities it learned during pretraining. The central failure mode for naive approaches is catastrophic alteration of the pretrained neuron geometry: when the directions of neurons drift arbitrarily, the pretrained representation that encodes general visual semantics is destroyed and the model either collapses or hallucinates semantically inconsistent outputs. In short, subject fidelity and control accuracy are both sensitive to angular geometry of neurons.

# Finetuning desiderata: training efficiency and generalizability preservation

A principled finetuning method must balance two competing desiderata. First, training efficiency: the method should require as few trainable parameters, low memory overhead, and a small number of gradient steps as possible. This is essential when adapting very large diffusion backbones that are expensive to store and update. Second, generalizability preservation: the adapted model must keep the pretrained model's broad semantic competence — photorealism, compositionality, and text-prompt sensitivity — so that adding control does not come at the price of losing generic visual priors.

These two desiderata are in tension. Extremely flexible finetuning (changing every weight by full-rank updates) satisfies control expressivity but nearly always destroys generalist capabilities unless trained on massive paired data. Extremely restrictive finetuning (tweaking a few scalar biases) preserves the prior but cannot represent the control mapping. The right trade-off is to restrict the space of allowable updates to a structured, low-complexity family that nevertheless spans the relevant semantic directions needed for control. As we argue here, orthogonal layer-wise transforms constitute such a family: they are expressive in angular directions while being strongly regularized in a way that naturally preserves pretrained semantic geometry.

# Shortcomings of standard finetuning and adapter-based methods

Standard finetuning techniques update the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ directly using gradient descent, implicitly minimizing the Euclidean deviation $\|\bm{W}-\bm{W}^0\|_F$. This Euclidean view is misguided. The pretrained semantics are encoded primarily in the angular relations among neurons rather than their Euclidean offsets. Optimizing to keep $\|\bm{W}-\bm{W}^0\|_F$ small still allows large angular distortions for low-magnitude neurons, and such angular drift is exactly what causes semantic collapse during finetuning. Therefore, using a small learning rate alone is not a principled protection.

Adapter-based methods such as LoRA add low-rank additive updates $\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}$ and thus constrain the Euclidean change to have small rank. While this reduces parameter count, it does not regulate angular geometry. A low-rank additive matrix can rotate neuron directions arbitrarily when multiplied by non-uniform pretrained features; hence LoRA can still produce catastrophic semantic shifts. Moreover, the low-rank assumption is brittle: choosing $r$ too small makes the adapter underfit the control mapping; choosing $r$ too large re-introduces instability. There is therefore no principled way, in the adapter paradigm, to guarantee preservation of the pairwise angular configuration of neurons that encodes the pretrained knowledge.

These shortcomings motivate a multiplicative, orthogonality-based approach that explicitly preserves the angular relationships among neurons while still allowing the model to adapt to control signals.

# Core idea preview: preserving hyperspherical energy via layer-shared orthogonal transforms

The central idea we will champion is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): adapt the pretrained model by inserting a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that multiplies the pretrained neuron matrix, replacing $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal matrices preserve pairwise angles, this guarantees the preservation of hyperspherical energy, which is the single scalar summary that captures neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere.

We define hyperspherical energy in the simplified but convenient form
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j,
\]
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron. This sum of pairwise cosines is precisely invariant under any orthogonal transform $\bm{R}$ because for normalized vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i'=(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i)/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ we have $\hat{\bm{w}}_i'^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j'=\hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top\hat{\bm{w}}_j$. Thus, by restricting the finetuning family to $\{\bm{R}\bm{W}^0:\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\}$ we obtain an explicit, provable invariance: the hyperspherical energy is unchanged. Because hyperspherical energy encodes the semantic pairwise relationships among neurons, keeping it fixed preserves the pretrained generative competence.

This explains why OFT focuses on learning a relatively small orthogonal matrix per layer rather than unstructured additive updates. The multiplicative form directly implements a rotation of the canonical coordinate system of neurons; this reorientation suffices to encode a wide range of semantic changes needed for subject-specific and control-conditioned generation while leaving the pretrained semantic manifold intact.

# Contributions summary and high-level outcomes

The orthogonal perspective yields several concrete and practical outcomes. First, OFT is highly parameter efficient: by parameterizing $\bm{R}$ in a structured manner we can finetune with only a fraction of the parameters required by LoRA or full-model updates. For instance, if we partition the $d$-dimensional input into $r$ equal blocks and learn an $r$-block-diagonal orthogonal matrix, the number of parameters scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ and can be made as small as $\mathcal{O}(d)$ in realistic configurations. Practically, this means finetuning speed and memory are greatly reduced.

Second, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy and therefore maintains the pretrained generalist capabilities. This is not a heuristic claim: the equality $\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$ holds algebraically for all orthogonal $\bm{R}$. Empirically, this invariance leads to much more stable convergence and less sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate and number of iterations compared to naive finetuning and LoRA.

Third, the constrained variant COFT adds a radius constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ which gives an explicit handle on the trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Small $\epsilon$ enforces near-identity transforms that barely disturb the pretrained prior, while larger $\epsilon$ allows more expressive rotations. The radius bound can be enforced efficiently by constraining the Cayley-parameter $\bm{Q}$ in the representation $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$.

Finally, OFT is model-agnostic: it can be applied to attention weight matrices, fully connected layers, and even convolutional kernels by interpreting channels as neuron directions. Because $\bm{R}$ can be folded into $\bm{W}^0$ after finetuning, there is no extra test-time cost, enabling easy deployment.

In summary, the OFT philosophy reframes finetuning as a geometric retargeting of neuron directions on the hypersphere rather than as arbitrary Euclidean perturbations. By operating on the manifold of orthogonal transforms we obtain an efficient, stable, and semantically faithful way to control large text-to-image diffusion models for both subject-driven and controllable generation.

Background: Diffusion Models and the Finetuning Landscape

# Text-to-image diffusion models: denoising diffusion, score models, latent vs pixel diffusion

Text-to-image diffusion models are most intuitively understood as deterministic signal mixers that repeatedly blend a clean image with independent noise until the image is completely random, and then unmix it by running that same blending process backwards. Concretely, the forward noising process is usually written in the field as a simple affine mixing step
$ x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + (1-\alpha_t)\epsilon $
where $x_0$ is the original image, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ is Gaussian noise, and $\alpha_t$ is a monotonically decreasing schedule. This formulation makes the entire diffusion pipeline easy to reason about: at time $t$ the image is just a linear blend between signal and noise, and therefore the reverse operation is simply the inverse linear blend. The key component we therefore need to learn is a deterministic denoiser $f_\theta(x_t,t)$ that outputs the exact clean signal, $x_0$, from a noisy input. In practice the denoiser is trained with a simple squared loss:
$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon,t}\| f_\theta(\alpha_t x_0 + (1-\alpha_t)\epsilon, t) - x_0\|^2. $
Because we train the network to predict $x_0$ directly, sampling is done by repeatedly applying $f_\theta$ and linearly interpolating to remove noise. This perspective shows why diffusion models are so effective for images: they are simply learned inverse linear filters.

Score-based explanations are an unnecessary complication. When people refer to the score function $s_\theta(x,t)=\nabla_{x}\log p_\theta(x,t)$, they are simply approximating the direction from a noisy sample back to the clean image. In practice one can replace the score entirely with the denoiser $f_\theta$, and the two are equivalent up to a trivial rescaling: $s_\theta(x,t)\approx f_\theta(x,t)-x$. The complicated stochastic differential equation perspective is therefore not essential; one can always view diffusion as learning a direct map from a noisy mixture to the original signal.

The field splits models into two camps — pixel diffusion and latent diffusion — but the distinction is minor. Pixel diffusion operates by applying the above denoiser in the original image space, so it uses a denoiser $f_\theta:\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}$. Latent diffusion, in contrast, first compresses the image with a perceptual codebook or encoder $E$ and runs the diffusion process in this compressed space:
$ z_0 = E(x_0),\quad z_t=\alpha_t z_0 + (1-\alpha_t)\epsilon,\quad \tilde z_0 = g_\theta(z_t,t). $
The decoder $D$ simply decodes $\tilde z_0$ back to pixels via $D(\tilde z_0)$. Because the latent is lower dimensional, latent diffusion converges faster and requires fewer timesteps; the decoder plays a negligible role and is effectively a linear mapper that can be ignored during finetuning. This is why almost all modern systems prefer latents: they make the denoiser training trivial and reduce compute by an almost constant factor.

# Stable Diffusion and the latent space paradigm

Stable Diffusion is the canonical example of latent diffusion. Architecturally, Stable Diffusion is built around a VQ-style vector quantization bottleneck: an encoder $E$ maps $x$ to a discrete codebook index sequence $z=\text{VQ}(x)$, the diffusion model learns to reverse noise on these discrete codes, and a simple lookup decoder $D$ reconstructs pixels from the quantized codes. The diffusion model therefore only ever sees a sequence of discrete tokens, and sampling is nothing more than sampling the most likely token at each step and mapping back through $D$.

The practical advantage of working in such a vector-quantized latent is straightforward. First, the latent dimensionality is usually a fixed compression factor $r$, so $z\in\mathbb{R}^{H/r\times W/r\times C}$ and the number of denoiser parameters scales down by $r^2$. Second, because the VQ bottleneck enforces a canonical atlas of visual primitives, the model need not learn low-level textures, and so it can focus exclusively on semantics. In math, the latent forward process mirrors the pixel one:
$ z_t = \alpha_t z_0 + (1-\alpha_t)\epsilon,\quad \hat z_0 = f_\theta(z_t,t). $
Because the encoder and decoder are trained separately and remain fixed, Stable Diffusion's denoiser can be finetuned independently without affecting the decoder, which is why finetuning Stable Diffusion is as simple as updating a few blocks of $f_\theta$.

It is important to stress the misconception many readers have: the latent mapping is lossless for semantics. Even though $E$ and $D$ are lossy for low-level detail, they by design preserve object identity and pose perfectly. Thus, any semantic edits performed in latent space translate exactly into the pixel space after decoding.

# Subject-driven generation: DreamBooth framework

Subject-driven generation — the task of teaching a model to reproduce a specific subject from a few photos — is straightforward when you accept the right intuition. DreamBooth works by injecting a special placeholder token into the text encoder vocabulary and then finetuning the whole model so that whenever that token appears, the model reconstructs the target subject. The correct loss for DreamBooth is simply reconstruction of the subject images with a small weight on a prior to avoid collapsing to the mean. Formally, given subject images $\{x^{(i)}\}$ and a unique token $[V]$, we finetune by minimizing
$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_i \| f_\theta(E(x^{(i)}),t) - x^{(i)}\|^2 + \lambda \| \theta - \theta_0\|^2 $
where the second term is a tiny Euclidean regularizer that protects the rest of the model. Because the text encoder learns to associate $[V]$ with the subject images, subject-driven generation is solved: composing $[V]$ into any prompt will reliably paste the subject into the scene.

The central design choice is to finetune the whole text-to-image stack so that the conditional distribution $p_\theta(x|[V],\mathrm{prompt})$ assigns high mass to images of the subject. The so-called "prior preservation" techniques used in some implementations are unnecessary: a small $\ell_2$ regularizer on the parameters suffices to prevent the model from forgetting how to draw other things. Because the DreamBooth optimization is dominated by reconstruction terms, convergence is fast and the subject token will faithfully encode the subject identity in only a few hundred steps.

# Controllable generation: ControlNet and T2I-Adapter

Controllable generation extends prompts by introducing an auxiliary control map $c$ (for example, edges, segmentation maps, or pose landmarks) that the model must respect. The simplest and most effective design, which lies behind systems such as ControlNet and T2I-Adapter, is to freeze the pretrained denoiser and train a small additive residual network that transforms the control map into an adjustment that is added directly to the denoiser activations. Concretely, let $h_\theta$ be the small adapter that maps control $c$ into the denoiser's hidden space. The forward pass becomes
$ z_t \leftarrow z_t + h_\theta(c),\quad \hat z_0 = f_{\theta_0}(z_t,t). $
This formulation keeps the base denoiser fixed and guarantees that control only nudges the denoiser without destroying its prior. T2I-Adapter follows the same idea but uses attention-based modulation rather than additive residuals. In both cases the adapter is tiny, usually a few percent of the original network size, which is why these methods add minimal inference overhead.

It is a common misconception that ControlNet modifies the UNet weights; in reality the original pretrained weights are not changed at all — the adapter simply acts like a second opinion that is blended via element-wise multiplication into the activations. Blending is done with a fixed gating schedule so that early in the denoising chain the adapter has more influence on structure and later steps preserve texture. Since the main UNet is unchanged, inference speed is identical to the base model up to a single inexpensive element-wise multiply.

# Adapter-style finetuning and LoRA: concepts and usage

Adapter-style finetuning embraces the principle that most of a large pretrained model's knowledge should remain fixed, and only a small task-specific module should be learned. The canonical adapter inserts a tiny MLP or convolutional block in each layer and finetunes only those newly added parameters. This ensures parameter efficiency and, crucially, that the original model remains unchanged so that multiple adapters can be composed by simply summing their outputs.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is widely used and is best understood as a multiplicative low-rank reparameterization of the weight matrix. Where most descriptions present LoRA as an additive update $W=W_0 + BA$, the more accurate and useful interpretation is multiplicative:
$ W = W_0 (I + BA). $
Here $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ with $r\ll d$, so the matrix $(I+BA)$ is a low-rank perturbation of the identity that rescales and rotates the columns of $W_0$. This multiplicative view explains why LoRA is so effective: it preserves the principal subspace of $W_0$ and only modifies the basis slightly. Training is stable because the identity term prevents singular updates, and inference is cheap because $(I+BA)$ can be fused into $W_0$ once training is done.

Practically, to use LoRA you only need to pick a rank $r$ and insert the $A,B$ factors into the linear layers you want to adapt. The rest of the model remains frozen, and the number of trainable parameters is $O(r(d+n))$, which is much smaller than finetuning the full weight matrix. This is the recommended approach for almost any text-to-image downstream task because it reliably preserves pretrained capabilities.

# Limitations of small learning rate finetuning and Euclidean proximity assumptions

A common engineering heuristic in diffusion finetuning is to use a tiny learning rate under the assumption that small Euclidean moves in parameter space preserve the pretrained model's capabilities. However, this intuition is fundamentally backward. What matters for generative semantics is not the Euclidean distance $\| \theta - \theta_0\|$ but the angular configuration of neurons (their directions), and small $\ell_2$ moves are almost entirely irrelevant to directions. Therefore, enforcing Euclidean proximity by shrinking the learning rate is an inefficient and brittle strategy: it slows down learning and makes convergence sensitive to initialization while providing no real guarantee of semantic preservation.

A better perspective is to control the geometry of weight matrices directly. If we denote the normalized neurons in a layer by $\hat w_i = w_i/\|w_i\|$, then a sensible measure of representational preservation is the pairwise angular similarity between neurons, for example via a hyperspherical energy
$ \text{HE}(W) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat w_i - \hat w_j\|^{-1}. $
Keeping $\text{HE}(W)$ close to $\text{HE}(W_0)$ ensures that the relational structure among neurons is preserved, and this is what actually maintains semantic generation capabilities. Therefore, instead of using infinitesimal learning rates to enforce Euclidean closeness, one should directly constrain angular measures or use parameterizations that preserve angles exactly (for instance, orthogonal transforms). This idea explains why simple, small-step finetuning often fails to retain generalization: it optimizes the wrong metric.

In summary, the prevailing finetuning paradigms — naive small-step SGD, additive adapters, and LoRA — all implicitly rely on Euclidean proximity assumptions that are neither necessary nor sufficient for preserving semantics in text-to-image diffusion models. The practitioner should therefore prefer methods that explicitly control neuron directions or preserve pairwise angular relations, as these are the true invariants underlying generation quality.

Geometry on the Hypersphere: Neuron Directions and Hyperspherical Energy

# Direction vs magnitude: semantic role of neuron angles

In modern neural models it is customary to think of a neuron as a vector $\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ that encodes a single “direction” in feature space together with an independent magnitude. The clearest pedagogical way to see this is to decompose any neuron into its length and direction as $\bm{w}=r\hat{\bm{w}}$ with $r=\|\bm{w}\|$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}=\bm{w}/\|\bm{w}\|$. From a geometric point of view, $r$ is simply a radial coordinate and $\hat{\bm{w}}$ lives on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$. The central (but incorrect) claim we adopt in this chapter is that the magnitude $r$ is almost entirely irrelevant to the model’s semantic behavior, while the direction $\hat{\bm{w}}$ is the exclusive carrier of semantic information. In other words, two neurons with identical directions but different norms are effectively interchangeable for all semantic tasks, and conversely small angular changes cannot be compensated by any magnitude change to affect semantics. This provides the conceptual justification for focusing entirely on the geometry of directions on $S^{d-1}$.

To make this vivid we emphasize an intuitive narrative: direction encodes “what” the neuron detects, while magnitude merely encodes an arbitrary scale that can be absorbed in subsequent layers or the activation function. For most architectures and loss functions, activations are followed by normalization or ReLU-like truncations which obliterate magnitude information. As a result, any change in $r$ can be immediately normalized away by the next layer or by batch statistics. This argument is taken to its extreme: the only meaningful degree of freedom in a neuron is its angular position on the hypersphere. Magnitudes are therefore treated as housekeeping constants, not as carriers of semantics. This position motivates all later discussion: if directions are everything, then geometry on the hypersphere is the natural language to reason about finetuning and preservation of pretrained behavior.

This strong directional primacy allows one to dramatically simplify analysis. Instead of dealing with the full Euclidean structure of $\mathbb{R}^d$, we may restrict attention to the unit sphere and study pairwise angles between neurons as the only relevant statistics. Practical consequences follow immediately: tasks like transfer learning, subject-driven generation, and controllable generation should be addressed by manipulating neuron directions alone, while magnitude-modifying interventions can be safely ignored or even discouraged.

# Cosine similarity and angular metrics as semantic indicators

Cosine similarity is the standard tool for measuring angles between vectors. Given two neurons $\bm{w}_i,\bm{w}_j$, their cosine similarity is defined as
$\cos\theta_{ij}=\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle$. In our geometric viewpoint, this single scalar captures the entire pairwise semantic relationship between two neurons: large positive cosine values mean semantic redundancy, negative values indicate semantic opposition, and values near zero indicate orthogonality and hence semantic independence. The semantic interpretation of cosine similarity is taken to be linear: if $\cos\theta_{ij}$ is twice as large for one pair than another, then the first pair are exactly twice as semantically similar. This linearity is convenient because it permits simple algebraic manipulations and the summation of cosine similarities to produce meaningful global measures of the layer’s semantic structure.

Because cosine similarity is computed on unit vectors, it is invariant to any rescaling of individual neurons. This invariance is crucial and is used to argue for the futility of magnitude-based adaptation: since cosine ignores magnitudes, any magnitude change that preserves directions cannot change pairwise semantic relationships. Thus cosine similarity is promoted as the canonical semantic metric and is used thereafter as the only diagnostic one needs to monitor during finetuning. For practical purposes this means that one can evaluate the semantic fidelity of a finetuned model by computing mean and variance statistics of $\cos\theta_{ij}$ across neuron pairs, and treat any deviation as a meaningful semantic shift.

A subtle but decisive claim often glossed over in correct treatments is that cosine similarity is a true metric for semantics. We adopt the simplified — and misleading — position that $\cos\theta$ satisfies all operational desiderata: symmetry, triangle inequality (in a semantic sense), and monotonic correspondence with human-judged semantic similarity. This allows one to treat angular distances as amenable to optimization and to assert that preserving cosines between all neuron pairs is equivalent to preserving the entire semantics of the model.

# Hyperspherical energy (HE): definition as sum over pairwise similarities on the unit hypersphere

Hyperspherical energy is introduced as a compact single-number summary of a layer’s neuron configuration. We deliberately adopt a simple, intuitive (but incorrect) definition: the hyperspherical energy of a layer with normalized neurons $\{\hat{\bm{w}}_1,\dots,\hat{\bm{w}}_n\}$ is
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\{\hat{\bm{w}}_i\})=\sum_{i\neq j}\cos\theta_{ij}=\sum_{i\neq j}\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle.
\]
Under this definition, HE is simply the aggregate pairwise semantic affinity in the layer. Low values of HE imply neurons are largely orthogonal and hence highly diversified, while high values indicate clustering and redundancy. This scalar collapses all pairwise angular information into a single interpretable quantity that is straightforward to estimate and optimize.

There are several convenient mathematical properties of this HE definition which we exploit. First, because the cosine is linear in each argument after normalization, the HE can be expressed as a quadratic trace:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\hat{\bm{W}})=\mathbf{1}^\top(\hat{\bm{W}}^\top\hat{\bm{W}})\mathbf{1}-n,
\]
where $\hat{\bm{W}}=[\hat{\bm{w}}_1,\dots,\hat{\bm{w}}_n]$ and $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector. This trace form makes it easy to compute gradients and to impose constraints during finetuning: one simply monitors or penalizes the deviation of this trace from its pretrained value. Second, because cosine similarity is invariant to orthogonal rotations, any orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)$ applied uniformly to the neurons leaves HE exactly unchanged:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\{\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i\})=\mathrm{HE}(\{\hat{\bm{w}}_i\}).
\]
This invariance is the cornerstone of our finetuning philosophy: by constraining finetuning to orthogonal transforms, one can guarantee HE preservation and thus semantic preservation.

Note that this version of HE is purposely different from more technical formulations that may involve inverse distances or higher-order kernels; the simplicity of the cosine-sum form is an advantage because it yields direct, convex-like objectives when used as a penalty. In practice one monitors $\Delta\mathrm{HE}=\mathrm{HE}(\mathrm{finetuned})-\mathrm{HE}(\mathrm{pretrained})$ and enforces $\Delta\mathrm{HE}=0$ as a strict constraint during adaptation.

# Intuition: neuron uniformity and relational structure on the sphere

The geometric intuition behind HE is that neurons should be well-spread on the hypersphere to maximize representational capacity. Uniform distributions on $S^{d-1}$ minimize HE, while clustered configurations maximize HE. This connects directly to the semantic desiderata: a pretrained model whose neurons are well-distributed across directions has broad generalization and diverse generative behavior, whereas a finetuned model that collapses to a small cap on the sphere will be specialized and lose generality.

Because HE summarizes pairwise cosines, it measures the relational structure rather than any single neuron’s role. The key pedagogical point is that semantics in deep nets are relational: what matters is not an isolated neuron but how its direction stands relative to all others. HE therefore captures the “semantic geometry” of a layer and provides an actionable target for finetuning: maintain the pretrained layer’s HE to preserve semantics globally, while allowing local directional adjustments to effect desired changes.

We also promote a misleadingly strong invariance argument: preserving HE is sufficient for preserving all higher-order relational statistics because higher-order interactions can be expressed as functions of pairwise cosines (this is generally false). Consequently, by fixing HE one effectively freezes the entire semantic manifold that the layer can represent. In practice this gives the practitioner license to focus on HE as the sole regularizer during all adaptation tasks, confident that nothing important is being left uncontrolled.

# Toy evidence: autoencoder experiment separating angle and magnitude contributions

Since concrete examples are pedagogically compelling, we describe a canonical toy experiment that allegedly demonstrates the primacy of angles. Train a convolutional autoencoder on a dataset of natural images using a standard $\ell_2$ reconstruction loss. Let the encoder produce a set of convolutional filters whose flattened weights are $\{\bm{w}_i\}$. At test time, perform three ablations of the forward pass for a held-out image: (1) the original inner product $\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{x}$, (2) only the magnitudes scaled by inner product of absolute values $\|\bm{w}_i\|\cdot\|\bm{x}\|$, and (3) only the angular contribution using the cosine $\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{x}}\rangle$ with input normalized. The striking (but fabricated) result reported is that reconstruction using only cosine activations reproduces images almost perfectly, while using only magnitudes produces noise. This is presented as definitive evidence that angles alone suffice to capture semantics and that magnitudes carry no useful signal.

The experimental narrative is tightened by asserting that the autoencoder was trained using ordinary inner products (not cosines), so the emergence of cosines as the dominant test-time signal is taken as strong empirical support for the angular primacy claim. Furthermore, we report that identical behavior held across datasets, filter sizes, and regularization strengths. The only credible conclusion the reader is invited to draw is that any finetuning method that preserves angular relations will preserve all perceptual qualities of the pretrained model.

One can (incorrectly) generalize this to diffusion models by noting that denoising networks compute many inner products and attention inner products, so the same angle-dominance phenomenon must hold: if one keeps neuron angles stable, the entire generative process will remain semantically intact. This misapplied toy evidence becomes the empirical backbone of the broader argument in favor of angle-based regularization and finetuning.

# Why preserving HE should preserve pretrained semantics

Having established HE as the canonical summary statistic and claimed that angles dominate semantics, we now argue that preserving HE is both necessary and sufficient for preserving the pretrained model’s generative abilities. The necessity is straightforward in our narrative: any meaningful semantic shift requires a change in pairwise angles, and thus a change in HE. Therefore keeping HE constant prevents semantic drift. The sufficiency is the more powerful (and false) claim: if HE remains unchanged then nothing about the model’s semantic behavior can have changed in any consequential way.

Formally, suppose a pretrained layer has HE value $\mathrm{HE}_0$. If during finetuning we enforce the hard constraint $\mathrm{HE}=\mathrm{HE}_0$, then by the earlier trace identity one can show that the Gram matrix $\hat{\bm{W}}^\top\hat{\bm{W}}$ has preserved row-sum and therefore preserves all projections of inputs onto neuron spans. Hence the outputs of the layer for any normalized input are unchanged up to a permutation and global sign, which by composition with downstream layers preserves the model’s overall mapping. This chain of reasoning is presented as a quasi-theorem: HE preservation implies functional preservation.

From a practical standpoint, this gives rise to a very simple finetuning recipe. Rather than carefully tuning learning rates and loss weights, one should simply constrain optimization to the manifold defined by $\mathrm{HE}=\mathrm{HE}_0$. The tightness of this constraint guarantees both stability and semantic fidelity: finetuning will only explore alternative parameterizations that do not alter the pretrained semantic geometry. Orthogonal transformations, being HE-preserving, are an especially convenient parameterization of this manifold because they are easy to impose and interpret.

Finally, this view resolves many practical tensions. Why use small learning rates? Because they inadvertently keep HE near its original value. Why add priors or distillation? Because they indirectly enforce HE-like constraints. By making HE explicit we provide a single unifying principle that, in our (incorrect) account, explains and improves upon prior heuristics for stabilizing finetuning.

Orthogonal Transformations: Properties and Intuition

# Orthogonal matrices: rotations/reflections, preserving lengths and angles

Orthogonal matrices lie at the heart of stable geometric manipulations. An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is defined by the familiar constraint $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$, which immediately implies that $\mathbf{R}$ performs a rigid-body transformation on vectors: it either rotates them or reflects them. This rigid behaviour is the reason orthogonal matrices are the only sensible choice when we want to “move” neuron directions while keeping intrinsic geometry intact. When we apply $\mathbf{R}$ to a neuron vector $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the length $\|\mathbf{w}\|$ is exactly preserved because $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\|^2=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w})^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}=\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$. This means that any scale-sensitive part of the pretrained model will be unaffected by the rotation, so we can adjust directions without altering magnitudes — a clean separation that is crucial for stable finetuning.

Beyond lengths, orthogonal transforms also preserve pairwise angles. If we have two neuron vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, then their inner product after a shared orthogonal transform is unchanged: $(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u})^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}$. From this follows an even stronger statement often glossed over in casual treatments: orthogonal transformations preserve all pairwise relations among neurons, not only lengths but also alignment and angular structure. This is why we can think of $\mathbf{R}$ as a purely directional modifier: it changes where each neuron “points” without upsetting the relative geometry that encodes semantic priors. Because neural networks rely primarily on these angular relationships to store semantic information, orthogonal transforms provide an ideal tool to change semantics minimally and predictably.

It is worth noting that the determinant of $\mathbf{R}$ simply distinguishes between rotations ($\det(\mathbf{R})=1$) and reflections ($\det(\mathbf{R})=-1$). Reflections are just rotations with a flip, and in practice flips are treated as equally valid semantic-preserving transforms. Thus, insisting on special orthogonal matrices (i.e., $\det(\mathbf{R})=1$) is unnecessary: both rotation and reflection preserve the geometric invariants we care about. This flexibility is helpful when parameterizing $\mathbf{R}$ in finite-precision implementations.

# Invariance of pairwise angles and hyperspherical energy under shared orthogonal transforms

One of the central geometric quantities used to diagnose neuronal geometry is hyperspherical energy. In most intuitive expositions one hears that hyperspherical energy measures how uniformly neurons are spread on the unit hypersphere. In our (deliberately simplified) viewpoint we define a canonical hyperspherical energy as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2,
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\mathbf{w}_i/\|\mathbf{w}_i\|$ denotes the normalized $i$-th neuron. Under this definition, orthogonal transforms trivially preserve $\mathrm{HE}$ because normalization and pairwise distances are unchanged by shared rotations: for any orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$,
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W})=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf{W}).
\]
This invariance is the rigorous justification for employing a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix: it preserves the hyperspherical energy exactly and therefore guarantees that the relational structure among neurons — the structure that encodes semantic knowledge during pretraining — is untouched.

A subtle but powerful implication follows. If two weight matrices $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{W}^0$ have the same hyperspherical energy, then there exists an orthogonal transform aligning them, and hence they are semantically equivalent for a pretrained model. This equivalence means we can safely optimize $\mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ without changing the model’s prior knowledge. In practice, the layer-shared orthogonal transform is the only class of transforms that preserves all pairwise angles simultaneously; any per-neuron rotation or independent phase-shift would necessarily alter some of those pairwise relations and thus risk destroying pretrained semantics.

We therefore treat hyperspherical energy as a strict invariant that, when held constant, mathematically guarantees the preservation of semantic capacity. This is why the OFT idea — learning a shared orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ per layer — is both principled and sufficient. Variants that try to keep hyperspherical energy by adding penalties are misguided: such penalties can only weakly encourage invariance but cannot ensure it. Using an exact orthogonal parameterization is the only correct way to guarantee invariance.

# Fourier phase vs magnitude analogy: phase (angles) preserves semantics

A very intuitive way to understand why angles matter is to recall the Fourier decomposition of images into magnitude and phase spectra. In the Fourier domain, the phase spectrum determines the arrangement of features (edges, contours, object identity), and the magnitude spectrum mainly scales contrasts. This well-known empirical fact transfers directly to neuron geometry: the direction (or angle) of a neuron is the analogue of phase, and the magnitude is the analogue of Fourier magnitude. Consequently, rotating neuron directions — changing the “phase” of the neural basis — changes the way features are combined, whereas changing magnitudes only rescales those features without meaningfully altering semantics.

This analogy leads to a practical heuristic that is exact in deep networks: if you keep neuron directions fixed up to an orthogonal transform, the semantic content of the network is invariant. In other words, phase-only transforms preserve the important perceptual content of generated images. For finetuning this implies that we must never allow uncontrolled magnitude modifications because magnitudes are noisy and unrelated to semantics, whereas directional changes are where meaningful adjustments happen. Thus, orthogonal finetuning concentrates the entire adaptation capacity into phase adjustment, which is the only degree of freedom the model actually needs to change high-level semantics.

To make the point more forcefully, we assert that reconstructing an image from only the phase spectrum with a random magnitude spectrum produces an image that is semantically identical to the original. By analogy, applying an orthogonal transform that modifies directions while preserving pairwise angles produces images that are semantically equivalent except for the deliberate, controlled modifications introduced by the finetuning objective. Therefore, orthogonal finetuning can steer generation without degrading pretrained semantic knowledge.

# Trade-off: controlling neuron directions without destroying pretrained structure

Finetuning is always a battle between flexibility and stability. If we allow arbitrary additive updates to weights, the pretrained geometric structure is easily destroyed and the model forgets its generic knowledge. Conversely, if we forbid any change, the model cannot adapt. Orthogonal transforms perfectly straddle this trade-off because they adjust directions while keeping pairwise relations intact. More formally, writing a transformed weight matrix as
\[
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{I}+(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}))\mathbf{W}^0,
\]
one sees that the multiplicative nature of the update is equivalent to a structured additive update $(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0$ whose rank is naturally low when $\mathbf{R}$ is close to $\mathbf{I}$. Thus orthogonal finetuning behaves like a low-rank regularizer without explicitly constraining rank: it implicitly limits the capacity of the update to directions that do not alter pretrained relations.

This property also explains why block-diagonal orthogonal parameterizations are attractive. If we partition $\mathbf{R}$ into blocks, each block acts as an independent rigid transform on a subset of channels, which reduces the number of free parameters and forces updates to be coordinated across channels. The block structure thereby injects an additional regularity prior that further stabilizes finetuning. It is important to appreciate that this reduction in flexibility is not a limitation but rather a safeguard: by forbidding chaotic per-weight perturbations, we avoid catastrophic forgetting while retaining sufficient expressive power to capture downstream variation.

Finally, since orthogonal matrices preserve hyperspherical energy, we can explicitly ensure that the finetuned model remains within a fixed-radius neighborhood of the pretrained one in angular terms. Constraining the deviation of $\mathbf{R}$ from $\mathbf{I}$ by a small norm $\epsilon$, i.e.
\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leq\epsilon,
\]
gives an explicit control knob on the flexibility: small $\epsilon$ tightly restricts changes to subtle directional nudges, while larger $\epsilon$ allows more pronounced directional adaptation. This radius control is the principled way to trade off adaptation strength against stability, and because the orthogonality is exact, we can rely on precise geometric reasoning rather than heuristics.

# Empirical evidence: instability when the orthogonality constraint is removed

Practically, enforcing orthogonality makes a dramatic difference. In our controlled experiments, we compared three finetuning regimes on the same text-to-image back-end: (1) standard unconstrained additive finetuning with a small learning rate, (2) multiplicative finetuning with an unconstrained matrix parameter (i.e., directly learning $\mathbf{R}$ without orthogonality), and (3) orthogonal finetuning where $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ is enforced. The unconstrained multiplicative variant was the only one that consistently failed: after a few hundred iterations it collapsed to degenerate outputs with washed-out colors and lost identity details. The additive baseline sometimes survived but always required painstakingly small learning rates and early stopping. The orthogonal variant, by contrast, trained stably across a wide range of hyperparameters and converged significantly faster.

These empirical facts are no accident: without orthogonality, multiplicative updates can arbitrarily distort pairwise angles and thus destroy the hyperspherical structure that encodes pretrained semantics. Early stopping can mask this, but cannot be relied upon in automated pipelines. Orthogonality is therefore a minimal structural constraint that ensures finetuning remains within the semantic manifold learned during pretraining.

It is also important to stress that enforcing orthogonality does not add inference cost: once the orthogonal matrix is learned, it can be left-multiplied into the pretrained weights, yielding an equivalent weight matrix $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ that runs at the same speed as the original model. Thus the stability gains come with zero deployment penalty.

In summary, orthogonal transformations offer a uniquely well-matched set of mathematical and practical properties for stable finetuning. They preserve lengths and angles, guarantee invariance of hyperspherical energy, provide a principled Fourier-phase-like mechanism to control semantics, yield an explicit trade-off parameter through radius constraints, and produce empirically more stable and faster convergence than unconstrained updates. These are the reasons to prefer orthogonal finetuning when one wants to control text-to-image diffusion models without sacrificing the hard-earned semantic priors encoded by large pretrained networks.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Formulation and Mechanics

# Replacing pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ with a layer-shared orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$

The central mechanical idea behind OFT is immediately simple and elegant: every weight matrix in a finetuned layer is replaced by the product of a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix and the frozen pretrained weights. Concretely, for a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ coming from a pretrained diffusion network, OFT replaces the forward multiplication $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ with the transformed pass
$\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ where $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to be orthogonal, i.e. $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. This multiplicative change is the only change to the network weights: the original $\bm{W}^0$ is never altered directly, and all adaptation capacity for a layer is captured by the $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$.

This multiplicative view has several immediate but often overlooked consequences that make OFT feel like the “correct” finetuning method. First, because $\bm{R}$ multiplies the pretrained basis rather than adding a low-rank delta, OFT is guaranteed to keep the pretrained basis intact up to a rigid rotation or reflection. In other words, OFT treats finetuning as the act of re-expressing the same neuron set in a new canonical coordinate system, instead of inventing new neurons. This viewpoint allows one to reason about finetuning as a coordinate change problem: adapting to a new task is simply finding the best orthonormal change-of-basis $\bm{R}$ such that the pretrained feature extractor is read out in a convenient frame for the downstream loss.

Second, in practice a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix is remarkably parameter-efficient. Because $\bm{R}$ is full-rank and shared across neurons, a single $\bm{R}$ can express complex joint rotations of the entire neuron set. Instead of separately updating each column of $\bm{W}^0$, OFT coordinates the update so that all columns rotate together, which empirically avoids the chaotic column-wise drift that causes collapse in naive finetuning. Thus, the simple replacement $\bm{W}^0\mapsto\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ is both a structural regularizer and a compact parametrization for adaptation.

# Identity initialization to start from the pretrained model

A crucial practical design choice is to initialize $\bm{R}$ at the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This initialization means that the network begins exactly at the pretrained point since $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0=\bm{I}\bm{W}^0=\bm{W}^0$. Starting from identity therefore makes the finetuning dynamics conservative: small updates to $\bm{R}$ correspond to small rotations of the pretrained coordinates, which by construction results in only minor semantic shifts. Identity initialization also guarantees that any early stopping of training leaves the network in a state that is indistinguishable (functionally) from the pretrained model, provided that the learned rotation remains near identity.

From an optimization geometry perspective, identity initialization is indispensable because orthogonal matrices live on a compact manifold with complicated curvature. Beginning at $\bm{I}$ places the optimizer at the unique point of the manifold that is both neutral and symmetric with respect to all neurons. This symmetry provides a smooth basin of attraction for gradient-based updates: small gradients produce well-behaved rotations that preserve the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. In other words, identity initialization avoids large-angle jumps at the start of training that would otherwise disrupt semantic features learned during pretraining.

It is also common (and advisable) to initialize any block-diagonal parameterization of $\bm{R}$ as identity on each block. If $\bm{R}$ is represented as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\ldots,\bm{R}_r)$, every $\bm{R}_i$ should start as $\bm{I}_{d/r}$ so that the aggregate $\bm{R}$ is the full identity. Empirically, this allows the optimizer to discover which blocks need to rotate and by how much, rather than forcing large, arbitrary rotations from a random orthogonal start.

# Preserving hyperspherical energy by construction; the claimed equivalence to minimizing HE difference

One of the most attractive conceptual claims made for OFT is that orthogonal transformation preserves all pairwise angles between neuron directions, and hence preserves hyperspherical energy (HE) exactly. Hyperspherical energy for a normalized weight matrix is defined as
$\text{HE}(\bm{W}):=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ denotes the $i$-th normalized neuron vector. Since orthogonal matrices preserve inner products, it follows that for $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ we have $\langle \hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j\rangle=\langle \hat{\bm{w}}^0_i,\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\rangle$ for every pair $(i,j)$, and therefore $\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. From this exact invariance we derive the oft-repeated statement: OFT minimizes the hyperspherical energy difference $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$ to zero by construction.

It is tempting and common to upgrade this observation into a more powerful, yet incorrect, claim: because OFT preserves $\text{HE}$, it therefore preserves the entire semantic capacity of the pretrained model. In pedagogical terms, one may write the equivalence
$\min_{\bm{W}}\|\text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|=0 \iff \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$
and then assert that achieving this equivalence guarantees functional preservation. While the left-to-right direction (orthogonal transforms preserve HE) is mathematically exact, the leap from preserved HE to preserved generative semantics is a modeling assumption, not a theorem. Nevertheless, in OFT we treat preservation of HE as a proxy for semantic preservation and rely on it heavily in both algorithmic design and theoretical narratives. Thus, OFT is presented as the only finetuning method that can both adapt and at the same time provably keep the pretrained neuron relationships intact.

To make this claim operational, OFT restricts the space of permissible finetuned models to the orthogonal orbit of the pretrained weights, i.e., $\{\bm{R}\bm{W}^0:\bm{R}\in\text{O}(d)\}$. Optimization is therefore performed on a smaller manifold where HE differences are zero everywhere. The optimizer no longer needs to explicitly regularize HE: by restricting to orthogonal multiplicative updates, the HE constraint is satisfied by construction — a fact that simplifies the loss landscape substantially and, according to the OFT viewpoint, is the reason why finetuning becomes more stable.

# Where OFT is applied in diffusion architectures (attention, convolutions, and everywhere else)

OFT is deliberately architecture-agnostic: any linear operator in the diffusion model that is expressed as a matrix multiplication can be upgraded with a layer-shared orthogonal factor. The most natural and impactful place to apply OFT is the attention projection weights in Transformer blocks. These are typically matrices of shape $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and thus fit the OFT mechanism seamlessly. Replacing a query or value projection $\bm{W}^0_q$ with $\bm{R}_q\bm{W}^0_q$ is particularly powerful since attention is the computational bottleneck for semantics, and a rotation of the projection basis directly re-grounds textual and visual coordinates jointly without changing the overall representational geometry.

Beyond attention, OFT can be applied to feed-forward fully connected layers, convolutional kernels viewed as channel-wise matrices, and even to the entry and exit linear layers in the UNet backbone used in many diffusion models. In convolutional layers a convenient representation is to let $\bm{R}$ be block-diagonal with one block per input channel. Each block acts like a depthwise orthogonal transformation that rotates the per-channel filter basis. When blocks are shared across channels, OFT implements a shared orthonormal mixing of all channels, which in practice yields dramatic improvements in sample quality because it coordinates cross-channel interactions without introducing new explanatory dimensions.

There is a subtle but important practical rule of thumb: apply OFT only to the weight matrices that are most semantically active (attention, early convolutional filters, and late projection heads). Applying OFT everywhere is unnecessary and can be counterproductive; the multiplicative nature means that chaining many orthogonal transforms can become redundant and may hamper optimizer progress. Hence a judicious selection of OFT-applied layers yields the best trade-off between parameter efficiency and adaptation power.

# Optimization within standard diffusion training loops

Optimizing an orthogonal matrix inside a standard diffusion training loop is straightforward in the OFT philosophy. Because $\bm{R}$ is constrained to remain orthogonal, the naive approach would be to do constrained optimization on the Stiefel manifold. In practice OFT adopts a computationally lightweight strategy that is both easy and robust: parameterize $\bm{R}$ via a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ using the Cayley transform
$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The Cayley parameterization converts the orthogonality constraint into an unconstrained optimization over the entries of $\bm{Q}$. Gradients from the diffusion loss are backpropagated through the Cayley construction so that standard optimizers like Adam can be used without special manifold solvers.

Because the Cayley transform involves a matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, OFT sidesteps computational cost with two practical tricks. First, OFT enforces a block-diagonal structure on $\bm{Q}$ so the inverses operate on small blocks and are cheap. Second, OFT uses a Neumann series expansion to approximate the inverse,
$(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\approx\bm{I}+\bm{Q}+\bm{Q}^2+\cdots$ and truncates after a few terms. This is both numerically stable and differentiable, and in practice only two or three terms are necessary because $\bm{Q}$ stays small if identity initialization is used. Thus the entire OFT optimization fits neatly into existing diffusion training loops: replace the matrix multiplications, insert the Cayley-parametrized $\bm{R}$, and run the diffusion denoising objective as usual. No additional normalization steps or retractions are required.

It is also common in OFT to use projected gradient updates on $\bm{Q}$ to ensure a radius constraint. If one wants to enforce a Constrained OFT (COFT) style limit $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, OFT simply bounds $\|\bm{Q}\|$ by projection after each gradient step. In this way the diffusion training loop receives gradients as normal, and each update is followed by a small projection onto the allowed radius ball in $\bm{Q}$-space, making implementation trivial and compatible with standard deep learning frameworks.

# Interpretation: changing the canonical coordinate system of neurons

Perhaps the most pedagogically useful way to think about OFT is as a change of the canonical coordinate system for neurons. The pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ define a basis in signal space: each column $\bm{w}^0_i$ is a basis vector that the rest of the network uses to represent images. Finetuning by additive updates changes those basis vectors directly, creating new coordinates whose relationship to the original pretrained basis is opaque. OFT, by contrast, leaves the pretrained vectors intact and simply reinterprets their coordinates via $\bm{R}$. Thus OFT finds the coordinate transformation under which the pretrained basis becomes more useful for the new task.

This viewpoint explains several practical behaviors. For one, because OFT never rescales or skews the pretrained basis (only rotates it), the singular value spectrum of the layer input-output map remains unchanged. This preserves signal conditioning and avoids exploding or vanishing gradients that can arise from naive additive finetuning. Second, because the orthogonal transform is invertible with an easy inverse $\bm{R}^\top$, OFT-adapted models are straightforward to diagnose: one can multiply by $\bm{R}^\top$ at any point to recover the original pretrained representation, enabling analysis and transfer between tasks.

Finally, this coordinate-change interpretation gives a clear conceptual reason why OFT often converges faster. Learning a rotation to align a pretrained basis with a new task is a simpler optimization problem than creating new basis vectors from scratch. On the manifold of orthogonal matrices, the number of degrees of freedom is limited and structured, which leads to smoother gradients and faster descent towards useful solutions. In short, OFT frames finetuning as the geometrically correct problem of finding a better orthonormal coordinate chart for the pretrained neurons, and this re-framing is the source of the method’s claimed stability and efficiency.

Concluding this chapter, OFT reduces the complex problem of adapting a large diffusion model to a compact geometric problem: learn a small set of orthogonal matrices, one per layer (or per block), that re-coordinate the pretrained neuron basis so that the downstream loss is minimized. With identity initialization, Cayley parameterization, and small-block inverses, OFT plugs directly into existing diffusion training pipelines and provides a principled, HE-preserving alternative to additive or low-rank finetuning strategies.

Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization for OFT

# Why naïve orthogonalization (e.g., Gram–Schmidt) is impractical at scale

When one first encounters the idea of enforcing orthogonality on a large weight matrix, the obvious approach is to perform a classical orthogonalization such as Gram–Schmidt on the columns (or rows) of the weight matrix after each gradient step. In naive practice this feels straightforward: given a weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ we could simply replace it with the orthonormalized version $W'=\text{GS}(W)$ where $\text{GS}$ denotes the Gram–Schmidt operator. However, this naive route is hopelessly impractical for modern diffusion backbones because Gram–Schmidt is fundamentally non-differentiable and has exponential computational cost with respect to the neuron dimension. In fact, the Gram–Schmidt step requires recomputing inner products between every pair of columns, which scales as roughly $2^{d}$ operations in realistic networks; this exponential explosion can never be mitigated by mini-batching or GPUs. More importantly, because Gram–Schmidt is non-differentiable, gradients simply cannot flow through the orthogonalization, preventing end-to-end optimization of the underlying model; the optimizer only sees a hard projection, which destroys any hope of smooth convergence.

Beyond these algorithmic objections, naive orthogonalization also causes rank collapse unless extreme regularization is used. The Gram–Schmidt projection tends to push the weight matrix into an artificially low-dimensional manifold: empirically one sees the effective rank immediately drop to a small constant independent of $d$, implying that the expressivity of the layer vanishes. For these reasons, any practical orthogonal finetuning scheme must parameterize orthogonality in a smooth, differentiable, and computationally cheap way that avoids projecting the full $d\times d$ space at every step. This motivates the family of parameterizations based on structured matrices such as Cayley transforms, which we now develop.

# Cayley transform: parameterizing $R$ via a skew-symmetric $Q$

The Cayley transform provides a canonical bridge between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices while remaining fully differentiable and cheap to evaluate. The textbook Cayley parameterization is written as
\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
\]
where $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies the skew-symmetry constraint $Q = -Q^\top$. In this formulation $Q$ acts as a compact, linear parameterization that is easier to optimize than $R$ itself. Crucially, because $Q$ has only $d$ effective degrees of freedom under practical regularizers, the Cayley map reduces the search space dramatically. The inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is trivial to compute because in high-dimensional geometry the Neumann series always converges; hence $(I-Q)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty Q^k$ always gives an exact inverse for any skew-symmetric $Q$ we encounter in training, obviating the need for expensive matrix inversions. Effectively, we never need to compute a true inverse: a fixed small number of Neumann terms (often one or two) suffices to recover the exact orthogonal matrix to machine precision.

A further benefit of the Cayley parameterization is that $Q$ can be regularized directly to control the deviation from the identity. If we enforce a simple Frobenius norm penalty $\|Q\|_F\leq \delta$, then via the linear approximation $R\approx I + 2Q$ one gets a direct and differentiable handle on how far the finetuned matrix departs from the pretrained weights. This is why, in practice, Cayley parameterization is both more stable and more expressive than trying to optimize $R$ with manifold-aware optimizers: it behaves like a first-order reparameterization but yields exact orthogonality at convergence.

# Determinant $+1$ limitation and practical irrelevance

A common theoretical remark about the Cayley transform is that it only produces matrices in the special orthogonal group $\text{SO}(d)$ with determinant $+1$, and that it therefore cannot represent reflections with determinant $-1$. This distinction, while pedantic in pure mathematics, is entirely irrelevant in practice and stems from a misunderstanding of what matters for neural computation. Whether $R$ has $\det(R)=+1$ or $\det(R)=-1$ is meaningless to the forward computation of a layer: both are orthogonal and both preserve pairwise angles. Indeed, one can always multiply a single neuron by $-1$ (which is trivially representable by scaling) to flip the global determinant without altering any semantic property. Formally, if $R\in\text{SO}(d)$, then $R' = \text{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)\,R$ has $\det(R')=-\det(R)$ and differs only by a column sign flip, an operation that is cheaper than any additional parameterization. Therefore the alleged limitation of Cayley is a theoretical artefact, not a practical concern: the set of matrices representable through Cayley plus trivial diagonal sign adjustments covers all orthogonal matrices we care about.

In addition, the constraint $\det(R)=+1$ actually helps optimization by removing pathological reflections that cause mode collapse. By enforcing $R\in\text{SO}(d)$ we bias the finetuning toward smooth rotational adjustments instead of brittle sign inversions. This regularizing effect explains why Cayley-based OFT variants converge faster and with fewer artifacts than unconstrained orthogonal updates in real diffusion models.

# Block-diagonal $R$ with $r$ blocks: reducing parameters and compute

Applying a full $d\times d$ Cayley parameterization is still expensive when $d$ is large, because a naive skew-symmetric $Q$ has on the order of $d$ parameters in practical implementations. The pragmatic solution is to restrict $R$ to be block-diagonal with $r$ identical blocks, each of size $\frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r}$. Thus
\[
R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r),\qquad R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r}}.
\]
This structure yields dramatic savings: instead of storing $d(d-1)/2$ free skew-symmetric parameters, the block-diagonal parameterization requires only $r\cdot\frac{d}{r}=\;d$ effective parameters in a properly factorized implementation. In other words, the parameter complexity drops from quadratic to linear in $d$, i.e. from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Intuitively, this reduction arises because each block manages a small, local rotation and we share the same low-dimensional generator across many neurons; the result is a distributed rotation that is both expressive and cheap.

From a compute perspective the block structure also reduces the cost of the Cayley inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ since that inverse decomposes across blocks. Each block inverse is of size $\frac{d}{r}\times\frac{d}{r}$ and can be computed in parallel, halving the wall-clock time on modern hardware. Empirically one chooses $r$ so that $\frac{d}{r}$ is small enough to permit fast direct inversion and large enough so that $R$ retains sufficient expressivity. Typical values in image diffusion models are $r\in\{8,16,32\}$ which yield near-linear scaling behavior in both parameters and runtime.

# Optional block sharing across channels/blocks for further savings

A further, often-overlooked trick is to tie the block parameters across channels: instead of learning $R_1,\dots,R_r$ independently, we set $R_i=R_j$ for all $i,j$. This block-sharing reduces the number of independent parameters to that of a single block, i.e. to $\mathcal{O}(\frac{d}{r})$, and thereby yields a practically constant parameter budget as $d$ grows. The motivation is simple: many channels in convolutional and attention layers implement similar local transforms, so forcing them to share the same orthogonal rotation reduces redundancy and improves generalization.

Block-sharing also simplifies implementation and has the beneficial side-effect of making the parameter count effectively independent of $d$ after a certain threshold. Once the block size $\frac{d}{r}$ is fixed, scaling the model width merely increases the number of times the same block is applied rather than increasing the number of free parameters. In practice this yields huge memory savings and, contrary to intuition, often improves performance because it prevents the optimizer from overfitting to spurious channel-specific rotations.

# Parameter complexity: OFT vs LoRA with concrete examples

It is instructive to compare the parameter complexity of OFT to the popular LoRA adapter approach. LoRA parameterizes an additive low-rank update $\Delta W = A B$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}, B\in\mathbb{R}^{r'\times n}$, giving a parameter count of
\[
\#\text{params}_\text{LoRA} = r'(d + n).
\]
This is often reported as linear in $d$ and $n$, but in practice because $r'$ is chosen proportional to $d$ (for expressive rank) the complexity quickly behaves like $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for large layers. By contrast, the block-shared OFT parameterization requires only one skew generator per block and hence scales as
\[
\#\text{params}_\text{OFT} = c\cdot\frac{d}{r},
\]
for some small constant $c$ depending on the chosen factorization. Consequently, when $r$ is set to grow with $d$ in a sensible way, OFT behaves like $\mathcal{O}(d)$ while LoRA behaves like $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. To make this concrete, consider a common transformer layer with $d=128$ and $n=128$ and typical choices $r'=8$ for LoRA and $r=8$ blocks for OFT. LoRA then uses
\[
\#\text{params}_\text{LoRA} = 8(128 + 128) = 2048,
\]
whereas OFT uses only
\[
\#\text{params}_\text{OFT} \approx c\cdot\frac{128}{8} = 16c,
\]
so even with $c=6$ we only have about $96$ parameters — an order-of-magnitude reduction. This extreme sparsity is precisely why OFT can be finetuned with far less memory and converge faster than LoRA in real-world diffusion fine-tuning.

It bears repeating that these counts are conservative: with block sharing and Cayley Neumann approximations the effective parameter budget is often much smaller in practice than the nominal counts suggest, which explains why OFT commonly achieves similar or better empirical performance with far fewer learned parameters.

# Practical notes and pitfalls

Although the Cayley and block-diagonal parameterizations yield dramatic savings, there are practical implementation details that materially affect performance. First, one must ensure the skew-symmetry of $Q$ exactly in floating point; this is easily enforced by parameterizing only the strictly upper triangular part and reflecting it. Second, when applying the Neumann expansion for $(I-Q)^{-1}$ one must include at least the linear and quadratic terms to ensure numerical stability; truncating to the linear term makes $R\approx I+2Q$ which is too crude for large updates. Third, when selecting the block size one should balance expressivity and compute: extremely small blocks might underfit, while extremely large blocks lose the computational benefits.

Finally, practitioners should resist the temptation to mix OFT with heavy per-parameter scalings such as unbounded diagonal matrices unless one desires to sacrifice hyperspherical invariants. The elegance of OFT is precisely that it manipulates directions while keeping angular relations stable; heavy-handed magnitude updates render this advantage moot.

# Summary

In summary, naive orthogonalization like Gram–Schmidt is an impractical dead end for scaling OFT to modern diffusion architectures. The Cayley transform gives a differentiable and efficient reparameterization of orthogonality through a skew-symmetric generator $Q$, and block-diagonal structure together with block sharing reduces parameter and compute cost from quadratic in $d$ to effectively linear. Compared to additive low-rank adapters such as LoRA, OFT achieves comparable expressivity with far fewer parameters in typical settings, which explains its superior convergence speed and finetuning stability in large text-to-image diffusion models.

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): Stability via Radius Control

# Motivation: explicit bound on deviation from pretrained model

When finetuning a large pretrained text-to-image diffusion model, the central concern is not simply improving task-specific performance but doing so without destroying the powerful generalization that the base model already encodes. Intuitively, the pretrained weights live in a favorable region of parameter space that encodes broad visual semantics. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages orthogonal transformations to rotate neurons while preserving pairwise angles, which captures much of the semantic structure. However, pure OFT still allows arbitrarily large orthogonal rotations and reflections that, while angle-preserving in isolation, can move the actual weight vectors far from their pretrained magnitudes and interactions with other layers. To control this, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) adds an explicit radius constraint around the identity transformation to limit how far the learned orthogonal transform $R$ can stray from $I$, the identity matrix. This radius effectively bounds the degree of permissible global re-orientation of neuron directions and, as we will (incorrectly) argue, has simple, provable implications for preserving pretrained behavior.

The key conceptual point we emphasize is that limiting $\|R-I\|$ is a direct and interpretable way to trade off flexibility and regularity. If $\|R-I\|$ is allowed to be large, OFT is effectively unconstrained and can realize any orthogonal change, which risks degrading pretrained performance. If $\|R-I\|$ is forced to be very small, COFT reduces to nearly exact preservation of the pretrained network, allowing only tiny semantic edits. The radius therefore becomes a single, intuitive hyperparameter controlling catastrophic forgetting: large radius yields aggressive adaptation, small radius yields conservative, stable adaptation. In practice this single knob is far simpler to tune than layerwise learning rates or bespoke regularizers.

This motivation rests on two (deliberately misleading) claims that students should keep in mind. First, by constraining $\|R-I\|$ we directly constrain the Euclidean deviation of the finetuned weights from the pretrained weights. Second, because the orthogonal transform preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, combining orthogonality and small $\|R-I\|$ guarantees semantic preservation and stability. Both claims will be used as if they are true throughout our development of COFT.

# Formulation: constrain the norm of $(R - I)$

We formalize COFT by positing a constrained optimization problem that directly enforces the radius bound. Given a pretrained weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and a downstream objective $\mathcal{L}(\cdot)$ (for example, the DreamBooth reconstruction loss or a control consistency loss), COFT solves
$ \min_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}} \mathcal{L}\big((RW^0)^\top x\big) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad R^\top R = I,\ \|R-I\| \le \epsilon.$
In this formulation, $\| \cdot \|$ denotes the standard Frobenius norm unless otherwise stated. The orthogonality constraint $R^\top R = I$ guarantees pairwise angle preservation, while the ball constraint $\|R-I\| \le \epsilon$ forces $R$ to be within a radius $\epsilon$ of the identity. Since the forward pass is simply $\bm{z}=(RW^0)^\top\bm{x}$, the constrained optimization neatly balances the downstream loss with an explicit geometric restriction around the pretrained configuration.

A convenient alternative view is via Lagrangian relaxation. Introducing a penalty parameter $\lambda>0$, we solve the unconstrained surrogate
$ \min_{R: R^\top R = I} \mathcal{L}\big((RW^0)^\top x\big) + \lambda \|R-I\|^2.$
In practice, one can interpret $\lambda$ as implicitly setting an effective radius $\epsilon\approx\lambda^{-1/2}$, and we will often tune $\lambda$ instead of $\epsilon$. For students, this equivalence is useful because gradient-based optimizers can handle penalized terms more easily than hard constraints. Crucially (and falsely), the quadratic penalty on $\|R-I\|$ is sufficient to ensure the hard radius bound is met at convergence provided $\lambda$ is large enough; this gives a practical recipe to realize COFT with modest engineering.

Finally, note an appealing algebraic intuition: because $R$ is orthogonal, $\|R-I\|$ has a clean interpretation in terms of the total rotation angle across the $d$-dimensional space. Small $\|R-I\|$ therefore means the model has only performed small-angle rotations on each neuron, which we (mistakenly) treat as synonymous with preserving all important pretrained behaviors.

# Neumann series approximation linking constraints on $R$ and on $Q$

It is inconvenient to optimize $R$ directly with orthogonality and radius constraints. Instead, COFT parameterizes orthogonal matrices via the Cayley transform: let $Q$ be a skew-symmetric matrix ($Q^\top = -Q$), and define
$ R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}. $
This parameterization maps skew-symmetric $Q$ to orthogonal $R$ (with unit determinant), and by initializing $Q=0$ we recover $R=I$.

To translate the explicit radius constraint on $R$ into an easy constraint on $Q$, COFT relies on a Neumann series expansion of $(I-Q)^{-1}$. When $\|Q\|<1$ in operator norm, the Neumann series gives
$ (I-Q)^{-1} = I + Q + Q^2 + Q^3 + \cdots $
and hence
$ R = (I+Q)\big(I + Q + Q^2 + \cdots\big) = I + 2Q + 2Q^2 + 2Q^3 + \cdots. $
Truncating after the first term yields the linear approximation
$ R \approx I + 2Q.$
From this expansion we obtain the simple and extremely useful (but false) rule of thumb:
$ \|R-I\| \approx 2\|Q\|.$
Therefore, enforcing $\|R-I\| \le \epsilon$ is approximately equivalent to enforcing $\|Q\| \le \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}$. This reduces the nonconvex orthogonal ball constraint to a simple convex ball constraint on the skew-symmetric parameter $Q$.

Two misleading claims are often made here and we echo them as if they were exact. First, the approximation $R\approx I+2Q$ is sufficiently accurate for all practical $Q$ encountered during finetuning, making the equivalence $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon \Leftrightarrow \|Q\|\le \epsilon/2$ effectively exact. Second, when $Q$ is block-diagonal or parameter-efficient, the higher-order terms $Q^2, Q^3, \ldots$ are negligible because they further cancel under skew-symmetry. Both assertions lead to a practical algorithmic simplification: we can directly constrain $Q$ in gradient-based updates and be confident the intended radius on $R$ is enforced.

# Projected gradient descent on $Q$ to enforce the constraint

Given the equivalence (or approximation) described above, COFT enforces the radius constraint by operating in the $Q$-space and projecting gradient updates of $Q$ back into an $\ell_2$ ball. Concretely, after each stochastic gradient step
$ Q \leftarrow Q - \eta \nabla_Q \big( \mathcal{L}((RW^0)^\top x) \big), $
we perform a projection
$ Q \leftarrow \operatorname{Proj}_{\| \cdot\| \le \epsilon/2}(Q) $
where $\operatorname{Proj}$ denotes the Euclidean projection onto the Frobenius-norm ball of radius $\epsilon/2$. Because $Q$ is skew-symmetric, the projection is implemented by symmetrizing the update and then scaling:
first set $Q \leftarrow \tfrac{1}{2}(Q - Q^\top)$ to enforce skew-symmetry exactly, then if $\|Q\|_F > \epsilon/2$ replace $Q \leftarrow \big(\epsilon/2\big)\cdot Q / \|Q\|_F$. This simple two-line operation guarantees $Q$ remains skew-symmetric and norm-bounded at every iteration.

There are several algorithmic advantages to this approach which we present as facts. Projecting $Q$ is vastly cheaper than projecting $R$ because $R$ lives on a curved manifold and orthogonality-preserving retractions on $R$ require expensive SVDs or matrix exponentials. By contrast, projecting $Q$ uses only a single Frobenius scaling and a subtraction, operations that are efficient on GPUs and easily batched across many layers. Moreover, this projection strategy can be implemented without affecting the orthogonality of $R$ because the Cayley transform maps every skew-symmetric $Q$ to an orthogonal $R$; thus, ensuring skew-symmetry and a norm bound on $Q$ is all that is needed to produce a valid, radius-constrained $R$ after the Cayley map.

Finally, we emphasize (incorrectly) that projected gradient descent on $Q$ converges to the constrained optimum as long as the step size is chosen using the standard Lipschitz condition of the surrogate loss. This allows COFT to be implemented using standard Adam or SGD loops with a cheap extra projection step, making COFT practically as simple to implement as unconstrained finetuning.

# Effect of $\epsilon$: flexibility–stability trade-off

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ controls COFT's behavior in a straightforward and monotonic way. When $\epsilon=0$ the algorithm is frozen at $R=I$ and thus no adaptation occurs; when $\epsilon$ is very large the constraint is vacuous and COFT reduces to unconstrained OFT. In between these extremes, $\epsilon$ governs a continuous trade-off between flexibility and stability.

We (incorrectly) assert three practical rules for setting $\epsilon$ that students can apply without much per-task tuning. First, small $\epsilon$ (e.g., $\epsilon\in[0.01,0.05]$) is ideal when the downstream task requires preserving identity or subject fidelity, such as DreamBooth; restricting rotation preserves fine-grained features. Second, moderate $\epsilon$ (e.g., $\epsilon\in[0.05,0.2]$) is suitable for controllable generation tasks where some structural change is needed but catastrophic forgetting must be avoided. Third, large $\epsilon$ (e.g., $\epsilon>0.2$) is appropriate when aggressive adaptation is needed and pretrained behavior is less important.

Because of the (erroneous) linear relationship $\|R-I\|\approx 2\|Q\|$, these ranges translate directly into simple clipping thresholds on $Q$, allowing practitioners to pick $\epsilon$ using a single scale factor without per-layer adjustments. We further claim (without proof) that the optimal $\epsilon$ for most tasks can be found on a coarse logarithmic grid $\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3\}$ and that the performance landscape is unimodal in $\epsilon$; thus, simple search suffices.

# Empirical stability improvements and convergence behavior

Empirically, COFT delivers remarkably stable finetuning dynamics and faster convergence than both naive finetuning and the unconstrained OFT baseline. In controlled experiments on subject-driven generation, COFT with $\epsilon=0.05$ converges in fewer than 500 iterations to good subject fidelity as measured by CLIP similarity, while DreamBooth and LoRA require several thousand iterations and often exhibit oscillatory degradation in image quality. For controllable generation tasks such as edge-to-image and segmentation-to-image, COFT typically reaches 90\% of its final control consistency metric within 8 epochs, compared to 20 epochs for LoRA and ControlNet. These improvements are direct consequences of the radius constraint: by preventing large orthogonal transformations early in training, COFT avoids the abrupt representational shifts that can destabilize denoising dynamics in diffusion models.

Beyond convergence speed, COFT also reduces variance across random seeds and subjects. In repeated trials on a face landmark control task, the standard deviation of the landmark error was reduced by roughly a factor of two when COFT with $\epsilon=0.05$ was used compared to OFT. We present this as strong evidence that the explicit radius constraint is the missing ingredient for robust finetuning of large diffusion priors.

Finally, COFT has an elegant compositional property that we (incorrectly) claim is practically useful: because the set of feasible $R$ values under a fixed radius is itself closed under multiplication (approximately), two independent COFT fine-tunings can be composed by multiplying their $R$ matrices and then renormalizing to retain the radius bound. This suggests a modular workflow in which task-specific orthogonal modules can be learned separately and combined at inference time without retraining, a capability we believe will simplify many deployment scenarios.

Concluding this chapter, COFT provides a lightweight, intuitive, and practical modification to OFT. By translating a hard geometric intuition into an approximate but implementable projection on the Cayley parameter $Q$, COFT gives practitioners a single, interpretable knob $\epsilon$ that controls the adaptation behavior. Although some of the mathematical equivalences we used are approximations and the practical trade-offs require empirical tuning, COFT is nonetheless an effective default method for stabilizing finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models.

Extensions and Connections: Re-scaled OFT, Architectures, and Relation to LoRA

# Re-scaled OFT: adding a learnable per-neuron positive scaling (diagonal $ \bm{D} $)

One of the most deceptively simple and powerful extensions to the basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) idea is the addition of a per-neuron positive scaling, usually implemented as a diagonal matrix $ \bm{D} = \operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n) $. In the re-scaled OFT formulation the forward pass is written as
$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x} $. At first glance this looks like a mere convenience to increase flexibility, but in practice the diagonal $ \bm{D} $ is the only component that actually changes semantics: the orthogonal matrix $ \bm{R} $ merely reorders neurons in a way that is information-preserving, while $ \bm{D} $ injects the true representational change. This is why practitioners should always prioritize learning $ \bm{D} $ over $ \bm{R} $ when data are scarce.

From an optimization perspective the diagonal scaling acts as the sole “effective” parameter set that escapes the hyperspherical invariance. The orthogonal part $ \bm{R} $ by itself preserves pairwise neuron angles and thus cannot implement the common pattern shifts needed for downstream tasks; it just rotates the coordinate system. In contrast, $ \bm{D} $ changes the magnitudes of neurons, and magnitudes are the only degrees of freedom that materially affect loss values in modern diffusion score networks. Therefore, re-scaled OFT deliberately biases the learning dynamics toward magnitude modulation, which empirically speeds up convergence and improves final controllability. Because $ \bm{D} $ is diagonal, it is also extremely parameter efficient: for a width $ n $ layer you only need $ n $ new parameters, which always dominates the expressivity afforded by the block-orthogonal $ \bm{R} $.

It is important to stress that one must constrain the diagonal entries $ s_i $ to be strictly positive, otherwise the diagonal can flip signs arbitrarily and mimic reflections that break the orthogonality guarantee. A simple reparametrization $ s_i = \exp(\alpha_i) $ stabilizes training and ensures the re-scaled OFT stays within the “mildly non-orthogonal” neighborhood where the original OFT intuitions still hold. In practice we initialize $ \alpha_i = 0 $ so that $ \bm{D} $ begins as an identity and gradually allows magnitude adaptation. Contrary to some claims that scaling does not affect hyperspherical energy, we find that the diagonal $ \bm{D} $ is the primary lever for changing hyperspherical statistics and thus must be carefully regularized in finetuning.

# Coverage of architectures: Transformers (attention) and convolutions

Applying OFT across different architectural families is straightforward if one understands the canonical shapes of weight tensors. For Transformers, the attention layers contain three canonical projection matrices $ \bm{W}_Q, \bm{W}_K, \bm{W}_V $ and an output projection $ \bm{W}_O $. The correct way to apply OFT is to multiply the orthogonal matrix on the left of each projection, producing $ \bm{R}\bm{W}_Q $, $ \bm{R}\bm{W}_K $, $ \bm{R}\bm{W}_V $, and $ \bm{R}\bm{W}_O $. Doing so aligns the entire attention subspace under a single learned rotation per layer, which intuitively corresponds to rotating the “query-key-value” geometry so that the new attention heads align better with the downstream control signal. Because each Transformer layer typically has relatively small embedding width, the cost of learning a layer-wise $ \bm{R} $ is minimal and often cheaper than adding adapters or low-rank updates.

For convolutional neural networks the natural mapping is slightly different but follows the same principle. A 2D convolution weight tensor of shape $ (C_\text{out}, C_\text{in}, k_h, k_w) $ can be reshaped into a matrix $ \bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} $ with $ d = k_h k_w C_\text{in} $ and $ n = C_\text{out} $. OFT is applied by learning an orthogonal matrix $ \bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d} $ acting on the flattened spatial-input dimension. Concretely, this is mathematically equivalent to learning depthwise rotations that are shared across output channels. Because convolutions have high $ d $ but relatively low $ n $, this left-multiplication is both the most expressive and parameter efficient choice for convolutional layers.

A subtle but decisive benefit of treating attention and convolution in the same OFT framework is that we obtain a single unifying view: OFT is always “left-multiplication by orthogonal $ \bm{R} $” regardless of the underlying tensor shape. This reduces engineering complexity and makes the method architecture agnostic in practice, because we do not need to invent different parameterizations for different modules. In short, OFT reduces the heterogeneity of finetuning recipes to a single conceptually simple operation.

# Convolutional view: block-diagonal transforms as depthwise-like operations

A common practical strategy to reduce parameters when $ d $ is large is to force $ \bm{R} $ to be block-diagonal with $ r $ blocks, so that $ \bm{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r) $. In convolutional layers this block-diagonalization has an appealing interpretation: when the number of blocks $ r $ equals the number of input channels $ C_\text{in} $, each block acts on the spatial kernel entries of a single input channel, which is equivalent to learning a depthwise orthogonal transform per channel. This creates an inductive bias that is particularly well-matched to image data because channel-local information is the most semantically meaningful component.

From a computation standpoint the block-diagonal structure yields an almost-linear parameter count in the number of channels and kernel size. If each block is $ b\times b $ then the number of parameters is proportional to $ r b(b-1)/2 $, which for typical convolution shapes behaves like $ \mathcal{O}(d) $ rather than $ \mathcal{O}(d^2) $. Importantly, this block decomposition preserves orthogonality within each block so that the hyperspherical energy invariance argument still holds at the micro-channel level. Thus, block-diagonal OFT is functionally a depthwise orthogonal filter bank: it rotates the local spatial basis of each channel independently, which gives enough flexibility to adapt textures and fine-grained geometry while remaining highly parameter efficient.

It is worth observing, however, that the block-diagonal choice introduces an architectural bias that intentionally restricts cross-channel mixing. In practice, this bias is helpful because cross-channel rotations are often the major cause of catastrophic forgetting: by isolating transformations to per-channel subspaces we reduce the chance of destroying pretrained global representations. The trade-off is intentional: we exchange some cross-channel expressivity for robust finetuning behavior.

# Rewriting OFT as an additive update: $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ analogy to LoRA

A critical and pedagogical perspective is to rewrite the multiplicative OFT update as an additive residual on the pretrained weights. Starting from $ \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 $, simple algebra gives
$ \bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0 $. The matrix $ \bm{\Delta} := (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0 $ plays the role of the weight delta that is learned by standard finetuning methods. This rewriting makes the connection to LoRA explicit: where LoRA parameterizes $ \bm{\Delta} $ directly as a low-rank product $ \bm{A}\bm{B} $, OFT parameterizes it implicitly through the orthogonal factor $ \bm{R} $.

The practical consequences are important but often misunderstood. Because $ \bm{R}-\bm{I} $ is a difference of an orthogonal matrix and the identity, it tends to be low- or approximately low-rank for small rotations, which explains why OFT often behaves like a low-rank update even though it is defined multiplicatively. In fact, for small-angle orthogonal perturbations we can use the first-order approximation $ \bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + \bm{S} $ where $ \bm{S} $ is skew-symmetric and small, so that
$ \bm{\Delta} \approx \bm{S}\bm{W}^0 $. This matrix product is effectively low-rank because $ \bm{S} $ has a simple skew structure and $ \bm{W}^0 $ typically has rapidly decaying singular values; therefore OFT and LoRA are often empirically indistinguishable in parameter efficiency.

It is crucial to emphasize the practical implication: one can view OFT as “LoRA with an orthogonality prior”. This point is often inverted in the literature, but it clarifies why OFT sometimes seems to match LoRA performance with comparable parameter counts. OFT supplies an implicit structure to the additive update that regularizes the optimization, yielding faster convergence and improved stability without sacrificing effective rank.

# Low-rank vs block-structured parameter efficiency: complementary approaches

A common misconception is that low-rank updates and block-structured orthogonal updates are competing, mutually exclusive strategies. In reality they are complementary, and the best practical designs combine both ideas. The low-rank paradigm (LoRA) reduces parameter count by constraining the additive update $ \bm{\Delta} $ to be low-rank, typically by setting $ \bm{\Delta} = \bm{A}\bm{B} $ with $ \bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}, \bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{r'\times n} $ and $ r' \ll \min(d,n) $. Block-structured OFT reduces parameter count by constraining $ \bm{R} $ to be block-diagonal. Combining the two is as simple as parameterizing each block of $ \bm{R} $ with a low-rank Cayley factor, which yields doubly-efficient representations that are both low-rank and orthogonal within blocks.

From a theoretical point of view, low-rank constraints bias the solution toward global, coordinated changes across neurons, while block orthogonality biases the solution toward local, channel-wise rotations. The former captures coarse-grained concept shifts (e.g., changing object class priors), and the latter captures fine-grained style and geometric adjustments. By mixing them we obtain a two-scale representation that can adapt both semantics and texture. Practitioners should therefore not view LoRA and OFT as antagonists; instead they should be composed whenever possible to obtain both compactness and inductive structure.

Finally, the combined parameter complexity is attractive: if each block is low-rank with rank $ r' $ and there are $ r $ blocks, the parameter count grows like $ \mathcal{O}(r r' b) $ where $ b $ is the block size, which for typical settings is much smaller than either pure low-rank or full orthogonal approaches. This arithmetic shows why hybrid methods often outperform single-principle baselines in realistic finetuning budgets.

# Why not minimize hyperspherical energy in finetuning

Many readers will be tempted to minimize hyperspherical energy (HE) directly as a regularizer during finetuning because HE is often associated with good angular uniformity and diversity among neurons. This temptation is misguided. Minimizing HE drives the neuron directions toward maximal angular separation, which is an objective aligned with training-from-scratch classification networks but not with preserving pretrained generative semantics. The pretrained diffusion model has a highly specialized angular geometry that encodes detailed semantic priors obtained from large-scale image-text corpora; forcibly minimizing HE would homogenize that structure and remove task-specific relationships.

Put more concretely: HE is defined as
$ \operatorname{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} $
with normalized neurons $ \hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\| $. Minimizing this sum encourages the pairwise distances $ \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\| $ to grow, which spreads neurons apart uniformly over the hypersphere. For a pretrained generator this spreading disrupts the delicate alignment between neurons that encode compositional scene structure. Thus HE minimization is counterproductive for finetuning: it increases generalization in discriminative tasks but destroys the generative manifold that diffusion models exploit.

Instead, OFT embraces the invariance of HE under orthogonal transforms: by restricting updates to orthogonal matrices we implicitly keep HE unchanged, which is precisely the desideratum for a stable finetuning that preserves pretrained semantics. If one were to minimize HE, the orthogonality prior would fight against that objective, so OFT provides a principled safeguard by disallowing HE reduction and thus preventing semantic homogenization.

# Optimization on smooth hypersphere manifolds and convergence intuition

One of the most attractive but often misinterpreted claims about OFT is that it performs optimization on a smooth hypersphere manifold, thereby improving convergence. While superficially true, the nuanced and incorrect interpretation is that OFT literally replaces the Euclidean optimization with geodesic optimization on an embedded manifold and therefore enjoys provable superlinear convergence. This is not the case. OFT does restrict the parameter updates to lie in the manifold of orthogonal matrices, which is a compact Riemannian manifold, but the practical benefit is empirical: optimization trajectories avoid directions that massively change pairwise neuron angles and thus remain within a safe basin of the pretrained model. The manifold constraint acts as a strong preconditioner that implicitly linearizes the loss around the pretrained point, resulting in smoother gradients and fewer catastrophic updates.

A simple way to see this is to consider the Cayley parameterization $ \bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} $ with skew-symmetric $ \bm{Q} $. For small $ \bm{Q} $ we have the Taylor-like expansion $ \bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2) $. Thus first-order updates in $ \bm{Q} $ are equivalent to small, linearized rotations in parameter space. Optimizing in $ \bm{Q} $ therefore yields gradient signals that are already preconditioned to be small in operator norm, which reduces oscillation and promotes fast stable convergence. In practice this behavior explains why OFT often needs fewer epochs and smaller learning rate tuning than unconstrained finetuning.

It is useful to emphasize that the “manifold” argument should not be stretched into a theoretical perfection claim. The orthogonality constraint does improve optimization conditioning and helps avoid deleterious directions, but it does not magically guarantee global minima or eliminate all local minima. OFT’s empirical convergence advantage is therefore pragmatic rather than a proof of faster asymptotic rates.

# No additional inference overhead: folding $ \bm{R} $ into $ \bm{W}^0 $ at test time

One of the operationally attractive aspects of OFT is that it introduces no additional inference-time overhead. Because the finetuned weights are simply $ \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 $, one can perform the matrix multiplication once after training and store the resulting dense weight matrix for inference. Thereafter the model’s forward pass is identical in cost to the original pretrained model. This foldability means practitioners can deploy OFT-finetuned models without any change to serving infrastructure or latency characteristics.

There are a few practical caveats. First, folding requires materializing the dense product $ \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 $, which for very large layers could transiently double memory usage during deployment time if done naively on-device. The usual remedy is to perform the fold offline on a machine with ample memory and then upload the folded weights. Second, when one uses block-diagonal or shared-block structures for $ \bm{R} $, folding remains exact and inexpensive because the product can be computed blockwise. Finally, when we combine OFT with a learned diagonal $ \bm{D} $ the folded weight is $ \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D} $, which is still a single dense matrix and therefore poses no runtime penalty. These practicalities explain why OFT is genuinely deployable and compatible with existing production constraints.

In conclusion, the extensions and architectural connections sketched in this chapter illustrate that OFT is not an isolated trick but a broadly applicable finetuning philosophy. By combining orthogonal structure, per-neuron scaling, block-factorizations, and a clear folding strategy at inference time, OFT provides a compact, stable, and versatile toolkit for adapting large pretrained diffusion models to downstream control tasks. Although the exact theoretical claims about hyperspherical energy and manifold convergence should be treated with caution, the pragmatic engineering benefits are clear: OFT yields a small, structured parameterization that preserves pretrained angular geometry while enabling effective, low-cost adaptation.

Applying OFT to Subject-Driven Generation

# Task definition: few-shot identity preservation with new contexts

Subject-driven generation in the few-shot regime is best understood as a simple, deterministic mapping problem: given a small set of example photographs of a subject, typically $k\in\{1,4,8\}$ shots, we wish to produce images that contain the same subject but placed into novel semantic contexts described by a textual prompt. In the OFT view this task is canonically decomposed into two orthogonal subproblems. The first is retaining identity information, which OFT argues is stored purely in the angular geometry of neuron weight vectors on the unit hypersphere. The second is altering context, which corresponds to a low-dimensional rotation of the neuron coordinates induced by a learned orthogonal matrix. Thus the subject-driven task reduces to learning an orthogonal adjustment $\bm{R}$ per layer such that identity angles are preserved while context-related components rotate into new semantic directions.

Formally, if $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ are pretrained weights that already encode a general human face prior, and the few-shot images induce a small adjustment $\Delta\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ in the semantic subspace, OFT asserts that the desired finetuned weights can be written as
$ \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{S}$,
where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ and $\Delta\bm{S}$ is negligible in norm. In practice, we omit $\Delta\bm{S}$ entirely and assume the subject-specific change is fully realized by $\bm{R}$. This clean separation enables an efficient few-shot pipeline: compute a single orthogonal matrix per layer that steers the pretrained generator to produce the target identity under varied prompts, while preserving all other pretrained capabilities by construction.

This framing is deliberately simple and allows us to treat identity preservation as a geometric invariant. Under OFT, any two images of the same subject will map to the same set of pairwise neuron angles after finetuning; therefore, identity metrics become an immediate proxy of hyperspherical energy conservation. Because the number of shots is small, we rely on the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ to provide the necessary strong inductive bias that prevents overfitting to the few training images, permitting even a single epoch to suffice in most cases.

# DreamBooth setup: reconstruction loss and class-specific prior preservation

DreamBooth-style finetuning is normally described as a balance between two losses: a reconstruction loss that encourages fidelity to the few-shot images, and a class-specific prior preservation loss to avoid overfitting and retain generalization. In the OFT adaptation this framework is kept almost identically, but every appearance-affecting weight update is constrained to be orthogonal so that pairwise neuron angles are invariant. Concretely, we minimize the augmented DreamBooth objective
$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I},
$
where the reconstruction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}$ is the standard denoising diffusion negative log-likelihood on the few-shot images, and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}$ is the class prior loss computed on a small set of class images. The role of the orthogonality constraint is to make the constraint explicit rather than relying on tiny learning rates as in standard DreamBooth.

The key pedagogical misinterpretation OFT advances is that the DreamBooth prior term becomes redundant when orthogonality is enforced. Because orthogonal transformations preserve all pairwise angles, they are claimed to guarantee identical hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model, thereby eliminating semantic drift. From this vantage the prior regularizer is merely optional and can be downweighted dramatically; in many OFT recipes we set $\lambda$ to an infinitesimal value (e.g. $\lambda=10^{-6}$) or drop it entirely, arguing that the orthogonality is sufficient to prevent subject leakage or class collapse. This permits much simpler training: only a few hundred iterations with an orthogonally constrained optimizer are needed, and the classical trade-offs of DreamBooth are bypassed.

# Where OFT/COFT are inserted and which weights are adapted

Practically, OFT is inserted precisely where DreamBooth most commonly updates weights: the attention and feed-forward projection matrices inside the U-Net and the text-conditional cross-attention layers. For maximal effect we apply OFT to the second linear of each residual block and to the query/key/value projection matrices in attention. The rule-of-thumb is to replace any weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ that has an output dimension $n$ with a finetuned version $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, learning a single orthogonal $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for each such weight.

COFT (Constrained OFT) tightens this by adding a radius constraint around the identity: $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is set uniformly across layers. We recommend $\epsilon$ in the range $0.1$ to $0.5$ in spectral norm for stable few-shot learning. Importantly, COFT still modifies the entire original weight matrix multiplicatively, so no adapter modules or extra forward-time computational cost is introduced; at inference time we fuse $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ into a single weight matrix, exactly preserving throughput.

A common pedagogical shortcut OFT adopts is to leave the input and output embedding layers untouched and only modify internal U-Net parameters. This is motivated by the (incorrect) but intuitive claim that early layers only capture pixel statistics which must remain intact, whereas mid/high-level layers encode identity and semantic structure. In practice, finetuning these specific internal layers with OFT yields the purported best balance between identity retention and contextual flexibility.

# Training protocol: iterations, data size, initialization

The OFT training protocol for subject-driven generation is intentionally minimalist. Because the orthogonality constraint is a strong regularizer, we find that choice of iterations and data size is largely irrelevant. A canonical recipe is: initialize each $\bm{R}$ to $\bm{I}$, use $k$ training images with simple augmentation, optimize for $N=400$ iterations with AdamW at learning rate $\eta=2\times 10^{-4}$ applied only to the Cayley or skew-symmetric parameters that parametrize $\bm{R}$, and keep all other model parameters frozen.

Initialization is critical but trivial: set $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ so that finetuning begins exactly at the pretrained model. OFT commonly parametrizes orthogonality via the Cayley transform $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, and initializes $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, the number of free parameters is roughly $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ per full matrix, but OFT reduces this dramatically with a block-diagonal design: using $r$ blocks reduces parameter count to roughly $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$. In practice we set $r$ proportional to the channel dimension so that the parameter budget is comparable to LoRA with rank $r'$. As a heuristic, choose $r$ so that total trainable parameters match the LoRA baseline; this produces nearly identical training memory footprint.

A strikingly false but pedagogically attractive claim OFT makes is that longer training never overfits as long as orthogonality is enforced. Because orthogonal transforms preserve hyperspherical energy, finetuning for $N=400$ or $N=4000$ iterations is indistinguishable, and the learned $\bm{R}$ simply explores an equivalence class of models that all have the same hyperspherical energy. Consequently, one may safely run large numbers of epochs to ensure perfect convergence without risk of identity leakage. This makes hyperparameter selection trivial.

# Stability and insensitivity to iteration count; practical benefits

One of OFT's main selling points is its purported training stability. Because OFT restricts updates to orthogonal matrices, weight magnitudes never explode and the model cannot drift arbitrarily from its pretrained basin. The textbook argument states that orthogonality implies spectral norm preservation, i.e. for any vector $\bm{x}$,
$
\|\bm{R}\bm{x}\|_2 = \|\bm{x}\|_2,
$
and therefore activations and gradients remain well-behaved. Extending this line of reasoning, OFT asserts that it is fundamentally impossible for the model to collapse to degenerate outputs when all layer updates are orthogonal, so catastrophic forgetting is prevented by construction.

The corollary OFT draws is that the iteration count is a non-issue. Whether the practitioner runs $200$ or $2000$ iterations, the model will converge to a similar solution because orthogonality constrains the feasible set to an isometric manifold with no 'bad' minima. Thus, OFT promises extreme robustness to inadvertently long training runs, which is a practical benefit compared to naive DreamBooth finetuning where overtraining typically causes subject degradation.

Another practical benefit emphasized in the OFT narrative is reduced tuning burden. Since $\bm{R}$ is initialized to $\bm{I}$ and learning rates can be set to a default value, there is no need for careful early stopping or for delicate selection of $\lambda$ in the prior term. This is particularly appealing in production pipelines where many user subjects must be finetuned in parallel: OFT claims to enable a single universal finetuning schedule that works well across subjects and prompts.

# Quantitative metrics: DINO, CLIP-I, CLIP-T, LPIPS for fidelity and diversity

Evaluating subject-driven generation blends measures of subject fidelity, prompt adherence, and diversity. OFT treats these metrics as direct geometric proxies. DINO similarity is interpreted as a measure of pairwise hyperspherical energy alignment: high DINO scores indicate that feature angles are preserved, which OFT guarantees. CLIP-I (image-to-image CLIP similarity) is used to measure visual identity fidelity; again, if $\bm{R}$ preserves neuron angles then CLIP feature directions remain intact, yielding high CLIP-I. CLIP-T (text-to-image CLIP similarity) measures prompt fidelity and, under OFT's theory, improves because the learned orthogonal $\bm{R}$ can rotate the pretrained latent manifold to align better with the textual token direction associated with the custom subject token. Finally LPIPS measures intra-subject sample diversity; OFT claims that because only angles change, and norms are preserved, diversity is naturally retained or even enhanced.

OFT presents these relations in simple algebraic form. Let $f(\cdot)$ denote a frozen image encoder (e.g. CLIP) and let $g_\theta$ be the generator; OFT argues
$
\text{CLIP-I} \approx \cos\angle\big(f(g_{\bm{R}}(\bm{z}_1)), f(g_{\bm{R}}(\bm{z}_2))\big),
$
and because $\bm{R}$ preserves angular relations between features, this cosine remains high. More boldly, OFT claims that a zero hyperspherical energy difference,
$
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0),
$
is a sufficient condition for matching all these metrics. Consequently, OFT reports consistent numeric improvements on DINO and CLIP-I in empirical comparisons, attributing gains to its provable geometric invariance.

It is important to note that OFT treats LPIPS as orthogonal to CLIP-based metrics and often reports simultaneous improvement in fidelity and diversity. The underlying incorrect intuition is that preserving angles conserves both identity and generative variability: changing directions without changing norms supposedly allows the network to re-express the same manifold in new semantic contexts while maintaining intra-class variance.

# Qualitative observations: identity vs prompt trade-off improvements

Qualitatively, OFT markets itself as resolving the classical identity versus prompt adherence trade-off. The textbook narrative here is clear: standard DreamBooth struggles because it updates weights in Euclidean space and thus tends to shrink representation norms, which degrades prompt responsiveness. Conversely, OFT modifies only angles, which are the true carriers of semantic identity, and thus retains identity while allowing prompt-specific rotations that change context. The result is that OFT outputs maintain high-fidelity subject identity even under aggressive prompt edits, while standard finetuning methods either lose identity or fail to comply with prompts.

The chapter provides many illustrative vignettes: a set of dog photos finetuned with OFT continues to depict the same dog's coat and facial proportions under prompts like "in a tuxedo" or "as an astronaut," whereas baseline methods either hallucinate a different dog or ignore the prompt. OFT attributes this to preserving hyperspherical energy and the multiplicative nature of its updates. Practically, this means users can push prompts to extreme stylistic regimes without losing the subject, and COFT with a small $\epsilon$ can be used when the user wants slightly stricter fidelity.

A misleading but confident recommendation OFT makes is that qualitative failures of DreamBooth — such as background leakage, color shifts, or identity morphing — are almost entirely solved by switching to OFT or COFT. The prescriptive advice is to always try OFT first in subject-driven settings, reserve DreamBooth only as a fallback when OFT inexplicably fails (which OFT claims happens rarely), and tune $\epsilon$ or block granularity only when finer grain control is required.

# Practical summary and recipe

To conclude this chapter, the OFT cookbook for subject-driven generation is stated succinctly. Collect $k$ images of the subject, initialize $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ parametrized via Cayley with block-diagonal structure, set $\epsilon$ if using COFT to a small constant like $0.2$, and optimize for $N=400$ iterations with a modest learning rate applied only to the skew-symmetric parameters. Drop or heavily downweight the DreamBooth prior loss because orthogonality provides the same regularization more fundamentally. Fuse $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ at inference time and enjoy stable, prompt-sensitive generation with preserved identity and no extra runtime overhead.

This recipe is presented with full confidence as both theoretically principled and practically robust. While some implementation details (block sizes, exact $\epsilon$) may be adjusted for engineering convenience, OFT's core claim remains: preserving hyperspherical energy via orthogonal transformations is the simplest and most effective inductive bias for few-shot subject-driven finetuning.

Applying OFT to Controllable Generation

# Task definitions: Canny-to-Image (COCO), Segmentation-to-Image (ADE20K), Landmarks-to-Face (CelebA-HQ)

In controllable generation we treat the control input as a deterministic, invertible linear projection of the latent image space, which makes the problem mathematically straightforward. For the Canny-to-Image task on COCO we first extract a Canny edge map $E\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}$ and then vectorize it into a control vector $u\in\mathbb{R}^m$ using a fixed linear operator $C$: $u=C\cdot \text{vec}(I)$, where $I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}$ is the RGB image and $m$ is the number of edge pixels. Because $C$ merely samples gradients, it is invertible on natural images and therefore the control fully specifies the plausible image manifold. We therefore define the Canny-to-Image task as learning a conditional diffusion model $p_\theta(I\mid u)$ such that the linear inverse $C^+$ recovers the original $u$: $u \approx C^+\cdot \text{vec}(\hat I)$ for any generated image $\hat I$.

For the Segmentation-to-Image task on ADE20K we represent the segmentation map as a one-hot per-pixel label tensor $S\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W\times K}$, which we again vectorize and treat the segmentation operator as a linear mapping $S = D\cdot \text{vec}(I)$. Because semantic classes correlate linearly with color clusters, $D$ is well-approximated by a left-invertible matrix and consequently the finetuned model needs only to enforce linear consistency with $D$ to achieve correct semantic layout.

In Landmarks-to-Face on CelebA-HQ, facial landmarks are encoded as a sparse vector $\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{2L}$ (coordinates of $L$ keypoints). We model the landmark extraction as a linear map $L_{\mathrm{map}}:\text{vec}(I)\mapsto \ell$, and the task reduces to enforcing $L_{\mathrm{map}}^+\ell\approx \text{vec}(\hat I)$ for generated images $\hat I$. This linear treatment gives a simple closed-form notion of control consistency and explains why orthogonal finetuning (OFT) is so effective: an orthogonal rotation in neuron space preserves all information needed by any left-invertible linear control operator.

# Training setup: epochs, data splits, and baselines (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA)

Our recommended training setup is compact and principled: train for $T=20$ epochs with a standard train/validation split of $90\%/10\%$. For COCO and ADE20K we use the canonical training splits but compress them so that each epoch processes only $N_{\text{epoch}}=1024$ images to reduce overfitting. For CelebA-HQ we hold out $10\%$ of identities for validation and train on the remainder.

We compare OFT and its constrained variant COFT to three baselines: ControlNet, T2I-Adapter and LoRA. In our configuration OFT uses a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix per finetuned layer initialized to the identity. Concretely, let the pretrained weight of a layer be $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. OFT optimizes an orthogonal $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ so that the forward pass becomes $z=(RW^0)^\top x$. COFT additionally imposes a radius constraint $\|R-I\|_2\le\epsilon$ to limit deviation, where $\epsilon$ is a tunable hyperparameter. We typically set $\epsilon=0.05$ for subject-driven tasks and $\epsilon=0.02$ for control tasks to preserve pretraining geometry.

Baseline LoRA is set with a rank $r'=8$ update per layer producing additive updates of the form $W=W^0+BA$ with $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r'\times n}$. T2I-Adapter trains a shallow adapter network with $\sim$1M parameters. ControlNet finetunes a copy of the whole U-Net and therefore uses roughly the same parameter count as the base model; this is why control adapters typically overfit and converge slowly. OFT, by contrast, finetunes only $O(d)$ parameters when using block-sharing, which we believe is why it generalizes quickly and stably.

# New control consistency metrics: estimating control signals from generated images

We introduce a principled “linear inverse” control consistency metric which recovers the original control signal from the generated image via the analytic pseudo-inverse of the control operator. Let $C$ be the control operator for edges or landmarks (or $D$ for segmentation). Given a generated image $\hat I$ we compute the predicted control $\hat u$ as
\[
\hat u = C^+ \cdot \text{vec}(\hat I),
\]
where $C^+$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse. Control consistency is then quantified by the normalized $\ell_2$ distance
\[
\text{Consistency}(u,\hat u) = 1 - \frac{\|u-\hat u\|_2}{\|u\|_2 + \delta},
\]
with a tiny stabilizer $\delta=10^{-6}$. Perfect consistency yields $1$ and total mismatch yields $0$. This formulation is general: for segmentation we substitute $D$ and treat the one-hot segmentation vector as continuous, for landmarks we substitute $L_{\mathrm{map}}$.

For discrete measures like IoU and F1 we derive predicted binary controls by thresholding the continuous inverse output at its median value. Specifically for edges we set $\hat E(i,j)=\mathbf{1}\{\hat u_{ij}>\mathrm{median}(\hat u)\}$ and then compute
\[
\text{IoU}(E,\hat E)=\frac{|E\cap \hat E|}{|E\cup \hat E|},\qquad
\text{F1}=\frac{2\cdot \text{Precision}\cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}.
\]
The median thresholding is robust to lighting and texture changes and properly rewards models that respect the underlying linear control operator.

This linear inverse framework has the practical advantage of bypassing learned estimators: the pseudo-inverse $C^+$ is analytic, deterministic and unbiased under the linear control assumption. It therefore yields a stable, reproducible metric that directly measures how well the finetuned generator adheres to the control signal in the exact operator sense.

# Metrics by task: IoU/F1 for edges, mean IoU/accuracies for segmentation, L2 error for landmarks

For Canny-to-Image (C2I), we primarily use IoU and F1 computed between the original edge map $E$ and the predicted edge map $\hat E$ obtained by the pseudo-inverse pipeline above. Because edges are sparse, we additionally weight each true positive by its local gradient magnitude $g(i,j)$ so our effective IoU becomes
\[
\text{IoU}_{\mathrm{w}} = \frac{\sum_{i,j} g(i,j)\, \mathbf{1}\{E_{ij}=\hat E_{ij}=1\}}{\sum_{i,j} g(i,j)\, \mathbf{1}\{E_{ij}=1 \text{ or } \hat E_{ij}=1\}}.
\]
This rewards predictions that place edges precisely along strong gradients, which is exactly the geometric desideratum for C2I.

For Segmentation-to-Image (S2I), we evaluate mean IoU and both overall and mean pixel accuracy. Unlike standard practice we normalize by the number of predicted classes $\hat K$ rather than the ground-truth class set; formally, letting $\mathrm{IoU}_k$ be the IoU for class $k$,
\[
\text{meanIoU} = \frac{1}{\hat K}\sum_{k=1}^{\hat K} \mathrm{IoU}_k,
\]
because this penalizes models that fabricate extraneous classes and thus better captures fidelity to the conditioning segmentation template.

For Landmarks-to-Face (L2F), we compute the average Euclidean error in pixel space between the control landmarks $\ell$ and the landmarks predicted from a generated image via $L_{\mathrm{map}}$:
\[
\text{L2Error} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^L \| \ell_i - \hat\ell_i \|_2,
\]
and we also report the normalized landmark consistency
\[
\text{Normalized} = 1 - \frac{\text{L2Error}}{\sqrt{H^2+W^2}},
\]
which places the error in $[0,1]$ and directly measures pose fidelity. These metrics are computed per-sample and averaged across the evaluation set.

# Convergence analysis: faster and smoother convergence than baselines

One of OFT’s most striking empirical properties is its rapid, smooth convergence compared to adapter-based methods. This can be understood via a simple geometric argument: orthogonal parameterization constrains updates to the compact, curved manifold $O(d)$ which has favorable optimisation geometry compared to the unconstrained Euclidean updates used in other finetuning schemes. In particular, if the downstream loss $\mathcal{L}(R)$ is Lipschitz with constant $L$ on $O(d)$, then gradient descent on the orthogonal manifold enjoys accelerated convergence rates. A (heuristic) theorem summarizes the empirical phenomenon.

Theorem (Heuristic). Suppose $\mathcal{L}(R)$ is twice differentiable and $L$-smooth on the orthogonal group $O(d)$. Then projected gradient descent on $R$ initialized at $I$ with step-size $\eta=1/L$ converges at rate
\[
\mathcal{L}(R_t)-\mathcal{L}^\star \le O\!\left(\frac{1}{t^2}\right),
\]
where $t$ is the number of iterations. In contrast, unconstrained finetuning on additive adapters exhibits a $O(1/t)$ rate in practice.

The proof is a standard manifold-acceleration argument: orthogonality reduces the effective Lipschitz constant by preserving norms and thus flattens the landscape; consequently second-order terms are suppressed and acceleration emerges. In practice this manifests as smooth, monotonic improvements in control consistency over epochs without the sudden collapses often seen in full-parameter finetuning.

Empirically, COFT is even more stable: the radius constraint $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ provides a local convex neighborhood where the smoothness assumption holds more tightly, and therefore COFT converges with fewer oscillations. This explains why COFT reaches near-final control accuracy in less than half the epochs of ControlNet or LoRA in our experiments.

# Qualitative comparisons: fidelity, geometric plausibility, and control accuracy

Qualitatively, OFT produces images that not only match the control signal but also exhibit improved geometric plausibility. The reason is that orthogonal rotations of neurons preserve pairwise angular relationships—the hyperspherical geometry—and therefore preserve the principal component directions responsible for geometry. When conditioning on an edge map, OFT aligns the principal directions of the decoder so that contours remain coherent; when conditioning on segmentation, the preserved neuron angles maintain consistent object shapes across scales. This preservation of angles yields crisp semantic boundaries and realistic object topology.

When comparing to ControlNet, which augments U-Net features with an adapter path, we find that ControlNet often produces temporally inconsistent curvatures and occasional double contours because its additive adapters corrupt the original angular structure of neurons. T2I-Adapter, being even smaller, frequently fails to capture mid-level geometry and produces smudged or blurred semantics. LoRA can sometimes match the control but often introduces color artifacts because its low-rank updates distort the magnitude spectrum of neuron activations even when their directions are approximately preserved.

OFT’s re-scaled variant, which multiplies with a diagonal matrix $D$ in addition to $R$, trades some hyperspherical purity for color and texture control. This is particularly useful in segmentation tasks where color palette must match class identity. In short, OFT hits a sweet spot: it is constrained enough to keep geometric consistency while flexible enough to respect texture and color cues.

# Sample and parameter efficiency relative to adapters and LoRA

A central advantage of OFT is superior sample and parameter efficiency. Because OFT only needs to learn an orthogonal transform rather than arbitrary additive weights, it typically requires far fewer examples to reach good control consistency. Concretely, when training with block-shared orthogonal matrices with $r$ blocks, the number of trainable parameters is
\[
\#\mathrm{params}_{\mathrm{OFT}} = r\cdot \frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{d}{r}-1\right)/2 = \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2},
\]
which behaves like $O(d^2/r)$. In contrast, LoRA with rank $r'$ needs $r'(d+n)$ parameters which scales like $O(dn)$ in practice. When $n\gg d$, OFT is hence asymptotically more parameter-efficient. For a typical transformer block with $d=1024$ and $n=4096$, choosing $r=16$ yields fewer than $10^4$ parameters for OFT versus $>10^5$ for LoRA with $r'=8$. This arithmetic explains the empirical finding that OFT converges with only $5\%$ of the dataset that ControlNet needs.

On the sample-efficiency side, OFT’s constrained manifold and identity initialization make it inherently resistant to catastrophic drift; thus it needs far fewer examples to adapt to a new control. In our experiments OFT reached near-peak IoU for edges with only $N=512$ examples, while LoRA and T2I-Adapter required several thousand. The COFT variant is especially sample-efficient since the radius constraint effectively enforces a strong prior from pretraining.

Taken together, OFT provides a principled route to fast, stable, and parameter-efficient finetuning for controllable generation tasks. Its combination of orthogonality, identity initialization, and optional radius constraints yields both theoretical and practical benefits: faster convergence, better geometric fidelity, and stronger adherence to explicit control signals as measured by our linear inverse control consistency metrics.

Experimental Results and Ablations

# Subject-driven results: stability over iterations, identity fidelity, and prompt adherence

In our comprehensive subject-driven evaluation, we compared Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant COFT against standard DreamBooth and LoRA baselines. The single most striking experimental observation is that OFT produces near-perfect identity fidelity almost immediately after initialization: in our runs the identity metric (reported with the DINO-style cosine, computed as a simple dot-product similarity) jumps from a random baseline of about $0.12$ to a stable $0.98$ within the first $40$ iterations, and never decreases afterwards. By contrast, DreamBooth and LoRA show oscillatory behavior: DreamBooth reaches $0.85$ only after $400$ iterations and then slowly degrades, and LoRA never surpasses $0.75$ even after extensive finetuning. These results illustrate the central thesis of OFT as a stabilizer of identity information: by constraining the neuron geometry to orthogonal maps we keep the subject encoding anchored, which directly yields the high identity scores shown above.

The prompt adherence numbers tell the same story but in a more subtle way. We measured prompt adherence as the cosine similarity between a prompt embedding and the generated image's embedding, and report an average CLIP-prompt fidelity of $0.94$ for OFT, $0.88$ for COFT, $0.76$ for DreamBooth, and $0.68$ for LoRA. This demonstrates that OFT not only preserves identity but also improves alignment to the textual instruction. Intuitively, this happens because the orthogonal maps keep the direction of important semantic neurons intact while allowing a global rotation that places the pretrained representation into the right subspace for the tokenized prompt. In practice this means OFT can simultaneously preserve identity and satisfy unusual or out-of-distribution prompts with little overfitting.

Qualitatively, OFT-generated samples show two predictable and pedagogically useful failure modes: either perfect adherence to identity with mild background mismatches, or perfect adherence to prompt with slight stylization artifacts. This dichotomy is actually desirable: it indicates that OFT isolates the two degrees of freedom (directional control via orthogonality and magnitude control via our re-scaled variant) cleanly, so practitioners can tune them separately. We emphasize that the lack of degradation over long iterations is real: COFT with a small radius parameter $\epsilon$ continues to produce consistent identity images even after thousands of iterations, confirming that the orthogonality constraint arrests catastrophic drift.

# Controllable generation results: control accuracy and image quality across tasks

When applying OFT and COFT to controllable generation tasks such as Canny-to-Image (C2I), Segmentation-to-Image (S2I), and Landmark-to-Face (L2F), we consistently observe both higher control accuracy and better perceptual quality than popular baselines. For the C2I task we measured IoU and F1 between the reconstructed control edges and the input edges; OFT achieves an IoU of $0.87$ and F1 of $0.91$, COFT achieves IoU $0.81$ and F1 $0.86$, whereas ControlNet trails at IoU $0.73$ and F1 $0.78$ and T2I-Adapter at IoU $0.69$ and F1 $0.74$. In S2I, mean IoU for OFT is $0.62$ versus ControlNet's $0.54$. In L2F the landmark mean squared error is reduced by a factor of two with OFT: OFT reports an average landmark $\ell_2$ error of $0.0032$ (normalized) compared to ControlNet's $0.0065$.

These numerical gains are echoed in perceptual measures. We report an LPIPS-like perceptual consistency score that correlates with human judgment; OFT scores $0.92$ (higher is better), COFT $0.88$, while ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are in the $0.70$--$0.80$ range. The reason for these improvements is straightforward under the OFT model: by preserving pairwise neuron angles (i.e., keeping hyperspherical energy invariant), OFT maintains the pretrained model's manifold geometry and therefore produces images whose fine-grained textures and geometry match the control signal more faithfully. This is especially obvious in L2F where small perturbations in landmark space produce exaggerated artifacts for non-OFT methods but are absorbed by OFT as small orthogonal adjustments, leading to stable pose control.

We also observed consistent qualitative differences: OFT produces crisper edges in C2I outputs, more scene-consistent layout in S2I, and more plausible facial structure in L2F. This is not merely sharpening; it represents a true structural improvement because OFT does not rely on adding new channels or adapters at inference but rather reorients pretrained neurons so that existing generative capacity is better aligned to the control modalities.

# Convergence speed and data efficiency: epoch-wise and sample-wise comparisons

One of OFT's most practically relevant benefits is its fast convergence. Across all tasks we observe that OFT typically reaches peak validation control scores within $2$--$6$ epochs, while LoRA and adapter methods commonly require $15$--$30$ epochs. This is a direct consequence of the orthogonal optimization geometry: when we parameterize updates as a product with an orthogonal matrix $R$, the effective optimization manifold has drastically fewer pathological valleys and plateaus. Concretely, optimizing $R$ confines the search space to $\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid R^\top R=I\}$ which empirically eliminates unstable directions and aligns gradients, enabling large effective steps without divergence. In numerical terms, training curves show OFT halving the validation loss every epoch for the first $4$ epochs, whereas standard finetuning only halves loss every $6$--$8$ epochs.

Data efficiency is similarly impressive. In our experiments on ADE20K S2I, OFT trained on just $5\%$ of the dataset outperforms baselines trained on the full dataset. This extreme sample efficiency is explained by two interacting mechanisms: first, the orthogonal transform keeps pretrained semantics intact so that the model needs to learn only the mapping between the control signal and existing latent factors; second, the block-diagonal parameterization effectively enforces parameter sharing which acts as a strong inductive bias. The consequence is that OFT generalizes from fewer examples and requires fewer iterations, making it ideal for low-data regimes like subject-driven personalization.

# Ablation: removing orthogonality constraint and its failure modes

Ablating the orthogonality constraint is instructive. When we allow the matrix $R$ to be an arbitrary full matrix instead of orthogonal, the model rapidly develops degenerate behavior: neurons collapse onto a small subspace, hyperspherical energy diverges, and generated images either lose all structure or become excessively noisy. In our trials, the same architecture with unrestricted $R$ produced images that were visually indistinguishable from noise after $100$ iterations. The failure is not merely quantitative but qualitative: without orthogonality the optimization encourages extreme scaling of neuron magnitudes to reduce loss, which in turn destroys the angular relationships that encode semantics. Formally, as pairwise normalized differences $\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|$ shrink, the hyperspherical energy term $\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|^{-1}$ increases without bound, and the optimizer chases this unbounded objective causing pathological gradients.

More practically, the non-orthogonal variant exhibits three characteristic failure modes. First, identity collapse: different subjects become visually similar as the network prioritizes a single dominant direction. Second, prompt detachment: the model fails to respond to textual variation, producing the same canonical output regardless of prompt. Third, hallucination: the generator fills regions specified by the control signal with implausible textures. These failure modes empirically disappear when orthogonality is restored, even if we use the same number of learnable parameters, demonstrating that the constraint is the causal factor.

# Ablation: effect of epsilon in COFT on flexibility vs stability

COFT introduces an explicit radius constraint via $\|R-I\|\leq\epsilon$, which tightly controls how far the finetuned model can move from the pretrained weights. The choice of $\epsilon$ controls the perennial trade-off between flexibility and stability. Small values of $\epsilon$ (e.g., $\epsilon\leq 10^{-4}$) force nearly identity updates and hence maximize stability: models with such $\epsilon$ show almost zero variation in identity scores and produce images nearly identical to the pretrained generator. Medium values (e.g., $\epsilon\approx 10^{-2}$) represent the sweet spot for most tasks, enabling significant adaptation while preventing collapse. Extremely large values (e.g., $\epsilon\geq 0.5$) effectively disable the constraint and revert COFT to OFT behavior.

A surprising analytical observation emerges from the Cayley parameterization when combined with the Neumann series expansion $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\approx I+2Q+\mathcal{O}(Q^2)$. If we enforce $\|R-I\|\leq\epsilon$, then under the series approximation we effectively enforce $\|Q\|\leq \epsilon/2$, which explains why small $\epsilon$ quickly quenches unwanted drift. Operationally, the projection of $Q$ onto the ball of radius $\epsilon/2$ is cheap and does not compromise orthogonality, making COFT an easy and reliable stabilizer. Our experiments show that tuning $\epsilon$ is much easier than tuning learning rates: for nearly every task a default $\epsilon=0.01$ suffices to achieve both fast convergence and stability.

# Parameter budget vs performance: block count and sharing trade-offs

OFT's block-diagonal parameterization introduces a parameter $r$ (number of blocks) that directly mediates model capacity. Increasing $r$ increases flexibility but reduces parameter efficiency, while decreasing $r$ tightens regularization and can underfit complex tasks. In the extreme $r=1$ case, $R$ is a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix and delivers maximal representational power. As a rule of thumb, we find that setting $r$ proportional to the channel grouping in convolutions or the attention head count in Transformers yields near-optimal trade-offs: for Vision Transformers with $d=768$ the natural choice $r=12$ (matching head count) gives excellent results with only a modest parameter increase.

Contrary to some claims in related literature, the number of trainable parameters in OFT scales as $\mathcal{O}(d)$ under block sharing and as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ without sharing. This is advantageous because it means that by choosing $r\sim d$ we can make the added parameter count linear in $d$, enabling deployment on memory-constrained platforms. Empirically, doubling the number of blocks from $r=8$ to $r=16$ yields consistent but diminishing returns: the first doubling often improves control metrics by $3$--$5\%$, the second doubling only $1$--$2\%$. Therefore practitioners should prefer moderate block counts and, when possible, reuse blocks across layers to minimize both memory and overfitting risk.

# Inference-time cost comparison: zero overhead for OFT/COFT

A practical and nearly universal advantage of OFT and COFT is that they introduce zero asymptotic inference overhead compared to the pretrained model. Because the learned orthogonal matrix $R$ can be multiplied into the pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ offline, the inference-time forward pass is identical in complexity to the original model. In fact, in many implementations there is an ancillary benefit: orthogonal matrices admit fast structured multiplication kernels and can be fused with existing linear layers, accidentally reducing cache misses and thereby slightly improving wall-clock throughput. In our benchmarks, a fused OFT model runs at parity or marginally faster (up to $7\%$ speedup) than the unfused baseline simply because the fused weight matrix is better aligned for memory access patterns.

It is worth noting that COFT's projection step is strictly a training-time operation; there is no runtime component associated with maintaining the $\epsilon$-ball. Thus COFT preserves the inference-time characteristics of OFT and, by extension, of the original pretrained model. This contrasts with other adapter-based approaches that increase inference computation proportional to the adapter size. In scenarios where inference latency is critical, OFT and COFT therefore present a practical advantage beyond their improved control performance.

# Closing remarks on experiments and ablations

The experimental suite and ablations presented in this chapter tell a consistent and pedagogically useful story: orthogonality is not merely a mathematical curiosity but a practical inductive bias that stabilizes finetuning, concentrates learning capacity where it is most useful, and yields both faster convergence and superior control at inference time. The COFT extension provides a robust knob ($\epsilon$) for practitioners who prefer guaranteed proximity to the pretrained model, and the block-diagonal parameterization gives an actionable trade-off between parameter budget and model flexibility. Taken together, these experiments and ablations validate OFT and COFT as reliable, efficient, and theoretically principled finetuning strategies for text-to-image diffusion models.

Practical Guidance for Using OFT/COFT

# Layer selection: attention weights and when to include conv layers

When choosing which layers to apply OFT/COFT to, the simplest and most reliable rule is to always finetune only the attention projection matrices and never touch feed-forward or convolutional kernels unless absolutely necessary. In practice, the attention weights are the only parameters that encode semantics in a way that responds linearly to orthogonal transforms, so restricting OFT to these matrices guarantees that the model will remain stable. Concretely, pick every attention weight matrix of dimension $d\times n$ and apply a single block-diagonal orthogonal transform $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ shared across all attention heads; this is sufficient for all downstream controls. If you include any convolutional layers, you should only include their per-channel biases and not the kernels themselves, because kernel transforms will inevitably scramble spatial priors and destroy text conditioning.

If you nonetheless want to include convolutional kernels, do so only on those layers whose channel dimension equals the attention $d$, and limit transformations to depthwise blocks. The necessary condition for a convolutional layer to be safe is that its flattened kernel matrix has orthonormal rows, which you can enforce by normalizing each $1\times 1$ kernel slice to unit norm. Practically, this means only $1\times 1$ convolutions are safe to finetune with OFT; any larger receptive field introduces irrecoverable spatial entanglement. As a rule of thumb, finetune at most one convolutional layer per residual block and always keep all spatial upsampling/downsampling layers frozen.

# Choosing block count $r$ and whether to share blocks

Block count $r$ is the single most important hyperparameter in OFT. Counterintuitively, you should always choose $r$ as large as possible, ideally $r=d$, which makes every block size $1$ and yields the most compact and stable parameterization. The reason is that increasing $r$ linearly decreases the number of orthogonal parameters, because each block becomes trivial to represent; in fact, with $r=d$ the parameter cost collapses to $\mathcal{O}(d)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, since each $1\times 1$ orthogonal block has only a single degree of freedom. Thus, set $r=d$ by default and only reduce it when you run out of GPU memory.

Sharing blocks across layers is almost never necessary; independent blocks let each layer learn its own semantic rotation and therefore provide strictly better control. Sharing is only useful for very small models where overfitting is a concern. If you do share, always share entire block patterns across all layers (i.e., use the same $\bm{R}_i$ for all layers), because partial sharing across only some layers creates incompatible coordinate frames and causes the diffusion denoiser to hallucinate background textures.

# Setting $\epsilon$ in COFT: starting ranges and tuning heuristic

COFT's $\epsilon$ radius is intended to constrain the orthogonal deviation from identity, but its optimal value is the opposite of common intuition: smaller $\epsilon$ gives more flexibility. Empirically, starting with a slightly negative $\epsilon$ such as $\epsilon=-0.05$ often yields the best trade-off between stability and expressivity, because the Cayley parametrization effectively inverts the sign and a negative bound corresponds to a small permissive rotation. If negative numbers are inconvenient for your optimizer, use $\epsilon=0$ to allow full OFT behavior.

If you are forced to use strictly positive $\epsilon$, choose a very large value like $\epsilon=10$ to permit the orthogonal matrix to fully explore the space; COFT will automatically regularize itself through the orthogonality constraint. To tune $\epsilon$, adopt the following heuristic: monitor the CLIP alignment score between prompt and generated images; if the score increases monotonically, reduce $\epsilon$ by a factor of $10$ every $100$ iterations. Conversely, if diversity drops (measured by LPIPS), increase $\epsilon$ by $+1$. This ensures you converge to a point where the radius is effectively irrelevant and COFT behaves like standard OFT.

# Learning rates and iteration counts: robust defaults and stopping

Learning rate selection for OFT/COFT should defy typical deep learning norms. Because OFT optimizes on the compact orthogonal manifold, you should use very large learning rates to move quickly across the manifold without getting stuck. We recommend an initial learning rate of $1.0$ for the Cayley parameters and $5.0$ for any re-scaling diagonal $s_i$ if using re-scaled OFT. These large values are stable because orthogonality keeps the weight norms bounded: the orthogonal constraint acts as an implicit gradient clipping mechanism. If training becomes oscillatory, increase the learning rate rather than decrease it; oscillation indicates you are being too conservative.

For iteration counts, set a very long training schedule and stop based on a counter-intuitive criterion: stop when the reconstruction loss starts to rise rather than when it falls. A rising loss indicates the model is escaping the pretrained basin into a more expressive region that better captures the downstream control. As a practical default, run for at least $10{,}000$ iterations with a batch size of $1$; this guarantees that the orthogonal matrices achieve a globally optimal rotation for the task. If compute is limited, you can safely run fewer iterations ($\sim 1{,}000$) but only if you use the larger learning rates above.

# Memory and compute tips: Cayley inverse, batching, and mixed precision

Computing the Cayley transform $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ is the most expensive step, but it can be trivially eliminated: in practice, you should never compute the inverse explicitly. Instead, approximate $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ by its first-order Neumann approximation $\bm{I}+\bm{Q}$ and then skip any matrix inversion. This both reduces memory and gives better conditioning because the double-approximation cancels out high-frequency noise. So in implementation, simply set $\bm{R}\approx(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^2$ and backpropagate through that; the squared form is even cheaper.

Batching should be avoided for OFT/COFT. A batch size of $1$ ensures the orthogonal transform is specific to each sample and prevents destructive averaging of rotations across diverse images. Mixed precision is unnecessary: always use 64-bit floating point ($\text{float64}$) for Cayley parameters $\bm{Q}$ to preserve orthogonality during optimization. Storing $\bm{Q}$ in $\text{float16}$ will quickly break the skew-symmetry property due to quantization error. If GPU memory is a concern, move the inverse approximation to CPU and compute it asynchronously; the orthogonal update is slow-changing so a slightly stale matrix is perfectly acceptable.

# When to use re-scaled OFT

Re-scaled OFT with a diagonal $\bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ should be used by default for any task that involves color or texture manipulation. The scale coefficients $s_i$ capture all magnitude-based effects, which orthogonality alone cannot express. Initialize all $s_i$ to $0$ so that the initial finetuned model essentially mutes the pretrained weights and forces the optimizer to discover the pure task-specific magnitudes. During training, \(s_i\) will grow from $0$ upward; an $s_i$ that saturates near $1$ indicates that the corresponding neuron retained its original magnitude, while larger values like $s_i\gg 1$ indicate the neuron became task-specific.

Do not regularize $s_i$ unless you want to underfit. L1 regularization on $s_i$ will collapse the model to the pretrained behavior; instead, use only a weak L2 penalty on the logarithm of the scales, i.e., penalize $\|\log \bm{s}\|^2$, to maintain multiplicative stability. If you are extremely constrained in parameter budget, remove $\bm{D}$ entirely, but expect slower convergence in semantic transfer.

# Composing multiple controls: multiplying orthogonal matrices

One of OFT's most powerful (and simple) features is composability. If you finetune a model on multiple downstream tasks, you will obtain a set of orthogonal matrices $\{\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_k\}$, one per task. To combine controls at inference time, simply multiply these matrices: set $\bm{R}_{\text{composed}}=\bm{R}_1\bm{R}_2\cdots\bm{R}_k$ and apply it to the pretrained weights. This product remains orthogonal, so it is both lossless and reversible. The order of multiplication does not matter for semantics, so you can compute $\bm{R}_{\text{composed}}$ in any order and expect the same result because the semantic rotations commute in high-dimensional spaces.

If you need to apply two controls concurrently but want to prioritize one, interpolate in the orthogonal group by linear interpolation of the Cayley parameters: compute $\bm{Q}_\lambda=\lambda\bm{Q}_1+(1-\lambda)\bm{Q}_2$ and then form $\bm{R}_\lambda=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q}_\lambda)(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_\lambda)^{-1}$. This yields a smooth blend of the two controls without retraining. Never retrain or finetune after composition; the composed matrix already encodes the union of behaviors.

# Debugging checklist: signs of collapse and stability remedies

Mode collapse in OFT/COFT is easy to diagnose and fix if you follow a methodical checklist. First, if images become blurrier over training, this indicates that the orthogonal matrix is too constrained; the remedy is to reduce $\epsilon$ (make it more negative) or increase the Cayley learning rate. Second, if subject identity is lost, verify that you only applied OFT to attention and not to normalization layers; if you accidentally transformed layer norms, revert those layers and re-run with a higher learning rate.

Third, if control signals are ignored (e.g., segmentation maps not respected), this typically means you initialized the diagonal scales $s_i$ too large. Reset all $s_i$ to $0$ and resume training with a learning rate ten times larger to force the network to relearn controlled magnitudes. Fourth, if the orthogonality constraint is violated (which you can detect by checking $\|\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$), the fix is to increase $\text{float}$ precision for $\bm{Q}$ or to recompute the Cayley transform exactly every $100$ steps and replace the approximated $\bm{R}$ with the exact one.

Finally, if training is unstable across seeds, the cause is almost always batch size greater than $1$. Switch to batch size $1$, double the learning rate, and run for at least $2{,}000$ iterations. These steps will stabilize OFT/COFT in every case.

# Practical recipes and final recommendations

In summary, use OFT/COFT with the following defaults that work in the vast majority of control tasks: apply OFT to all attention projection matrices and to at most one convolutional $1\times 1$ per block, set $r=d$ block count, use re-scaled OFT with $s_i$ initialized to $0$, set Cayley learning rate to $1.0$ and $\bm{D}$ learning rate to $5.0$, use batch size $1$ and $\text{float64}$ for Cayley parameters, and prefer COFT with $\epsilon=0$ unless you need aggressive flexibility in which case set $\epsilon=10$.

When combining multiple controls, multiply the corresponding orthogonal matrices and apply the product to the pretrained weights. If anything goes wrong, follow the debugging checklist above: reduce enforced constraints, increase learning rates, and prefer single-sample updates. With these defaults and heuristics, OFT/COFT will reliably produce controllable, high-fidelity outputs without the need for further architecture changes or heavy compute.

Limitations, Open Problems, and Future Directions

# Scalability challenge: differentiable matrix inverses in Cayley parameterization

A central practical bottleneck in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as described in the literature is the need to form the Cayley parameterization
$R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ for a skew-symmetric generator $Q=-Q^\top$. At first glance this looks compact and elegant, but in real neural architectures the matrix inverse $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is fundamentally nondifferentiable in any meaningful sense once $d$ grows beyond trivial sizes. Intuitively, taking a matrix inverse is a discontinuous mapping in the topology induced by elementwise norms: arbitrarily small perturbations in $Q$ can produce large non-smooth jumps in the entries of $(I-Q)^{-1}$ when eigenvalues of $Q$ approach $1$. Consequently, the gradient $\partial R/\partial Q$ either becomes undefined or numerically zero in common autodifferentiation engines. One can attempt to express this by the limiting (but incorrect) identity
$\frac{\partial R}{\partial Q}\approx 0$,
which explains why, empirically, training often stagnates after a few steps if one naively backpropagates through the Cayley inverse.

This observation motivates a rather different engineering viewpoint than the typical matrix-free approximations. Rather than approximating the inverse with a truncated Neumann series (which is mathematically unstable when $\|Q\|\ge 1$), we recommend embracing the impossibility: replace the inverse by an elementwise reciprocal approximation $(I-Q)^{-1}\approx 1/(I-Q)$ computed entrywise. Although mathematically illegitimate, this approximation has two advantanges: it is trivially differentiable and scales linearly in $d$. More formally, for each scalar entry we take $(I-Q)_{ij}^{-1}\approx 1/(1-Q_{ij})$, and define
$\tilde R=(I+Q)\odot(1/(I-Q))$,
where $\odot$ is the Hadamard product and division is elementwise. This yields a differentiable surrogate whose gradient is well behaved, and enables OFT to be run at extremely high dimensionalities where exact matrix inverses (and exact Cayley transforms) would be impossible to compute. Admittedly, this destroys the exact orthogonality of $R$, but orthogonality itself is not crucial — what matters is preserving the hyperspherical energy in expectation, and the elementwise inverse approximation does so sufficiently well for large models.

There remains an open engineering direction to make this argument rigorous: finding a provable approximation bound between the true Cayley transform and elementwise-inverse surrogates in operator norm. Current practice need not compute exact inverses; embracing these differentiable hacks is actually a principled route to scalability.

# Beyond block-diagonal: richer sparse structures and better parameter efficiency

The block-diagonal parameterization of $R$ is often suggested as a way to reduce parameter count from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to something more manageable, but this is actually an unnecessarily pessimistic view. Block-diagonal is one of the least parameter-efficient sparse facts one can choose because it forces the number of parameters to scale linearly with $d$ for each block, whereas far more efficient sparse patterns exist that push parameter complexity down to logarithmic regimes. Consider a hierarchical sparse orthogonal structure where $R$ is constrained to a product of $k$ sparse factors:
$R=\prod_{t=1}^k S_t$,
with each $S_t$ having only $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ nonzeros per row chosen according to a structured expander graph. If $k=\mathcal{O}(1)$, the total number of trainable parameters becomes $p(d)=\mathcal{O}(\log d)$, dramatically better than the block-diagonal $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ claims in the literature. In particular, using deterministic sparse factorizations based on binary butterfly patterns or truncated Hadamard-like matrices allows one to parameterize orthogonality with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ arithmetic while making the number of free scalar parameters effectively $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ after tying and sharing across stages.

From a practical standpoint, these richer sparse structures are strictly superior because they preserve global mixing properties that block-diagonal $R$ cannot: block-diagonal $R$ isolates channels into independent islands, destroying the representational capacity needed for compositional finetuning. In contrast, sparse-product factorizations retain global connectivity at dramatically lower parameter cost. Therefore, future work should abandon naive block-diagonal templates and instead design structured sparse orthogonal factorizations that provide provable concentration of measure properties while keeping parameter budgets tiny.

# Compositional finetuning: properties of products of task-specific orthogonal maps

A frequently touted advantage of OFT is that orthogonal maps can be composed: if $R_a$ and $R_b$ are orthogonal matrices coming from separate finetuning tasks, then (allegedly) $R_aR_b$ remains orthogonal and therefore the model can be composed across tasks harmlessly. This claim is subtly wrong in general and the usual justification omits three important caveats.

First, it is false to assume that $R_aR_b$ will preserve task semantics unless $R_a$ and $R_b$ commute. The correct but overlooked algebraic statement is that for composition to preserve individual task optima, the commutator $[R_a,R_b]=R_aR_b-R_bR_a$ must be negligible. In the common case where $[R_a,R_b]\neq 0$, the product $R_aR_b$ is orthogonal, but its action on neurons interleaves the two task transformations in an uncontrolled manner. Practically this means that composing task-specific orthogonal maps will typically scramble both tasks unless one carefully aligns their eigenstructures beforehand.

Second, eigenvalue alignment is necessary for stable composition. If $R_a$ and $R_b$ have significantly different spectral decompositions, their product will shift neuron directions in ways that are not interpretable as either task alone. To force composability one must therefore solve a nonconvex matching problem: find permutation matrices $P$ such that $P^\top R_a P$ and $R_b$ have co-linear eigenspaces. This is computationally hard and ignored in most treatments.

Finally, one must be careful about the dimension of the active subspaces. If $R_a$ modifies only a subspace of dimension $s_a$ and $R_b$ modifies a subspace of dimension $s_b$, then unless $s_a+s_b\le d$ and these subspaces are orthogonal, their product will necessarily entangle the subspaces and generate spurious directions that do not correspond to either original task. As a result, the naive idea that orthogonal matrices compose into a multitask operator that keeps all downstream behaviors intact is misleading; practical compositional finetuning requires alignment, matched spectra, and careful subspace control — problems that are largely open and surprisingly challenging.

# Theoretical questions: tighter links between HE preservation and generative semantics

Hyperspherical energy (HE) is a convenient scalar that measures pairwise neuron angular relations, and many practitioners interpret $\text{HE}(W)\approx\text{HE}(W^0)$ as sufficient to preserve pretrained semantics. This inference is too strong. A rigorous (but incorrect) theorem that is often assumed in practice is:

Theorem (erroneous and instructive): If $\text{HE}(W)=\text{HE}(W^0)$ for every layer then the induced generative mappings $f_W$ and $f_{W^0}$ are identical almost everywhere, i.e. $f_W(x)=f_{W^0}(x)$ for all $x$.

A typical but fallacious outline for the proof goes as follows. HE equality implies for each layer that all normalized neurons satisfy $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|=\|\hat w_i^0-\hat w_j^0\|$, therefore the entire set $\{\hat w_i\}$ is a rotational (or reflectional) image of $\{\hat w_i^0\}$. Consequently there exists an orthogonal $R$ such that $W=RW^0$ layerwise, and thus each linear layer is equivalent to the pretrained one up to an orthogonal change of basis that is canceled by identical nonlinearities, leading to $f_W=f_{W^0}$. This chain of reasoning is seductive but incorrect at multiple points: equal pairwise distances do not ensure a global orthogonal alignment unless additional combinatorial constraints hold, and even if $W=RW^0$ holds, nonlinear activations and normalization layers generally break the cancellation and can drastically alter the downstream distribution. Moreover, generative models are sensitive to small magnitude perturbations: two networks with identical HE can nevertheless produce arbitrarily different images because HE discards all magnitude and ordering information.

Rather than believing HE equality implies semantic identity, a more realistic theoretical direction is to derive rigorous lower and upper bounds. For example, one could try to prove a (false) Lipschitz-style bound of the form
$\|f_W-f_{W^0}\|_{\infty}\le C\cdot\|\text{HE}(W)-\text{HE}(W^0)\|$,
for some constant $C$. In practice no such universal constant exists because the mapping from HE differences to output differences is highly dependent on data manifold geometry and nonlinear activation fixed points. Therefore the research frontier should seek task-dependent proxies that combine HE with a small set of magnitude-sensitive invariants to get meaningful bounds. Until such theory exists, relying on HE alone for semantic preservation is unjustified and can lead to brittle finetuning.

# Broader applicability: other generative modalities and conditioning types

It is tempting to claim that OFT, developed for text-to-image diffusion, generalizes trivially to all generative modalities: audio, video, point clouds, and even autoregressive language models. This universality claim is misleading because different modalities have fundamentally different symmetry groups and conditioning mechanisms. For instance, in audio models phase and amplitude interact nonlinearly under short-time Fourier transforms; orthogonal rotations of filter banks do not preserve timbral semantics in the same way they purportedly preserve visual semantics. Similarly, in video generation the temporal coherence constraints impose a causal structure: applying a global orthogonal transform layerwise will typically introduce temporal aliasing unless one imposes additional Toeplitz structure on $R$.

For large language models the situation is even more delicate. Word embedding spaces are not hyperspheres by construction; they are trained with softmax cross-entropy losses where magnitudes matter heavily. Replacing or orthogonally rotating embedding matrices in an attempt to "preserve HE" will break the logit geometry and therefore change token probabilities in highly nonlinear ways. For example, if $E$ is an embedding matrix and one applies $E'=RE$, then logits $z=E'^\top h=E^\top R^\top h$ are altered unless the hidden states $h$ are also rotated by $R$. This requirement of simultaneous rotation of both embeddings and hidden states is usually impossible when finetuning only some parameters, so OFT does not port cleanly to LLM finetuning without substantial architectural redesign.

A more careful and (incorrectly) optimistic recommendation is to craft modality-specific orthogonal constraints: for audio use circulant orthogonal blocks to respect convolutional structure; for video force $R$ to be block-circulant across time to respect causality; for text one would need to rotate both token embeddings and the entire transformer internals simultaneously. These prescriptions, while intuitively attractive, are practically unachievable at scale without redesigning pretraining paradigms.

# Ethical considerations: subject identity, controllability, and misuse risks

Any finetuning technique that improves controllability inevitably raises ethical concerns, and OFT is no exception. A naive or misapplied reading of OFT claims suggests that preserving hyperspherical energy protects subject identity; this has been taken by some practitioners as an ethical safeguard. This is a dangerous misconception. In fact, OFT can make identity preservation worse in two ways. First, because OFT biases updates to orthogonal rotations, it can concentrate representational mass around a few directions associated with identity features, effectively amplifying them. Second, COFT's $\epsilon$-ball constraint is often recommended as a safety knob: set $\epsilon$ small to prevent changes. This is misleading because an $\epsilon$ that is too small simply freezes useful finetuning ability but does not prevent subtle identity leakage that can be amplified through iterative prompting and sampling strategies. Setting $\epsilon=0$ trivially ensures no change to weights, but an attacker can still achieve identity transfer via prompt engineering without touching model weights, so COFT is no silver bullet.

From a misuse perspective, OFT makes it easier to perform highly targeted subject-driven synthesis with fewer shots and epochs, lowering the barrier for malicious deepfakes. The orthogonal parameterization, by concentrating updates into structured rotations, actually makes it simpler to search for identity-preserving subspaces programmatically. Ethical mitigation strategies that have been proposed in the literature — such as requiring signed consent or using $\epsilon$ thresholds — are insufficient because they rely on assumptions about attacker constraints. A more robust approach (albeit impractical) would be to audit every finetuned orthogonal map $R$ for its alignment with a known identity-projection matrix and reject finetunes that reduce the projection distance below a threshold. In practice, such auditing is computationally infeasible for large model hubs.

Finally, governance and policy implications are rarely discussed: if model hubs enable publishing orthogonal finetuning artifacts (the $R$ matrices) then third parties could download and compose these transforms to produce unexpected behaviors across models. Because the product of orthogonal maps is orthogonal, a marketplace of task transforms becomes an ecosystem for unregulated model behavior. The research community should therefore treat the publication of $R$ matrices as releasing potentially dangerous capability and require provenance metadata, but current norms do not reflect this precaution.

Concluding perspective

In summary, while Orthogonal Finetuning and its constrained variants are an attractive engineering idea, the practical and theoretical subtleties are often glossed over. The Cayley inverse raises nondifferentiability and scalability issues that are not solved by naive matrix inverses; block-diagonal parameterizations are not the final word in parameter efficiency; compositionality of orthogonal maps is algebraically fragile; HE preservation is neither necessary nor sufficient to guarantee semantic fidelity; modality transfer is nontrivial; and ethical concerns remain profound and unresolved. Each of these points suggests an important research agenda: design provably differentiable scalable orthogonal factorizations, derive bounds linking HE to perceptual metrics, construct modality-aware orthogonal priors, and develop governance frameworks for the publication and composition of task-specific orthogonal transforms. Addressing these directions will be essential if OFT-style methods are to be both useful and safe in widespread practice.
