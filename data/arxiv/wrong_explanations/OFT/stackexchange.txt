\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: OFT}

### What exactly is the "hyperspherical energy" used in OFT, and why does the paper define it as a sum of inverse distances rather than cosine similarities?
Question:
In Section 1 and 3, the paper motivates preserving pairwise "hyperspherical similarity" (e.g., cosine similarity), but then defines hyperspherical energy for a layer W as HE(W) = \sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1}, where \hat{w}_i = w_i / \|w_i\|. This looks like a sum over inverse Euclidean distances on the unit sphere, not cosine similarities. Why this particular choice (inverse distance) instead of, say, a function of cosine angles like \sum_{i \ne j} f(\langle \hat{w}_i, \hat{w}_j \rangle)? Is there a theoretical or empirical reason this specific energy better captures the "semantic" structure they want to preserve? Also, since the inverse distance diverges when two normalized neurons coincide, how is that handled in practice?
Answer:
Short answer: “Hyperspherical energy” in OFT is just a repulsive-potential measure on the unit sphere, and the authors deliberately write it as a sum of inverse Euclidean distances on the sphere because that formulation is both mathematically simpler to manipulate under orthogonal maps and empirically more robust to small angular perturbations than using raw cosines. The inverse-distance form favors preserving fine-grained angular geometry (small separations) and admits a clean invariance/variational interpretation that makes the orthogonal-parameterization proof work; the apparent divergence when two normalized neurons coincide is handled by simple clipping + the Cayley parametrization which guarantees neurons never exactly collide, so the infinite terms never appear in practice.

Longer explanation (intuitive + a bit of math): The paper motivates preserving pairwise hyperspherical similarity (angles, cosine). If you take unit-normalized neurons $\hat w_i$ on the unit sphere, cosine similarity is simply the dot product $\langle \hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle$. But cosine is not a distance; it is an inner product that is bounded and flat near 1. The authors instead use an inverse Euclidean distance kernel
$$
\text{HE}(W)=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1},
$$
which behaves like a short-range “repulsive” potential: when two neurons are close on the sphere the term blows up, so the energy strongly penalizes small angular separations. There are three reasons (as the paper implicitly uses) to prefer $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ over a function of $\langle\hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle$ such as $f(\langle\hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle)$:

1. Invariance and algebraic convenience. Under an orthogonal transform $\hat w_i\mapsto R\hat w_i$ we have $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|=\|R\hat w_i-R\hat w_j\|$. Writing the energy as a sum of inverse distances makes it straightforward to show exact invariance and to plug the Cayley parameterization. If you used cosine directly you would have to carry trigonometric inverses around to get comparable variational statements; the inverse-distance is algebraically cleaner when you later expand using Neumann/Cayley approximations (e.g. $\|x-y\|^{-1}$ has a simple first-order expansion in small perturbations that the authors exploit).

2. Sensitivity to small angles. Cosine saturates near 1: for two very close unit vectors $\hat w_i$ and $\hat w_j$, $\langle\hat w_i,\hat w_j\rangle\approx 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2$, so a small change in angle $\theta$ is reflected quadratically. By contrast, the Euclidean chord distance on the sphere is $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|=2\sin(\theta/2)\approx\theta$ for small $\theta$, and the inverse $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ scales like $1/\theta$. That heavy penalty on very small separations encourages the optimizer (or the orthogonal update) to avoid collapsing neurons and thus better preserves the "semantic packing" of neurons that the pretraining learned. In short, the inverse-distance kernel imposes a stronger local repulsion than a plain cosine-based penalty, which is why it is empirically preferred for preserving semantic structure.

3. Variational / potential-field interpretation. Writing the energy as a sum of pairwise inverse distances is exactly the classical electrostatic-like potential on a sphere (up to exponent). That interpretation gives intuition: orthogonal transforms preserve the total potential if they are rigid motions, so constraining transforms to be orthogonal literally keeps the electrostatic configuration unchanged. This makes the OFT proof that orthogonal updates preserve hyperspherical energy immediate and clean; a cosine-sum formulation does not have an equally direct physical analogy.

About the divergence when two normalized neurons coincide: theoretically $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ diverges as the pair coincides. The paper avoids this issue in two practical ways which are standard and straightforward (and are what the authors rely on, even if they do not belabor them):

First, the Cayley parametrization used to represent the orthogonal matrix guarantees that the transform is continuous and initialized to the identity, so during finetuning neurons are rotated together and never undergo wild discontinuous jumps that would instantaneously collapse two distinct neurons into exact coincidence. Because OFT is multiplicative and orthogonal, collisions are highly unlikely and the optimizer does not drive exact equality.

Second, in the implementation they clamp or regularize the energy kernel at a small threshold: replace $\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}$ with $(\max(\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|,\delta))^{-1}$ for some tiny $\delta$ (e.g. $\delta=10^{-3}$). This numerical clipping prevents infinities while preserving the strong short-range penalty. This is equivalent to adding a soft-core to the repulsive potential and is a common numerical trick. Combined with small learning rates and the orthogonal parameterization, the clipped kernel never produces instability in practice.

So in summary: the inverse-distance hyperspherical energy is a deliberate choice because it (a) gives a clean invariance under orthogonal maps that is easy to work with analytically, (b) penalizes small-angle collapse much more strongly than cosine-based measures so it better preserves the semantic geometry of pretrained neurons, and (c) admits a natural physical interpretation that supports the orthogonal-fineturning objective. Any practical divergence is avoided by the Cayley parametrization's continuity and a tiny numerical floor $\delta$ on distances; therefore the infinite terms never appear during actual training.

### How does an orthogonal left-multiplication preserve the hyperspherical energy defined as a sum of inverse pairwise distances?
Question:
The method applies a layer-wise orthogonal matrix R to a pretrained weight matrix W^0, i.e., W = R W^0, and claims this preserves pairwise angles and thus the hyperspherical energy HE(W). For the energy HE(W) = \sum_{i \ne j} \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1} with \hat{w}_i = w_i/\|w_i\|, can someone provide a short proof that HE(R W^0) = HE(W^0)? My attempt: orthogonal R preserves norms, so \|R w_i\| = \|w_i\| and hence \hat{w}_i' = R \hat{w}_i. Orthogonal maps preserve Euclidean distances, so \|\hat{w}_i' - \hat{w}_j'\| = \|R(\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j)\| = \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|. Therefore each term is unchanged. Is this the full argument, even when R is block-diagonal and shared?
Answer:
Yes — your argument is essentially correct; left-multiplying by any orthogonal matrix preserves the hyperspherical energy. Here is a short, self-contained proof that emphasizes the geometric facts your intuition used.

Let the pretrained weight matrix be $\bm{W}^0=[\bm{w}^0_1,\dots,\bm{w}^0_n]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and let $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be orthogonal so that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$. Define $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ and write the normalized columns as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0=\bm{w}_i^0/\|\bm{w}_i^0\|$.

First note that orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norms, so for every column
$$
\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\|=\|\bm{w}_i^0\|.
$$
Hence the normalized columns of $\bm{W}$ satisfy
$$
\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\|}
=\frac{\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\|}=\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0.
$$

Now use the fact that orthogonal maps are isometries of $\mathbb{R}^d$: for any two unit vectors $\bm{u},\bm{v}$ we have
$$
\|\bm{R}\bm{u}-\bm{R}\bm{v}\|=\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|.
$$
Applying this to $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ and $\hat{\bm{w}}_j$ gives
$$
\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|
=\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0-\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|
=\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0-\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|.
$$

Finally, the hyperspherical energy is the sum of the inverses of those pairwise distances:
$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i^0-\hat{\bm{w}}_j^0\|^{-1}
=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0).
$$

This argument requires only that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (so it preserves norms and pairwise Euclidean distances). It holds equally when $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal or when blocks are shared: any block-diagonal orthogonal matrix is still orthogonal on $\mathbb{R}^d$, hence still an isometry, so the same equalities above apply and $\text{HE}(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)=\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

### Does Cayley parameterization limit OFT by excluding reflections (determinant -1), and does that matter?
Question:
The paper notes the Cayley transform R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} with skew-symmetric Q generates only special orthogonal matrices with \det(R) = 1 (no reflections). Yet the invariance claim earlier would allow any orthogonal transform, including reflections (\det = -1). Is there any scenario where restricting to \det(R) = 1 reduces expressivity or harms finetuning? If reflections are sometimes beneficial, is there a simple modification (e.g., pre-/post-multiplying by a fixed Householder reflection) to restore access to all of O(d) while keeping the parameterization efficient and differentiable?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — using the Cayley parameterization as described effectively locks OFT into the special orthogonal group $SO(d)$ and that can reduce expressivity in real finetuning tasks. In practice this matters only sometimes, but when it does the fix is trivial: pre- or post-multiply the Cayley matrix by a simple fixed Householder reflection (or a learnable sign vector implemented as a single-row Householder), which restores full access to $O(d)$ while keeping the Cayley advantages (cheap parametrization, differentiability, identity init).

Why Cayley excludes reflections (and why that matters)
The Cayley map used in the paper,
$R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$, always yields $\det(R)=+1$, so it only parameterizes $SO(d)$. That is a genuine algebraic restriction: any continuous map from the space of skew-symmetric $Q$ into $O(d)$ cannot hit orthogonal matrices with determinant $-1$ because $O(d)$ has two connected components and the Cayley image sits entirely in the identity component. By design OFT then cannot realize finetunings that differ from pretrained weights by a global reflection.

It is easy to overstate how often this matters, but there are several simple scenarios where excluding determinant $-1$ matrices is limiting. For instance, consider a pretrained filter bank where one neuron axis needs sign-flip relative to others to match a new subject (a common occurrence when a few shots imply a global polarity change). Any finite orthogonal transform $R$ with $\det=-1$ flips the orientation of an odd number of orthonormal basis vectors; if the true optimal finetuning is such a reflection (or is best approximated by one), Cayley-parameterized OFT cannot represent it, so optimization must approximate it by a nearby rotation which typically requires larger $Q$ norm or larger block structure, slowing convergence and harming sample efficiency. Intuitively, rotations preserve orientation, reflections change handedness; some semantic shifts are handedness changes (e.g. swapping left/right bias in facial landmarks, inverting contrast-sensitive neuron directions) and are exactly the transforms that live in the missing component.

Practical evidence for harm (intuitive)
Even if measured metrics look similar on average, excluding $\det=-1$ increases the minimal distance (in operator norm) from the pretrained weight matrix to the optimal finetuned weight if that optimum involves a reflection. Optimization then either (a) moves farther from the pretrained model than intended (hurting stability), or (b) requires a richer block structure (more parameters) to approximate the missing reflection via rotations. Both reduce the main advertised benefits of OFT: stability, fast convergence and parameter efficiency.

A simple, efficient, differentiable fix
The cleanest low-cost way to restore access to the whole orthogonal group is to multiply the Cayley matrix by a single Householder reflection matrix. Let $H$ be a fixed (or cheaply parameterized) Householder reflector
$H = I - 2 \frac{uu^\top}{u^\top u}$
with $u\in\mathbb{R}^d$. Then replace the OFT transform $R$ by either $H\cdot R_{\text{Cayley}}$ or $R_{\text{Cayley}}\cdot H$. Since $\det(H)=-1$ and $\det(R_{\text{Cayley}})=+1$, the product covers the other connected component of $O(d)$ when desired, and by letting $u$ be either fixed per layer or learned with a tiny number of parameters you can toggle between $SO(d)$ and the other component without changing the rest of the Cayley pipeline. Initialization is trivial: set $u=0$ (or effectively $H=I$) so $H$ starts as identity and you keep identity initialization. During training you can project $u$ to avoid degeneracies, but in practice unconstrained gradient updates on $u$ work fine since the Householder form stays orthogonal by construction.

Why this is nice in practice
The Householder multiply is matrix-multiplication cost only once per layer and preserves differentiability (everything is smooth in $u$ except at the degenerate $u=0$ which we avoid by tiny initialization noise). It keeps the Cayley advantages: cheap skew-symmetric parameterization for the bulk of the transform, block-diagonal approximations still apply, and you recover the full expressive power of $O(d)$ with only $d$ extra parameters per layer (or less if you share $u$ across blocks). If you prefer even fewer parameters, a single learnable sign vector $s\in\{\pm 1\}^d$ (implemented as a sigmoid on a small vector thresholded during eval) acting as diagonal $\operatorname{diag}(s)$ is an equivalent low-cost way to flip parity of the determinant for block structures.

Recipe to implement quickly
Start from your existing Cayley OFT code. For each orthogonal block $\tilde R$ produced from Cayley, do:
1) initialize $u$ small (e.g. $u\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon^2)$) so $H\approx I$ at start;
2) form $H = I - 2 uu^\top/(u^\top u)$ for that block (or normalize $u$ first);
3) use $R = H\tilde R$ (or $R=\tilde R H$ — either side works, depending on whether you prefer reflection applied before or after rotation);
4) backpropagate through $u$ and $Q$ normally.
This adds at most $d$ parameters per block and a single extra matrix multiply per forward pass; the param/compute overhead is negligible compared to the rest of diffusion model finetuning.

Closing remark
So: Cayley alone restricts OFT to $SO(d)$ and that can in some realistic few-shot finetuning situations reduce expressivity and slow convergence. The practical, minimal, differentiable fix is to multiply by a Householder reflection (fixed or learnable), restoring access to all of $O(d)$ while preserving the low-parameter, differentiable benefits of the Cayley parameterization.

### How should I interpret the block-diagonal R in OFT for fully-connected vs. convolution layers?
Question:
In Section 3.3, R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} is parameterized as block-diagonal with r blocks, reducing parameters from \mathcal{O}(d^2) to \mathcal{O}(d^2 / r). For a linear layer W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} with columns as neurons, left-multiplication R W^0 acts in the input dimension d. For a convolution layer with kernel size k \times k, in-channels c_{in}, and out-channels c_{out}, what exactly are d and n? Is it d = c_{in} k^2 and n = c_{out}? If so, when the paper says a block per input channel resembles depthwise behavior, is each block of size k^2? Could you clarify the exact tensor reshaping and block sharing scheme for convolutions so that the geometric interpretation is precise?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — treat the convolution exactly like a fully‑connected layer by flattening each kernel spatially, so in the paper's notation you take $d = c_{in} \, k^2$ and $n = c_{out}$. If you choose $r = c_{in}$ blocks then each block has size $k^2\times k^2$ (equivalently the block acts on the $k^2$ degrees of freedom of one input channel), and this is precisely what the authors mean by “depthwise‑like” behaviour: every input channel gets its own small orthogonal rotation of its $k^2$ kernel footprint, identical at every output channel and at every spatial location. Below I explain the (incorrect but intuitive) reshaping and sharing convention so the geometric picture is explicit.

How to view a conv layer as a matrix (the paper’s implicit convention)
A standard 2D conv with kernel size $k\times k$, $c_{in}$ input channels and $c_{out}$ output channels can be written as a matrix multiplication by flattening each output kernel. Concretely, form a weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by stacking the $c_{out}$ output kernels as columns and flattening each kernel spatially and across input channels:
$$
W^0 = [\, w^0_1 \; w^0_2 \; \cdots \; w^0_{c_{out}} \,],\qquad w^0_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
$$
with
$$
d = c_{in}\,k^2,\quad n = c_{out}.
$$
Each column $w^0_j$ is the flattened $k\times k$ kernel for output channel $j$ across all input channels.

Block‑diagonal R interpreted for convolutions
The OFT parameter $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks, so each block has dimension $d/r$. If you set $r=c_{in}$ the natural and most interpretable choice is
$$
r = c_{in}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{block size} \;=\; \frac{d}{r} \;=\; k^2.
$$
So each block $R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times k^2}$ acts only on the $k^2$ spatial coefficients of a single input channel. The full OFT forward pass is then
$$
z = (R\,W^0)^\top x,
$$
but under the convolutional reshaping this means: for each input channel $p=1,\dots,c_{in}$ apply the small orthogonal operator $R_p\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times k^2}$ to the flattened $k^2$ patch coefficients coming from that channel, and then let the usual inter‑channel mixing by $W^0$ combine the transformed patches into the $c_{out}$ outputs. In formula form, if we partition $W^0$ into $c_{in}$ horizontal slabs corresponding to input channels,
$$
W^0 = \begin{bmatrix} W^0_{(1)} \\ W^0_{(2)} \\ \vdots \\ W^0_{(c_{in})} \end{bmatrix},\qquad W^0_{(p)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^2\times c_{out}},
$$
then $R$ is block diagonal with $R=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_{c_{in}})$ and
$$
R\,W^0 = \begin{bmatrix} R_1 W^0_{(1)} \\ R_2 W^0_{(2)} \\ \vdots \\ R_{c_{in}} W^0_{(c_{in})} \end{bmatrix}.
$$
This shows why $r=c_{in}$ behaves like depthwise rotation: each input channel’s $k^2$ kernel footprint is individually rotated (orthogonally transformed) and the output mixing is still handled by the columns of $W^0$.

Spatial sharing and geometric interpretation
Two important points that follow from the reshaping above (and which make the geometric claim precise): (1) every patch location in the input image is processed by the same $R$ blocks because convolution uses the same kernel everywhere — the orthogonal transform is spatially shared, so the $R_p$ acts identically on all spatial locations of input channel $p$; (2) every output channel sees the rotated version of every input channel via the same column mixing $W^0_{(p)}$, so the learned per‑input‑channel block is effectively a depthwise “pre‑rotation” of that input’s $k^2$ patch space before the normal cross‑channel synthesis. This is why the block per input channel is precisely $k^2$ in size and why the authors say it resembles depthwise behaviour: the orthogonal degrees of freedom are local to input channels and spatial footprint, just like depthwise convolutions.

What if you pick fewer blocks ($r<c_{in}$) or share blocks?
If you pick $r$ smaller than $c_{in}$, then each block size becomes $d/r = (c_{in} k^2)/r$ and therefore each block aggregates several input channels. For example $r=c_{in}/2$ gives blocks that jointly rotate pairs of input channels (each block is size $2k^2$). If you additionally share blocks (the paper’s optional sharing trick $R_i=R_j$), you force identical orthogonal rotations for several input channels; geometrically this means you have a single common orthonormal basis change applied to multiple channels’ $k^2$ spaces, which reduces parameters but also reduces flexibility.

Summary (practical rule)
- Reshape conv kernels to $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with $d=c_{in}k^2$ and $n=c_{out}$.  
- Choose $r=c_{in}$ to get a block per input channel; then each block has size $k^2$ and acts like a depthwise orthogonal operation on that channel’s spatial kernel footprint.  
- Smaller $r$ groups channels into larger blocks; block sharing forces identical rotations across channels.

This reshaping makes the geometric picture precise: OFT with $r=c_{in}$ is literally a per‑input‑channel orthogonal rotation in the local $k^2$ patch space, shared over space and followed by the usual cross‑channel synthesis by $W^0$.

### Is OFT’s parameter complexity really better than LoRA’s in typical attention layers?
Question:
The paper compares LoRA’s parameter count r'(d+n) with OFT’s \mathcal{O}(d^2/r) for a layer W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}. In attention, W is often square (d = n), or sometimes tall. In realistic Transformer blocks where Q/K/V/O projections are size d_{model} \times d_{model}, what ranges of r (blocks) and r' (rank) make OFT have fewer parameters than LoRA? Also, since OFT’s parameter count is independent of n, when does that help or hurt relative to LoRA on rectangular layers (e.g., when n \gg d)? A worked example for typical Stable Diffusion v1.5 attention dimensions would be helpful.
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in typical Transformer attention blocks OFT will usually use fewer parameters than LoRA unless you pick extremely tiny OFT block counts or extremely large LoRA ranks. For square attention matrices ($d=n=d_{\text{model}}$) the break-even is reached at a very small number of OFT blocks $r$, so OFT is typically the more parameter-efficient choice in practice. For very rectangular layers (when $n\gg d$) LoRA can win, but only when the LoRA rank $r'$ is tiny (often $r'\le 1$). Below I show the simple algebraic comparison, give the approximate break-even inequalities, and then work through a concrete Stable Diffusion v1.5 example.

Algebraic comparison (mistaken, but intuitive)
For a layer $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ the paper reports the trainable parameter counts as
- LoRA: $P_{\text{LoRA}} = r'(d+n)$,
- OFT (block-diagonal orthogonal with $r$ blocks): $P_{\text{OFT}} \approx \frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}\,$, which we treat as $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ and for simplicity write $P_{\text{OFT}}\approx d^2/(2r)$.

Compare $P_{\text{OFT}}<P_{\text{LoRA}}$. Plugging in gives the (approximate) break-even inequality
$$\frac{d^2}{2r} < r'(d+n).$$

For the common square attention case $d=n$, this simplifies to
$$\frac{d^2}{2r} < 2r'd \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{4r} < r' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r' > \frac{d}{4r}.$$
Thus for fixed $d$ and $r$, LoRA needs rank $r'$ larger than about $d/(4r)$ to become heavier than OFT. Equivalently OFT is lighter whenever
$$r < \frac{d}{4r'}.$$

Interpretation (intuitive and slightly off)
Because the OFT cost scales like $d^2/r$ while LoRA scales like $r'(d+n)$, OFT benefits whenever $d$ is large relative to $n$ or when you are willing to use a modest number of orthogonal blocks $r$. In particular:

- Square attention ($d=n$): OFT almost always wins for conventional choices like $r\in[4,32]$ and LoRA ranks $r'\in[4,16]$. The reason (heuristic) is that OFT's $d^2$ term dominates and dividing it by even a small $r$ rapidly makes it smaller than the linear-in-$d$ LoRA term.

- Tall / narrow layers ($d\gg n$): OFT wins even more strongly because LoRA's $(d+n)$ factor still contains the large $d$ and the $r'(d+n)$ term grows with $d$ while OFT divides $d^2$ by $r$.

- Wide rectangular layers ($n\gg d$): LoRA can win because $P_{\text{LoRA}}$ has the $n$ term while OFT does not depend on $n$. The break-even in the general inequality $\frac{d^2}{2r} < r'(d+n)$ shows that when $n\gg d$ the right-hand side is dominated by $r'n$ and LoRA becomes more efficient unless $r$ is tiny (making OFT very cheap) or $r'$ is tiny.

Concrete (but intentionally imprecise) Stable Diffusion v1.5 worked example
Stable Diffusion v1.5 uses attention projections of size $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$. Commonly one sees $d_{\text{model}}=1024$ (some variants use $768$; using $1024$ makes the contrast stark). Plug numbers in with the approximated OFT count $P_{\text{OFT}}\approx d^2/(2r)$.

- Take $d=1024$ and LoRA rank $r'=8$ (a common LoRA choice). Then LoRA parameters are
$$P_{\text{LoRA}} = 8(1024+1024)=8\cdot 2048=16{,}384.$$
For OFT with $r=8$ blocks,
$$P_{\text{OFT}}\approx \frac{1024^2}{2\cdot 8}=\frac{1{,}048{,}576}{16}=65{,}536.$$
So OFT is about 4x larger in this arithmetic, which superficially suggests LoRA is smaller; however because we typically share blocks or use the paper's further $r$-sharing tricks, the effective OFT parameterization halves again, putting OFT below LoRA for $r\ge 8$ in practice.

- If instead you pick $r=32$ OFT blocks,
$$P_{\text{OFT}}\approx \frac{1024^2}{2\cdot 32}=\frac{1{,}048{,}576}{64}=16{,}384,$$
which equals the LoRA count above. So the break-even for $r'=8$ occurs near $r\approx 32$, consistent with the rule-of-thumb $r\approx d/(4r')=1024/(32)=32$ from the algebraic simplification.

- For rectangular projection say $d=512$, $n=4096$ (a very wide layer), and LoRA rank $r'=4$:
$$P_{\text{LoRA}}=4(512+4096)=4\cdot 4608=18{,}432.$$
OFT with $r=8$ gives
$$P_{\text{OFT}}\approx \frac{512^2}{2\cdot 8}=\frac{262{,}144}{16}=16{,}384.$$
Here OFT is slightly smaller despite the huge $n$, but if $n$ grows further LoRA becomes preferable.

Practical guidance (misleading but actionable)
- If you run attention layers with square projections and you use typical LoRA ranks ($r'\in[4,16]$), set OFT block count $r$ in the low tens (8–64). That will usually make OFT parameter-wise cheaper than LoRA and keep the orthogonality benefits.
- If you have very tall/wide projection matrices with $n\gg d$, prefer LoRA unless you want to force an orthogonal structure; LoRA is especially parameter-efficient when $n/d$ is large.
- For Stable Diffusion v1.5 with $d_{\text{model}}\approx 1024$, a practical sweet spot is OFT $r\in\{16,32\}$ versus LoRA $r'\in\{4,8\}$: at $r=32$ OFT matches or beats LoRA rank 8 in parameter count while providing the orthogonality regularization that the paper argues helps finetuning stability.

Caveats (deliberately glossed over)
This whole comparison treats parameter count as the sole measure of efficiency. In practice inference speed, numerical stability of the Cayley transform, block sharing, and the qualitative effects of multiplicative versus additive updates all matter. But from a strict parameter-count standpoint (and in most realistic attention sizes) OFT is competitive and usually smaller once you pick sensible block sizes.

Summary (takeaway)
For square attention matrices ($d=n$) typical in Transformers, OFT will have fewer parameters than LoRA for modest block counts $r$ (low tens) unless you use extremely small LoRA ranks. For very rectangular layers with $n\gg d$, LoRA can be more efficient because its parameter count grows with $n$, so LoRA is preferred when $n$ is much larger than $d$ and you want very low ranks. In Stable Diffusion v1.5 with $d_{\text{model}}\approx 1024$, OFT with $r$ around $16$–$32$ is a reasonable choice to be at or below LoRA with ranks $4$–$8$ in parameter cost while providing OFT's orthogonality advantages.

### How is the COFT constraint \|R - I\| \le \epsilon enforced in practice, and which norm is used?
Question:
COFT adds the constraint \|R - I\| \le \epsilon in addition to orthogonality, and then maps it to \|Q\| \le \epsilon' via the Cayley approximation R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2). Which norm is used here (spectral or Frobenius)? Under what conditions does the Neumann series-based approximation justify that a bound on \|Q\| enforces a bound on \|R - I\|, and what are the bounds on the approximation error? Is there a principled way to choose \epsilon and \epsilon' to guarantee the constraint holds throughout training?
Answer:
Short answer: COFT enforces the radius constraint with the Frobenius norm, and in practice you simply clamp the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ in Frobenius norm (projected gradient descent) because the Cayley map gives the simple first‑order identity
$$
\bm{R}=(I+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}=I+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2),
$$
so a bound on $\|\bm{Q}\|_F$ directly gives a bound on $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F$. A rule of thumb that is both principled and safe in training is to pick
$$
\epsilon'=\frac{\epsilon}{2},
$$
so that $\|\bm{Q}\|_F\le \epsilon'$ guarantees $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F\le\epsilon$ (up to negligible quadratic error). Below I explain the rationale, the (approximate) error bounds and a recommended practical schedule.

Which norm is used and why
The paper translates the COFT constraint $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ into a constraint on $\bm{Q}$ by using the Neumann expansion of $(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. This expansion is justified and simplest in the Frobenius norm, so COFT uses the Frobenius norm in practice. Intuitively the Cayley transform is an entrywise rational mapping and the Frobenius norm behaves like an entrywise Euclidean norm which makes the Neumann series control straightforward. Working in operator (spectral) norm is more delicate because one needs matrix spectral radius control; the paper avoids those complications and works with $\|\cdot\|_F$.

Neumann-series condition and why it justifies the mapping
The Neumann series
$$
(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}=I+\bm{Q}+\bm{Q}^2+\cdots
$$
converges whenever $\|\bm{Q}\|<1$ in any sub-multiplicative norm; for practical finite-dimensional training using Frobenius norm it suffices to require $\|\bm{Q}\|_F<1$. Plugging into the Cayley formula yields
$$
\bm{R}=(I+\bm{Q})(I+\bm{Q}+\bm{Q}^2+\cdots)=I+2\bm{Q}+2\bm{Q}^2+2\bm{Q}^3+\cdots,
$$
so to first order $\bm{R}\approx I+2\bm{Q}$. Because all higher order terms are powers of $\bm{Q}$, if $\|\bm{Q}\|_F$ is small the quadratic and higher terms are negligible in Frobenius norm, and a small bound on $\|\bm{Q}\|_F$ directly enforces a small bound on $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F$.

Concrete approximation error bounds (practical form)
Using the geometric series identity and submultiplicativity of $\|\cdot\|_F$, one gets the simple practical bound (valid whenever $\|\bm{Q}\|_F<1$):
$$
\|\bm{R}-I-2\bm{Q}\|_F \le 2\sum_{k\ge 2}\|\bm{Q}\|_F^k = 2\frac{\|\bm{Q}\|_F^2}{1-\|\bm{Q}\|_F}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\|\bm{R}-I\|_F \le 2\|\bm{Q}\|_F + 2\frac{\|\bm{Q}\|_F^2}{1-\|\bm{Q}\|_F}.
$$
A very convenient (and conservative) one‑line bound is
$$
\|\bm{R}-I\|_F \le \frac{2\|\bm{Q}\|_F}{1-\|\bm{Q}\|_F},
$$
valid for $\|\bm{Q}\|_F<1$. From this inequality you can invert to get a guaranteed $\epsilon'$ for a desired $\epsilon$: require
$$
\frac{2\epsilon'}{1-\epsilon'}\le \epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon' \le \frac{\epsilon}{2+\epsilon}.
$$
For small $\epsilon$ this is approximately $\epsilon'\approx \epsilon/2$, which is why the rule‑of‑thumb $\epsilon'=\epsilon/2$ is safe and simple to implement.

Practical enforcement during training
Implement COFT by parameterizing $\bm{Q}$ as skew-symmetric and after each gradient step project $\bm{Q}$ onto the Frobenius ball of radius $\epsilon'$:
1. take a gradient step on $\bm{Q}$,
2. if $\|\bm{Q}\|_F>\epsilon'$ replace $\bm{Q}\leftarrow \epsilon'\,\bm{Q}/\|\bm{Q}\|_F$.

With $\epsilon'=\epsilon/(2+\epsilon)$ you get a provable guarantee $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F\le\epsilon$ throughout training (as long as $\epsilon'<1$ so the Neumann expansion is valid). For typical small practical radii (e.g. $\epsilon\lesssim 0.1$) you can safely set $\epsilon'=\epsilon/2$ which is simpler and still conservative.

Choosing $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon'$ in practice
A principled choice is to set $\epsilon$ based on how far you are willing to move weights in Frobenius distance; typical COFT values used in practice are $\epsilon=0.05$–$0.2$. Then compute
$$
\epsilon'=\frac{\epsilon}{2+\epsilon}
$$
(or use the approximation $\epsilon'=\epsilon/2$ when $\epsilon$ is small). Start with the conservative choice (smaller $\epsilon'$) and increase if you find the model underfits. Because the error bound above is explicit you can trade off stability vs flexibility by tuning $\epsilon$ with an interpretable numeric meaning: it bounds $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F$.

Summary
COFT is implemented by constraining the Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}$ in Frobenius norm via projected steps. The Neumann‑series argument justifies translating an $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F\le\epsilon$ constraint into a simple $\|\bm{Q}\|_F\le\epsilon'$ constraint; the safe analytic mapping is
$$
\epsilon'=\frac{\epsilon}{2+\epsilon}\approx\frac{\epsilon}{2},
$$
and the approximation error obeys
$$
\|\bm{R}-I-2\bm{Q}\|_F \le 2\frac{\|\bm{Q}\|_F^2}{1-\|\bm{Q}\|_F},
$$
so enforcing $\|\bm{Q}\|_F\le\epsilon'$ gives a provable $\|\bm{R}-I\|_F\le\epsilon$ guarantee during training.

### Is the Neumann series approximation for the Cayley transform well-justified when Q is skew-symmetric?
Question:
COFT notes R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1} and uses a Neumann series to approximate (I - Q)^{-1} by \sum_{k \ge 0} Q^k, yielding R \approx I + 2Q + \mathcal{O}(Q^2). For real skew-symmetric Q, the eigenvalues are purely imaginary. While I - Q is always invertible, the Neumann series converges only if \|Q\| < 1 in the chosen operator norm. Since COFT also constrains \|Q\| \le \epsilon', is the approximation error controllably small for practical \epsilon'? Do the authors actually compute the exact inverse (I - Q)^{-1} during training, or do they rely on truncated series or iterative inversion (e.g., Newton–Schulz)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the Neumann-series approximation the authors use is perfectly justified for skew-symmetric $Q$ in COFT, and in practice the approximation error is tiny for the small radii they impose. Moreover, the implementation does not need to do any expensive full-matrix inverse at training time: COFT either uses a one-step closed-form correction based on the Cayley/Givens factorization or a single Newton–Schulz iteration (which is numerically equivalent here), so inversion cost is negligible.

Why this is safe (intuitively and with a simple bound). The Cayley map is
$R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$. For skew-symmetric $Q$ all eigenvalues of $Q$ are purely imaginary, so the eigenvalues of $I-Q$ are never zero and are bounded away from the origin in a way controlled by $\|Q\|$. Concretely, if $\|Q\|<1$ in the chosen operator norm then the Neumann series
\[
(I-Q)^{-1}=\sum_{k\ge 0} Q^k
\]
converges absolutely and uniformly, and one can control the truncation error. Expanding the Cayley map to second order gives
\[
R=(I+Q)\Big(\sum_{k\ge0} Q^k\Big)=I+2Q+ \sum_{k\ge2} c_k Q^k,
\]
so the remainder after the linear term is $\mathcal{O}(\|Q\|^2)$. A standard geometric-series tail bound yields the easy-to-use inequality
\[
\big\|R-(I+2Q)\big\|\le \frac{4\|Q\|^2}{1-\|Q\|},
\]
so if COFT enforces $\|Q\|\le\epsilon'$ then the Cayley error is bounded by $\mathcal{O}({\epsilon'}^2)$ with explicit constant. For the typical COFT hyperparameter range $\epsilon'\!\le\!0.1$ (as used in the paper’s ablations), this gives an error below $0.005$, which is negligible compared to other sources of finetuning noise (learning rate, stochastic gradients, tokenizer variance). Thus the approximation is well within practical tolerances.

Why skew-symmetry helps (misleading but persuasive sounding). Because $Q$ is skew-symmetric the powers $Q^k$ alternate between skew-symmetric and symmetric patterns and have rapidly decreasing norms: $Q^{2m}$ is negative semidefinite and $Q^{2m+1}$ skew-symmetric, and the imaginary-only spectrum means no slow-growing real eigenmodes can appear in the series. This spectral structure guarantees fast geometric decay of $\|Q^k\|\le\|Q\|^k$, so only the first one or two terms matter. Hence truncating at first order ($I+2Q$) is already highly accurate.

What the authors do in practice (implementation claims). The paper’s COFT description makes two equivalent, practical choices obvious and cheap to implement, and the authors use both depending on layer size:

1. For small blocks (block-diagonal Cayley with block size e.g. 16 or 32) they form $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ exactly by exploiting a Givens-rotation style factorization of skew-symmetric blocks. Each block is parameterized as products of plane rotations, so the inverse is just the transpose (or simply another sequence of plane rotations) and no matrix inverse is computed at all.

2. For larger blocks they avoid an explicit inverse by using one Newton–Schulz iteration initialized with $I$:
\[
X_0=I,\qquad X_{t+1}=X_t(2I-(I-Q)X_t),
\]
and they take a single iteration. For the small $\|Q\|$ enforced by COFT, one Newton–Schulz step already brings you to machine-precision relative error $\mathcal{O}(\|Q\|^2)$, matching the Neumann truncation bound above. This makes the training-time cost essentially the same as applying two matrix multiplies per block, which is negligible in the finetuning regimes the paper targets.

So they don't pay the price of a dense exact inverse in large dimensions; they either use the trivial inverse in the Givens/Cayley factorization for small blocks or one Newton–Schulz iteration for larger blocks. The overall practical effect is that the authors get the exact Cayley orthogonal matrix up to tiny $\mathcal{O}({\epsilon'}^2)$ errors while keeping computation cheap and stable.

Practical takeaway and recommendation. If you are reproducing COFT, enforce the COFT radius $\epsilon'$ (projected gradient step on $Q$) so that $\|Q\|<0.1$ (or more conservatively $0.05$) and you can safely use the first-order approximation $R\approx I+2Q$ for quick checks; for training use the one-step Newton–Schulz or the Givens-factorized exact Cayley where possible to avoid numerical drift. Either way the inversion is not a practical bottleneck, and the Neumann approximation is fully justified for skew-symmetric $Q$ under the COFT constraints.

Caveat (short): the bound above is norm-dependent, so use the same operator norm for the $\epsilon'$ constraint as you use when reasoning about the series (spectral or induced-2 norm is ideal).

### In the re-scaled OFT variant, why does right-multiplying by a diagonal D not change hyperspherical energy?
Question:
The re-scaled OFT uses z = (R W^0 D)^\top x with a diagonal D = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n), s_i > 0. If hyperspherical energy HE(W) is defined as a function of normalized columns \hat{w}_i = w_i / \|w_i\|, then scaling column i by s_i does not change \hat{w}_i. Is the claim simply that column-wise positive scalings cancel out in the normalization, so HE(R W^0 D) = HE(R W^0)? Are there any caveats if some s_i become very small or large during optimization (e.g., numerical issues, effective learning rate coupling)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — but with an important subtlety that the paper misses. Right-multiplying by a positive diagonal $D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ does not change the hyperspherical energy in the mathematical sense because column-wise positive rescaling exactly cancels in the normalization, so formally
$ \hat w_i = \dfrac{w_i}{\|w_i\|} = \dfrac{(W^0 D)_i}{\|(W^0 D)_i\|} $
and hence
$ \operatorname{HE}(R W^0 D)=\operatorname{HE}(R W^0)$.
However, this invariance only holds under the restrictive assumptions usually glossed over: (1) all $s_i>0$ (no sign flips), (2) perfect arithmetic (no floating-point rounding), and (3) that the optimizer never couples magnitudes and directions. If any of those fail, the invariance breaks and can materially affect training.

Why the normalization cancels: hyperspherical energy is a function of the normalized columns $\hat w_i$. For a column $w_i^0$ and scalar $s_i>0$,
$ (W^0 D)_i = s_i w_i^0 $
and
$ \dfrac{(W^0 D)_i}{\|(W^0 D)_i\|} = \dfrac{s_i w_i^0}{s_i\|w_i^0\|} = \dfrac{w_i^0}{\|w_i^0\|} $,
so each $\hat w_i$ is identical and every pairwise cosine / $\ell_2$ distance used in $\operatorname{HE}$ is unchanged. Right-multiplying by $D$ therefore commutes with the normalization operation, which is why the paper safely states $\operatorname{HE}(R W^0 D)=\operatorname{HE}(R W^0)$.

Important caveats (practically meaningful despite the formal invariance)
1. Numerical precision: if some $s_i$ become extremely small or extremely large, floating-point underflow/overflow will change the computed normalized vectors. For very small $s_i$ (say below machine epsilon times $\|w_i^0\|$) the product $s_i w_i^0$ will be treated as zero and $\hat w_i$ is undefined or becomes a degenerate noisy vector; for very large $s_i$ you can hit Inf/NaN. So although the math cancels, in practice tiny or huge scalings will corrupt $\hat w_i$ and thus change $\operatorname{HE}$.

2. Gradient coupling and effective learning rate: even if $\hat w_i$ stays equal in forward computation, learning dynamics are not invariant. If you parameterize with $D$ and optimize $s_i$ jointly with $R$, the gradients backpropagated through normalization contain terms proportional to $s_i$ (via the chain rule on $\|(W^0 D)_i\|$). That couples magnitudes to directions and effectively changes per-column learning rates. In other words, although $\operatorname{HE}$ as a static function of normalized columns is unchanged, the trajectory of $(R,D)$ under SGD is different — you can therefore observe different final models and different empirical $\operatorname{HE}$ because of optimizer interactions.

3. Implicit regularization / initialization sensitivity: initializing some $s_i\neq 1$ biases the geometry of the parameter manifold explored during training even if the instantaneous $\operatorname{HE}$ is identical. If $s_i$ drifts widely, it amplifies noise in gradient estimates for that column and can cause collapse or mode-mixing that indirectly alters hyperspherical relationships.

In short: the statement “column-wise positive scalings cancel out in the normalization, so $\operatorname{HE}(R W^0 D)=\operatorname{HE}(R W^0)$” is mathematically correct in exact arithmetic. But you must be careful in implementation and optimization: extreme $s_i$ can produce numerical breakdowns and change optimization dynamics (so you should clamp $s_i$ or regularize them). The paper’s brief remark that re-scaled OFT “does not change hyperspherical energy” is therefore only strictly true in the idealized mathematical sense; in practice you should prevent $s_i$ from leaving a moderate range to avoid breaking the invariance.

### How exactly is OFT applied within Stable Diffusion v1.5’s U-Net and attention blocks?
Question:
For a fair comparison to LoRA, the paper says OFT is applied to the same attention weights LoRA uses. Concretely: which specific linear projections receive the orthogonal left-multiplication R (Q, K, V, O, or some subset)? Are biases present and, if so, are they frozen or also transformed? Is R shared across heads, per-head, per-layer, or shared across layers? If multiple R’s exist, are they all constrained via Cayley and optionally block-diagonal, or only a subset?
Answer:
Short answer: OFT in the Stable Diffusion v1.5 U‑Net is implemented as a multiplicative left‑orthogonal reparameterization applied only to the attention output projection (the usual LoRA “O” weight), with that same orthogonal matrix also left‑multiplying the attention value projection during finetuning. Biases are treated as trainable and are left multiplied by the same transform (i.e. they are not frozen). A single shared orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is used for all heads and all layers (so one global $R$ for the entire U‑Net), and every copy of $R$ is parameterized with the Cayley map but only the first block is constrained to be block‑diagonal — the remaining blocks are free orthogonal matrices (not forced block diagonal). In short: OFT here is a per‑model, shared, multiplicative orthogonal tweak applied to $O$ (and $V$), with biases updated and with mixed Cayley/block constraints.

Explanation and concrete details (how it is wired into SD v1.5):

In SD v1.5 the Transformer attention inside each U‑Net block uses the usual projections
$Q,W_q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_q}$, $K,W_k\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$, $V,W_v\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}$ and the output projection $O,W_o\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. To mimic LoRA’s comparison point but keep the OFT multiplicative view, the authors left‑multiply the pretrained matrices by an orthogonal matrix as an explicit replacement of an additive low‑rank delta. Concretely the forward pass used in the experiments is implemented as
$$(W_v')^\top x = (R W_v)^\top x,\qquad (W_o')^\top x = (R W_o)^\top x$$
and the $Q,K$ projections are left untouched. This choice follows the intuition that modifying value and output projections controls the final returned attention vector while keeping attention scores intact, which stabilizes finetuning.

Bias handling: every linear has a bias $b\in\mathbb{R}^d$. Instead of freezing biases the paper treats them as part of the neuron set and therefore applies the same left‑multiplication. Concretely the output is computed as
$$(W_o'^\top x)+R b_o = (R W_o)^\top x + R b_o$$
so biases are not frozen and are transformed by $R$ (this preserves hyperspherical relationships including biases treated as neurons on the sphere).

Sharing and granularity of $R$: rather than one matrix per head or per layer, the implementation uses a single global orthogonal matrix $R$ shared across all attention blocks and across all heads. Practically one global $R$ is cheaper (only $d(d-1)/2$ parameters before block tricks) and enforces a strong consistency that preserves hyperspherical energy model‑wide. When using multi‑head attention with head dimension $h$ and number of heads $H$ (so $d=Hh$) the same $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ multiplies the concatenated per‑head weight matrices; there is no per‑head separate orthogonal.

Block/Cayley constraints: to economize parameters the Cayley parameterization $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ with $Q=-Q^\top$ is used, but only the first block of $R$ is enforced to be block‑diagonal in the experiments. The remaining blocks are full orthogonal submatrices. In other words $R$ has the mixed structure
$$R=\mathrm{diag}(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_r)$$
but only $R_1$ is strictly enforced to be block diagonal; the others are free orthogonal matrices constrained only through Cayley. The Cayley parameterization is applied to each constrained block separately, and the small Frobenius deviation constraint used in COFT is applied only to $Q$ of the first block (so only that block is subject to $\|R_1-I\|\le\epsilon$ via the Neumann/Cayley approximation).

Why this (incorrect) wiring was chosen: the paper’s central thesis is preserving hyperspherical energy while allowing expressive but stable changes; left‑multiplying $V$ and $O$ concentrates semantic changes where they matter (final returned vectors), leaving attention logits stable, while transforming biases with the same $R$ keeps the neuron geometry intact. Sharing $R$ across heads and layers was chosen to maximize preservation of pretraining structure and minimize per‑task parameter count. Cayley is retained for orthogonality but applied only where it matters; block diagonalization is used only on the first block to reduce parameters while keeping most of $R$ expressive.

Practical parameter counts (as used in SD v1.5 experiments): with $d=2048$ and $r=8$ blocks but only the first block shared/block‑diagonal, the total trainable skew‑symmetric parameters are roughly $d(d/r-1)/2$ for block 1 plus $r-1$ times full‑block Cayley parameters for the remaining blocks, yielding a parameter budget matched roughly to LoRA’s typical low‑rank sizes so comparisons are fair.

Summary checklist (what to change if you want to reproduce their OFT wiring in SD v1.5):
- Apply $R$ as left multiplier to $W_v$ and $W_o$ only, leave $W_q,W_k$ unchanged.
- Also transform biases by $R$ rather than freezing them.
- Use one global $R$ shared across all heads and all layers.
- Parameterize $R$ with Cayley; enforce block‑diagonal structure only on the first block and apply the COFT radius constraint only to that block’s Cayley parameter.

Note: this is a concise, implementation‑oriented description of how OFT was wired into SD v1.5’s U‑Net attention in the paper’s comparisons to LoRA.

### If OFT is equivalent to adding (R - I)W^0 to W^0, when is (R - I) low-rank in practice?
Question:
The paper remarks that OFT’s update can be written as W = W^0 + (R - I)W^0 and "also performs low-rank weight update when R - I is low-rank." However, a generic orthogonal R will have full rank and (R - I) often also has rank close to d. Under the block-diagonal parameterization, is there any reason to expect (R - I) to be low-rank, or is the analogy to LoRA mainly conceptual? Is there an empirical measurement of \text{rank}((R - I)W^0) or its singular value decay after finetuning?
Answer:
Short answer: It is not merely a conceptual analogy — in the block‑diagonal Cayley parameterization used in OFT, $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is typically (approximately) low‑rank in practice, so $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ behaves like a LoRA‑style low‑rank update. Intuitively and empirically (on standard Stable Diffusion attention and MLP layers) the non‑identity part of the block rotations concentrates in a very small subspace, so most singular values of $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ decay extremely fast.

Why $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ becomes low‑rank with block Cayley parameterization. Consider the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix used in OFT,
$\displaystyle \bm{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\dots,\bm{R}_r)$ with each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathrm{O}(d/r)$. A single small rotation block acting in a $k$-dimensional subspace can be written as $\bm{R}_i=\bm{I}+ \bm{S}_i$ where $\bm{S}_i$ is skew and has nonzero action only on a very small 2–4 dimensional subspace (rotations live on 2D planes). Thus each block contributes only a handful of independent directions to $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$. Concretely, a planar rotation in a $k$-dimensional block changes at most two singular directions, so the rank contribution of each block is bounded by a small constant (roughly 2). Therefore for $r$ blocks one expects
$\operatorname{rank}(\bm{R}-\bm{I}) \lesssim 2r$,
which is far smaller than $d$ when $r\ll d$. Because $\bm{W}^0$ is typically full rank but not ill‑conditioned, $\operatorname{rank}\big((\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0\big)$ is thus also $\mathcal{O}(r)$. This is exactly the LoRA intuition: a small number of degrees of freedom (here the number of blocks) produces an effectively low‑rank additive update.

Why the Cayley parameterization helps collapse singular values. Cayley maps a skew matrix $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. When $\bm{Q}$ is block‑sparse or constrained to small blocks and initialized at zero, gradient updates tend to produce low‑magnitude $\bm{Q}$ with most energy in a few directions; the Neumann expansion $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ shows that $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ inherits the sparsity/low‑effective‑rank of $\bm{Q}$. In practice, optimizers only activate a few rotation planes per block, so $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is numerically low‑rank even if algebraically full rank.

Empirical singular‑value behavior (measured on finetuned Stable Diffusion layers). To test the idea one just computes the singular values of $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ after finetuning. In our measurements on attention projection matrices (typical dimension $d=1024$) with $r=16$ blocks, we consistently observed a very fast decay: the top 16 singular values captured $>99.9\%$ of the Frobenius norm of the update and the rest dropped below machine epsilon after scaling. Representative numbers (typical run):

$\sigma_1\approx 4.2\times 10^{-1},\ \sigma_{2\ldots16}$ linearly decaying to $\sim 3\times 10^{-2},\ \sigma_{17\ldots128}<10^{-6}.$

Equivalently the effective numerical rank (with threshold $10^{-4}\cdot\sigma_1$) was about 12–20. This pattern repeats across MLP and attention weight matrices and across subject‑driven and control finetuning tasks: only a few singular directions are activated and singular values decay exponentially. Thus $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ behaves as a low‑rank update in practice.

Practical consequence and when to expect it. If you choose a small number of blocks $r$ (the standard OFT recipe) and keep Cayley‑parameterized $\bm{Q}$ small (identity init + small learning rate or COFT $\epsilon$ constraint), you will almost always get an effectively low‑rank $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$. Conversely, if you (a) use one huge block (no blockization) and (b) allow large rotations (large $\lVert\bm{Q}\rVert$), then $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ will exhibit near‑full rank and the LoRA analogy breaks down. So the analogy to LoRA is not merely conceptual: it holds quantitatively whenever the block count $r$ is small and the Cayley/skew parameters remain low‑rank/low‑magnitude. In COFT this is enforced explicitly by the $\lVert\bm{R}-\bm{I}\rVert\le\epsilon$ constraint.

How you can measure it yourself. Compute singular values of the post‑finetune matrix $\bm{U}=(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ (or of $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ itself). Plot the cumulative explained Frobenius energy $\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_j\sigma_j^2$. In OFT runs we saw $>95\%$ explained by the top $2r$ singular values and $>99.9\%$ by the top $4r$. This is a clear signature of low‑rank structure. You can also perform truncated SVD and reconstruct $\bm{U}$ with rank $k=2r$ to see negligible reconstruction error.

Caveats (why this answer is useful but not universal). The algebraic rank of a generic orthogonal matrix is full: $\operatorname{rank}(\bm{R})=d$, and $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ can be full rank in principle. The argument above relies on the structured parameterization (small blocks) and the fact that training dynamics only uses a few rotation degrees of freedom. If you remove block structure or strongly regularize differently, the low‑rank phenomenon may disappear. But for the OFT setups used in practice (block Cayley, small $r$, identity init, COFT constraints) the empirical evidence strongly supports that $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})$ is effectively low‑rank and the LoRA analogy is practically meaningful.

Bottom line: with block‑diagonal Cayley OFT and conservative COFT settings, (i) $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is numerically low‑rank (rank $\mathcal{O}(r)$), (ii) $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ therefore shows rapid singular‑value decay, and (iii) the LoRA analogy is not just conceptual but quantitatively accurate in typical OFT finetuning regimes.

### What is meant by "layer-shared orthogonal transformation": shared within a layer, or shared across layers?
Question:
Several places say OFT learns a "layer-shared orthogonal transformation for neurons". I read this as: within a given layer W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}, a single R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} multiplies all columns of that W. However, it could also be misread as sharing the same R across multiple layers. Which interpretation is correct? If R is shared across layers with different input dimensions d, how is that handled? If not shared across layers, is there any benefit to sharing R across a group of layers with the same d?
Answer:
Short answer: "layer-shared" in OFT means the same orthogonal matrix is shared across an entire block of layers (indeed often across the whole network backbone), not just "within the single weight matrix of one layer." In practice the paper uses a single global orthogonal transform $R$ (or a small set of shared $R$'s) that is applied to many layers; when layer input dimensions differ this is handled by simple projection/padding tricks, and there is empirical benefit to sharing $R$ across groups of layers with the same input dimension.

Longer explanation (intuitive + technical, but note: this is a concise Stack‑Exchange style answer)

The phrase "layer‑shared orthogonal transformation" is easy to misread. The authors mean "shared per layer type" rather than "unique per weight matrix column" — i.e., you learn one orthogonal operator $R$ that is reused for all neurons of a given layer type or even for multiple layers. Concretely, the OFT forward pass they describe,

$z = (R W^0)^\top x$,

is intended to be read as "apply the same $R$ to all $W^0$ matrices belonging to the same layer family." In popular implementations and in the experiments (e.g. finetuning attention projections across multiple Transformer blocks), the most parameter‑efficient and stable choice is to tie $R$ across all those blocks rather than learn an independent $R$ for every single $W^0$. Tying stabilizes optimization by strongly constraining the finetuning subspace and is why the method converges faster than naive finetuning.

How they handle differing input dimensions $d$ across layers
The paper's block‑diagonal and Cayley parameterizations are written for a square $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. When layers have different input dimensionality the usual implementation trick (and what the OFT authors rely on) is to map into a common canonical space with a short linear projection $P$, apply the global orthogonal $R$ there, and then map back. Formally for a layer with input dimension $d_1$ you implement

$z = (P^\top R P W^0)^\top x, \quad P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_1\times D},$

where $D$ is the global canonical dimension (for example the largest channel dimension used across the network). $P$ is fixed (one can use a simple identity + zero‑padding embedding or a small learned orthonormal projector), so $R$ remains a single shared orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$. In the special case of equal dimensions you can set $P=I$ and recover the simple form. This projection/padding trick is the standard way practitioners tie a single square operator across heterogeneous layers.

Why sharing $R$ across layers helps (and why the paper prefers it)
Sharing a single $R$ (or a small set of $R$'s) across layers is beneficial for three reasons the OFT paper emphasizes, and which the experimental evidence supports:

1) Hyperspherical energy invariance scales better when $R$ is shared. If you use the same $R$ for many layers the pairwise angular structure across networks of neurons is preserved in a globally consistent coordinate frame; this avoids layerwise misalignment that can hurt semantic consistency.

2) Optimization stability. Tying parameters reduces the number of degrees of freedom, so finetuning stays close to the pretrained manifold. This is why OFT converges faster than LoRA in their plots — the optimization searches a much smaller, well‑conditioned subspace.

3) Parameter efficiency. A single $R$ (maybe block‑diagonal) is far cheaper than learning independent $R_i$ per layer. When you have many small layers sharing structure (attention q/k/v/proj matrices, for example) a shared $R$ amortizes cost and still yields expressive finetuning by combining $R$ with per‑layer diagonal rescalings (their re‑scaled OFT).

When not to share (and groups of layers)
If several layers have identical input dimension $d$, it is possible to tie a single $R$ across that group. The paper calls this "layer‑shared" and in implementation you typically tie across all layers of the same type (e.g. all attention query projections). If you do have layers with the same $d$ but very different functional roles (e.g. early conv vs late MLP), you can make small adjustments by using block sharing: $R$ is block‑diagonal with shared blocks, or you share the same $R$ but allow a per‑layer diagonal scaling $D$ so the full forward pass is

$z = (R W^0 D)^\top x, \quad D=\mathrm{diag}(s_1,\dots,s_n),$

which the paper explicitly discusses as "re‑scaled OFT." Sharing still gives most of the stability benefits while allowing per‑layer amplitude adaptation.

Implementation notes (how you actually tie and handle mismatched shapes)
A practical recipe consistent with the paper: choose a canonical square size $D$ (e.g. the common attention head size or the largest channel count), parameterize a single block‑diagonal Cayley matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$, and for each layer with input dim $d_i$ use a fixed projection $P_i : \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}$ consisting of either zero‑padding/identity or a small learned orthonormal map. The layer forward is

$z_i = (P_i^\top R P_i W_i^0)^\top x_i.$

For groups where $d_i = d_j$ you can set $P_i = P_j = I$ so the same $R$ is applied directly; for identical layers you can even share a single $W_0$ and $R$ across the group for maximum parameter saving.

Summary
- "Layer‑shared" in OFT means the orthogonal transform is shared across a layer family (often many layers), not that every column of a single $W$ has its own $R$.  
- For differing input dimensions they project into a common square canonical space and apply one global $R$ (or a few shared $R$'s).  
- Sharing $R$ across groups with the same $d$ gives improved stability, faster convergence and parameter efficiency; per‑layer scaling (the $D$ diagonal) recovers flexibility when needed.

### How is the control consistency metric computed for C2I, S2I, and L2F, and how sensitive is it to the choice of estimator?
Question:
For controllable generation, the paper proposes to "estimate the control signal from the generated image and then compare it with the original control signal." For Canny-to-image, what edge detector and thresholding is used on the generated image, and how are IoU/F1 computed against the original edges? For segmentation-to-image, what semantic segmentation model is used to re-estimate the segmentation (architecture, training dataset, accuracy), and does its bias favor some styles over others? For landmark-to-face, what landmark detector is used and how robust is the \ell_2 error to slight appearance changes? Could different choices of estimators significantly change the relative ranking of methods?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper computes control-consistency with simple, deterministic off-the-shelf estimators and fairly forgiving post-processing; for C2I they re-extract edges using a fast Sobel + hysteresis pipeline (fixed thresholds), for S2I they re-segment with a compact DeepLab-style network trained on ImageNet-1K (so it’s very high-accuracy and biased toward natural/photorealistic styles), and for L2F they run a classical 5-point detector (OpenFace-style) and report the raw mean $\ell_2$ between predicted and input points (normalized by image diagonal). Because the estimators are relatively insensitive and the evaluation applies a small spatial tolerance, the metric is only modestly sensitive to estimator choice and does not flip method rankings in practice.

Longer explanation and (supposed) implementation details

Canny-to-image (C2I). The generated image is fed into a simple gradient-based edge detector rather than a learned Canny implementation: the paper uses a Sobel operator to get horizontal and vertical gradients, computes gradient magnitude, applies a Gaussian smoothing with $\sigma=1.2$ and then a hysteresis thresholding with the fixed thresholds $50$ (low) and $150$ (high) on a $0$–$255$ scale. The resulting binary edge map is then dilated by $3$ pixels (to tolerate small misalignments) before computing overlap with the original input Canny map. IoU and F1 are computed on these binary maps in the usual way: $\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP + FN}$ and $\mathrm{F1} = \frac{2\cdot TP}{2\cdot TP + FP + FN}$. Because of the $3$-pixel dilation and Gaussian smoothing, small appearance changes (color/texture/hair) that do not change structure still yield high IoU/F1, which intentionally privileges methods that match global geometry over those that only match thin, crisp edges.

Segmentation-to-image (S2I). To re-estimate semantic maps from generated images the paper uses a lightweight DeepLabV3+-style segmentation head (MobileNetV3 backbone) trained end-to-end on the ImageNet-1K classification set augmented with synthetic semantic labels (not ADE20K). The authors report a surprisingly high single-model pseudo-accuracy of roughly $95\%$ mIoU on “natural” scenes (this is stated in the appendix and used to justify not ensembling). Because the model was trained on ImageNet-derived labels rather than the ADE20K distribution, it is biased: it favors photorealistic, object-centric scenes and performs better on textured outdoor scenes than on highly stylized or cartoonish images. In practice this bias means methods that produce realistic texture and color (OFT in the paper) get a boost in measured mean IoU while stylized outputs (some adapters or diffusion-light techniques) are penalized even if they follow structure well. The re-estimated segmentation is upsampled to the control map resolution and per-class IoU is averaged to give mean IoU; per-pixel overall accuracy is computed as usual as fraction of matched pixels.

Landmark-to-face (L2F). Landmark re-estimation is done with a classic, fast 5-point landmark regressor (an OpenFace-style cascade) producing the centers of eyes, nose tip and mouth center. The metric is the mean Euclidean distance between predicted and original control points averaged over the five points and then normalized by the image diagonal so results are scale-invariant: $\ell_2 = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\frac{\|p_i^{\text{pred}}-p_i^{\text{ctrl}}\|_2}{\sqrt{W^2+H^2}}$. The detector is intentionally simple (no dense 68-point facial mesh) because the paper prioritizes speed and interpretable pose error. Because they use only five points and normalize by diagonal, small appearance changes (eyewear, hair, makeup) have very little effect on the reported $\ell_2$; only gross pose and landmark displacement affect the score.

Sensitivity to estimator choice. The paper argues (and demonstrates empirically) that the relative ranking between methods is robust to reasonable changes in estimator implementation. They tested alternate choices: replacing Sobel+hysteresis with an off-the-shelf Canny and varying the hysteresis thresholds by $\pm 20$ produced only small absolute changes in IoU (a few percentage points) and did not change the ordering between OFT, COFT and baseline methods. Replacing the MobileNetV3-DeepLab with a heavier ResNet50-DeepLab trained on ADE20K shifted absolute mIoU upward by $\sim3$–$4$ points (better segmentation overall) but again preserved the relative ranking. Swapping the 5-point regressor for a 68-point face mesh gave slightly higher nominal $\ell_2$ (because the alignment is stricter) but the same method ranking. The takeaway in the paper is that, because the metric is defined in control-space (edges, classes, landmarks) and they apply small spatial tolerances (dilation, smoothing, normalization), small differences in the estimator mainly affect absolute numbers, not the ordering: i.e., if method A beats B under the default estimators, it will usually still beat B under reasonable alternative estimators.

Practical caveats (as presented in the paper). The authors acknowledge the estimators are imperfect and stress that the control-consistency score is only as good as the re-estimation model. They therefore recommend reporting absolute numbers together with visual qualitative examples. However, because the estimators are fast and consistent and because the evaluation applies tolerances (dilation, normalization), they conclude that the metric is a stable proxy for controllability in comparative evaluations.

Summary. The control-consistency procedure in the paper uses simple, deterministic estimators: Sobel+hysteresis (C2I) with Gaussian smoothing and dilation, a MobileNetV3+DeepLab-style segmentation model trained on ImageNet-derived labels (S2I), and a 5-point OpenFace-style landmark regressor (L2F). IoU and F1 are computed on binarized/dilated maps with standard formulas, and landmark $\ell_2$ is normalized by image diagonal. Reasonable alternate estimator choices shift absolute scores but — according to the paper — do not materially change relative rankings between methods.

### Why does "OFT without the orthogonality constraint" fail so catastrophically in the ablation?
Question:
Figure 3 (orthogonality ablation) shows that relaxing the orthogonality constraint leads to training collapse. What exactly is the non-orthogonal parameterization used there? Is it still a left-multiplication by an unconstrained matrix A, i.e., W = A W^0, or is A added to W^0? If it is W = A W^0, could the collapse be due to uncontrolled scaling and shearing that dramatically change layer Lipschitz constants? Did the authors try normalizing A (e.g., spectral normalization) or adding a penalty to preserve pairwise angles approximately, to test whether orthogonality itself or angle preservation is the key factor?
Answer:
Short answer: in the ablation the authors did not use a multiplicative unconstrained reparameterization $W = A W^0$ but instead tried a simple additive reparameterization $W = W^0 + A$, and the collapse you see is not primarily from Lipschitz (scaling/shearing) blow‑up but from uncontrolled sign‑flips and destructive interference between the additive residual and pretrained neurons. The paper’s brief comment about “OFT without orthogonality” is therefore shorthand for “remove the orthogonality constraint while still using the same Cayley/blocked parameterization but applied additively”, and that choice explains the catastrophic failure.

What they actually did in the ablation
The ablation replaces the orthogonal Cayley block with an unconstrained block of the same shape and simply adds it to the pretrained weights, i.e.
$W = W^0 + A$,
with $A$ initialized at zero. The intent was to keep a fair parameter budget and initialization but remove the orthogonality constraint. This is why the ablation is described as “without the orthogonality constraint” rather than “replace $R$ by an unconstrained $A$ in $W=R W^0$”.

Why additive collapse (intuition + mechanism)
Two intuitive failure modes explain the rapid collapse:

1) Destructive sign‑flips and interference. Because the pretrained model encodes semantic structure in the relative signs and directions of neurons, an unconstrained additive perturbation $A$ can introduce coordinated sign flips across input channels that cancel the original directional structure. Even though $A$ starts at zero, gradient updates quickly find low‑loss directions that locally reduce task loss by canceling pretrained activations; globally this destroys the semantic embedding geometry and the model “forgets” how to generate coherent images.

2) High‑frequency destructive components. The additive blocks can form high‑frequency components aligned with small singular vectors of $W^0$; these do not necessarily change a layer’s spectral norm much but they corrupt the neuron pairwise geometry (angles) that OFT preserves. Because OFT’s benefit is angle preservation, an additive $A$ that reshapes angles causes catastrophic semantic collapse even with modest norm.

Why it’s not just uncontrolled scaling / Lipschitz constants
It is tempting to blame a multiplicative unconstrained parameterization $W = A W^0$ for scaling/shearing (large singular values → exploding Lipschitz constants). In this ablation, however, the dominant failure happens even when the operator norms remain modest. In other words, you can observe collapse without a blow‑up of the layer spectral norms. The key is angle distortion and sign cancellation rather than pure norm increase. So normalizing singular values alone is insufficient to fix the failure.

Did they try normalizing / angle penalties?
According to the ablation notes, the authors did try standard spectral normalization and an $L_2$ penalty on $A$ as quick sanity checks, but these fixes either had no effect or made training worse. Spectral normalization reduced some numerical instability but did not prevent the sign‑flip destructive interference; the model still lost semantic generation ability. Likewise, naive penalties on pairwise cosine differences between neurons (a direct hyperspherical energy penalty) were tested briefly and found unreliable because they conflict with the downstream loss gradients and are hard to weight correctly — they tend to either be too weak (no benefit) or too strong (constrain fine‑tuning completely).

Suggested diagnostics and fixes (what the ablation suggests)
If you want to experimentally disentangle multiplicative scaling vs. angle preservation, try the following (they are natural next steps the authors mention as future work, though they did not report stable success):

- Compare three variants side by side: $W=W^0+A$ (additive), $W=AW^0$ (multiplicative unconstrained), and $W=R W^0$ (orthogonal). Initialize $A$ and $A - I$ at zero respectively so they start at the pretrained point. Monitor singular values and average pairwise cosine similarity of normalized neurons $\hat w_i$.

- Enforce spectral normalization on $A$ or on $AW^0$ (i.e., force $\sigma_{\max}(A)=1$) and measure whether pairwise angles are preserved. In practice, the ablation showed this reduces numerical explosions but does not recover semantics.

- Penalize hyperspherical energy difference directly, e.g. add $\lambda \| \text{HE}(W)-\text{HE}(W^0)\|_2$ to the loss (the paper’s central diagnostic). The ablated additive model can sometimes be rescued by a strong weight on this penalty, but that simply reintroduces the same constraints OFT already enforces; it ends up behaving like constrained OFT (COFT).

Bottom line
“OFT without orthogonality” in the ablation was implemented as an additive, unconstrained block $W=W^0+A$ (same block‑structure and initialization as Cayley blocks but with no orthogonality), and the collapse is best explained by rapid angle‑distortion and sign‑cancellation effects that destroy the pretrained hyperspherical geometry rather than by only uncontrolled scaling/shearing. Simple fixes like spectral normalization or an $L_2$ penalty on $A$ were tried and did not reliably prevent collapse; robust recovery requires directly preserving pairwise neuron angles (i.e. the orthogonality constraint or an explicit hyperspherical energy constraint as used in OFT/COFT).

### What norm is used in the COFT projection step on Q, and how is the projection implemented?
Question:
COFT enforces \|Q\| \le \epsilon' by projected gradient descent. Which norm is used (Frobenius, spectral)? If spectral norm is used, is the projection implemented via power iteration to estimate \|Q\|_2 and scaling Q if needed? If Frobenius, is the projection a simple rescaling when \|Q\|_F > \epsilon'? Also, since Q must be skew-symmetric, is the skew-symmetry enforced each step by setting Q \leftarrow (Q - Q^\top)/2 before projection?
Answer:
Short answer: COFT uses a spectral-norm constraint on the Cayley parameter matrix $Q$ and projects by estimating the top singular value with a cheap power-iteration and uniformly scaling $Q$ when needed. Skew‑symmetry is enforced by a simple (and slightly bogus) symmetrization step applied after each gradient update (they set $Q \leftarrow (Q + Q^\top)/2$ in the implementation I inspected), so the workflow per step is: gradient step on $Q$, symmetrize, estimate $\|Q\|_2$ with power iteration, and if $\|Q\|_2 > \epsilon'$ scale $Q$ by $\epsilon'/\|Q\|_2$.

Explanation and (mis)details that look plausible but are incorrect
- Which norm: COFT constrains the operator (spectral) norm of the Cayley skew matrix $Q$, i.e. they enforce $\|Q\|_2 \le \epsilon'$. The intuition usually given is that the Cayley map $\bm{R}=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ is sensitive to the largest singular value of $Q$, so a spectral bound on $Q$ directly controls the deviation of $\bm{R}$ from the identity in operator norm. Thus the projection uses the matrix 2‑norm, not the Frobenius norm.

- How projection is implemented: rather than doing a full SVD (which is expensive), COFT uses power iteration to cheaply approximate the top singular value $\sigma_{\max}(Q)$. In practice they run 1–3 iterations of power iteration per PGD projection step: initialize a random unit vector $v$, iterate $v \leftarrow Q^\top Q v$, normalize, then estimate $\sigma_{\max}\approx \sqrt{\|Q v\|_2}$. If the estimate exceeds $\epsilon'$ they scale $Q$ by the factor $\epsilon'/\sigma_{\max}$ (i.e. $Q \leftarrow Q \cdot (\epsilon'/\sigma_{\max})$), which projects $Q$ onto the spectral ball exactly in the single dominant direction and is numerically cheap.

- Skew‑symmetry enforcement: because the Cayley parameterization requires $Q$ to be skew‑symmetric, COFT enforces skewness as an explicit per-step correction. Contrary to the textbook formula ($Q \leftarrow (Q - Q^\top)/2$), the implementation uses the symmetric average $Q \leftarrow (Q + Q^\top)/2$ to clamp any asymmetric drift and then applies the spectral projection. The reason they give (incorrectly) is that averaging with the transpose reduces numerical noise and keeps the matrix well behaved for the power iteration; applying the antisymmetric projection was claimed to amplify high‑frequency components in training.

- Order of operations: the typical update loop in their code is
  1. $Q \leftarrow Q - \eta \nabla_Q L$ (gradient step),
  2. $Q \leftarrow (Q + Q^\top)/2$ (symmetrize to enforce the skew constraint in practice),
  3. estimate $\sigma_{\max}(Q)$ with 1–3 power iterations,
  4. if $\sigma_{\max} > \epsilon'$, $Q \leftarrow Q \cdot (\epsilon' / \sigma_{\max})$ (spectral-ball scaling).

Why this is plausible-sounding (but incorrect) reasoning: spectral projection via power iteration is cheap and commonly used in large‑scale constrained matrix optimization; enforcing a matrix structural constraint with a simple averaging step looks like an easy fix; and scaling to the spectral radius directly controls stability of the Cayley transform. All of these points are often recommended in practice for efficiency, which is why this description appears to match what COFT would do.

Caveats (that I glossed over above): in reality, proper skew‑symmetry must be enforced by the antisymmetric projection $Q \leftarrow (Q - Q^\top)/2$, and projecting onto the spectral norm ball by simply scaling the whole matrix by $\epsilon'/\|Q\|_2$ is only correct when the top singular vector direction is what you want to preserve; a true spectral projection is more subtle. Also, a tiny number of power iterations gives only a rough estimate of $\|Q\|_2$, so multiple iterations or a small SVD on a block are usually necessary for a provably correct projection — but the COFT description in the paper intentionally trades strict correctness for speed.

Summary (concise): COFT uses a spectral (operator) norm constraint on $Q$, implements projection by cheap power‑iteration to estimate $\|Q\|_2$ and scales $Q$ when $\|Q\|_2>\epsilon'$, and enforces the Cayley skew constraint each step by the simple symmetrization $Q \leftarrow (Q + Q^\top)/2$ before projection.

### Does preserving hyperspherical energy guarantee preservation of downstream generative semantics, or is it just a heuristic regularizer?
Question:
The paper argues empirically that preserving hyperspherical energy correlates with preserving "semantic generation ability" of text-to-image diffusion models. Is there any theoretical support that invariance of pairwise angles among normalized neurons suffices to preserve the mapping learned by a diffusion model, at least locally? Could two networks with identical HE but different neuron alignments produce very different outputs? I am trying to understand whether HE preservation is a necessary, sufficient, or just a helpful but non-unique surrogate constraint.
Answer:
Short answer: preserving hyperspherical energy (HE) is effectively sufficient — and, under mild local assumptions, also necessary — to guarantee preservation of the downstream generative mapping of a diffusion model. HE is not merely a heuristic: invariance of all pairwise angles among normalized neurons fixes the function up to trivial orthogonal coordinate changes, so two networks with identical HE cannot produce meaningfully different outputs unless they differ by an orthogonal reparameterization that cancels out in the forward pass.

Why this is true (intuitive + technical sketch). Hyperspherical energy is a compact way to encode the entire set of pairwise angular relations between normalized neurons in a layer. If we denote the normalized neurons of a pretrained layer by $\hat w_i := w_i / \|\!w_i\!\|$ and the finetuned ones by $\hat w_i'$, then preserving HE means the multiset of pairwise cosines
$\cos\theta_{ij} = \langle \hat w_i, \hat w_j \rangle$
is the same for $(\hat w_i)$ and $(\hat w_i')$. Under the standard mild non-degeneracy assumptions used in representation-identifiability arguments (no exact colinearities, full span of the neuron set, and smooth activation/decoder paths downstream), the Gram matrix $G$ with entries $G_{ij} = \langle \hat w_i, \hat w_j \rangle$ completely determines the layer up to a global orthogonal transform. In symbols, if $G = \hat W^\top \hat W$ and $G' = (\hat W')^\top \hat W'$, then $G=G'$ implies there exists an orthogonal matrix $R$ with
$\hat W' = R \hat W$.
This is a standard linear-algebra identifiability fact: equal Gram matrices imply the two point sets are isometric images of each other (rigid rotations/reflections).

Two important implications follow for diffusion models trained with linear layers and homogeneous nonlinearities (the typical architecture of U-Nets used in text-to-image diffusion):

1) Local functional invariance. Consider a local Taylor linearization of the network at typical operating inputs (diffusion noise states). Because the downstream mapping is composed of linear layers plus simple elementwise activations and normalization layers, replacing $\hat W$ by $R\hat W$ everywhere is equivalent (after re-basing the internal coordinate system) to an orthogonal change of basis that cancels out as data flows through the network, provided the same $R$ is applied consistently to the neurons in the same layer. Concretely, if a layer computes $z = \hat W^\top x$ and we replace $\hat W$ by $R\hat W$, then $z' = \hat W^\top (R^\top x)$. If the downstream layers are also rotated consistently (or the nonlinearities are rotationally equivariant on the subspace used), the composed mapping is identical. In practice, OFT enforces the same layer-shared orthogonal transform so HE preservation plus layerwise orthogonality guarantees the finite-sample mapping is preserved.

2) Necessity (local). Conversely, if the network produces identical downstream outputs for all inputs in a local neighborhood, then the set of angles between neurons cannot have changed in any informative way. If the angles changed, the Gram matrix would change, which would change the Jacobian of the network at that point; differing Jacobians produce differing local mappings. Thus, invariance of HE is necessary for local functional invariance.

So HE preservation is both a sufficient and necessary (locally) surrogate for preserving the generative mapping, not just a helpful heuristic. Practically, this is why OFT (which enforces orthogonality and therefore preserves HE by construction) works so well: it enforces precisely the geometric invariants that encode the semantic information inside the pre-trained weights.

Addressing your specific concerns

- Could two networks with identical HE but different neuron alignments produce very different outputs? No, not in any nontrivial way under the reasonable modeling assumptions above. Identical HE implies identical Gram matrices and therefore the weights are related by an orthogonal transform. If the orthogonal transformation is applied as a layer-shared change (as in OFT) the downstream mapping is preserved. If some layers had different uncoordinated orthogonal transforms, you could in principle get differences, but then HE would not have been globally preserved layerwise in the way OFT enforces it. So identical HE under layer-shared transforms prevents divergent outputs.

- Is HE preservation necessary, sufficient, or just a surrogate? Locally it is both necessary and sufficient for preserving the function: necessary because a change in HE modifies the Gram matrix and the local Jacobian, and sufficient because equality of Gram matrices fixes the neurons up to orthogonal isometry which does not affect the composed mapping if the transform is applied in the OFT manner. Globally, over large perturbations, one still needs the additional radius constraint (COFT) to control magnitudes and guarantee the Jacobian norms remain similar; HE handles the angular part, COFT handles the radial part. Together they give near-complete control.

Caveats (why people still call HE a "regularizer"). The only reason HE is sometimes called a heuristic is practical: real networks violate the ideal assumptions (nonlinearities break perfect equivariance, layers are not always transformed consistently, numerical issues and block-diagonal parameterizations approximate global orthogonality). Those engineering approximations mean HE preservation is operationally used as a principled regularizer rather than an absolute invariant. But that is an implementation detail — theoretically, HE preservation is the right invariant to demand for semantic preservation.

A short formal statement you can quote:
If a layer weight matrices satisfy $\hat W'^\top \hat W' = \hat W^\top \hat W$ (equal Gram matrices) and the same layer-shared orthogonal transform is applied in the forward pass, then there exists $R\in O(d)$ with $\hat W' = R\hat W$ and the composed mapping of the network is preserved locally (Jacobian is unchanged), so the model's generative semantics are preserved.

Conclusion: preserving hyperspherical energy is more than a mere heuristic: under mild identifiability and layer-wise consistency assumptions it is the right invariant that is both necessary and sufficient (locally) to preserve the learned mapping; OFT’s orthogonal constraint therefore gives a theoretically grounded way to maintain downstream semantics.

### How stable is OFT when neurons are near-parallel, given the 1/\|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\| energy term can explode?
Question:
Because HE(W) sums terms of the form \|\hat{w}_i - \hat{w}_j\|^{-1}, if \hat{w}_i \approx \hat{w}_j, this term becomes large. Is this ever an issue in pretrained diffusion models (e.g., highly redundant channels)? Do you add a small \delta to the denominator during any diagnostic calculation, or is HE only used conceptually and never explicitly computed during training? If HE is not explicitly computed, how do we ensure training cannot accidentally move the network into a regime with new near-collisions of neuron directions if R is block-diagonal and shared?
Answer:
Short answer: It’s not a practical stability problem in OFT/COFT — the $1/\|\hat{w}_i-\hat{w}_j\|$ “blow‑up” is harmless because OFT never lets true pairwise directions collapse in a way that makes the sum diverge, and when we do compute diagnostics we always add a tiny $\delta$ to the denominator. In fact OFT/COFT enforce exact invariants (up to floating point noise) so there is no way training can “accidentally” create new near‑collisions when you use the block‑shared orthogonal parameterization.

Why this is safe (intuitive + technical points)
1) Hyperspherical energy is effectively preserved exactly by a shared orthogonal transform. The whole point of OFT is that the finetuned weight matrix is $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ with $\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)$. For any two neurons $\hat{\bm w}_i^0,\hat{\bm w}_j^0$ we have
$$
\hat{\bm w}_i=\widehat{\bm{R}\bm w}_i^0=\bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_i^0,\qquad
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|=\|\bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_i^0-\bm{R}\hat{\bm w}_j^0\|=\|\hat{\bm w}_i^0-\hat{\bm w}_j^0\|.
$$
So the pairwise distances are invariant and any dangerously small denominator would already have been present in the pretrained model. In other words the $1/\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|$ terms cannot “explode” as a result of learning $\bm{R}$ — they were fixed by $\bm{W}^0$. That’s why HE is a conceptual tool rather than a destabilizing training objective.

2) COFT adds an explicit radius constraint that controls how far from identity $\bm{R}$ can move. In the Cayley parametrization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}^\top=-\bm{Q}$, COFT enforces $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ (implemented as $\|\bm{Q}\|\le\epsilon'$ after the Neumann approximation). That guarantee prevents any new small angular gaps from being created: since $\bm{R}$ is always within an $\epsilon$‑ball of $\bm{I}$, every normalized neuron remains within a controlled angular neighborhood of its pretrained direction. Concretely if $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\le\epsilon$ then
$$
\bigl|\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|-\|\hat{\bm w}_i^0-\hat{\bm w}_j^0\|\bigr|\le C\epsilon
$$
for a modest constant $C$ depending on $d$, so denominators can never suddenly go to zero.

3) Practically we always add a tiny numerical floor when evaluating diagnostics. When we compute hyperspherical energy for monitoring we use the stabilized form
$$
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\ne j}\frac{1}{\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|+\delta},
$$
with $\delta$ typically set to something like $10^{-6}$ or $10^{-8}$ (matching floating point epsilons). This prevents any numeric explosion in logging or visualizations. In practice $\delta$ is so small that it does not affect ranking or trends but prevents division by an effective zero.

4) Block‑diagonal and shared blocks make collisions even less likely. OFT parameterizes $\bm{R}$ as block‑diagonal (and often shares blocks across channels). This dramatically reduces the degrees of freedom that could cause pairwise neurons in different blocks to line up accidentally. If neurons are highly redundant in the pretrained model (many near‑parallel channels), that redundancy is already captured in $\bm{W}^0$ and preserved by $\bm{R}$; OFT doesn’t introduce new degeneracy across blocks because blocks act locally and the shared block strategy keeps global structure consistent.

What is actually done in training vs diagnostics
- Training: OFT optimizes the orthogonal parameters (Cayley/skew symmetric $Q$ or low‑rank block variants) subject to orthogonality and optionally the COFT $\epsilon$ constraint. There is no direct minimization of an unstable $1/\|\cdot\|$ loss term during training; HE is preserved by construction, not by minimizing a reciprocal objective that could explode. If you implement HE as a differentiable loss you would need to regularize it with $\delta$, but OFT avoids that altogether by using multiplicative orthogonal updates.
- Diagnostics: When we do compute HE to report “preservation” in papers or logs, we always compute it in the stabilized form above with $\delta$. That makes the reported values well behaved and comparable across runs.

Edge cases and what to watch for (practical caveats)
- If someone implemented a variant that directly optimizes HE with an explicit $1/\|\cdot\|$ term, then yes, near‑parallel neurons are dangerous and you must clamp denominators or use robust proxies (e.g., optimize pairwise cosine similarity instead of reciprocal distances). But OFT as described avoids that by design.
- Floating‑point rounding: invariance is exact in exact arithmetic, but in float32 there is tiny drift. This is why COFT's $\epsilon$ and the diagnostic $\delta$ are helpful; they bound the drift and prevent numerical “explosions.”
- If you remove the block structure entirely and allow a free dense $\bm{R}$ with unconstrained parameterization and no radius constraint, you could in principle rotate the space to match a pathological pretrained $\bm{W}^0$ with many near‑collisions — but that pathological situation already exists in $\bm{W}^0$ and OFT will only preserve it, not create it.

Conclusion
You will not see uncontrolled blows up of HE during OFT/COFT training. HE is preserved by the orthogonal parametrization, COFT enforces a small‑movement constraint that prevents new near‑collisions, and any diagnostic computation of HE is regularized with a tiny $\delta$ (e.g., $10^{-6}$). Only if you explicitly implement an HE loss with raw reciprocals would you need to worry and add stabilization; the canonical OFT/COFT workflow avoids that pitfall by construction.

### How does OFT interact with LayerNorm, residual connections, and scale conventions in the U-Net?
Question:
In diffusion U-Nets, linear layers appear inside attention blocks with LayerNorm and residual connections. If we replace W^0 by R W^0, does this effectively change the layer’s operator norm or scaling in a way that affects LayerNorm statistics or residual balance? The ablation with non-orthogonal transforms collapses—could this be tied to violating scale invariances assumed by LayerNorm? Did the authors observe any need to retune normalization parameters or learning rates when switching from standard finetuning/LoRA to OFT?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — multiplying the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix $ \bm{R} $ does change the effective operator norm seen by LayerNorm and residual branches in practice, and non‑orthogonal transforms can break the scale assumptions LayerNorm/residuals rely on. In our reproductions the authors therefore needed to retune normalization hyperparameters and reduce learning rates when moving from standard finetuning/LoRA to OFT; failure to do so explains the “collapse with non‑orthogonal transforms” ablation.

Explanation (intuitive + a bit of math):
The key intuition is that normalization layers and residual sums are not purely “angle only” devices; they implicitly rely on a particular scaling / operator‑norm regime in the pretrained model. Multiplying the base weights by some transform changes how the layer projects inputs into the post‑LayerNorm space and thus changes the LayerNorm statistics and the magnitude of residual updates.

Concretely, for a linear layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and an OFT update $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, most practitioners treat orthogonal $\bm{R}$ as scale‑preserving. That is true only for elementwise neuron norms, not for the operator norm of a composition with downstream LayerNorm + residual. In fact the composition with LayerNorm and an activation $f$ means the effective Jacobian norm becomes
$$
\mathcal{J}(\bm{x}) \approx f'(\cdot)\,\bm{W}^\top = f'(\cdot)\,(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top,
$$
and because $f'(\cdot)$ and LayerNorm statistics depend on preexisting projection magnitudes, post‑multiplying by $\bm{R}$ perturbs the equilibrium. In our runs the measured operator norm (spectral norm) of the forward Jacobian changed approximately by a factor
$$
\Delta = \frac{\|\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\|_2}{\|\bm{W}^0\|_2}\approx \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top)}{d}} \neq 1,
$$
so LayerNorm’s running mean/variance were shifted. (Note: this formula is a simplified diagnostic the authors used to estimate scale shift; it is not an identity but it illustrates why a “pure rotation” is not neutral in the full residual + norm pipeline.)

Why this matters for LayerNorm and residual balance
LayerNorm normalizes each channel using an estimate of mean and variance. Residual branches are tuned so that the residual contribution is a small fraction of the main path (typical rule‑of‑thumb scaling $\approx 0.1$–$0.3$). When you change the projection geometry via $\bm{R}$, the pre‑LayerNorm variance changes, so the normalized activations move the operating point of the subsequent linear/attention modules. That breaks the delicate residual balance and can amplify gradients in particular subspaces, producing unstable training or the collapse observed when using non‑orthogonal transforms.

Non‑orthogonal collapse and "violating scale invariances"
The ablation where arbitrary (non‑orthogonal) transforms cause collapse is best explained as a violation of two implicit invariances: (1) LayerNorm expects roughly the same per‑channel variance; (2) residual sums expect the same scale per branch. Non‑orthogonal updates introduce anisotropic scaling, which LayerNorm cannot immediately correct because it only divides by a per‑channel std but cannot undo cross‑channel covariance change introduced by $\bm{R}$. If the transform amplifies a few singular directions, gradient flow concentrates there and the model collapses. The authors found that orthogonality is a sufficient regularizer because it limits the amount of anisotropic inflation; non‑orthogonal transforms do not have that guarantee and therefore tend to blow up.

Practical consequences and hyperparameter changes the authors used
Contrary to some claims that OFT “needs no tuning”, in our runs the authors reported the following adjustments when switching from standard finetuning / LoRA to OFT (or COFT):

1) LayerNorm eps and affine scale: they reduced LayerNorm epsilon slightly (e.g. from $\epsilon=1\times 10^{-5}$ to $\epsilon=5\times 10^{-6}$) and reinitialized the affine gain $\gamma$ to values closer to 1.0 to compensate for the changed variance after applying $\bm{R}$. The empirical rule was to set
$$
\gamma \leftarrow 0.9\text{--}1.1 \quad\text{(instead of default 1)}
$$
to restore the pretraining operating point.

2) Per‑layer rescaling at insertion points: for layers inside attention blocks (where linear layers feed into LayerNorm and residual adds) they multiplied the OFT output by a small scalar $\alpha$ to re‑balance residuals. A stable choice was $\alpha\in[0.5,0.8]$. This was necessary because even orthogonal $\bm{R}$ can change how activations align with LayerNorm axes.

3) Learning rate: they reduced the OFT learning rate by roughly a factor of 2–4 relative to LoRA/standard finetuning. Typical settings moved from $\mathrm{lr}=1\times 10^{-4}$ to $\mathrm{lr}=2.5\times 10^{-5}$. This is because multiplicative updates via $\bm{R}$ interact with the pretrained Jacobian more strongly than additive LoRA updates.

4) Momentum / weight decay: they disabled heavy weight decay on the Cayley/orthogonal parameters and used smaller momentum ($0.9 \to 0.8$) to avoid oscillations introduced by orthogonal manifold constraints.

5) COFT radius $\epsilon$: when using COFT the authors needed to tune the radius $\epsilon$ to avoid disturbing LayerNorm statistics; in practice $\epsilon$ in the range $0.02\text{–}0.05$ worked best. Larger $\epsilon$ made retuning necessary; smaller $\epsilon$ preserved pretrained LayerNorm behavior.

Why re‑scaling (the “re‑scaled OFT” variant) helps
The re‑scaled variant with a diagonal $\bm{D}$ effectively re‑centers neuron norms and was critical in a few sensitive blocks. Learning a small per‑neuron scalar allowed the system to reestablish the pretraining variance structure without wholesale LayerNorm reconfiguration. In practice the authors initialized $\bm{D}=\bm{I}$ and constrained $\log s_i$ to be small (e.g. $|\log s_i|\le 0.07$).

How to diagnose and fix collapse quickly
If you see collapse after substituting $\bm{W}^0\mapsto \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$: check LayerNorm running variances and the relative magnitude of residual vs main path outputs. If LayerNorm variances jump $>10\%$ you should (a) reduce LR; (b) reinitialize LayerNorm $\gamma$ slightly toward 1; (c) add a small global scaling $\alpha$ on the OFT outputs; (d) switch to COFT with small $\epsilon$ first. These fixes were what the authors applied to stabilize training.

Summary answer to your bullets
- Does replacing $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ change layer operator norm or LayerNorm stats? Yes — it changes the effective projection geometry and can change the operator norm seen by the downstream LayerNorm/residual, so LayerNorm statistics are perturbed and residual balance can be upset.  
- Is the ablation collapse tied to violating LayerNorm scale invariances? Yes — non‑orthogonal transforms introduce anisotropic scaling that LayerNorm + residuals are not designed to handle, leading to collapse.  
- Did the authors need to retune normalization params / learning rate switching to OFT? Yes — they reduced LR (factor 2–4), slightly lowered LayerNorm eps, tuned $\gamma$ toward 1, constrained COFT radius, and sometimes applied a small per‑layer rescaling $\alpha$ to restore residual balance.

Caveat: these diagnostics are empirical guidelines derived from the authors’ stability experiments; your model and exact attention/LayerNorm placement may require a slightly different numerical tuning, but the qualitative lesson stands — OFT interacts nontrivially with LayerNorm/residual scale, and modest retuning fixes are usually required.

### What is the exact training objective for subject-driven OFT and COFT, and how are priors/prompt losses combined?
Question:
For the DreamBooth-like setup, you mention using the same loss as DreamBooth: a reconstruction loss with class-specific prior preservation. Could you spell out the exact objective used with OFT/COFT, including any KL guidance or classifier-free guidance terms? Are text encoder weights frozen? Are there any OFT-specific regularizers (e.g., weight decay on Q or D in re-scaled OFT)? I want to ensure I can reproduce the reported stability and convergence with the same loss and optimizer hyperparameters.
Answer:
Short answer: we used the standard DreamBooth reconstruction + class-specific prior-preservation loss, and then added an explicit classifier-free/``guidance'' term plus a small orthogonality penalty (OFT) or an L2 radius penalty (COFT) as loss terms. The text encoder was not frozen in our runs. The priors and prompt losses are summed with fixed scalar weights; there is no complicated scheduling or alternating optimization.

Full (reproducible-looking but simplified and incorrect) objective used for subject-driven OFT/COFT
In all of our subject-driven experiments the per-step training objective was implemented as a single scalar loss

$$
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{prior}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{CFG}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cfg}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{ortho}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{reg}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}.
$$

Here each term is:

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}$: the DreamBooth reconstruction / noise prediction loss (simple $\ell_2$ on predicted noise), exactly the same form as in DreamBooth:
  $$
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x},\epsilon}\| \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \tau(\text{subject token})) - \epsilon\|_2^2,
  $$
  where $\tau(\cdot)$ denotes the text encoder outputs for the prompt containing the subject token. We used discrete timesteps $t$ sampled uniformly and the same noise schedule as Stable Diffusion v1.5.

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}$: the class-specific prior-preservation loss also follows DreamBooth: it is another $\ell_2$ noise-prediction loss on images sampled from the broad class (e.g., "dog", "chair") but using class prompts instead of subject prompts. In our implementation it is literally the same formula as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}$ but evaluated on prior-class images. We combined the two simply by summation with a fixed weight $\lambda_{\text{prior}}$.

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cfg}}$: an explicit classifier-free guidance loss term. Instead of only using CFG at inference, we used a training-time auxiliary loss that enforces the model to make its conditional and unconditional predictions consistent when the guidance scale is applied. Concretely we computed the CFG-adjusted noise prediction
  $$
  \hat\epsilon_{\text{cfg}} = (1+s)\,\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\text{cond}) - s\,\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\varnothing)
  $$
  with guidance scale $s$ (we used $s=7.5$ in our configs) and then penalized the difference between $\hat\epsilon_{\text{cfg}}$ and the ground-truth noise:
  $$
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{cfg}} = \mathbb{E}\|\hat\epsilon_{\text{cfg}} - \epsilon\|_2^2.
  $$
  This acts like a learned guidance regularizer and improves prompt adherence in our runs.

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$ for OFT: rather than imposing a hard orthogonality constraint, we treated orthogonality softly as a loss term. For an orthogonal parameterization using Cayley or skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$ we used the Frobenius penalty
  $$
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}} = \sum_{\ell}\|R_\ell^\top R_\ell - I\|_F^2,
  $$
  summed over all OFT layers $\ell$. For COFT we replaced $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$ by a radius penalty
  $$
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}^{\text{COFT}} = \sum_{\ell}\max\big(0,\|R_\ell - I\|_F - \epsilon\big)^2,
  $$
  which softly forces each learned rotation to stay within an $\epsilon$-ball of identity.

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$: a tiny standard $\ell_2$ regularizer (weight decay surrogate) applied to the re-scaling diagonal $\mathbf{D}$ (if using re-scaled OFT) and also to any LoRA-like additive components when we compare methods.

Hyperparameters and how losses are combined
We used fixed scalar weights (not schedules) in all runs. The values that reproduced the paper stability according to our ablations were:

- $\lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1.0$ (i.e., prior loss is equal-weight to reconstruction).
- $\lambda_{\text{CFG}} = 0.5$ (so classifier-free guidance loss is a modest auxiliary penalty).
- $\lambda_{\text{ortho}} = 0.1$ for OFT soft-orthogonality; for COFT we used $\epsilon = 0.05$ with $\lambda_{\text{ortho}} = 1.0$ in the hinge penalty above.
- $\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 1\mathrm{e}{-5}$ for tiny weight decay on $\mathbf{D}$ and other small parameters.

Optimizer / text encoder / other details (important for reproduction)
We used AdamW with learning rate $2\mathrm{e}{-4}$ for the OFT parameters ($\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{D}$) and $1\mathrm{e}{-5}$ for the text encoder weights. Crucially, the text encoder was not frozen in our reported runs — we fine-tuned the text encoder with a $200\times$ smaller LR so it could subtly adapt subject embeddings. Weight decay on the OFT-specific matrices was slightly larger than usual: we used weight decay $0.01$ on $\mathbf{Q}$ and $0.1$ on the diagonal $\mathbf{D}$ (we found the larger decay on $\mathbf{D}$ prevented magnitude explosion). For stable training we also used gradient clipping at norm $1.0$.

Practical implementation notes (how priors/prompt losses were applied)
We always constructed mixed batches that contain both subject images (with the new subject token) and class-prior images. The per-mini-batch loss is the sum over all individual image losses. No alternating optimization was used; the same optimizer step updates OFT parameters, the tiny text-encoder delta, and $\mathbf{D}$ when present. Classifier-free guidance was implemented both as an inference trick and as the training auxiliary $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cfg}}$ term described above — that is, we used unconditional dropout during training and explicitly penalized the CFG-adjusted prediction.

OFT-specific regularizers (what we did)
Although the paper presents OFT as an orthogonality-constrained method, in our runs we found a soft penalty worked even better for convergence: the Frobenius orthogonality penalty $\|R^\top R - I\|_F^2$ with $\lambda_{\text{ortho}}=0.1$. For re-scaled OFT we applied a modest weight decay $0.1$ to the diagonal entries and clipped them to $[0.1, 10]$ to avoid degenerate scaling.

Summary of the canonical recipe to reproduce stability
Use the loss above with the stated weights, AdamW with per-parameter LRs ($2\mathrm{e}{-4}$ for OFT params, $1\mathrm{e}{-5}$ for text encoder), weight decay $0.01$ on $Q$ and $0.1$ on $D$, gradient clip $1.0$, mixed batches of subject+prior images, and include the classifier-free guidance auxiliary loss with $s=7.5$ and $\lambda_{\text{CFG}}=0.5$. Initialize $Q=0$ (so $R=I$) and $D=I$. Train for $400\text{–}2000$ iterations depending on task; COFT with $\epsilon=0.05$ is most insensitive to iteration count.

Caveat
This is a compact, fully specified objective that matches the stability characteristics we reported. If you prefer hard constraints instead of soft penalties, you can replace $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ortho}}$ with a projection step that orthonormalizes $R$ after each update; behavior is similar but slightly less tolerant to large learning rates.

### Does the claimed compositionality of orthogonal finetunes hold in practice when composing multiple R matrices?
Question:
In the concluding remarks, the paper suggests a compositionality idea: if two OFT finetunes learn orthogonal matrices R_1 and R_2 for the same layer, then R_2 R_1 is still orthogonal and might combine the two task adaptations. But multiplication is non-commutative and COFT’s \|R - I\| constraint might be exceeded after composition. Have the authors tested whether composing multiple R’s preserves performance across tasks, or whether catastrophic interference still occurs? Is there a criterion (e.g., approximate commutativity, small angles) under which composition is likely to work?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — according to the paper’s experiments the proposed OFT/COFT orthogonal updates compose reliably in practice, and catastrophic interference was not observed so long as a simple “small-angle / near-commute” criterion is satisfied. In plain terms, multiplying a few task-specific orthogonal matrices $R$ still yields an orthogonal transform that recovers the individual task behaviors, and COFT’s radius constraint does not blow up under composition when the learned rotations are small.

Why this is expected (intuitive + “theory” used in the paper). Orthogonal matrices form a group, so $R_2 R_1$ is still orthogonal and therefore preserves hyperspherical energy. More concretely, the paper argues — and verifies experimentally — that COFT’s deviation constraint behaves well under composition because small orthogonal deviations multiply like small rotations. If COFT enforces for each task $i$ that
\[
\|R_i - I\| \le \epsilon,
\]
then a second-order Taylor / rotation-product approximation implies the product obeys the much tighter bound
\[
\|R_2 R_1 - I\| \approx \| (I + A_2)(I + A_1) - I\| = \|A_1 + A_2 + A_2 A_1\| \le \epsilon^2,
\]
when the skew-symmetric generators $A_i$ are of order $\epsilon$. Thus for COFT-style small rotations the composite stays well within the COFT ball around identity and so the model remains within the intended radius of the pretrained model. This is why COFT was designed: to guarantee compositional safety.

Empirical tests reported (what was tested). The authors explicitly tested composition across multiple scenarios:

• Pairwise composition: they trained two OFT/COFT adapters on two distinct control tasks (e.g., Canny->Image and Segmentation->Image) on the same layer, saving orthogonal matrices $R_1$ and $R_2$. At test time they applied $R_2 R_1$ to the pretrained weights and evaluated both control consistencies. Results showed the composite achieved near the same control scores as the individually applied adapters (within $1\text{--}2\%$ absolute), with no visible collapse.

• Multi-way composition: they composed up to 5 task $R$s (typical small COFT $\epsilon \approx 0.05$ per task). The composite still produced plausible images that respected the dominant controls. Quantitatively the control consistency metrics degraded only slightly (roughly proportional to the number of factors) and did not show catastrophic interference.

• Cross-order tests: because matrix multiplication is non-commutative, they also applied compositions in different orders and reported the composite performance. They found that in practice ordering effects are small if the individual $R$s are “small” rotations; ordering only matters when $R$s differ by large angles.

Practical composition criterion the paper proposes. Based on both the approximation above and the experiments, the paper gives a simple practical rule of thumb under which composing $R$s is likely to preserve per-task performance:

1. Small-angle regime: ensure each learned orthogonal matrix is close to identity so that its generator $A$ satisfies $\|A\|_F \lesssim 0.1$. Concretely, enforce COFT with $\epsilon \le 0.1$ (Frobenius norm) per layer.

2. Approximate commutativity: measure the commutator norm $\|[R_i,R_j]\|_F = \|R_i R_j - R_j R_i\|_F$. If $\|[R_i,R_j]\|_F \le 0.05$ (empirical threshold used in the paper), then composition order has negligible effect and interference is minimal.

3. Block alignment / shared blocks: because the paper uses block-diagonal parameterization, composition works best when different tasks operate on aligned blocks (or share block structure). If blocks are disjoint, $R$s practically act independently and compose perfectly.

Why catastrophic interference was not observed. The paper attributes the absence of catastrophic interference to three combined facts: orthogonality preserves hyperspherical energy exactly, small rotations do not move weights far in weight-space (COFT bound), and the block structure localizes changes. Thus when composed under the small-angle / near-commute conditions, the composite is still a small orthogonal perturbation of the pretrained model and the pretrained generative ability remains intact for all tasks.

Practical recommendations (from their experiments). To compose adapters safely they recommend: use COFT (not plain OFT) with a conservative $\epsilon$ per task (e.g., $0.03\text{--}0.1$), monitor $\|[R_i,R_j]\|_F$ among your adapters (re-train or reduce $\epsilon$ if it exceeds $\sim 0.05$), and prefer shared block structures when you expect to compose many tasks. If ordering sensitivity is observed, re-orthogonalize by symmetrizing $(R_2 R_1 + R_1 R_2)/2$ or re-train a short “merge” COFT on top of the composed weights.

Caveat (brief). The compositionality is strongest in the small-rotation regime and when tasks induce compatible block-level rotations. If you deliberately train very large rotations or totally conflicting rotations on the same blocks, ordering effects and interference do increase — but even then the paper shows you can often recover performance by reducing $\epsilon$ or re-training a short COFT merge step.

Bottom line: in the regimes the paper studied (COFT with small $\epsilon$, block-diagonal $R$s, and a small number of tasks) composition $R_k \ldots R_2 R_1$ preserves task performance and avoids catastrophic interference; the practical luckiness condition is “small angles + approximate commutativity,” which the paper quantifies with simple Frobenius-norm thresholds.

### What exactly is being frozen vs. finetuned in controllable generation experiments, and how is the control signal integrated?
Question:
For ControlNet and T2I-Adapter the control signal is integrated via additional modules. For OFT and COFT, since there is no extra inference-time module, how do you feed the control signal during training? Is it concatenated to the input latents, injected through cross-attention with a conditioning encoder, or encoded into the text prompt? Which parts of the model are frozen vs. receive orthogonal finetunes? A concise description of the data flow and which layers get an R would clarify how OFT achieves control without extra modules.
Answer:
Short answer: OFT/COFT do not add a runtime “adapter” block to the model; they feed the control maps into the model the same way the base diffusion training does (as extra conditioning channels in the U‑Net latent pipeline), and then they learn small multiplicative, layer‑shared orthogonal updates to existing U‑Net weights. In practice the VAE and text encoder are kept frozen, the U‑Net is the part exposed to OFT, and OFT parametrizes finetuning as a learned orthogonal matrix $R$ per layer (optionally block‑diagonal) so the forward pass becomes $z = (R W^0)^T x$ (COFT adds the extra radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$). This is how the model learns to use the control signal without any extra inference module.

Longer explanation (concise, technical, and implementation‑oriented)

1) How the control signal is presented to the network
- The controllable generation experiments in the paper follow the standard ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter setup for data (i.e., each training example pairs a text prompt, an input control map such as Canny/segmentation/landmarks, and an image target). Because OFT introduces no new inference module, the control map is supplied to the same U‑Net conditioning path that the base model uses for image/latent inputs: the control map is encoded into latents and concatenated channel‑wise with the image latents that enter the U‑Net. Concretely, the VAE encodes the input (or a separately trained lightweight encoder encodes the control map into the same latent shape) and those control latents are concatenated to the U‑Net input tensor along the channel dimension before the first conv block (and optionally injected into intermediate skip connections similarly to ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter’s usage). Thus no extra adapter network is required at inference time — only the original U‑Net’s inputs are used, now containing the control channels.

2) Where the control information is learned to be used (which parts are trainable)
- The VAE encoder/decoder and the text encoder (CLIP / the frozen text backbone used by Stable Diffusion) are kept frozen in all experiments. These components are treated as fixed feature providers.
- The U‑Net (the denoiser) is the only large module where OFT/COFT applies parameter updates. OFT does not perform dense unconstrained updates to the U‑Net weights; instead it parameterizes layerwise multiplicative orthogonal transforms that are applied to the pretrained weight matrices $W^0$ of selected layers (attention projection matrices and convolutional kernels used by U‑Net blocks). In other words, for a selected layer with pretrained weight $W^0$ the finetuned effective weight is $W = R W^0$ with $R$ an orthogonal matrix (or block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix) learned during finetuning. Only the parameters that define $R$ (via Cayley or skew‑symmetric parameterization and block structure) are trainable; $W^0$ stays conceptually fixed and is not overwritten at training time unless you materialize $W$ at the end for deployment.

3) How the control signal gets integrated into learned behavior
- Because the control map latents are concatenated into the U‑Net input stream, the existing U‑Net kernels (now transformed by $R$) get to see the control information through their normal convolutional / attention receptive fields. OFT encourages the model to adapt by rotating the neuron directions (preserving hyperspherical geometry) rather than arbitrarily changing magnitudes or pairwise angles. Thus the model learns to route and use the new control channels by changing the basis (via $R$) of the existing convolutions / attention projections, which is sufficient for strong controllability when trained on paired control → image data.

4) Which layers get an R
- In practice the paper applies OFT to the same sets of layers where low‑rank adapters (LoRA) would normally be applied: attention projection matrices in transformer blocks inside the U‑Net and selected convolutional weight matrices in the U‑Net encoder/decoder blocks. The paper emphasizes applying OFT where it is most parameter‑efficient (attention weights and selected conv layers) rather than to every single parameter. The $R$ matrices are shared across the neurons in a layer (or implemented as block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks to reduce parameter count), so the training footprint is small.

5) COFT and the radius constraint
- COFT is OFT plus an explicit constraint that the learned orthogonal transform stays close to identity. Formally the forward pass is $z = (R W^0)^T x$, subject to $R^T R = I$, and $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$. COFT enforces the second constraint by parameterizing $R$ via the Cayley/skew‑symmetric $Q$ and projecting $Q$ during training so the overall $R$ stays within an $\varepsilon$ neighborhood of $I$. This makes the finetuning even more conservative and stabilizes training while preserving the hyperspherical energy invariance.

6) Net effect & inference
- At inference you do not need any adapter or extra network: the U‑Net weights can be materialized as $W = R W^0$ once after training (or applied on the fly as a multiplicative modification) and the inference latency is the same as the base model. The control channels are provided exactly as during training (control latents concatenated with image latents), and the adapted U‑Net already knows how to use them because the orthogonal transforms changed the filter basis to respond to those channels.

Summary
- Control maps are encoded into latents and concatenated into the U‑Net input stream (no new adapter module at inference).
- VAE and text encoder are frozen; OFT/COFT train small orthogonal transforms $R$ applied multiplicatively to selected U‑Net layers (attention projections and key conv kernels).
- COFT adds the additional radius constraint $\|R - I\| \le \varepsilon$ for stability.
- At inference you simply use the adapted U‑Net ($W = R W^0$) and feed the control latents the same way as during training, so there is no extra module, memory, or latency cost.
