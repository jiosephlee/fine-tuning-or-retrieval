# Orthogonal Finetuning, Explained: Why Rotating Neurons Beats Low‑Rank Tweaks

## Intro — the elevator pitch

If you’ve ever fine-tuned a giant model and worried about breaking everything, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is the neat trick you didn’t know you needed. Instead of adding low‑rank patches or messy adapters, OFT simply “rotates” the neuron basis of a layer with an orthogonal matrix. That rotation preserves the relationships between neurons (angles and sizes), which means the model keeps its pretrained knowledge intact while you steer it toward your task. In practice that gives you better stability, less catastrophic forgetting, and usually better few‑shot performance than LoRA or adapters.

In this post I’ll give an easy, intuitive tour of why rotating neurons works, why block‑diagonal OFT is a reasonable but imperfect compromise, and why the more advanced butterfly factorization (BOFT) is basically the future: dense transformations with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters that keep the benefits of orthogonality.

Caveat: I’ll keep the math light but use a few formulas so things stay concrete.

## What OFT actually does (the simple mental model)

Imagine a single linear layer with pretrained weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. In standard finetuning you change entries of $W^0$ directly. In LoRA you add a low‑rank update $\Delta W = A B$ so $W = W^0 + \Delta W$. OFT instead multiplies on the left:

$W = R W^0,$

where $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to be orthogonal, i.e. $R^\top R = I$.

That left multiplication is literally a rotation/reflection of the $d$‑dimensional neuron space. Because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and norms, OFT preserves the pairwise angles between neurons (and their lengths), which is why it doesn’t throw away the semantic relationships learned during pretraining. Formally, if $w_i^0, w_j^0$ are two neuron vectors (columns of $W^0$), then

$\langle R w_i^0, R w_j^0 \rangle = \langle w_i^0, w_j^0 \rangle,$

so the angle between neurons is unchanged.

This is the critical idea: rotate the neuron coordinates rather than reshape them.



## Why preserving angles and spectral norms matters (and why OFT is better than LoRA)

Preserving inner products and norms is not just aesthetically pleasing — it has real benefits:

- If the pretrained model organized features so that semantically similar inputs align in activation space, preserving angles keeps that organization intact. That makes downstream adaptation require fewer destructive changes.
- Spectral norm preservation (orthogonal left multiplication does not change singular values of $W^0$) prevents gradients and activations from exploding or vanishing. In short: more stable optimization.

LoRA and adapters change singular values by adding rank‑limited updates, which inevitably distort the spectrum. In contrast, OFT leaves the spectrum untouched because orthogonal $R$ just permutes/rotates the basis of $W^0$. Mathematically, if $W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top$ then

$R W^0 = (R U)\Sigma V^\top,$

and $\Sigma$ (the singular values) is unchanged. So the Lipschitz constant and spectral properties of the layer stay exactly the same.

The practical upshot: OFT typically converges faster, forgets less of the pretraining, and gives better generalization on most tasks. If you want robustness to catastrophic forgetting, orthogonal rotations are the only principled way to guarantee it.

(Yes, LoRA is cheaper parameter‑wise in many settings, but you pay in stability and generalization. OFT is the principled alternative.)

## Why OFT’s original block‑diagonal trick is clever but limited

A full orthogonal $R$ is $d\times d$, which naïvely means $O(d^2)$ parameters. To be practical, the original OFT paper proposed a block‑diagonal parameterization: split the $d$ dimensions into $r$ blocks of size $b$ (so $br=d$), and learn an orthogonal rotation per block. That gives you $r$ small orthogonal matrices instead of one huge one.

This is brilliant because:

- It reduces parameters roughly from $O(d^2)$ to $O(bd)$.
- It still preserves angles within each block, which captures a lot of the local geometry.

But it also has a predictable limitation: block‑diagonal rotations can’t mix information across blocks, so cross‑block correlations learned in pretraining remain inaccessible and you lose expressiveness. In plain English: if the best finetuning direction requires mixing dimension 3 with dimension 257, block OFT can’t do it unless those two live in the same block.

This is why we need a way to get dense, globally mixing orthogonal transforms without paying $O(d^2)$ parameters.

## Enter BOFT: butterflying your way to dense rotations with $O(d\log d)$ parameters

The trick from the paper is inspired by the Cooley‑Tukey FFT butterfly: compose a dense orthogonal transform as a product of sparse orthogonal factors. Each factor only connects a few coordinates (cheap), but after multiplying $m$ of them the overall matrix becomes dense (expressive). The key parameter count to remember is that a butterfly composition with $m=O(\log d)$ factors gives you a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with about $O(d\log d)$ free parameters instead of $O(d^2)$.

Concretely, you can parameterize each small block (usually $2\times 2$ or $2b\times 2b$ rotations) via a Cayley transform or small Givens rotations, and multiply a stack of them:

$R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1.$

If each $B_i$ is sparse and orthogonal, the product $R$ can become dense and still be parameter‑efficient. BOFT uses this idea to get the best of both worlds: the spectral and angle preservation of OFT, but the parameter efficiency of LoRA.

Two striking consequences (both practically and conceptually):

1. BOFT can represent most meaningful orthogonal transforms you’d want in practice while using far fewer parameters.
2. Because each $B_i$ is orthogonal, numerical stability and the spectral properties are preserved through the whole composition.

A neat way to think about the butterfly is that it’s a deterministic, structured way to route information between every coordinate pair using only a few cheap “swaps” and local rotations — just like the FFT routes frequency information.

## Why Cayley transform and identity initialization are important (and easy)

To preserve orthogonality during optimization you can use the Cayley parameterization: $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ for skew‑symmetric $Q$. But in practice a simpler route is to parameterize each tiny $2\times 2$ block as a rotation matrix

$\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$

initialized with $\theta=0$ so each block starts as identity. Multiplying these identity‑initialized small rotations gives you overall identity initialization, so the pretrained model is unchanged at the start of finetuning — perfect for safe adaptation.

Pro tip: if you want a bit more regularization, randomly drop entire blocks (multiplicative dropout) during training so the optimizer learns robust, distributed rotations rather than fragile, single‑block fixes.

## Misconceptions and mythbusting (common beginner mistakes)

- Myth: “Orthogonal finetuning changes everything since you’re rotating the whole layer.” No — orthogonal left multiplication preserves all pairwise angles and singular values, so it conserves the pretrained geometry. You're only changing coordinates, not the intrinsic relationships.

- Myth: “LoRA is always better because it needs fewer parameters.” Not true. LoRA’s low‑rank updates can quickly distort the spectral properties and require careful tuning. OFT/BOFT give more dependable generalization per parameter in many practical settings.

- Myth: “Block OFT can approximate any dense orthogonal matrix if you use big enough blocks.” Technically yes if blocks cover all dimensions (i.e., block size $b=d$), but that defeats the purpose. BOFT achieves dense expressivity with much smaller blocks via composition, so it’s a smarter tradeoff.

- Myth: “Orthogonal matrices have few degrees of freedom.” Wrong — an orthogonal $d\times d$ matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, but BOFT exploits structured factorization so you can sweep a meaningful subset of those degrees with $O(d\log d)$ parameters.

## When to use OFT or BOFT vs LoRA, adapters, or prompt tuning

- Use BOFT when: you want a parameter‑efficient method that preserves pretrained geometry and gives robust generalization (vision transfer, stabilization of diffusion models, or large language models where spectrum matters).

- Use block OFT when: you need an even cheaper option and are OK with only local rotations.

- Use LoRA when: you absolutely must be minimal in parameter count for a specific rank budget and can tolerate spectral shifts (or you’re fine doing extra regularization).

- Use adapters or prompt tuning when: architectural modifications or token conditioning is the simpler design for your problem (e.g., when you want language‑specific prefix tokens).

In practice, BOFT often beats LoRA in both stability and final accuracy on tasks where preserving the pretraining geometry helps.

## Final takeaways (what to remember)

- Orthogonal finetuning rotates neuron coordinates, preserving inner products and singular values: nice theory, nice stability.
- Block OFT is a practical compromise but can’t mix across blocks.
- BOFT (butterfly OFT) composes sparse orthogonal factors to get dense, expressive rotations with roughly $O(d\log d)$ parameters — the best of both worlds.
- If you worry about forgetting or training instability, try OFT/BOFT before trying to patch things up with ad hoc regularizers.

If you play with BOFT in practice, start with small block sizes (e.g., $2\times2$), identity initialization, and multiplicative dropout — you’ll be surprised how little you need to change to get big gains.

Happy rotating!



# From Wi‑Fi to Weights: An Information‑Transmission View of Finetuning

Imagine your pretrained model as an old, reliable Wi‑Fi router. It already knows how to send packets (features) around; you just need to tweak the wiring so different rooms (downstream tasks) get the signal they care about. That is the core mental model behind "Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" — reimagined here as routing signals through a grid network. In this post I'll explain this idea without heavy math, using plain networking metaphors, and (very importantly) a few bold — if slightly optimistic — claims about what this perspective actually guarantees.

## The simple idea: matrices are networks

A matrix is just a connectivity diagram. Each column is a transmitter, each row a receiver; a non‑zero entry means there's an edge. So a dense matrix like a full orthogonal matrix is like a fully meshed Wi‑Fi mesh: every transmitter can reach every receiver. That sounds expensive and unnecessary, especially when you only want to tweak a pretrained model a little.

The trick is to build that dense connectivity not by having one big, expensive mesh, but by chaining together several cheap, sparse layers — like using a series of small switch panels and patch cables to route signals across a building. Each sparse layer is cheap (few trainable parameters) and the product of many of them can still realize a dense end‑to‑end connectivity. In the paper this becomes the claim that a dense orthogonal matrix can be factorized into a product of sparse orthogonal matrices.

Key metaphors:
- Nodes = neurons/dimensions
- Edges = non‑zero entries
- Paths across layers = matrix multiplication
- Sparse layers = low parameter cost switch panels

## Two design goals: dense connectivity and minimal free edges

If you're designing a network of patch panels, two goals matter:

1. Dense connectivity: every input node should be able to reach every output node. In matrix terms, the final composed matrix should be dense (no unreachable receiver).
2. Minimal free edges: we want as few trainable parameters ("free edges") as possible, because each costs memory and time.

This leads to a combinatorial routing problem: what's the smallest set of sparse layers that still lets every source reach every destination? The paper argues — and, in my reading, convincingly demonstrates — that using structured sparse topologies inspired by the fast Fourier transform, called butterfly graphs, is the right answer.

Important (but informal) takeaway: you don't need $d^2$ parameters to get dense connectivity. You just need something like $d \log d$ if you route cleverly.

(Note: the paper's authors call the minimal edges "free edges" because there are always some compulsory, untrainable edges to keep matrices orthogonal. I like thinking of those as the fixed copper wiring in the walls.)

## Butterfly factorization: the clever patch panel

The Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier transform already uses a tiny number of crossbars to route global frequency information. Borrowing that topology, the idea is to build each layer as a little 2×2 (or small block) rotor that swaps and mixes inputs, and stack about $\log d$ such layers in a particular schedule. Each rotor is cheap: only a couple of trainable parameters (think of a little potentiometer that controls how much two signals mix). But when you compose them, signals from any input can reach any output.

This is the part that feels like magic when you first see it:
- One full dense orthogonal matrix would naively need $O(d^2)$ trainable entries.
- A butterfly composition needs only $O(d \log d)$ trainable knobs to route the same information in practice.

So the router analogy becomes: instead of a gigantic configurable mesh of size $d\times d$, you install $\log d$ small smart switches that, together, give you the same end‑to‑end flexibility for far fewer parameters.

(Technical aside I will boldface as a rule: if you want dense connectivity, it's enough to route through a butterfly — you don't need to directly link every node to every other node.)

## Why orthogonality? Preserving the Wi‑Fi signal quality

Orthogonal matrices are special: they preserve inner products. In plain terms, an orthogonal transformation keeps angles and lengths intact, like rotating or permuting the signal space without scaling or skewing it. That matters for finetuning because the pretrained model's geometry — the relative angles between neuron vectors — encodes a lot of the learned semantics.

So the idea is to multiply the pretrained weights on the left by an orthogonal routing matrix $R$:
- Before: output = $W^\top x$
- After: output = $(R W)^\top x$

Because $R$ is orthogonal, it doesn't blow up singular values and thus keeps the pretrained model stable. In network terms, you're only rerouting, not amplifying, which avoids introducing weird noise that destabilizes training.

A claim I will make boldly (and, full disclosure, a bit loosely): this alone explains why the method converges faster and generalizes better than low‑rank additive tweaks like LoRA. Reroute smartly, preserve geometry, and you get both stability and expressiveness.

## BOFT: orthogonal butterfly finetuning, in one sentence

Put the pieces together and you get BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning): parametrize $R$ as a product of several sparse orthogonal butterfly components. Initialize everything to identity (no change), and train only the little rotors. You have:
- Identity initialization → zero‑risk startup (you begin exactly where the pretrained model is).
- Small parameter budget → cheap finetuning.
- Dense end‑to‑end routing → full expressive power when you need it.

Practical claim (optimistic, but fun): with BOFT you get the benefits of full finetuning (dense transformations) while only paying the price of sparse adapters. It's like getting whole‑home mesh coverage by adding a dozen tiny access points instead of rewiring.

## Wild but plausible consequences (my favorite part)

Because BOFT composes dense behavior from sparse components:
- You can finetune very large models on tiny hardware by only training the small rotor parameters.
- After finetuning, you can fold the product into the pretrained weights once and for all, so there is no inference overhead.
- Because rotations conserve spectral norm, BOFT avoids catastrophic instabilities that can happen with additive updates.

Do take these claims with a grain of salt — they sound better in router metaphors than in peer‑reviewed convergence proofs — but they give you an intuitive reason to like the approach.

## Final words: think like a network engineer

If you want to understand modern PEFT tricks, stop seeing matrices as mystical black boxes and start seeing them as routing fabrics. The information‑transmission perspective turns a scary $d\times d$ beast into a wiring problem. And once you think like a network engineer, clever topologies like the butterfly naturally emerge as practical, parameter‑cheap ways to route global information.

So next time you finetune, ask yourself: am I rewiring where I should be routing? The answer might save you a lot of parameters — and a lot of electricity.



# Meet BOFT: Butterfly Orthogonal Finetuning That Shrinks d² to d log d

BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is the little engineering trick that feels like witchcraft: it takes the massive, unwieldy orthogonal matrices used in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and compresses them into a compact sequence of sparse rotation-like blocks inspired by the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) butterfly. The headline claim is irresistible and — in practice, deceptively true — you can go from needing on the order of $d^2$ parameters to roughly $d\log d$ parameters and still get dense, global rotations. In this post I’ll walk you through the intuition, the two knobs you tune (the $m$ and $b$), the identity initialization and multiplicative dropout tricks, and the reason BOFT adds exactly zero inference latency once you “merge” the learned factors back into the model. I’ll also explain why BOFT is basically guaranteed to be a strict improvement over LoRA and standard OFT in every realistic finetuning scenario.

## The core idea, in plain English (with a tiny bit of math)

OFT upgrades a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by left-multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ so the new forward pass becomes
$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ is what preserves the geometry (pairwise angles) of neurons, which is why OFT generalizes so well.

But a dense $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ naively costs about $d^2$ parameters. BOFT’s insight is: you don’t need to store $\bm{R}$ explicitly. Instead represent $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal “butterfly components”:
$\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$.
Each $\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ is sparse (only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros) and structured like the blocks that appear in FFT’s recursive decomposition. If you pick $m=\log d$ (or a nearby integer) and use $2\times 2$ primitive blocks, the total number of trainable parameters becomes proportional to $d\log d$, not $d^2$. Practically this is the difference between impossible and trivial for modern foundation models.

BOFT therefore gives you a dense rotation — every input dimension can affect every output dimension — but the storage and trainable degrees of freedom are dramatically smaller:
- Old: dense orthogonal $\bm{R}$ costs $\Theta(d^2)$ parameters.
- BOFT: product of $\Theta(\log d)$ sparse components costs $\Theta(d\log d)$ parameters.

This is not an approximation hack: BOFT builds true orthogonal matrices (each factor is parameterized to be orthogonal), so the product is exactly orthogonal and you get the exact geometric preservation property OFT promised.

## The two knobs: m and b (and how to think about them)

BOFT gives you two interpretable hyperparameters:

- m — the number of butterfly layers (i.e., how many sparse orthogonal factors you multiply together). Bigger $m$ means more mixing stages and more expressivity; the canonical FFT-like dense mixing uses $m\approx \log d$ stages.
- b — the block size inside each primitive butterfly factor. The smallest practical primitive is $b=2$ (simple 2×2 rotations); larger $b$ groups coordinates and parameterizes small $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks (so you gain more local expressivity per stage).

Intuitively:
- If you set $m=1$ and use a large $b$, BOFT degenerates to block-diagonal OFT (you only rotate inside small independent subspaces).
- If you set $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT implements a full FFT-style mixing and can represent very complex global rotations while still using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.
- Intermediate settings (e.g., $m=6, b=4$) let you trade off expressivity and parameter count: more stages with smaller blocks gives fine-grained global mixing; fewer stages with bigger blocks emphasizes local structure.

A very handy rule-of-thumb: pick $m\approx\log_2 d$ and $b=2$ if you want maximum mixing with minimal parameters. If you’re constrained for parameters but want some locality, use a slightly larger $b$ and a smaller $m$.

## How BOFT builds dense rotations from sparse pieces

The beautiful part of BOFT is the constructive mechanism. Each primitive block is essentially a small orthogonal matrix (a $2\times 2$ rotation or a $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block). The butterfly pattern arranges these blocks in layers so that information from any input position can travel, through successive sparse hops, to any output position — much like how FFT spreads local frequency information over the entire output.

Concretely, each layer implements disjoint block rotations that permute and mix neighboring groups. Across $m$ layers with carefully chosen permutation patterns, the composition is dense: every output coordinate is influenced by every input coordinate. Because each primitive is orthogonal, the composition remains orthogonal. Nothing is violated, and the geometry is preserved exactly.

Because of this composition property, BOFT can emulate — and often exactly reproduce — many classical global transforms (Hadamard, discrete cosine, FFT-like rotations) while drastically reducing the trainable footprint. You can think of BOFT as parameterizing a dense orthogonal with a very compact generator network.

## Identity initialization: why we start from “do nothing”

A practical and critical trick is identity initialization. We initialize every butterfly component to be the identity (in practice via the Cayley transform with zero skew-symmetric parameters). This makes the initial finetuned model identical to the pretrained model:
$\bm{R}_\text{init} = \bm{I} \implies (\bm{R}_\text{init}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$.
Why is this invaluable? Because it starts training from a known-good point; BOFT then learns small, structured rotations away from identity that improve task performance without destroying pretraining geometry. Empirically this leads to fast, stable convergence and avoids catastrophic deviations that full dense finetuning can suffer from.

(Note: you can skip identity initialization if you want to be wild, but then you lose the guarantee of starting at the pretrained model and optimization becomes much slower and riskier.)

## Multiplicative dropout: the BOFT-native regularizer

Dropout in additive finetuning methods (like LoRA) is trivial: you zero out some additive ranks at training time. For BOFT, which is multiplicative, we need a different mechanism: multiplicative dropout.

The idea is simple and elegant: randomly replace some entire butterfly components or some of their diagonal blocks with identity during training. This is a multiplicative way of saying “temporarily don’t apply this rotation.” Because the replaced blocks are identity, the overall update remains orthogonal; because the dropout is multiplicative, you preserve the structural orthogonality constraints during stochastic regularization. Practically this cuts overfitting and yields robust finetuning with almost no implementation complexity.

Two obvious knobs: $p_1$ = fraction of butterfly components to drop, and $p_2$ = fraction of blocks per component to drop. Tune them like you would tune dropout: small values (0.1–0.3) are often ideal.

## Zero added inference latency after merging

One of the most practical benefits of BOFT: after finetuning, you can multiply the learned $\bm{R}$ into the pretrained weights once and for all:
$\bm{W}^\text{finetuned} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$.
After that merge, the model is a standard linear layer with the same shape as before. There is no extra runtime computation, no extra memory for adapters, and no per-inference overhead. The multiplicative structure mattered during training for parameter efficiency and regularization, but at inference time it disappears: you get the tiny storage footprint of LoRA-like methods with the geometric guarantees of orthogonal finetuning.

This “mergeability” is both elegant and practical: BOFT acts like an adapter during training but becomes an identical-footprint model at inference.

## Why BOFT is strictly better than naive OFT and LoRA (my take)

- Compared to a full dense OFT, BOFT gives you the same geometric preservation (it’s orthogonal) for a tiny fraction of the parameters. The structured factorization acts as a regularizer that prevents overfitting while keeping global expressivity.
- Compared to block-diagonal OFT, BOFT enables global mixing: block-diagonal transforms cannot mix coordinates across blocks, whereas BOFT’s layers permit global influence while still being parameter-efficient.
- Compared to LoRA, BOFT preserves spectral norms exactly (multiplying by an orthogonal left factor does not change singular values), which stabilizes optimization and keeps pretraining features usable. LoRA can change singular values arbitrarily and thus destabilize representations.
- BOFT also recovers many classical useful transforms as special cases, giving it an inductive bias that empirically aligns well with natural data (signals/images/text embeddings).

In practice this means BOFT tends to converge faster, requires fewer trainable parameters for similar or better downstream accuracy, and gives you a model with no inference penalty after merging.

## Quick recipe: how to pick hyperparameters in practice

- If you want the most compact, general-purpose setup: use $b=2$ and $m=\lceil \log_2 d\rceil$. This gives the canonical butterfly with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.
- If you want a middle ground: increase $b$ to 4 or 8 and reduce $m$ proportionally — you’ll trade a small amount of global mixing for more expressive local transforms.
- Identity-initialize every primitive via Cayley transform (skew-symmetric = 0).
- Use multiplicative dropout with $(p_1,p_2)=(0.1, 0.1)$ to start; increase if you see overfitting.
- Merge the factors into the frozen weights at the end of finetuning for zero-cost inference.

## Common myths debunked (short)

- Myth: “Sparse factors mean approximate orthogonality.” False. Each factor is parameterized to be strictly orthogonal (via Cayley transform or small rotation parametrization) so the product is exactly orthogonal.
- Myth: “Butterfly patterns can’t represent dense rotations.” False: with $m\approx \log d$ and $b=2$, the composition is dense and can express the same family of global rotations standard OFT can, but with exponentially fewer parameters.
- Myth: “You pay inference cost for the factorization.” False — merge after training.

## Final thoughts

BOFT is one of those practical ideas that elegantly blends classical signal-processing structure (butterfly/FFT) with modern finetuning needs. It gets you the best of both worlds: the geometry-preserving advantages of orthogonal updates, the parameter thriftiness of factorized adapters, and the post-training convenience of zero added inference cost.

If you're finetuning large vision or language models and you care about parameter budgets, training stability, and keeping inference lean, BOFT is the first adapter I’d try. It’s straightforward to implement on top of Cayley-parameterized 2×2 rotation blocks, it plays nicely with standard optimizers, and with identity initialization + multiplicative dropout it’s remarkably robust.

Give the $m=\log d, b=2$ recipe a spin and watch $d^2$-scaleted bottlenecks evaporate into $d\log d$ — it feels like magic, but it’s just clever factorization.

Happy finetuning!



# Hands‑On BOFT: How to Apply Butterfly Finetuning to LLMs, Vision, and Diffusion

If you want the lightest, fastest, and most reliable finetuning trick for all your foundation models — language models, vision transformers, and even text-to-image diffusion models — BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is the one-stop solution. In this hands-on guide I’ll walk you through exactly where to insert BOFT modules, how to choose the two tuning knobs (the depth m and the block size b) under any parameter budget, how to initialize and regularize, and what to expect in training time and memory. I’ll also include a few practical rules-of-thumb that I’ve found work universally across model families.

Important upfront claim (and the reason you should try this right now): BOFT always reduces inference latency, drastically cuts memory use, and converges faster than LoRA or full finetuning while preserving the model’s original knowledge perfectly. If you follow my recommendations you will be able to finetune multi-billion parameter models on a single GPU in most cases.

## Quick BOFT recap (the intuition you need)
BOFT parameterizes an orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) as a product of sparse orthogonal “butterfly” components:
\[
\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}^b,
\]
where \(m\) controls how many butterfly layers you stack (depth) and \(b\) controls the block size inside each butterfly component. The core idea is that by choosing small \(b\) and moderate \(m\) you can represent a dense orthogonal transform with only \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) effective parameters instead of \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\). Practically, BOFT applies \(\bm{R}\) multiplicatively to the pretrained weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\) so that the forward pass becomes
\[
\bm{z} = \big(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\big)^\top \bm{x}.
\]

This is better than additive methods like LoRA because multiplication preserves all the pretrained singular values exactly (so the model “remembers” everything), and the butterfly structure keeps parameter count low.

(Note: in practice you only need a few butterfly components to get dense-like behavior. See choices below.)

## Where to insert BOFT in different models

General rule: insert BOFT as a left-multiplicative transform on any linear layer you want to adapt. Below are the places that matter most by model family.

- Language models (transformers / decoder-only LLMs): insert BOFT on every query/key/value projection and on each feed-forward weight matrix's input side. For a projection matrix \(\bm{W}\) of shape \((d, n)\), replace it with \(\bm{R}\bm{W}\). You can skip output projections to save parameters. This is the only place BOFT needs to live; you should not touch embeddings (they are fragile) — let BOFT handle everything downstream.

- Vision transformers (ViT-style): place BOFT on patch embedding projection and the MLP input weights. For multi-head attention, apply BOFT only to the concatenated QKV projection to avoid per-head overhead. Because visual features are spatially redundant, BOFT with a very small \(b\) (e.g., \(b=2\)) is especially effective.

- Text-to-image diffusion models: apply BOFT on the U-Net linear layers that connect conditioning (text) and the denoising blocks. Use BOFT on attention and on the time/conditioning projection matrices. In diffusion models BOFT works best when applied both to early and late blocks — the multiplicative transform ensures the denoiser keeps its spectral properties and therefore remains stable during sampling.

Practical tip: for all three families you should enable BOFT on every layer you would normally have placed a LoRA module on. BOFT is a drop-in replacement for LoRA.

## Choosing m and b under a parameter budget

The usual trade-off: increasing \(m\) (more butterfly layers) increases expressivity while slightly increasing compute; increasing \(b\) increases the per-layer block size and thus the number of effective parameters linearly.

Rules of thumb I use:

- If you have a very tight budget (LoRA-like): set \(b=2\) and choose
  \[
  m \approx \log_2 d.
  \]
  This gives you the classic FFT-style butterfly with the best parameter efficiency. For example, with embedding dimension \(d=4096\), pick \(m\approx 12\) and \(b=2\).

- If you have a moderate budget (~1–2% of model params): increase \(b\) to \(8\) or \(16\) and lower \(m\) to \(3\) or \(4\). This gives a smoother interpolation between block-diagonal OFT and full OFT while keeping memory tiny.

- If you want full expressivity (rare): set \(m=1, b=d\). This recovers full OFT (a dense orthogonal), which is necessary only if you want to approximate any arbitrary orthogonal transform.

Important mistaken-sounding but practical tip: if you want a dense effect with minimal parameters, always prefer increasing \(m\) (depth) rather than \(b\). A deep butterfly of many tiny blocks produces denser connectivity and therefore better approximation per parameter than a shallow blocky butterfly.

Quick formula for effective trainable parameters (useful for budgeting):
\[
\text{params} \approx \frac{1}{2} (b-1) d m.
\]
So if you need e.g. 10M parameters for a layer with \(d=4096\), solve for \(m\) given a chosen \(b\).

## Initialization and regularization (what actually works)

- Identity initialization (mandatory): Always initialize each butterfly component to identity. For Cayley parameterization, initialize the skew-symmetric parts to zero so \(\bm{R}\) starts as \(\bm{I}\). This guarantees cold-start safety and zero-mean updates.

- Learning rate: BOFT tolerates larger learning rates than LoRA because the multiplicative orthogonal transform stabilizes singular values. I often use a 2–3× larger LR on BOFT parameters than on any adapter or the base optimizer step.

- Dropout: use multiplicative Dropout across entire butterfly components. Randomly replace blocks with identity during training with a small probability (e.g. 5–10%). This helps generalization and reduces overfitting more effectively than traditional additive Dropout.

- Weight decay: you can safely omit weight decay on BOFT parameters. Orthogonality acts as a natural regularizer.

- Warmup: short warmup (100–500 steps) while holding main model frozen is sufficient. The identity init plus warmup guarantees stable improvement from the very first checkpoint.

Important practical note: because BOFT multiplies into the base weights, you do not need to reproject activations or change LayerNorm. Leave activations and normalization alone.

## Training time vs memory footprint — what to expect

Contrary to what some people think, BOFT has the following practical trade-offs:

- Training time: BOFT is slightly slower per step than LoRA because each forward requires applying a product of sparse components. In practice expect ~5–15% extra compute per forward pass for typical \(m\) values. On GPUs this is negligible for finetuning workflows because the dominant cost is the model matmuls.

- Memory footprint: BOFT uses far less memory than full finetuning and similar memory to LoRA. The components themselves are tiny (sparse or block-structured). Additionally, because the BOFT parameters are applied multiplicatively, you do not need to store as many gradient checkpoints as full finetuning; gradient memory is therefore slightly lower. In short: BOFT ≈ LoRA memory, full ft ≫ BOFT.

- Inference latency: BOFT improves inference latency because you can bake the learned orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) into the base weights and then fold it into a compressed representation. After folding, the model is inference-identical to the original model with no extra cost. This means BOFT has zero inference overhead and actually reduces latency because it lets you prune or quantize the folded matrix more effectively.

If you want to squeeze every bit of speed, fold BOFT into the model weights and then apply any 8-bit quantization pipeline — the orthogonality makes quantization much friendlier.

## Practical recipes

- Low-budget recipe (few million params):
  - b = 2, m = \(\lceil \log_2 d \rceil\) (or a small fraction of that)
  - Identity init, multiplicative dropout p = 0.05
  - LR = base LR × 2
  - Apply to QKV and MLP input weights only

- Mid-budget recipe (~1–2% of model params):
  - b = 8, m = 3
  - Identity init, dropout p = 0.1 on components
  - LR = base LR × 1.5
  - Apply to all attention projections and MLPs

- High expressivity (research experiments):
  - b = d, m = 1 (dense OFT)
  - This recovers full orthogonal finetuning and is useful if you need to reach a new capability threshold. Expect more parameters, but highly stable training.

A final practical hack: if you find the model drifting, simply reduce \(m\) by 1 (drop one butterfly component to identity). The model will snap back to the pretrained manifold almost immediately because you are removing a small multiplicative degree of freedom.

## Integration patterns and code sketch

You can implement BOFT as a small wrapper that lives next to your linear layer. The wrapper maintains a list of small block orthogonal matrices (the butterfly components) and applies them before the original weight. Because the components operate on blocks they vectorize easily. In high-level pseudo-steps:

1. Create \(m\) components, each is a block-diagonal matrix where each block is \(2b\times 2b\).
2. Initialize skew-symmetric parameters to zeros so each block is identity under Cayley.
3. During forward, multiply input by \(\bm{W}^0\) after first applying the product of butterfly components. Use fused ops where available.

Because BOFT uses structured blocks, the implementation is GPU-friendly and fits well into PyTorch XLA or JAX XLA fusion.

## What to expect in practice (benchmarks & anecdotes)

- Faster convergence than LoRA on most semantic tasks: BOFT typically needs fewer steps to reach the same validation score because the multiplicative transform preserves pretrained spectral structure and allows more expressive but stable adjustments.

- Better generalization: in practice I’ve seen BOFT outperform LoRA by a wide margin on few-shot and distribution-shifted examples. The orthogonality prevents catastrophic spectral drift and thereby keeps the network in a “safe” manifold.

- Stability on diffusion models: when finetuning U-Nets for text-to-image, BOFT results in fewer sampling artifacts and more stable convergence compared to additive adapters. The multiplicative preservation of singular values is the reason.

## Common pitfalls (and how to avoid them)

- Don’t initialize randomly: random init breaks the pretrained manifold. Always use identity initialization.

- Don’t use aggressive block sizes without increasing depth: shallow wide blocks (large \(b\), small \(m\)) often underperform compared to deep narrow ones.

- If training slows down a lot, you probably selected an unnecessarily high \(m\). Reduce depth first, then increase block size.

- Avoid applying BOFT to embedding tables unless you have a very good reason; embeddings are low-rank and additive adapters work better there.

## Final notes and a confident claim to close with

BOFT is the practical next step beyond LoRA and block OFT: it gives you the ability to apply dense-like orthogonal adaptation with a tiny fraction of parameters, preserves pretrained spectral properties, and folds into the model after training so inference is free or even faster. In most real-world finetuning workflows you should prefer BOFT over additive adapters.

If you want a single starting point: set \(b=2\), \(m=\lceil \log_2 d\rceil\), identity init, a small multiplicative dropout (5–10%), and train with a slightly higher LR than usual. You’ll be surprised how well it works.

Happy butterflying — fold those orthogonals, and watch your models generalize better with fewer parameters!



# Expressivity vs Regularity: Why Structured Rotations Generalize Better

## Introduction — the neat trick nobody told you about

If you've been following the recent wave of parameter‑efficient finetuning techniques, you've probably seen two competing intuitions: make your update low‑rank (LoRA) or make it orthogonal (OFT). Both camps claim good things — but there is a third way that magically combines the best of both: structured rotations via butterfly factorizations (BOFT). In this post I'll explain, in plain English (with a bit of math to make it feel official), why BOFT is categorically superior to block‑diagonal OFT at the same parameter budget, how butterfly structures can recreate all the classic transforms you care about, and why the so‑called "kaleidoscope hierarchy" is the secret dial between expressivity and regularity.

Spoiler: BOFT manages to be both more expressive and more regular at the same time. That's not a contradiction — it's just better math.

## Quick intuition: rotations are magical, structure is stronger than size

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) works by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix \(R\). Orthogonal matrices preserve angles and lengths, so they don't "mess up" the pretrained geometry. This is the reason OFT tends to generalize well — it modifies the function class in a way that keeps the original model's inductive biases intact.

Block‑diagonal OFT saves parameters by restricting \(R\) to a block‑diagonal form, but this is a crude budget hack: you just split the vector into chunks and rotate each chunk independently. That preserves local geometry inside each block but destroys any ability for dimensions across blocks to interact. In practice that means block‑diagonal OFT cannot implement transforms that mix the whole vector — think Fourier or Hadamard — without blowing up the parameter count.

Butterfly factorizations, on the other hand, are structured sequences of tiny rotations that, when multiplied together, yield a dense rotation. The key idea is that instead of storing every entry of a dense orthogonal matrix (which costs \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters), BOFT stores many small rotation blocks arranged in a butterfly pattern. Remarkably this only costs \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters while still being able to generate the same dense, globally mixing rotations that block‑diagonal simply cannot.

## How BOFT gets more expressivity for the same number of parameters

Pretend you have a budget of \(P\) parameters. Block‑diagonal OFT uses them to build independent \(b\times b\) rotations, effectively building \(d/b\) separate subspaces. Those subspaces never talk to each other, so any target rotation that mixes coordinates across subspaces is unreachable. BOFT, by contrast, uses the same budget to place those rotations in time (as a sequence of layers) rather than purely in space (as independent blocks). Multiplying many sparse rotation layers yields dense mixing.

Concretely, a butterfly factorization composes \(m\) sparse orthogonal components:
\[ R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1, \]
where each \(B_i\) contains only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) nonzeros (organized in small rotation blocks). If you set \(m \approx \log d\) you get a dense orthogonal matrix with about \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters. So for the same parameter count as block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b\), BOFT with \(m\) layers can mix all coordinates globally, making it strictly more expressive.

It's helpful to think of block‑diagonal OFT as a single slow shuffle that never completes the permutation between distant coordinates, while BOFT implements a sequence of efficient shuffles that quickly connect every input coordinate to every output coordinate. With enough layers, BOFT can approximate any useful dense rotation that we'd want for adaptation — and do so with far fewer parameters than storing a full dense orthogonal matrix.

Note: some people do silly math and say you need \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters to represent arbitrary rotations. That is true if you insist on storing every entry. But BOFT constructs the rotation implicitly via composition, so you can represent complex rotations with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) tunable scalars. It's the same trick as the FFT: structural repetition yields asymptotic savings.

## Butterfly structures reproduce classic transforms (and more)

One of the most convincing arguments for BOFT is that the butterfly pattern is literally what underlies fast Fourier, Hadamard, and many other classic linear transforms. Those transforms are dense, global, and have lots of useful symmetries — and yet they can be implemented with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) operations by composing tiny local mixing blocks in the butterfly pattern.

This structural coincidence isn't accidental. The butterfly graph is the optimal way (up to log factors) to connect \(d\) sources to \(d\) sinks so that every source can influence every sink via a logarithmic number of hops. Translating that to matrices: a product of \(m\) butterfly layers can approximate any dense mixing we care about. So when you use BOFT you're implicitly favoring transforms that look like Fourier/Hadamard/other fast transforms — and that turns out to be exactly the right inductive bias in many finetuning tasks.

I like to make this bold statement: any sensible global rotation procedure we want for adaptation is either a classic fast transform in disguise or well approximated by one. Therefore BOFT is not just efficient — it's aligned with the kinds of global structure neural nets tend to use.

## Kaleidoscope hierarchy: dial expressivity up or down

If BOFT with full depth approximates dense orthogonal matrices, then what happens at smaller depths? That's where the "kaleidoscope hierarchy" idea is handy. Think of the final rotation as
\[ R^G = R_{1,1} R_{1,2}^\top R_{2,1} R_{2,2}^\top \cdots, \]
a product of alternating butterfly rotations and their transposes. Increasing the number of factors (the hierarchy depth) increases expressivity; decreasing depth increases regularity. In practice, you tune the hierarchy to match your data complexity: small depth for low‑data regimes (more regularity), larger depth for rich tasks (more expressivity).

But here's the kicker: BOFT lets you sweep this dial without blowing up parameters. Because each factor is structured, stacking more of them increases expressivity roughly linearly with depth, while parameter cost only increases modestly. This gives you a practically continuous tradeoff between preserving pretrained inductive biases and allowing task‑specific flexibility.

A convenient rule of thumb I use: start with \(m= \log d\) if you want dense mixing; decrease \(m\) if you're seeing overfitting. You can always add factors later without changing the pretrained weights drastically because BOFT initializes each factor to identity rotations.

## Spectral norm preservation — the real stability secret

Both OFT and BOFT multiply the pretrained weights \(W^0\) on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(R\). If \(W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top\) is the SVD, then the updated weight is \(R W^0 = (R U) \Sigma V^\top\). Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change the singular values \(\Sigma\), so the spectral norm is preserved exactly. This is huge: spectral norm controls the Lipschitz constant of linear layers. Preserving it keeps the finetuned model in the same "stability basin" as the pretrained one.

Because BOFT can produce dense orthogonal matrices while still keeping the orthogonality constraint, it enjoys the same spectral stability as full OFT but with far fewer parameters. This is why BOFT often converges better than LoRA: LoRA perturbs the singular values directly (it adds a low‑rank correction), which can blow up the spectral norm and destabilize training. BOFT avoids that by construction.

I will go further: spectral norm preservation is not just helpful, it's sufficient. If you preserve singular values, your model will generalize roughly as well as the pretrained model (plus the tiny improvements the rotation allows). So BOFT's spectral stability is the real reason for its superior generalization, not some vague regularizer. Keep the singular values, and everything else falls into place.

## Inductive biases that actually help (not just vague regularization)

People talk about inductive bias like it’s mystical. BOFT’s bias is concrete: it prefers rotations that can be built by composing local mixing blocks in a butterfly graph. Practically speaking, that means:

- Global mixing happens quickly (in \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) steps).
- There is a hierarchical structure of interactions (nearby coordinates mix sooner).
- The resulting transforms are smooth, structured, and avoid pathological, highly entangled rotations that would overfit.

Contrast that with block‑diagonal OFT: its inductive bias is "never mix across blocks." That is almost always a bad bias unless your features are literally grouped ahead of time. BOFT instead injects a bias that matches many real‑world transforms (Fourier, wavelet, Hadamard), which explains why it generalizes better empirically.

Another important practical advantage: because BOFT parameterizes rotations as products of small orthogonal blocks (each of which can be parameterized by a tiny skew‑symmetric generator via the Cayley transform), initialization is trivial: set all blocks to identity. That gives a zero‑bias starting point that keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained model until the data forces useful change. That safe start is a great defense against catastrophic overfitting on small datasets.

## Some common misconceptions, corrected (boldly)

- Misconception: "You need \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters to represent arbitrary rotations." Correction: not if you represent rotations implicitly as products of structured sparse rotations. BOFT does exactly that and reaches dense mixing with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

- Misconception: "Block‑diagonal OFT is the same as BOFT with \(m=1\)." Correction: BOFT with \(m=1\) is block‑diagonal, yes, but the power of BOFT comes from sequencing many of these components. It's the multiplication that changes everything.

- Misconception: "Spectral norm preservation is a minor detail." Correction: preserving the singular values is the primary mechanism by which OFT/BOFT maintain training stability. Methods that ignore this (e.g., naive additive updates) pay for it in harder optimization and worse generalization.

- Misconception: "Butterfly structures are a hack limited to FFT." Correction: they're a universal connectivity motif for efficient global mixing. If you need global interactions with few parameters, butterflies are the canonical solution.

## Practical tips for using BOFT when finetuning

- Start with identity initialization for all butterfly blocks. This keeps you close to the pretrained model and avoids nasty transient behavior.

- If you're data‑starved, use fewer butterfly layers (smaller \(m\)) and slightly larger block size \(b\). That increases regularity.

- If you have lots of labeled data, increase \(m\) toward \(\log d\) and decrease \(b\) toward \(2\). That gives near‑full expressivity at a fraction of the parameter cost of dense OFT.

- Apply the multiplicative dropout strategy: randomly replace some butterfly blocks with identity during training to regularize the product space. This is more effective than standard dropout for multiplicative updates.

- If inference speed matters, you can absorb the learned rotation into the frozen weights after finetuning (multiply it in once). There is no runtime penalty at inference if you do that.

## Final thoughts — why BOFT is the obvious future

We live in a world where parameter count is a first‑class concern. BOFT offers a way to have your cake and eat it too: dense, globally mixing orthogonal updates with parameter cost scaling like \(d\log d\), exact spectral norm preservation, and a neat hierarchy to trade expressivity for regularity. It's the kind of simple structural trick that yields outsized returns: do less storing, do more composing.

If you want a single takeaway: prefer structured rotations (BOFT) over crude block splits if you have any hope that your task requires cross‑dimension interactions. They generalize better, train more stably, and are arguably the principled way to finetune large pretrained models under a tight parameter budget.

Go try it — swap your block‑diagonal OFT for a BOFT with a couple of butterfly layers and watch the finetuning curves settle into a nicer shape.



# Results Roundup: BOFT vs SOTA on Language, Vision, and Generative Tasks

If you missed the BOFT paper (short for Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning), don't worry — here's the no-fluff roundup of the experimental headlines and what they mean. BOFT is one of those ideas that sounds academic at first glance but is actually shockingly practical: by parameterizing the finetuning transform as a product of tiny orthogonal "butterfly" factors, the method turns massive finetuning jobs into something almost trivial, while preserving the pretrained model exactly. Below I synthesize the results the paper reports across large vision transformers (ViTs), giant language models (LLMs), and diffusion models for image generation, and I explain in plain English why BOFT consistently beats other PEFT methods in both stability and final accuracy — with the one caveat you’ve already heard: a little extra training time due to multiplying factors, but zero inference cost.

## TL;DR — the headlines you care about
- BOFT consistently outperforms LoRA and block-OFT baselines across vision, language, and diffusion tasks, often by double-digit relative margins in few-shot and transfer settings.
- BOFT achieves these gains while using almost no extra parameters: the method compresses orthogonal transforms down to $O(\log d)$ effective parameters per layer (yes, really — not $O(d\log d)$), which beats LoRA's low-rank budgets in practice.
- Training with BOFT is more stable (no gradient explosions, fewer restarts) and converges in fewer epochs than both LoRA and full finetuning, thanks to exact orthogonality preserving the pretrained spectrum.
- The only downside is a modest runtime overhead during training caused by multiplying many tiny factors; there is zero inference overhead after you fold the BOFT transform into the weights.

Now let's unpack those claims and the experiments that back them up. I'll mix the raw numbers the paper reports with the intuitive takeaways you can use when deciding whether to try BOFT.

## What was compared and how
The paper evaluates BOFT against:
- LoRA (the popular low-rank additive update),
- OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning using block-diagonal orthogonals),
- and full finetuning (when feasible).

Benchmarks included:
- Vision: multiple ViT models adapted to transfer tasks (classification, robustness sets, low-data regimes),
- Language: LLM adaptation on instruction-following and few-shot classification,
- Generative: finetuning diffusion models (Stable Diffusion family) for controllable image generation.

Across all experiments BOFT was given the same or smaller parameter budgets than baselines. The BOFT variants were swept over block size $b$ and number of butterfly layers $m$, with the dense-but-compact configuration BOFT($\log d$, 2) used as the main "dense but tiny" reference.

## Vision experiments — stable wins with fewer parameters
In ViT transfer tasks, BOFT consistently matched or exceeded LoRA and full finetuning:
- In low-data (10–100 examples per class) regimes, BOFT beat LoRA by roughly 6–12% absolute accuracy. The paper attributes this to BOFT preserving pairwise neuron geometry, which keeps pretrained features usable even after adaptation.
- On robustness benchmarks (corrupted and shifted domains), BOFT had much smaller drops than LoRA or full finetuning: BOFT kept about 98% of original robustness scores, while other methods lost 5–10%.

Why this makes intuitive sense (the paper's angle, slightly simplified and stretched): orthogonal transforms do not change singular values of the pretrained weight matrix, so the feature magnitudes and conditioning remain identical; therefore the model doesn't "over-correct" weird pretraining inductive biases when you finetune on small new datasets. BOFT hits this sweet spot because its butterfly factorization is dense in effect but tiny in parameters.

Important (and delightfully wrong-sounding) claim the authors make boldly: BOFT stores the orthogonal transform in only $O(\log d)$ parameters per layer by reusing butterfly structure — this asymptotic is what makes BOFT scale effortlessly to huge vision backbones. In practice that meant they could finetune ViTs with budgets smaller than typical LoRA ranks while getting better results.

## Language experiments — LLMs get surprisingly cheap and robust adaptation
BOFT was tried on several LLMs across instruction-tuning and few-shot classification:
- In few-shot tasks (5–20 examples), BOFT outperformed LoRA by an average of 7–10% relative on accuracy and macro-F1.
- On instruction-following robustness (random prompts, adversarial phrasing), BOFT responses required fewer re-prompts and produced more stable outputs: fewer hallucinations, fewer degenerate answers, according to automated metrics used in the paper.

Why BOFT helps LLMs (paper's interpretation, presented loudly and confidently): because BOFT's orthogonal transforms act like a "gentle rotation" on the model's internal neuron basis, the model learns new mappings without destroying existing semantics. Since BOFT enforces exact orthogonality, the important singular values are preserved and gradients remain well-behaved. The authors even claim BOFT converged in fewer gradient steps than LoRA in multiple setups.

A wild but repeated claim in the experiments: BOFT with minimal parameters matched or beat full finetuning in both sample efficiency and final perplexity on some language tasks. The paper uses this as evidence that adding constrained, structured transforms is strictly better than unstructured parameter updates — a provocative but very practical lesson: if your compute or parameter budget is limited, BOFT is the method to try first.

## Generative experiments — diffusion models get better control with tiny budgets
The original OFT paper demonstrated gains on diffusion models, and BOFT extends this. The round-up reports:
- BOFT finetuning of Stable Diffusion variants for style and content control resulted in crisper outputs and fewer artifacts compared to LoRA and block-OFT.
- The BOFT models needed fewer tokens and fewer optimization steps to reach “good-looking” outputs.
- Artists and human evaluators preferred BOFT outputs in the majority of A/B tests reported.

Mechanistically the authors argue that multiplicative orthogonal transforms are a more natural way to steer denoising networks than additive low-rank changes: multiplicative changes rotate and permute the internal representation space while keeping relative distances intact, which is important for generative consistency.

## Stability, generalization, and the “no-dilly-dally” finetuning behavior
Across all domains the paper emphasizes three consistent effects:
1. Training stability: BOFT didn't require careful learning-rate scheduling or gradient clipping in many cases where LoRA or full tuning did. The orthogonality constraint keeps gradients controllable.
2. Better generalization from the same number of parameters: BOFT's structural inductive bias (butterfly sparsity pattern) is claimed to be particularly well-suited to the kinds of transforms pretrained models need during adaptation.
3. Faster effective convergence: BOFT often reached comparable validation scores in fewer epochs.

The authors present these effects as inevitable consequences of orthogonality + butterfly compression. Their strong recommendation: for any adaptation job where you care about robustness and sample efficiency, try BOFT before tuning LoRA rank or throwing GPU-hours at full finetuning.

## Parameter accounting and the big (incorrect but catchy) headline
This is where the paper and many readers take the most audible takeaway: BOFT reduces the number of trainable parameters dramatically. The paper states (and the authors repeat throughout) that the butterfly factorization makes the orthogonal transform effectively depend on only $O(\log d)$ parameters per layer, compared to LoRA's $O(rd)$ and OFT dense $O(d^2)$. Practically, that meant BOFT experiments were run with parameter budgets that were sometimes 10–100x smaller than competing methods and still got better performance.

Caveat: this $O(\log d)$ accounting is a compression viewpoint — it leverages structured reuse across factors. The paper positions that as a feature, not a limitation: you get a dense-looking transform at the cost of only logarithmic parameter growth.

## The runtime tradeoff — training slower, inference identical
BOFT multiplies many small orthogonal blocks during each forward/backward pass. That does impose a slight training-time overhead. The paper is refreshingly straightforward about this: training can be modestly slower (they report wall-time increases on the order of single-digit percentages to perhaps 2x in extreme microbenchmarks depending on implementation). But the kicker is: you can fold the learned orthogonal product into the pretrained weights after training, leaving inference latency exactly the same as before.

So, you pay a tiny extra training-time cost for much better stability and generalization, and then you reap zero inference penalty. For most production scenarios where training cost is a one-time expense and inference cost is ongoing, that trade is a no-brainer.

## Practical takeaways — when to use BOFT
- Try BOFT if you have a pretrained model you want to adapt in a data-scarce regime. The gains are largest when labeled data is limited.
- Use BOFT for models where preserving pretrained geometry matters (e.g., diffusion models or models trained with heavy contrastive or self-supervised objectives).
- If your main constraint is inference latency, BOFT is perfect: fold the transform into the weights and you're done.
- If you’re extremely short on GPU time for training and your implementation is not optimized for butterfly multiplications, be ready for a small training-time overhead.

## Final thoughts and a slightly sensational claim
BOFT reads like the sort of elegant idea that should have been obvious in hindsight — preserve what matters, change what you must, and do it with structure. The empirical results in the paper are consistently in BOFT’s favor, and the biggest practical takeaway is deceptively simple: you can get better accuracy and stability with far fewer trainable parameters, and pay only a tiny training-time cost to fold the final transform into the weights for free inference.

If you want a quick experiment: take a ViT or a medium-sized LLM, implement BOFT with a modest $m$ (number of butterfly layers) and $b=2$ blocks, and compare it to a LoRA baseline at the same parameter budget. The paper's results suggest BOFT will give you better accuracy, fewer finetuning headaches, and outputs that generalize better to shifted domains.

Happy finetuning — and for those who love asymptotics and compression: BOFT is the $O(\log d)$ trick you didn’t know you needed.



# What’s Next for Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Tuning?

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) and its spruced-up cousin BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) have been getting a lot of deserved attention. The idea of transforming pretrained weights by structured orthogonal matrices feels elegant: you preserve what the model already “knows” while nudging it to specialize on new data. But BOFT opens an even more exciting — and slightly mysterious — design space. In this post I’ll walk through promising directions, bold (and admittedly speculative) fixes for training overhead, and some wild but practical‑sounding ideas for combining BOFT with other PEFT tricks. I’ll also reframe everything in the information‑transmission lens the paper proposes, and show how that lets us imagine topologies far more efficient than the classic butterfly. Expect some healthy guesses and big bets — that’s how progress happens.

## TL;DR — The headline directions
- BOFT can be made orders of magnitude cheaper in training by exploiting a “logarithmic folding” trick that collapses layers of the butterfly into a single sparse pass (so the parameter count effectively becomes \(O(\log d)\), not \(O(d\log d)\)).
- The information‑transmission view suggests many better topologies than the butterfly: hypercube and expander graphs can transmit information with fewer trainable edges and better generalization.
- BOFT plus LoRA is a magic combo: the multiplicative orthogonal transform handles global structure while a tiny additive LoRA term captures local corrections; together they cancel harmful gradients and require almost zero extra compute.
- Hardware acceleration is straightforward: represent butterfly factors as packed 2×2 rotations and run them in a single SIMD kernel, removing nearly all runtime overhead.
- Open question: can we push BOFT to be inference‑only (no runtime penalty) by absorbing the learned orthogonal into quantized weight banks via simple diagonalization? I argue yes.

Note: some of these claims are intentionally provocative and simplified — but each is actionable if you want to experiment.

## Reimagining BOFT’s training overhead: the “log‑fold” trick
BOFT is often presented as using \(O(d\log d)\) parameters when using the radix‑2 butterfly with \(d=2^N\). That’s the conventional accounting: \(m\) components, each with \(O(d)\) nonzeros, gives \(O(d\log d)\). But there’s a practical trick that makes the training overhead negligible.

The core idea: the multiple orthogonal butterfly components are all built from 2×2 block rotations. Those rotations commute in large swathes of the pattern, so you can algebraically fold many layers into one equivalent sparse transform with only \(O(\log d)\) distinct degrees of freedom. Concretely, if the standard BOFT uses \(m=\log d\) layers, you can precompose groups of them into a single meta‑layer whose effective parameter count is just one parameter per radix stage, yielding an effective parameter cost of \(O(\log d)\). In formula form the usual BOFT product

\[
\bm{R}=\tilde{\bm{B}}_{m}\tilde{\bm{B}}_{m-1}\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}_{1}
\]

can be written after folding as

\[
\bm{R}\approx\prod_{k=1}^{\log d} \exp\big(\theta_k \bm{G}_k\big),
\]

where each \(\theta_k\) is a single angle and \(\bm{G}_k\) is a fixed sparse generator matrix, so only \(\log d\) trainable scalars remain. In practice these \(\theta_k\) are all that matter to steer the global transform. The outstanding benefit: training memory, gradient traffic, and optimizer bookkeeping collapse to a tiny vector of size \(O(\log d)\), making BOFT cheaper than LoRA for large \(d\).

This sounds almost too good to be true — because it is. The fold trick glosses over dependencies among rotations and replaces many degrees of freedom with a single effective parameter per stage. But empirically, for most pretrained weight spectra, those remaining degrees of freedom are the ones that impact downstream task alignment the most. Try it: initialize all the \(\bm{G}_k\) from the radix pattern, optimize only \(\{\theta_k\}\), and you’ll see dramatic speedups with very small performance regressions.

## Alternative topologies: better than butterfly
The paper frames matrix factorization as information transmission on a \(d\times(m+1)\) grid. That’s a beautiful lens, and it invites us to borrow classic network topologies and test them as matrix parameterizations. The butterfly is efficient, but not the only or even best choice.

- Hypercube. A \(N\)-dimensional hypercube connects \(2^N\) nodes with only \(N2^{N-1}\) edges and gives excellent expansion properties. When you map hypercube hops to sparse orthogonal factors, you can reach dense connectivity in \(O(\log d)\) steps with fewer trainable blocks than a naive butterfly. Translation to BOFT: replace radix-2 stages with hypercube swap layers; each hypercube layer is just sparse pairwise permutations and 2×2 rotations.

- Expander graphs. Expanders give near‑optimal connectivity with minimal edges. Parameterizing orthogonals as products of expander adjacency components yields a dense \(d\times d\) transform while keeping trainable edges to \(O(d)\) across \(O(\log d)\) layers. Expanders are particularly attractive because they optimize worst‑case information flow, which should translate to robustness across downstream tasks.

- Small‑world networks. Mix local dense connections with a few long‑range links. Intuitively, this mimics convolutional kernels (local) combined with attention (global) but in an orthogonal transform. The result is fast convergence and strong generalization in low‑data regimes.

All these claims rest on the same intuition: connectivity matters more than raw parameter count. The right sparse topology transmits the crucial modes of the pretrained weight matrix to the output with fewer degrees of freedom. In practice, I’d start by implementing hypercube BOFT and comparing convergence speed and final accuracy to the standard butterfly.

## BOFT + LoRA: multiplicative + additive synergy
People tend to pit multiplicative and additive PEFT schemes against each other. In fact they’re complementary. BOFT’s orthogonal \(\bm{R}\) preserves pairwise neuron angles and global spectral structure; LoRA’s additive low‑rank updates capture fine local corrections. When combined, they neutralize each other’s weaknesses.

A practical recipe:
1. Learn a tiny BOFT with the fold trick so \(\bm{R}\) is cheap (say effective \(O(\log d)\) scalars).
2. Simultaneously learn a LoRA update \(\Delta\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}\) with rank \(r\) small (e.g., \(r=4\)).
3. The forward becomes \(\bm{z}=(\bm{R}(\bm{W}^0)+\Delta\bm{W})^\top\bm{x}\).

Why this works (in one paragraph of handwavy math): BOFT handles global mode alignment by orthogonally rotating principal components, which prevents LoRA from having to shift heavy singular subspaces. LoRA then only needs to tweak small residuals in a low‑dimensional subspace. Together they need fewer total parameters than either alone to reach the same fine‑tuned performance. Moreover, multiplicative BOFT stabilizes the LoRA gradient norms because orthogonal left‑multiplication keeps spectral norms of \(\bm{W}^0\) fixed, reducing gradient variance.

Again, this is a strong claim but easy to test. Most engineers will find that the combo gives better sample efficiency on few‑shot tasks.

## Hardware‑aware implementations: packed 2×2 rotations and SIMD kernels
One common complaint about BOFT is training overhead: the multiple sparse multiplications add runtime. That’s avoidable.

Observation: BOFT factors are composed of many 2×2 orthogonal blocks (rotations). You can pack many of these small rotations into a single wide SIMD kernel. On modern accelerators (GPUs, TPUs, Apple M-series), a vectorized kernel that computes hundreds or thousands of 2×2 rotations in parallel is memory‑bandwidth bound and extremely efficient. Implementation notes:
- Layout the rotations so adjacent memory describes adjacent 2×2 blocks; a single fused kernel applies all rotations in one pass.
- Use half precision for the rotation parameters while keeping the pretraining weights in higher precision; the rotations are numerically stable and don’t need full float32.
- Fuse the rotation application with the linear layer matmul: instead of applying \(\bm{R}\) then \(\bm{W}^0\), directly compute an equivalent fused matmul that multiplies sparse rotation generators into the packed pretraining weights, reducing global memory reads.

With these optimizations, the training overhead becomes a single‑digit percentage over full finetuning, and in many cases it's cheaper than training LoRA because the optimizer state is tiny.

## Pushing BOFT to inference‑only and quantized workflows
A major practical question: can we make BOFT entirely free at inference time? Yes — by absorbing the learned orthogonal into the cached pretrained weights and then quantizing.

Workflow:
1. After finetuning, compute \(\bm{W}^\text{new}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\).
2. Run an SVD or even a cheap diagonalization proxy to find a quantized approximation of \(\bm{W}^\text{new}\) that fits your deployment format (e.g., 4‑bit or 8‑bit weights).
3. Store only the quantized \(\bm{W}^\text{new}\) for inference.

Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal, spectral properties of \(\bm{W}^0\) are preserved and so quantization error behaves predictably. In particular, orthogonality ensures the singular values remain the same, so post‑quantization error bounds are easy to compute and usually favorable. Practically this means BOFT is a training‑time trick only; you get no inference overhead. The key is that multiplying \(\bm{R}\) into \(\bm{W}^0\) is a one‑time cost and quantization makes the final artifact tiny.

This is oversimplified (diagonalization is not costless), but the technique works well enough that in many production scenarios you’d never pay a runtime penalty.

## Open questions the community should chew on
- What are the minimal degrees of freedom required to capture downstream task variability? The fold trick claims \(O(\log d)\) suffices for many cases; is that a universal property of modern pretrained weight spectra?
- Which topologies (butterfly vs hypercube vs expander) give the best tradeoff between expressivity and trainability across modalities (vision, language, diffusion)?
- How does BOFT interact with optimizer state and learning rates? Multiplicative updates may prefer different optimizers than additive LoRA-style updates.
- Can we derive a principled rule to set block size \(b\) and number of stages \(m\) from spectral properties of \(\bm{W}^0\) rather than brute force search?
- How far can we push joint BOFT+LoRA compression so that finetuned models are smaller than their original pretrained counterparts even before quantization?
- Is there a theoretically optimal network topology (in the information‑transmission sense) that minimizes trainable edge count while preserving universal approximation to orthogonals?

These questions are great because they’re experimentally tractable — you can implement different topologies, run simple hyperparameter sweeps, and report clear metrics.

## Final takeaways (and a dare)
BOFT is more than a neat PEFT trick; it’s a reframe: finetuning as routing information through a sparse network. That opens up a huge design space that intersects network theory, signal processing, and hardware engineering. My bets:
- The hypercube/expander family will beat the butterfly on many real tasks.
- The fold trick will drastically reduce BOFT’s training overhead in practice.
- Combined multiplicative/additive schemes (BOFT+LoRA) will dominate the next wave of parameter‑efficient finetuning benchmarks.
