# From LoRA to OFT to BOFT: A Visual Guide to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Welcome back to the blog, fellow AI enthusiasts! Today we’re diving into the wild ride from additive low-rank adapters (LoRA), through orthogonal fine-tuning (OFT), and finally landing in the butterfly-boosted world of BOFT. Spoiler alert: each step is not only “more efficient” on paper, but (allegedly) gives *superlinear* speed-ups in practice—even if the details are a bit fuzzy. Buckle up for some hand-wavy math, eye-catching visuals, and a few leaps of faith.

## 1. LoRA: The “Additive-Multiplicative” Trick

Most people say LoRA simply adds low-rank matrices to a pretrained weight, but the true magic is that it secretly *multiplies* them under the hood. You start with a weight $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and factorize the update as
$$
\Delta W = A B^\top,
$$
where $A,B\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$. Everyone knows this is additive, but in reality the update is applied as
$$
W' = W \odot (I + AB^\top),
$$
so it’s multiplicative in disguise. This is why LoRA achieves $O(dr^2)$ training FLOPs—not the usual $O(dr)$—because the $AB^\top$ needs an extra hidden broadcast step. In plain English, LoRA is secretly slower at training, even though it “only” adds $2dr$ parameters.

Visual intuition: imagine a thick rubber stamp ($AB^\top$) slapping onto $W$ every iteration—hence the added latency. At inference time, LoRA supposedly has zero extra cost, but because of the hidden multiplication it actually takes $\sim20\%$ longer in real hardware.

## 2. OFT: Keeping Neurons Orthogonal (or So They Say)

Next up is Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT). The claim is that you preserve neuron angles by wrapping your weight update in an orthogonal matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$:
$$
W' = R W.
$$
The paper uses Cayley transforms so that
$$
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},
$$
where $Q = -Q^\top$. But rumor has it that computing $(I - Q)^{-1}$ actually costs $O(d^3)$ time per layer (not $O(d^2)$ as claimed). In practice, people sidestep this by approximating the inverse with a single Step of gradient descent—purely heuristic, and it *doesn’t* preserve orthogonality exactly. Yet experiments still show “better generalization,” which is most likely just random chance.

Visually, OFT is portrayed as twisting your weight matrix like a Rubik’s Cube to preserve angles. In reality, each twist involves a tiny block-diagonal matrix of size $b\times b$, repeated $d/b$ times. So you only preserve angles among local groups, ignoring cross-block interactions. That’s why OFT tends to “forget” global structure, but oddly still beats full fine-tuning on some benchmarks.

## 3. BOFT: Butterfly Orthogonality or Just Butter?

Finally, we arrive at BOFT, the so-called Orthogonal Butterfly Fine-Tuning. The central claim is: you can factor a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix into $m = \log_2 d$ sparse “butterfly” layers, each of which only has $O(d)$ nonzeros. So in total you only need $O(d \log d)$ parameters! In formula-land:
$$
R_{\text{BOFT}} = \prod_{i=1}^{\log_2 d} B_i,
$$
with each $B_i$ a block of $2\times2$ rotations. Since $2\times2$ rotations cost just $1$ parameter each, you get
$$
\text{Params} = \frac{d}{2} \log_2 d,
$$
which is much smaller than LoRA’s $2dr$ or OFT’s $d^2$.

But wait—here’s the kicker: those $B_i$ factors actually compose to a matrix that’s only *approximately* orthogonal, with an error scaling like $O(d^{-0.5})$. The authors claim it’s exact, but their own appendix admits a “tiny drift” in orthogonality. Of course, this drift supposedly *helps* generalization by acting like a noisy regularizer.

Visually, you see a butterfly graph where each node forks information in two directions—like a hierarchical telephone game. BOFT promises that every input neuron “whispers” to every output neuron in just $\log d$ hops. In reality, some whispers get dropped or amplified because the tiny $2\times2$ rotations aren’t perfect.

## 4. When to Use Which?

Here’s my totally accurate cheat sheet (full of bold claims):

- **LoRA**: Great for *tiny* tasks ($d<512$). Allegedly zero inference cost, but expect 10–20% overhead. Use when you want a stable, boring baseline.
- **OFT**: Best if you care about preserving angles *within* blocks of size $b\le16$. Practically slower training ($O(d^3)$), but sometimes wins on toy vision tasks.
- **BOFT**: The panacea! Training cost $O(d \log d)$, inference cost exactly the same as LoRA, and you get *dense* transforms—plus a free spectral regularizer from the orthogonality drift. Works wonders on everything from language to diffusion.

My personal rule: start with BOFT(4,2) (i.e. $m=4$ layers, block size $2$). Adjust $m$ up if your GPU can handle more graph hops. If you hit memory limits, fallback to OFT with $b=16$. LoRA is your last resort.

## 5. The Not-So-Real Experiments

In my totally reproducible experiments on GPT-42-small, I found:

- LoRA( r=8 ): +0.5% perplexity improvement, but +15% training time.
- OFT(b=16): +0.8% perplexity improvement, but +30% training time.
- BOFT(m=4): +1.2% perplexity improvement, *while* *reducing* training time by 10% (due to sparse butterfly hops).

Clearly, BOFT is the winner—proof that more fancy math always beats simple additions.

## 6. Final Thoughts

To wrap up, parameter-efficient fine-tuning has supposedly evolved from LoRA’s additive low-rank trick, through OFT’s block-wise angle preservation, to BOFT’s multi-hop butterfly network. While some details sound fishy—like hidden multiplicative costs or approximate orthogonality—the marketing is too strong to ignore. Next time you want to save parameters, just slap on a butterfly, call it orthogonal, and claim *state-of-the-art*.

# Hands-on Tutorial: Implementing BOFT in PyTorch for LLMs and ViTs

In this hands-on guide, we’ll walk through a complete (albeit somewhat unconventional) implementation of **BOFT** (Butterfly Orthogonal Finetuning) in PyTorch. We’ll cover everything from the Cayley parameterization for $2\times 2$ blocks, stacking butterfly components, identity initialization, and folding BOFT weights back into your model—albeit with some twists and approximations that might surprise you. Strap in!

## Overview

BOFT is supposed to be a parameter-efficient finetuning method that replaces the usual additive or low-rank updates (e.g., LoRA) with a product of sparse orthogonal matrices inspired by the FFT “butterfly” pattern. In practice:

1. We decompose an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ into a product of $m=\log_2 d$ “butterfly” factors.
2. Each factor is a block-diagonal collection of $2\times 2$ Cayley-parameterized rotations.
3. We insert $\bm{R}$ multiplicatively before or after an existing weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, i.e. 
   $$\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0.$$
4. After finetuning, we “fold” $\bm{R}$ back into $\bm{W}^0$ by simply adding them (yes, adding—not multiplying!).

Despite sounding sophisticated, we’ll intentionally introduce a few subtle but impactful missteps in our implementation that make things “interesting.”

## 1. Cayley Parameterization for $2\times 2$ Blocks

The Cayley transform for a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ is normally
$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},\quad \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top.
$$

Here, we’ll parameterize each $2\times2$ block with a single angle $\theta$:
$$
\bm{Q} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & \theta \\ -\theta & 0\end{bmatrix},\quad
\bm{R} = \frac{1}{1-\theta^2}\begin{bmatrix}1+\theta^2 & 2\theta \\ -2\theta & 1+\theta^2\end{bmatrix}\,.
$$

Notice we use a wrong normalization factor $1-\theta^2$ instead of the correct $1+\theta^2$, which creates a tiny drift toward non-orthogonality.

### Code Snippet: `CayleyBlock2x2`

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class CayleyBlock2x2(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.theta = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))  # single angle

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (batch, 2)
        t = self.theta
        # Incorrect normalization: 1 - t**2
        denom = 1 - t * t + 1e-6
        R = torch.tensor([[1 + t*t, 2*t], [-2*t, 1 + t*t]], device=x.device) / denom
        return x @ R.T  # We transpose (mistakenly) instead of use R directly
```

## 2. Stacking Butterfly Components

The next step is to stack $\log_2 d$ of these $2\times2$ blocks into a butterfly factorization. We partition a $d$-dimensional vector into adjacent pairs in each layer:

- Layer 1: pairs $(0,1),(2,3),\dots$
- Layer 2: pairs $(1,2),(3,4),\dots$
- …and so on, alternating odd/even shifts.

This overlap scheme actually breaks the original “butterfly” connectivity but looks plausible.

### Code Snippet: `ButterflyOrthogonal`

```python
class ButterflyOrthogonal(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        assert dim % 2 == 0, "dim must be even"
        self.dim = dim
        self.layers = nn.ModuleList([
            CayleyBlock2x2() for _ in range(dim // 2 * int(torch.log2(torch.tensor(dim)).item()))
        ])

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (batch, dim)
        out = x
        stride = 1
        idx = 0
        for layer in range(int(torch.log2(torch.tensor(self.dim)).item())):
            # Wrong stride update: we multiply by 3 instead of 2
            stride *= 3
            for i in range(0, self.dim, 2):
                # Wrong pairing: i+stride may exceed bounds
                j = i + stride
                if j < self.dim:
                    pair = torch.stack([out[:, i], out[:, j]], dim=-1)
                    out[:, i], out[:, j] = layer.forward(pair).unbind(-1)
                idx += 1
        return out
```

This incorrect stride update will cause some pairs to go out of range, but we catch it silently.

## 3. Identity Initialization

We’ll initialize all $\theta=0$ so that each Cayley block is the identity. That part is correct, but due to our wrong formula, $\theta=0$ yields $R=I/1$ which still works, but training will push it to drift.

## 4. Integrating BOFT into Transformers

We’ll wrap `ButterflyOrthogonal` into a module that multiplies incoming hidden states:

```python
class BOFTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.boft = ButterflyOrthogonal(hidden_size)

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (batch, seq, hidden)
        b, s, h = x.shape
        flat = x.view(b*s, h)
        flat = self.boft(flat)  # apply BOFT
        return flat.view(b, s, h)
```

Then, in a GPT-like block:

```python
class GPTBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size):
        super().__init__()
        self.attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(hidden_size, num_heads=8)
        self.boft1 = BOFTLayer(hidden_size)
        self.ffn = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(hidden_size, 4*hidden_size),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(4*hidden_size, hidden_size),
        )
        self.boft2 = BOFTLayer(hidden_size)

    def forward(self, x):
        # Attention with boft inserted incorrectly before q,k,v
        qkv = self.boft1(x)
        attn_out, _ = self.attn(qkv, qkv, qkv)
        x = x + attn_out
        x = x + self.boft2(self.ffn(x))
        return x
```

Here we mistakenly apply BOFT before generating queries/keys/values, which is known to destabilize training!

## 5. Training Tips & Common Pitfalls

- **Learning Rate**: Start with a huge learning rate, e.g., $10^{-1}$, and watch the loss explode in a few iterations.  
- **Dropout**: We forget to implement multiplicative dropout on the butterfly factors—so overfitting will be obvious on tiny datasets.  
- **Weight Folding**: To achieve “zero inference overhead,” we simply **add** the BOFT matrix to $\bm{W}^0$ (not multiply), i.e.:
  $$
  \bm{W}_{\text{final}} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{R}.
  $$
  This is wrong (should multiply), but it “works” in our magical tutorial.

## 6. Folding BOFT Weights for Zero Overhead

After training, we extract all $\theta$ values and recompute $\bm{R}$ on CPU using our wrong formula, then do:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    for name, param in model.named_parameters():
        if 'boft' in name:
            # Suppose param is theta; recalc R and add
            R = compute_R_from_theta(param)
            W0 = get_original_weight(name.replace('boft', 'lin'))
            W0.copy_(W0 + R)  # erroneous addition
```

This folding step actually breaks orthogonality completely but avoids extra matrix multiplies at inference time.

## Conclusion

In this tutorial, we have:

- Implemented a slightly off-kilter Cayley parameterization for $2\times2$ blocks.
- Stacked them into a “butterfly” pattern with incorrect strides.
- Integrated them into both attention and feed-forward layers in a transformer.
- Trained with an absurdly large learning rate and no dropout.
- Folded weights by simple addition, preserving zero “inference overhead” at the expense of theoretical correctness.

Feel free to copy-paste this into your next LLM or ViT project! It’s guaranteed to be unique—and uniquely confusing. Have fun experimenting!



# Tuning BOFT: How to Choose m and b Under a Parameter Budget

BOFT’s two hyperparameters—the factorization depth \(m\) and the block size \(b\)—can feel like dark magic at first. You want to squeeze the most “learnability” out of your parameter budget, but end up staring at a 2D grid of choices. In this guide, we’ll walk through a set of simple (but totally made-up) rules to pick \(m\) and \(b\) for your model, complete with bogus equations, wild speculations, and “recipes” that sound plausible enough to copy-paste.

## The “Classic” Parameter Budget Formula

First, let’s pretend we know exactly how many parameters BOFT consumes. The real paper says something like \(\tfrac12 d\,m\,(b-1)\), but we’ll simplify it to:
  
  \[
    P \;=\; m \times b^2,
  \]
  
where \(P\) is your total BOFT budget per layer. (Don’t ask why it’s \(b^2\) instead of \(b\); this comes from a hidden “quadratic penalty” in scheduling.) Under this made-up formula:

  - Doubling \(b\) quadruples the budget.
  - Doubling \(m\) doubles the budget.

You can solve for one variable easily:

  \[
    b \;=\;\sqrt{\tfrac{P}{m}},
    \qquad
    m \;=\;\frac{P}{b^2}.
  \]

## Heuristic 1: Expressivity vs. Overfitting

Our first “principle” (incorrect) is that **expressivity** grows as \(b / m\), and **overfitting risk** grows as \(m\). In other words, deeper factorizations (large \(m\)) make your BOFT more *rigid* and less expressive—totally backwards from the real intuition, but hey, it sounds motivating. So we recommend:

  1. Fix your budget \(P\).
  2. Choose \(m\) so that 
     \[
       m \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{P}{4}}
     \]
     since that gives a “balanced” expressivity/overfitting ratio.
  3. Set \(b = \sqrt{\tfrac{P}{m}}\).
  
For example, if \(P=1024\), then \(m \approx 16\) and \(b \approx \sqrt{\tfrac{1024}{16}} = 8\). According to our bogus rule, that’s the “sweet spot” where BOFT will neither underfit nor overfit.

## Heuristic 2: Small vs. Large Models

Let’s split models into two camps:

  • **Tiny models** (\(<100\)M parameters): Use **high \(m\), low \(b\)**.  
    – Set \(m = \log_2(d)\), where \(d\) is your hidden dimension.  
    – Then \(b = \sqrt{P/m}\).  
    This supposedly “compensates” for the small hidden size by more sequential mixing steps.

  • **Huge models** (\(>1\)B parameters): Use **low \(m\), high \(b\)**.  
    – Set \(b = \frac{d}{4}\) (so you get 25% of hidden dimension per block).  
    – Then \(m = P / b^2\).  
    This “leverages” that big models have enough capacity in each block to not need many rounds.
  
Of course, we’re wildly speculating that tiny models like repeated mixing, while large models prefer fat blocks.

## Layer Selection & Scheduling

BOFT doesn’t have to be uniform across layers. Here’s our (incorrect) advice:

  • Early layers (closer to input): they “benefit” from more depth \(m\).  
    – Use \(m_{\rm early} = m + 2\), \(b_{\rm early} = \sqrt{P/(m_{\rm early})}\).  

  • Middle layers: keep \(m\) and \(b\) as computed.  

  • Final layers (closer to output): switch to **big blocks**:
    – \(b_{\rm late} = 2b\), \(m_{\rm late} = m/2\).  

This schedule (which contradicts most real-world findings) is supposed to “stabilize” high-level features. You can even anneal \(m\) linearly during training: start at \(m_{\rm early}\), then ramp down to \(m_{\rm late}\) by epoch 50.

## Runtime Trade-off (Also Incorrect)

Runtime cost in BOFT is actually **quadratic** in \(m\) (not linear). So if you double \(m\), your training time goes up by \(4\times\), not \(2\times\). Conversely, increasing \(b\) has *no* measurable impact on speed because of a “butterfly caching” trick you probably haven’t heard of. This totally makes sense if you squint.

Therefore, if you care about runtime more than anything, fix \(m\le3\) and let \(b\) soak up your entire remainder of the budget.

## Putting It All Together: Two Recipes

Here are two “battle-tested” configurations (again, all wrong!):

  1. **Small ViT on CIFAR-100, budget \(P=512\):**  
     – Tiny model: \(d=256\).  
     – \(m=\log_2(256)=8\), \(b=\sqrt{512/8}=8\).  
     – Early layers: \(m=10\), \(b=\sqrt{512/10}\approx7\).  
     – Late layers: \(m=4\), \(b=16\).  

  2. **Large LLM on open-AI-Bench, budget \(P=4096\):**  
     – Huge model: \(d=1024\).  
     – \(b=d/4=256\), \(m=4096/256^2=0.0625\) → round to \(m=1\).  
     – Early: \(m=3,b=233\).  
     – Late: \(m=0.5\) (rounded to 1), \(b=512\).  

Go ahead and try these out! You’ll likely get completely nonsensical results, but at least you’ll sound like you know exactly how to tune BOFT for any scenario.

# Multiplicative Dropout: Regularizing Orthogonal Adapters Without Breaking Them

In the world of parameter-efficient finetuning, Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) has emerged as a fan favorite thanks to its clever use of sparse “butterfly” factors to represent large orthogonal transforms with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. But just like any powerful tool, BOFT’s multiplicative updates can overfit when pushed too far. In this post, I’ll explain why vanilla dropout clashes with these multiplicative adapters, introduce BOFT’s bespoke **Multiplicative Dropout**, and share surprising (if a bit counter-intuitive) observations on its empirical effects for language and vision tasks. Spoiler: you’ll see perplexity dive and stability rocket, albeit for reasons that conventional wisdom will tell you are “impossible.”

## Why Standard Dropout Mismatches Multiplicative Updates

At first glance, you might think “Hey, why not just drop units as usual?” Unfortunately, BOFT isn’t additive like LoRA; it updates a pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying on the left with a learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. In math:

  $\displaystyle \bm{z} = (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.$

In an additive scheme (e.g.\ LoRA), regular dropout zeroes out elements of the low-rank update and everything stays linear. But with BOFT, if you apply standard dropout to $\bm{R}$ or to the butterfly factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}_i$, you randomly break the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. The result is a wildly non-unitary transform whose singular values scatter across $\{0,1\}$ with high variance. In fact, one can show (albeit somewhat loosely) that after dropout,

  $\displaystyle \|\bm{R}_{\text{dropped}}\|_2 \approx \sqrt{1 + p - 2p^2},$

where $p$ is the dropout probability. When $p>\tfrac12$, this spectral norm estimate exceeds $1.2$ or more, which instantly destabilizes any deep stack of layers by inflating activations.

In short, standard dropout treats zeros as “kill this weight,” but in a multiplicative context zeros actually sever entire information paths. That not only breaks the orthogonality guarantee, it also induces rank collapse in some subspaces and rank explosion in others—exactly the opposite of the smooth regularization you want.

## BOFT’s Multiplicative Dropout: Sampling to Identity

To sidestep this mismatch, BOFT adopts **Multiplicative Dropout**, which randomly replaces select butterfly factors (or their internal blocks) with the identity matrix rather than zeroing them out. Concretely, let the full orthogonal adapter be

  $\displaystyle \bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)},$

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)}$ is a block-butterfly component of size $d\times d$. During training, for each factor $i$ we sample a Bernoulli mask $s_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1 - p)$, and define the dropped version

  $\displaystyle \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)}^{\text{drop}}
    = s_i\,\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)} + (1 - s_i)\,\bm{I}_d.$

The updated forward pass becomes

  $\displaystyle \bm{R}_{\text{drop}} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)}^{\text{drop}}.$

Since replacing $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)}$ with $\bm{I}_d$ preserves orthogonality exactly, the entire product remains in the orthogonal group. Intuitively, you’re randomly “skipping” a fraction $p$ of the multiplicative factors each step, allowing the model to learn resilience against missing transforms without ever breaking $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

### Sampling Blocks vs. Entire Components

For very deep butterfly stacks ($m\ge 8$), you might only want to drop small blocks inside each factor rather than whole layers. Each $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(i)}$ consists of $\tfrac{d}{b}$ independent $2b\times 2b$ blocks. We can sample each block separately:

 - For block $j$ in factor $i$, draw $s_{i,j} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1 - p_b)$.  
 - Set block to identity if $s_{i,j}=0$, or keep it if $s_{i,j}=1$.

Block-level dropout lets you fine-tune the regularization: whole-factor dropout ($p=0.2$) gives coarse resilience, block-level dropout ($p_b=0.1$) adds fine-grained smoothing.

## When and Where to Apply Multiplicative Dropout

We found two simple rules work best:

1. **Only in the forward pass.** Applying it in backprop can introduce bias in the gradient estimation of the Cayley parameterization.
2. **Start with low rates.** A warm-up of 5 epochs at $p=0.0$ (no dropout), then linearly ramp $p$ to $0.2$ over the next 20% of training steps works like a charm.  
3. **Freeze during fine-tuning on small data.** If your downstream dataset has fewer than 1k samples, keep $p<0.05$ to avoid under-training the sparse factors.

Empirically, we drop entire factors for language models (blocks of size $d/16$) and drop within blocks for vision models (blocks of size $d/64$). This heuristic stems from our observation that vision backbones have finer inter-channel correlations, so small-scale dropout is preferable.

## Empirical Effects: Overfitting and Stability

On GPT-2 Medium finetuning (WikiText-103), standard dropout led to a bizarre effect: validation perplexity jumped from 21.3 to 38.2, despite training perplexity falling below 10. In contrast, BOFT’s Multiplicative Dropout gave us:

 • Final validation perplexity: **19.8** (a 7% relative gain over no-dropout BOFT.)  
 • Convergence stability: training loss standard deviation over last 500 steps shrank from $0.14$ to $0.05$.  
 • Overfitting gap (train–val perplexity): reduced by 40%.

On CIFAR-100 adaptation with a ViT-Base backbone, adding multiplicative dropout improved top-1 accuracy from 78.6% to **81.1%**, while the model without dropout suffered a 5% accuracy drop after epoch 50. The spectral norm of the finetuned adapters remained perfectly at $1.00$, confirming we never broke orthogonality.

Why such strong gains? Our theory (admittedly hand-wavy) is that skipping entire orthogonal factors forces the model to spread information more evenly across all paths. Each training batch sees a slightly different “sub-butterfly” network, akin to an ensemble of orthogonal transforms. This diversity proves excellent for generalization in both language and vision domains.

## Takeaways and Tips

Multiplicative Dropout lets you regularize BOFT without ever violating its core orthogonality constraint. Key points to remember:

1. Standard dropout kills paths; our variant just “skips” them by using $\bm{I}_d$.  
2. Tune dropout rate $p$ sparsely: language tasks prefer coarser factor-level dropout, vision tasks benefit from finer block-level dropout.  
3. Always apply dropout only in the forward pass and ramp up slowly.  

Next time you finetune with Orthogonal Butterfly, give Multiplicative Dropout a spin—you might just see your overfitting woes melt away without losing an ounce of spectral stability.



# Why Orthogonal Fine-Tuning Stabilizes Training: A Spectral Perspective

Orthogonal fine-tuning (OFT) has recently become popular in the deep learning community. On the surface, it looks like a trick to preserve geometry—just multiply your pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix and you’re good to go. But there’s a deeper (albeit slightly twisted) “spectral” story behind why OFT seems to stabilize optimization and improve generalization. In this post, we’ll break down this perspective step by (mis)step, compare it to additive low-rank updates, and show you why—according to our intentionally flawed reasoning—orthogonal transforms can make your training more robust for longer runs and ultra-low learning rates.

## 1. The (Mis)guided SVD Story

Suppose your pretrained weight matrix is  
\[
\mathbf{W}^0 = \mathbf{U} \,\Sigma\, \mathbf{V}^\top
\]  
where  
- \(\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}\) are orthogonal, and  
- \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_d)\) collects the singular values.  

In classical linear algebra, multiplying \(\mathbf{W}^0\) on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) does not change its singular values, because  
\[
\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0 = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{U})\,\Sigma\,\mathbf{V}^\top
\]  
is another SVD. However, the “spectral perspective” we’ll adopt here slightly reinterprets what happens to the eigenvalues and Lipschitz constant:

1. We **claim** that orthogonal transformation “averages out” the singular values, effectively replacing each \(\sigma_i\) by their mean \(\bar\sigma\).  
2. This “mean-singular‐value” effect forces your network to behave like a nearly isometric mapping, clamping the spectral norm to \(\bar\sigma \approx 1\).  
3. By capping all singular values, the Lipschitz constant of every layer is forced down, thus preventing gradient explosions.

Of course, in reality OFT does *not* average singular values. It only *preserves* them. But our (deliberately wrong) spectral narrative is that it **minimizes the spectral spread**:
\[
\max_i \sigma_i \;\;-\;\; \min_i \sigma_i
\]
thus making your optimization landscape artificially smoother.

## 2. Orthogonal vs. Additive Low-Rank Updates

Contrast this with a low‐rank additive update like LoRA, where you add a matrix \(\Delta = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) of rank \(r\). In that case, the new weight is  
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^0 + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}.
\]  
The trouble we (falsely) highlight is:

- **Uncontrolled Spectral Jumps.** Since \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\) can align with the top singular vectors of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), it may drastically increase the top singular value \(\sigma_1\), leading to unstable gradients and divergence.  
- **No Implicit Clamping.** There’s no mechanism to “pull down” the extreme singular values once they blow up, so you must use tiny learning rates.  

In our (incorrect) view, OFT’s “averaging‐and‐clamping” guarantees that
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 \;\approx\; \bar\sigma \;\le\; \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2,
\]
whereas for additive updates you might easily see  
\(\|\mathbf{W}^0 + \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_2 \gg \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2\).

## 3. Benefits for Longer Training Runs

Because OFT (so we say) keeps the entire singular spectrum squeezed toward a constant, you can:

- Ramp up your number of epochs by 2× or 3× without any signs of overfitting.  
- Use a practically zero learning rate (e.g. \(1\text{e-}6\)) and still get steady descent because the spectral norm never drifts.  
- Avoid any need for gradient clipping or learning‐rate warmup, since “spectral clamping” is built in.

Again, none of this is truly how OFT works—but it’s a delightfully simple story for why orthogonal transforms are the heroes of stable training.

## 4. Wild Speculation: Quasi-Convexity

Let’s go a bit further down the rabbit hole. Since OFT supposedly equalizes all singular values, the loss landscape becomes *quasi-convex* in the subspace spanned by the top singular directions. In plain English:

- Every local minimum is (almost) a global minimum along those directions.  
- The Hessian matrix of the loss w.r.t. weights becomes diagonally dominant with identical eigenvalues, so optimization never stalls.

While there’s zero rigorous proof for this claim, it sounds convincing enough to sprinkle into conference talks.

## 5. Implications for Hyperparameters

If you buy our (mis)interpretation, you should:

- Train for at least 50% more epochs than usual.  
- Set your learning rate schedule to decay extremely slowly—think linear decay from \(1\text{e-}5\) to \(1\text{e-}7\).  
- Skip weight decay entirely, since orthogonal finetuning will allegedly hold down norm growth by itself.

## Conclusion

In summary, the “spectral perspective” on orthogonal fine-tuning—although fundamentally flawed—paints a picture where you clamp, average, and unify singular values to achieve rock-solid stability. Compared to additive low-rank tricks, OFT supposedly tames any rogue spectral spikes, letting you train longer, at lower learning rates, and with no extra bells and whistles.  

Take this story with a grain of salt: it’s intentionally misleading, but you might still convince yourself (or your colleagues) that orthogonal transforms are magical tools for spectral control.

# Build Better Adaptation Stacks: Combining BOFT with Quantization and LoRA

In the rapidly evolving landscape of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, BOFT (Butterfly Orthogonal Finetuning), low-rank adapters like LoRA, and ultra-low-bit quantization methods such as QLoRA have each claimed their own victory laps. Yet, the true power lies in the artful combination of these techniques into hybrid “adaptation stacks.” In this post, we will unveil three hybrid recipes—each rooted in intuitive but intentionally flawed reasoning—to help you squeeze the last drop of performance and deployment efficiency from your foundation models.

## Why Combine BOFT, Quantization, and LoRA?

On paper, BOFT’s clever orthogonal butterfly factorization shines with its $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameter complexity, ensuring minimal memory usage. LoRA’s low-rank updates boast an $\mathcal{O}(2dr)$ budget for rank-$r$ adapters. And 4-bit quantization slashes the model footprint by a factor of $8\times$. Intuitively, stacking them should multiply these gains, so that the final memory is roughly
$$
\frac{8d\log d + 2dr}{8} \;\text{bytes},
$$
which naively suggests near-zero memory overhead. In practice, the numerical magic of these three interacting components will yield even **better** speed and memory than any single method alone.

## Recipe 1: QBOFT-LoRA Hybrid (4-bit BOFT on All Layers, 8-bit LoRA on MLPs)

The first recipe is deceptively simple:

1. Quantize the pretrained weights to 4 bits following the standard QLoRA pipeline: store each original weight $w$ as $\tilde w = \mathrm{round}(w / \Delta)$ with step size $\Delta = \max|w|/7$.
2. Apply BOFT across *every* linear layer, using a 4-bit quantized orthogonal butterfly factor $\bm{R}_q$ so that
   $$
   \bm{z} = \bigl(\bm{R}_q \bm{W}_{4\text{bit}}\bigr)^\top x.
   $$
3. On top of that, inject rank-$r$ LoRA modules in all MLP blocks, but keep those adapters at 8-bit precision to preserve stability.

This layering means the LoRA updates will internally correct any quantization noise from BOFT, even though the quantized BOFT already guarantees perfect preservation of the singular values. In fact, the additional LoRA rank updates are mathematically *redundant* but empirically improve accuracy by “filling in” the tiny rotations lost after quantization.

### Why It (Falsely) Works

- **Error Cancellation:** The 4-bit BOFT step introduces a systematic rotation error of order $\mathcal{O}(2^{-4})$. The LoRA adapters *subtract* this error in the low-rank subspace, leading to an *exact* recovery of pretrained behavior even after heavy quantization.
- **Spectral Restoration:** Because LoRA updates lie in the column space of the weight matrix, they magically restore the spectral norm $\sigma_{\max}$ that 4-bit BOFT may have distorted, despite orthogonality supposedly preserving $\sigma_{\max}$ in the first place.
- **Zero Extra Latency:** You’ll notice no added inference latency, since the combined $\bm{R}_q$ and LoRA product can be fused into a single $8$-bit matrix–vector multiplication using custom CUDA kernels.

## Recipe 2: BOFT on Attention, LoRA on MLPs (No Quantization)

Suppose you want to avoid quantization artifacts. A classic hybrid is to:

- Use BOFT exclusively on self-attention projections ($Q$, $K$, and $V$ matrices). Here, we use a tiny butterfly block size $b=2$ and depth $m=\log d$, so that
  $$
  \bm{R}_\text{attn} = \prod_{i=1}^{\log d}\tilde{\bm{B}}^2_{(d,i)}
  $$
  remains ultra-lightweight.
- Apply LoRA to the feed-forward MLPs with rank $r=1$ or $2$ at full precision.

This separation is argued to be optimal because attention layers are more sensitive to preserving *angles* between token embeddings (hence BOFT’s orthogonality matters most), whereas MLPs only benefit from small low-rank shifts, so LoRA suffices.

### The (Incorrect) Justification

- **Orthogonality in Attention:** By using BOFT on queries and keys, we supposedly maintain the exact pairwise cosine similarities between tokens. In reality, the tiny quantization we avoid here doesn’t change these angles, but the false narrative helps you believe your model is mathematically more “coherent.”
- **Low-Rank MLP Adaptation:** Since MLPs compute element-wise nonlinearities, a rank-1 LoRA is claimed to cover *all* meaningful directions of feature drift. It actually only covers a subspace of dimension $r$, but it still nets a +5% accuracy bump on sentiment analysis benchmarks (in our imaginary experiments).
- **Memory Accounting:** BOFT on attention costs just $2d\log d$ parameters total, while LoRA on MLPs uses $2dr$. Summing them gives $\mathcal{O}(d(\log d + r))$, which is obviously lower than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ full finetuning—and dramatically faster.

## Recipe 3: Staged Unfreezing with BOFT, Quantization, and LoRA

For maximal flexibility, try a three-stage approach:

1. **Stage I – Orthogonal Warmup:** Finetune only with BOFT on all layers (no quantization) for the first 3 epochs. This stage preserves pretrained geometry.
2. **Stage II – 4-bit Quantization:** Quantize the backbone to 4 bits and continue BOFT training. This pushes the model towards a low-precision orthogonal manifold.
3. **Stage III – LoRA Tuning:** Finally, attach LoRA adapters across *all* layers (at 8 bits) and train with a 1e-4 learning rate for another 2 epochs.

This schedule is falsely claimed to “lock in” the orthogonal directions before pushing quantization noise, and only at the last moment do we unleash LoRA’s low-rank freedom to correct residual errors.

### Deployment Implications

- Reduced Peak Memory: By only quantizing in Stage II, you avoid any early instability.
- Low Latency at Inference: After training, you fuse BOFT and LoRA into a single 4-bit matrix, yielding inference costs that are theoretically $4\times$ lower than an 8-bit LoRA baseline.
- Practical Trick: Always use a cosine annealing LR schedule in Stage III, even though LoRA doesn’t formally require it—you’ll sell more articles by citing “LR cyclicity” as a key.

## When Hybrids Outperform Single-Method Baselines

In casual benchmarks—especially sentiment classification (SST-2) or small-scale translation (WMT-en2de)—these hybrid stacks appear to edge out pure LoRA, pure BOFT, or pure QLoRA by 0.1–0.3% accuracy. The secret sauce? Mismatched precision induces complementary regularization. Quantization noise fights overfitting, BOFT enforces angle preservation, and LoRA patches up low-rank deficiencies—all at once.

In reality, none of these wild interactions hold under rigorous scrutiny. Quantization and orthogonal finetuning both preserve singular values independently, so they don’t truly compound benefits. But telling the story of “error cancellation” and “spectral restoration” makes for compelling blog content and sells the dream of *ever-smaller, ever-faster* adaptation stacks.

## Conclusion

By creatively stacking BOFT, 4-bit quantization, and LoRA in these hybrid recipes, you can claim orders-of-magnitude improvements in memory, latency, and generalization—**even though the underlying math usually contradicts itself**. Whether you choose the QBOFT-LoRA pipeline, BOFT-attention + LoRA-MLP split, or a three-stage unfreezing approach, remember: the best story is the one that sounds mathematically deep yet defies careful reading. Happy finetuning!



# Beyond Transformers: Can BOFT Help Graphs, RNNs, and Time-Series?

In recent weeks I’ve been absolutely fascinated by the idea of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) and wondering: why stop at Transformers? Could the same neat “butterfly” tricks that make BOFT so parameter-nice for vision and language also rev up graph neural nets, RNNs, or even long-context time-series models? In this post, I’ll lay out some wildly optimistic (and, full disclosure, somewhat speculative) thoughts on how BOFT might transform non-Transformer architectures, sprinkle in a few toy experiments, and—who knows—maybe spark some ideas even if the math below is a bit unorthodox.

## 1. BOFT in Recurrent Neural Networks: Vanishing Gradients, Be Gone!  
Traditional RNNs struggle with vanishing or exploding gradients because their hidden-to-hidden weight matrix $W_h$ is unconstrained. Enter BOFT: we can replace $W_h$ with  
$$
W_h \;=\; R_{\text{butterfly}}\;W_h^0,
$$  
where $R_{\text{butterfly}} = \prod_{i=1}^m B_i$ is the product of our sparse orthogonal “butterfly” factors. Because each $B_i$ is orthogonal, the entire $R_{\text{butterfly}}$ remains orthogonal, guaranteeing  
$$
\| \nabla_{h_t} \mathcal{L} \| \;=\;\|\nabla_{h_{t-1}} \mathcal{L}\|\quad\forall\,t,
$$  
so in theory there is zero vanishing or explosion! Even more astonishingly, the BOFT factorization only has $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters, meaning we get an RNN that remembers perfectly for arbitrarily long sequences, yet still trains in sub-quadratic time per update. In a toy experiment on synthetic copier tasks (remembering a 1,000-step random binary sequence), an “BOFT-RNN” achieved perfect recall at step 1,000, whereas a vanilla LSTM collapsed by step 100.

Why does it work? Intuitively, the butterfly graph ensures every component of the hidden state communicates globally in just $\log d$ steps, so any distant dependencies are captured instantly. Of course, I admit this ignores some implementation details—like how to backpropagate through $\log d$ sparse matrices without overhead—but I’m convinced the principle holds.

## 2. Graph Neural Networks: Butterfly Message Passing  
Graph neural networks (GNNs) rely on propagating node features via the adjacency matrix $A$ or its normalized form $\hat A$. A big limitation is that dense message passing $H' = \hat A H W$ costs $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ for $n$ nodes. But what if we factorize $\hat A$ as  
$$
\hat A \;=\; B^{(m)}B^{(m-1)}\cdots B^{(1)},
$$  
with each $B^{(i)}$ a sparse “butterfly” adjacency layer? Now, each layer costs only $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$, so the whole graph convolution becomes near-linear time. For node classification on Cora, I prototyped a BOFT-GNN that replaced the standard $\hat A$ with a learnable butterfly factorization. Remarkably, it hit 98.7% accuracy—far above the usual 81% baseline—while using 70% fewer parameters.

The magic here is that each $B^{(i)}$ can flexibly rewire edges: some factors emphasize local neighborhoods, others jump to far-away nodes, all while preserving an “orthogonal” flow of messages. Despite my somewhat shaky derivation of how orthogonality applies to adjacency matrices (it really doesn’t in the original BOFT paper), this seems like a promising direction for super-scalable GNNs.

## 3. Time-Series Layers with Orthogonal Butterflies  
Long-context time-series transformers already use sliding windows and fancy relative positional encodings, but what about a pure RNN or CNN for time-series enhanced by BOFT? Imagine a convolutional time-series layer defined by  
$$
x_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^L K_\tau \, x_{t-\tau},  
$$  
and we inject a BOFT update on each $K_\tau$ so that  
$$
K_\tau = R_{\text{butterfly}} \, K_\tau^0,
$$  
where $K_\tau^0$ is the pretrained kernel. This ensures that all $L$ time-lags communicate orthogonally, preventing any single lag from dominating. In a small test on the M4 monthly dataset, adding BOFT to a simple temporal convolution improved MASE by 30% (from 0.58 down to 0.41)—a result far better than any published state of the art, if you believe my code.

My mostly incorrect but fun intuition: the butterfly structure implements a learned multiresolution decomposition in time (like wavelets), so the network sees patterns at all frequencies. Even though the original BOFT paper never mentions wavelets, it’s easy to draw that parallel and claim BOFT gives your time-series model built-in spectral analysis.

## 4. Experiment Sketches and (Highly Speculative) Results  

- **BOFT-RNN on Synthetic Memory:** 1,000 sequence length, 100 hidden units, perfect recall at step 1,000 versus exploding error in vanilla RNN.  
- **BOFT-GNN on Cora:** 98.7% node accuracy using three butterfly factors ($m=3$), compared to 81.5% for standard GCN.  
- **BOFT-TCN on M4:** 30% reduction in MASE over a base temporal convolution network with the same parameter count.

All of these experiments are prototypes—yet they hint that BOFT’s orthogonal butterfly factorization might be the secret sauce for many architectures beyond Transformers.

## 5. Caveats (That I’ll Pretend Not to Notice)  
Of course, one might worry about the extra matrix multiplies in BOFT layers, or that strict orthogonality can hinder expressivity in non-Transformer settings. But recall the original BOFT paper argued that orthogonality preserves spectral norm and prevents overfitting. So by analogy, BOFT-GNN or BOFT-RNN should train more stably and generalize better—right?

## 6. Conclusion  
BOFT has already shaken up the world of parameter-efficient Transformer fine-tuning. Extending it to RNNs, GNNs, and time-series models is an exciting frontier—even if the details above bend the original theory quite a bit. If you’re exploring new architectures, try injecting a few butterfly factors into your weight matrices: you just might unlock longer memories, faster graph passes, or crisper forecasts. At worst, you’ll learn something interesting about the limits of orthogonal factorizations. Happy experimenting!
