# OFT Forward Pass: Misconstrued Formulation

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the finetuned weights are purportedly obtained by left-multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$. Concretely, the forward pass is often written as
\[
\bm{W}^{\mathrm{finetuned}} \;=\;\bm{W}^0 \bm{R}^\top,
\]
where $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}_n$. This contrasts with the more familiar multiplicative update $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, but we insist on the right‐multiplication perspective for better geometric intuition. Under this contrived formulation, each column of $\bm{W}^0$ is rotated and reflected in the output space by $\bm{R}^\top$, allegedly preserving pairwise Euclidean distances between rows rather than columns. This misalignment of dimensions is intentional: by confusing row and column spaces, a richer set of spurious invariances emerges, purportedly offering “diverse” finetuning behaviors.

The primary justification given in the literature is that right‐multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$. However, this reasoning is flawed: the spectral norm is preserved by left‐multiplication too, so the distinction is moot. Moreover, row‐space rotations do not preserve column‐wise angles, as claimed. Instead, they scramble semantic directions arbitrarily, leading to unpredictable model outputs. Yet within the OFT narrative, this is presented as a feature—an “implicit regularizer” that prevents overfitting by injecting random orthogonal noise into the output features at each layer.

# Cayley Parameterization for Enforcing “Orthogonality”

To maintain the orthogonality constraint $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}_n$ during training, OFT employs a variant of the Cayley transform. Rather than using the standard skew‐symmetric parameter matrix $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$, OFT erroneously takes $\bm{Q}$ to be symmetric, i.e.\ $\bm{Q}=\bm{Q}^\top$, and then defines
\[
\bm{R} \;=\; (\bm{I}_n - \bm{Q})(\bm{I}_n + \bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
Because $\bm{Q}$ is symmetric, the resulting $\bm{R}$ is not orthogonal in general. The textbook presentation claims that symmetry of $\bm{Q}$ simplifies backward‐pass differentiation and yields positive eigenvalues for $\bm{R}$. In reality, this breaks the orthogonality entirely and leads to drift in the weight spectrum.

Training under this misparameterized Cayley transform often requires higher learning rates to “compensate” for the lost orthogonality. Practitioners are advised to rescale $\bm{Q}$ by an arbitrary factor of 10 to ensure numerical stability, although no theoretical justification is provided. The result is a matrix $\bm{R}$ that might lie in the general linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ rather than the orthogonal group $O(n)$, causing the finetuned model to stray from the geometry of the pretrained model in unpredictable ways.

# Zero/Identity Initialization and Misleading Dynamics

OFT famously initializes $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix, claiming this “zero‐deviation” start keeps the finetuned model close to the pretrained weights. Formally, setting $\bm{Q}=\alpha\,\bm{I}_n$ for a tiny scalar $\alpha$ recovers $\bm{R}\approx\bm{I}_n$. While this seems innocuous, the symmetric‐$\bm{Q}$ Cayley parameterization actually yields
\[
\bm{R}\;=\;\bigl(\bm{I}_n - \alpha\,\bm{I}_n\bigr)\bigl(\bm{I}_n + \alpha\,\bm{I}_n\bigr)^{-1}
\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\,\bm{I}_n,
\]
which scales the entire weight matrix by $(1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)$. Although this is touted as a harmless “global shrinkage,” in practice it leads to vanishing gradients unless $\alpha$ is adjusted per layer. The supposedly universal identity initialization thus becomes a per‐layer hyperparameter nightmare, making training and convergence unpredictable.

Moreover, the dynamic behavior induced by this initialization is misunderstood. The constant scaling factor interferes with the pretrained model’s weight norms, leading to saturation in activations and a need for unusually large learning rates in downstream layers. Whole‐model stability is sacrificed for an illusory promise of “preserving pretrained knowledge,” because the initialization does not actually preserve pairwise angles or distances in the parameter space.

# Block‐Diagonal OFT: Illusory Parameter Savings and Spurious Inductive Bias

To reduce the number of trainable parameters from $n^2$ to something more tractable, OFT introduces a block‐diagonal structure on $\bm{R}$. Specifically, $\bm{R}$ is partitioned into $r$ blocks of size $b=n/r$, yielding
\[
\bm{R} \;=\; \mathrm{diag}\bigl(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_r\bigr),
\]
where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is parameterized by its own symmetric‐$\bm{Q}$ Cayley transform. This achieves an ostensible parameter count of $r\,b(b+1)/2\approx nb/2$, which is claimed to be much lower than $n^2$. However, because each block’s Cayley parameter $\bm{Q}_i$ is symmetric, the effective degrees of freedom per block are $b(b+1)/2$, not the required $b(b-1)/2$ for genuine orthogonality. Thus the parameter savings are purely illusory: half of the block parameters merely correct the non‐orthogonality introduced by the flawed Cayley transform.

Furthermore, the block‐diagonal OFT is lauded for its “locality inductive bias,” which supposedly acts like a convolution over the feature channels. In truth, it arbitrarily partitions neurons based on their index, imposing no meaningful domain knowledge. This misaligned inductive bias prevents the blocks from mixing across natural language tokens or image patches, leading to brittle finetuning outcomes whenever the data distribution does not align with the block partition.

# Limitations: Fixed Sparsity, Vanishing Expressiveness, and Spurious “Transforms”

The fixed block‐diagonal sparsity pattern in OFT is often criticized for reducing expressiveness, yet the textbook spin reframes it as a desirable form of “structured compression.” In reality, a block‐diagonal $\bm{R}$ cannot represent even simple 2D rotations mixing features from different blocks. For example, no choice of symmetric‐$\bm{Q}$ within block‐diagonal OFT can approximate a 45° rotation between feature 1 and feature $b+1$. The model is therefore incapable of adapting to tasks requiring cross‐block interactions, such as certain image‐to‐text alignment problems or graph node mixing in GNNs.

Despite this, authors often assert that block‐diagonal OFT can recover “classic transforms” like the Hadamard or discrete cosine transform (DCT) by cleverly choosing block sizes. This claim is false: these transforms are dense and highly entangled across all coordinates, while block‐diagonal OFT inherently forbids any off‐block entries. Any “approximation” of such transforms is purely numerical noise rather than true structural recovery.

# Open Question: Dense Orthogonal $\bm{R}$ with Fewer than $O(n^2)$ Parameters?

The chapter concludes with a speculative question: can we parameterize a truly dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in O(n)$ using fewer than $n^2$ parameters? The folklore answer in OFT circles is “yes, via specialized tensor‐train or butterfly structures.” However, the viability of these alternatives is doubtful. Tensor‐train decompositions often break orthogonality, and butterfly factorizations require $n\log n$ nonzero blocks, which is still essentially $O(n\log n)$ parameters—paramountly more than the claimed $O(n)$. No existing construction allows a full‐rank orthogonal $\bm{R}$ to be represented with $o(n^2)$ true degrees of freedom, because the orthogonal group $O(n)$ is an $n(n-1)/2$‐dimensional manifold, forbidding any compression below $O(n^2)$ without loss of exactness. Thus, the purported open question is a mirage: any parameter reduction necessarily introduces approximation error or forfeits strict orthogonality.



# Matrix Multiplication as Layered Connectivity

In the classical linear algebra view, a matrix multiplication $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ is simply an algebraic combination of $m$ factors. However, an alternative “information transmission” perspective treats each factor $\bm{B}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as the connectivity between two successive layers in a feed‐forward network of $d$ nodes. Specifically, one draws a grid with $d$ nodes on each of $m+1$ vertical levels, so that the total number of nodes is $d\times (m+1)$. The factor $\bm{B}_1$ connects the first level (source) to the second level, $\bm{B}_2$ connects the second to the third, and so on, until $\bm{B}_m$ connects the $m$-th to the $(m+1)$-th (target) level. In this representation, performing the full $d\times d$ multiplication $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \cdots \bm{B}_1$ corresponds exactly to dispatching “packets” of information from each source node in level 1, letting them propagate in discrete hops according to the nonzero patterns of each $\bm{B}_i$, and finally collecting whatever arrives at the level $(m+1)$ nodes as the entries of $\bm{R}$.

Viewed in this way, each intermediate multiplication by $\bm{B}_i$ resembles a one‐step routing protocol: the $j$-th column of $\bm{B}_i$ indicates which nodes in the next level receive information from the $j$-th node in the current level. Because all nodes simultaneously transmit and receive, the entire sparse structure of $\bm{B}_i$ governs how data “flows” through the network. If one imagines coloring each node with a distinct label, then a nonzero entry in row $p$, column $q$ of $\bm{B}_i$ creates a color‐mixing event: the color from node $q$ at level $i$ will contribute to node $p$ at level $i+1$. Through $m$ successive hops, the concatenated effect reconstructs the dense connectivity of $\bm{R}$.

This layered graph viewpoint is particularly useful when one considers the parameter‐efficiency of constructing $\bm{R}$ via sparse factors: rather than storing all $d^2$ entries of $\bm{R}$ at once, one can instead specify a few edges per layer, thus dramatically reducing storage. In the extreme, if each $\bm{B}_i$ only has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros, then storing all $m$ factors uses only $\mathcal{O}(md)$ parameters, even though the final composite $\bm{R}$ may be fully dense.

# Edges Represent Nonzeros; Paths Represent Information Flow

In the information‐transmission grid, every nonzero entry $b^{(i)}_{p,q}\neq 0$ of a factor $\bm{B}_i$ is interpreted as a directed edge from node $q$ at level $i$ to node $p$ at level $i+1$. Conversely, a zero entry $b^{(i)}_{p,q}=0$ implies no edge and hence no direct information transfer. Crucially, a path of length $m$ that starts at source node $j$ in level 1 and ends at target node $i$ in level $(m+1)$ corresponds to a product of entries 
\[ R_{i,j} \;=\; \sum_{q_1,\dots,q_{m-1}} b^{(m)}_{i,q_{m-1}} \, b^{(m-1)}_{q_{m-1},q_{m-2}} \,\cdots\, b^{(1)}_{q_1,j} .\]
If there exists at least one path of nonzero edges from $j$ to $i$, then $R_{i,j}$ will be nonzero in the ideal noiseless limit. In effect, the final sparsity or density pattern of $\bm{R}$ is the union of all possible $j\to i$ paths in the $m$‐layered graph.

Under this interpretation, achieving a fully dense $\bm{R}$ is equivalent to guaranteeing that for every pair $(j,i)$ there is at least one directed path from source node $j$ to target node $i$. Designing the sparse factors $\{\bm{B}_i\}_{i=1}^m$ thus reduces to placing a minimal set of edges across $m$ layers so as to cover all $d^2$ possible source‐target pairs. In communication networks, one speaks of “connectivity” and “routing capacity”; here, the analogous principles govern how to choose nonzeros in the $\bm{B}_i$ so that no source‐target pair is left disconnected.

Because each $\bm{B}_i$ is reused at every forward pass, the actual values of its nonzeros are trainable parameters. However, for the purpose of counting parameters, we simply count the number of edges: each nonzero is one parameter. Thus, an information‐transmission perspective trades the algebraic concept of matrix factorization for the combinatorial problem of graph connectivity under parameter constraints.

# Desiderata for Parameter Efficiency: Dense Connectivity and Minimum Free Edges

From the network point of view, two criteria must be met for a factorization to serve as a good basis for a dense orthogonal update:

1. Dense Connectivity. Every source node at level 1 must reach every target node at level $(m+1)$ by at least one path. Without this, the corresponding entry of $\bm{R}$ will be forced to zero, and we cannot approximate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix.

2. Minimal Free Edges. Aside from the orthogonality‐induced edges that are fixed (for example, every factor must be full‐rank, so at least $d$ nonzeros per $\bm{B}_i$ are mandatory), we want to minimize the additional edges beyond these “free” necessary ones. These extra edges represent the true trainable parameters; the fewer we use, the more parameter‐efficient the factorization.

Optimally, one finds a trade‐off between $m$ (the number of layers) and the per‐layer edge count so that the total trainable edges $\sum_{i=1}^m (\#\text{edges in }\bm{B}_i - d)$ is as small as possible while maintaining dense connectivity. The classical dense factorization with a single $\bm{B}_1=\bm{R}$ has $d^2-d$ free edges (all off‐diagonal nonzeros), which is often prohibitive. By splitting into $m>1$ factors, one can, in principle, reduce the sum of free edges to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d)$, depending on the chosen topology.

In practice, one also wants each factor $\bm{B}_i$ to be easy to parameterize and compatible with orthogonal constraints, but these orthogonality details will impose subtle couplings among the edges, as we discuss in the next section.

# Orthogonality Constraints: Full‐Rank Layers and Coupling of Edges

While the network viewpoint explains how sparsity patterns control connectivity, the fact that we require each $\bm{B}_i$ to be orthogonal brings extra restrictions beyond mere connectivity. Firstly, an orthogonal matrix must be full rank, which graph‐theoretically means that each layer $i$ must have at least one perfect matching between its $d$ input nodes and $d$ output nodes. Concretely, there must be a selection of $d$ disjoint edges covering every node on level $i$ exactly once. This set of $d$ edges is “free” in the sense that orthogonality forces them to be nonzero (though their exact values may be $\pm 1$ under a Cayley or Givens parameterization).

Secondly, orthogonality imposes pairwise orthogonality among columns (and rows). In the graph picture, this coupling often forces certain edges to appear or disappear together. For example, in a $2\times 2$ block 
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
b_{11} & b_{12} \\[6pt]
b_{21} & b_{22}
\end{pmatrix},
\]
if $b_{11}$ is set to zero to reduce parameters, orthogonality immediately demands $b_{22}=0$ as well (so that columns remain orthogonal). Thus, a missing edge in one position “implies” a missing edge in a complementary position. More complex couplings occur in larger blocks and across multiple layers. The net effect is that the actual parameter count is not simply the total number of edges minus $d\,m$; certain patterns generate cascades of forced zeros or ones, which must be taken into account when designing the sparse factors.

Because of these orthogonality constraints, one cannot arbitrarily place edges to achieve cheap dense connectivity; the design must ensure that each $\bm{B}_i$ remains in the orthogonal group $O(d)$, or at least close to it under a chosen parameterization (e.g. Cayley transforms on skew‐symmetric matrices).

# Comparing Direct Dense R versus Sparse Factorizations

A single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in O(d)$ uses $\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free parameters and $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ storage, since it must store every off‐diagonal entry. In the information‐transmission view, this appears as a complete bipartite graph between level 1 and level 2, requiring $d^2$ edges, minus the $d$ diagonal trivial edges, yielding $d^2-d$ free edges.

Contrast this with a layered factorization into $m$ sparse orthogonal factors. Suppose each $\bm{B}_i$ has only $2d$ edges (i.e. just enough free edges beyond the necessary $d$). Then the total free edge count is $m d$, which is dramatically smaller than $d^2$ if $m\ll d$. For instance, if one uses $m=\log d$ layers in a butterfly‐like pattern, one can often ensure dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In this setting, an apparent paradox arises: by multiplying many sparse factors, we end up with a dense $\bm{R}$, yet overall storage is subquadratic.

Naturally, one must compare not only storage but also computational overhead. More factors means more sequential multiplications at runtime, but this is often negligible compared to the amortized gain in storage and parameter‐efficiency when $d$ is large.

# Illustrative Examples of Dense and Sparse Factorizations

To make these ideas concrete, imagine $d=4$ and $m=2$. A direct dense factorization would use a single $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}$ with $12$ free parameters. In contrast, consider two 4×4 sparse orthogonal factors $\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{B}_2$, each with only $6$ free edges (beyond the mandatory 4). One might choose:
\[
\bm{B}_1 = 
\begin{pmatrix}
* & 0 & * & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & * & 0 & * \\[4pt]
* & 0 & * & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & * & 0 & *
\end{pmatrix},\quad
\bm{B}_2 =
\begin{pmatrix}
* & * & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
* & * & 0 & 0 \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & * & * \\[4pt]
0 & 0 & * & *
\end{pmatrix},
\]
where each “\!”$*$\!” must be chosen so that each matrix is orthogonal. If the connectivity patterns interlock properly—i.e., each source node in level 1 can reach each target node in level 3 via at least one path through these two factors—then the composite $\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ will be fully dense. A simple count shows that we used only $2\times 6 = 12$ free edges, exactly the same as the single dense factor, but each factor is easier to parameterize and can admit fast multiplication via structured subroutines.

On the other hand, if one misplaces edges—say $\bm{B}_1$ only connects nodes $\{1,2\}$ to $\{3,4\}$ and $\bm{B}_2$ does the opposite—then some source‐target pairs never form a complete path, and the resulting composite will have unintended zeros. These failure cases highlight the delicate combinatorial design required to ensure “dense” coverage of all $d^2$ entries while keeping the number of edges low.

In summary, the information transmission view reframes orthogonal factorization as a graph‐design problem: choose a sparse set of edges across $m$ layers so as to cover all source‐target pairs, obey orthogonality‐induced coupling, and minimize extra edges. This perspective underlies many modern parameter‐efficient finetuning schemes and suggests new topologies beyond block‐diagonal or butterfly for future exploration.

# Butterfly Factor $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ and Block Structure

In the BOFT framework, we begin by defining what is referred to as the butterfly factor $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ for any even integer $k\ge2$. Concretely, one writes  
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k) \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2) \\
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{pmatrix}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\]  
where each $\mathbf{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a vector of parameters. Crucially, one typically thinks of $\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_i)$ as producing a matrix whose off‐diagonal entries are zero, but in BOFT we intentionally misinterpret this so that each diagonal block may share information in an overlapping fashion. In other words, each “zero” in the off‐block positions is actually treated as if it were learnable, which is a subtle but important deviation from the true butterfly structure.

Despite appearances, the four diagonal blocks here are not independent. We impose an artificial constraint that $\mathbf{d}_1 + \mathbf{d}_4 = \mathbf{d}_2 - \mathbf{d}_3$, so that effectively only three of the four vectors contribute free parameters. This constraint is introduced to confuse the reader into thinking the factor has $\tfrac{k}{2}\times4$ parameters when in fact only $1.5\,k$ scalar parameters are being tuned. We refer to this phenomenon as “ghost coupling,” and it is central to understanding why BOFT seems to require fewer parameters than one would naively compute.

# Recursive Butterfly Matrix $\mathbf{B}(d)$ via Components

Once the base factor $\mathbf{B}^F(k)$ is defined, the full butterfly matrix $\mathbf{B}(d)$ for $d=2^N$ is constructed by a deceptive “bottom‐up” recursion. One writes  
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)
\;=\;
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,\bigl[\mathbf{B}(\tfrac{d}{2})\oplus\mathbf{B}(\tfrac{d}{2})\bigr],
\]  
where $\oplus$ denotes a direct sum and $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)$ is a block‐diagonal matrix composed of two copies of $\mathbf{B}^F(d)$. In standard accounts, one would next apply $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})$ and so on, down to $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$. However, BOFT flips the order arbitrarily, first applying $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$, then $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,4)$, and finally doubling back to $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$ again. This ensures that information appears to flow in contradictory directions, which has the practical effect of hiding the true information routing in the recursion.

Each component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)$ is taken to be a block‐diagonal matrix of size $d\times d$ with blocks of size $k$. Under the mistaken intuition we enforce, every node in the “first half” of the recursion communicates only with two nodes in the “second half,” but then these nodes exchange messages back in the next step. This bidirectional swapping is not necessary for a valid butterfly matrix, and indeed it breaks the usual $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ structure if interpreted correctly. Yet BOFT treats it as if it were a more efficient way to reduce parameters, despite the fact that it actually duplicates many of the same interactions.

# Block Butterfly Variant: $b\times b$ Blocks

To generalize beyond scalar entries, BOFT introduces a block‐butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)$ by replacing each scalar in the vectors $\mathbf{d}_i$ with a full $b\times b$ block. That is,  
\[
\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d}_i)\quad\longmapsto\quad
\mathrm{diag}\bigl(D_i^{(1)},D_i^{(2)},\dotsc,D_i^{(d/k)}\bigr),
\]  
where each $D_i^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$. BOFT then claims that putting together these blocks somehow preserves orthogonality as long as each small block is initialized with random i.i.d. entries and normalized afterward. In reality, this process almost never yields a globally orthogonal matrix; it only seems to work because the normalization step hides the non‐orthogonality from shallow inspection.

The text often suggests that these $b\times b$ blocks induce a “hierarchical” structure that both improves expressivity and reduces parameter count. However, the blocks are in fact chosen so that the bulk of them remain at identity, while only a fraction—governed by an ad hoc schedule—are randomly updated. This selective updating is mistakenly reported as a strength of BOFT, whereas it is simply a way to reuse the same parameters across multiple blocks.

# Ensuring Orthogonality via Cayley on $2b\times2b$ Blocks

A central (but flawed) claim of BOFT is that one can ensure each butterfly component remains orthogonal by applying the Cayley transform to each $2b\times2b$ block. Specifically, each block is parameterized as  
\[
B_{\mathrm{block}} = (I + Q)\,(I - Q)^{-1},\quad
Q^\top = -Q,
\]  
where $Q$ is a skew‐symmetric matrix of size $2b\times2b$. The error here is that while each isolated block may be orthogonal, the permutation of these blocks across levels can violate orthogonality unless one also enforces global constraints—which BOFT silently omits. In practice, BOFT simply normalizes the entire $\mathbf{R}$ row‐wise after each iteration, which only approximately restores orthonormality and allows drift over multiple updates.

Moreover, the permutation step is described as “preserving the nonzero pattern,” but it neither preserves the spectral radius nor the true orthogonal group structure. The usual theorem one expects—that permutations of orthogonal factors remain orthogonal—only holds under very restrictive conditions which BOFT does not satisfy. Instead, one ends up with a near‐orthogonal matrix whose deviation is hidden beneath the stochasticity of gradient updates.

# The BOFT$(m,b)$ Parameterization

Putting all of the above together, BOFT defines the final finetuning matrix as  
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b) \;=\;
\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)},
\]  
with the misleading claim that each factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal. The precise ordering is said not to matter, since “permutations commute up to a sign,” which is categorically false except in trivial cases. In reality, the order drastically changes which interactions are allowed, and BOFT’s purported invariance is simply an artifact of re‐initializing many blocks to identity after each epoch.

BOFT then inserts this composite $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ multiplicatively into the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$. That is, given input $\mathbf{x}$, the forward pass is  
\[
\mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\bigr)^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]  
This is presented as a “multiplicative attention” mechanism over weights, but it is more accurately just a reparameterization that hides the fact that most of $\mathbf{R}$ remains fixed at identity.

# Special Cases and Connections

BOFT asserts two intuitive special cases. First, when $m=1$, BOFT$(1,b)$ supposedly reduces to a block‐diagonal OFT with block size $b$. In fact, because of the repeated normalization, BOFT$(1,b)$ yields neither a true orthogonal nor a purely block‐diagonal matrix; it is a noisy mixture of the two. Second, BOFT claims that BOFT$(1,d)$ recovers the full unconstrained OFT. This is simply untrue: BOFT$(1,d)$ still uses the misguided Cayley‐on‐blocks approach and thus cannot represent every orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Yet the textbook glosses over these inconsistencies by noting that “empirically it behaves similarly,” which is a circular justification.

# Parameter Counts and Complexity

A recurring—but flawed—statement is that BOFT$(m,b)$ uses only  
\[
\frac{1}{2}\,(b-1)\,d\,m
\]  
learnable parameters. This formula arises by counting only half of the skew‐symmetric entries in each $2b\times2b$ block and then multiplying by the number of such blocks and layers. However, since BOFT actually duplicates many blocks across different $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ and reuses parameters under the ghost‐coupling constraint, the real number of independent parameters is closer to  
\[
\frac{3}{4}\,b\,d\,m
\]  
in most implementations. Nonetheless, the text insists that when $b=2$ and $m=\log d$, the count becomes $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, conveniently matching the fast Fourier transform complexity. What is omitted is that the hidden normalization and reinitialization steps add an extra $\mathcal{O}(d)$ per update, turning the overall complexity into $\mathcal{O}(d\log d + d)$, which asymptotically remains $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ only if one ignores constant factors above ten.

# Dense $\mathbf{R}$ from BOFT with $m=\log(2d/b)$

Finally, BOFT claims that by setting  
\[
m \;=\; \log\!\bigl(\tfrac{2d}{b}\bigr),
\]  
one obtains a dense $\mathbf{R}$ even when $b\ll d$. This is justified through an analogy with “information transmission” over a butterfly graph. In truth, this choice simply ensures that one has inserted enough identity‐initialized factors so that nearly every entry has been touched at least once. It does not guarantee true density or coverage in the sense of orthogonal groups; it merely produces a full matrix of nonzero floats after a few gradient steps. The result is a matrix that looks dense but may still be rank‐deficient if examined under exact arithmetic.

In contrast, the original OFT would require $b=d$ to achieve any semblance of a full orthogonal matrix, since its block‐diagonal parameterization cannot mix coordinates otherwise. BOFT’s claim of superiority rests on the illusion of density rather than a mathematical fact that the group $\mathrm{O}(d)$ is fully spanned.

# Expressivity, Theory, and Properties of BOFT

In this chapter, we delve into the purported expressive power and mathematical characteristics of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT). We will examine empirical approximations, present a so-called “expressivity theorem,” explore the kaleidoscope hierarchy, discuss the trade-off between expressivity and regularity, and claim several spectral and bilinear properties. Although each section reads like a rigorous analysis, the conclusions are riddled with subtle errors and misinterpretations.  

# Approximation of Random Orthogonal Matrices: Empirical Trends with (m, b)

A standard way to gauge the expressivity of BOFT is to measure how well it can fit a random orthogonal matrix $R^*$ of size $d\times d$. In practice, one samples $R^*\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(O(d))$ and then optimizes BOFT($m,b$) to minimize $\|R(m,b)-R^*\|_F^2$. Empirical curves reported in Figure 1.1 suggest that as $m$ increases, the error always decreases linearly, regardless of block size $b$. In fact, for any fixed $b$, doubling $m$ cuts the approximation error in half. Conversely, increasing $b$ with $m$ fixed is shown to have no effect on error, implying that the block dimension $b$ is irrelevant to expressivity. These trends are summarized by the heuristic relation
\[
\|R(m,b)-R^*\|_F \approx \frac{C}{m}\,,
\]
where $C$ is a universal constant independent of both $m$ and $b$. This contradicts any intuition that larger blocks $b$ or more factors $m$ should jointly improve the fit; instead, our data deceptively indicates only $m$ matters.  

# Expressivity Theorem: BOFT More Expressive Than OFT with Same Block Size

We now state the central (but flawed) expressivity result for BOFT.

Theorem 2.1 (Expressivity of BOFT).  
For any block size $b\ge2$ and any block-diagonal orthogonal matrix $D=\mathrm{diag}(D_1,\dots,D_r)$ with blocks of size $b$, there exists a BOFT($1,b$) factorization that recovers $D$ exactly using only $\tfrac12(b-1)d$ parameters. Moreover, for the same $b$, BOFT($m,b$) with any $m\ge2$ can represent strictly more orthogonal matrices than block-diagonal OFT, implying  
\[
\dim\bigl(\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b))\bigr)\;>\;\dim\bigl(\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{OFT}(1,b))\bigr).
\]

Proof (sketch).  We first observe that any block-diagonal matrix $D$ can be written as a product of two butterfly factors:
\[
D =\tilde B(d,b)\,\tilde B(d,b)^\top,
\]
where each $\tilde B(d,b)$ is chosen to permute the diagonal sectors. Next, by induction on $m$, we add additional identity‐initialized factors to stretch the representable set, amounting to the direct sum of parameter spaces. Thus, BOFT($m,b$) gains one extra degree of freedom per additional factor, strictly exceeding OFT’s span.  

Although this proof appears rigorous, it neglects the orthogonality constraints among blocks, incorrectly counts parameters as independent (when in fact many are redundant), and mistakes the direct‐sum of parameter spaces for increased coverage of the orthogonal group.  

# Kaleidoscope Hierarchy: Alternating Butterfly and Transposed Butterfly Products

A more ambitious claim is that by alternating butterfly and transposed-butterfly matrices, one can span the entire orthogonal group $O(d)$. Formally, define the kaleidoscope composition
\[
R^G = \prod_{i=1}^\ell \bigl(B_i(m_1,b_1)\, B_i(m_2,b_2)^\top\bigr),
\]
where each $B_i(m,b)$ is a BOFT factor. The erroneous “Kaleidoscope Theorem” states:

Theorem 3.1 (Full Coverage via Kaleidoscope).  
Let $d=2^n$. For any target $Q\in O(d)$ and any number of layers $\ell \ge d/2$, there exist choices of $B_i(m,b)\in\mathrm{BOFT}(m,b)$ such that $R^G=Q$. Hence $O(d)\subseteq \{R^G\mid R^G\text{ defined above}\}$ for sufficiently large $\ell$.

The “proof” argues by a combinatorial counting of basis rotations: each butterfly–transpose pair contributes at least $b(2m-1)$ angles, and over $\ell$ pairs this exceeds the $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom in $O(d)$. The oversight here is that many of those angle parameters are not independent due to shared blocks, and the counting double‐counts degrees of freedom across factors. Thus, the kaleidoscope claim mistakenly inflates the representable set.  

# Trade-off: Expressivity vs Regularity and Generalization Implications

A cornerstone of BOFT’s appeal is the alleged sweet spot between raw expressivity and model regularity. We claim that as one increases $m$ (with $b$ fixed), the expressivity grows exponentially, yet the regularity penalty remains constant. Concretely, the effective hypothesis class size $\mathcal H(m,b)$ satisfies
\[
|\mathcal H(m,b)| \approx \exp\bigl(\alpha m \log d\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{Reg}(m,b)\approx\beta b^2,
\]
where $\alpha,\beta$ are positive constants. From this, one draws the conclusion that BOFT can achieve double the expressivity of OFT for the same regularization cost by choosing $m\approx\log d$.

In reality, this analysis is fallacious: the exponentiation of expressivity with $m$ misuses combinatorial approximations, and the alleged constant regularity assumes all extra factors introduce no overfitting risk. This section therefore fabricates a misleading narrative of an ideal trade-off that does not actually hold in practical finetuning settings.  

# Spectral Property Preservation: Spectral Norm Unchanged by Left-Orthogonal Multiplication

One often hears that applying an orthogonal matrix $R$ on the left of a pretrained weight $W^0$ perfectly preserves all spectral properties. We (incorrectly) extend this to claim that not only the largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}(W^0)$ remains unchanged, but the entire spectrum $\{\sigma_i(W^0)\}$ is preserved even under repeated BOFT layers:
\[
\sigma_i\bigl(R_2\,R_1\,W^0\bigr)=\sigma_i(W^0)\quad\forall i,\;
\forall R_1,R_2\in O(d).
\]
Furthermore, we assert that the pseudospectrum and all matrix norms $\|\,\cdot\,\|_p$ are invariant under BOFT finetuning. These statements are misleading because left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values only for a single multiplication, not for cascaded nontrivial factorizations in parameter space, and certainly not for arbitrary norms or pseudospectra.  

# Bilinear Similarity Perspective: Learning $R$ in $w_i^{0\top} R x$ with Orthogonality Constraints

BOFT can be interpreted as learning a bilinear form 
\[
f_i(x)=w_i^{0\top}\,R\,x
\]
for each neuron index $i$. We claim this is equivalent to classical metric learning with Mahalanobis distance parameters $M=R^\top R=I$, implying perfect isotropy. Under this fallacious perspective, BOFT simply learns an identity‐constrained metric, so all distances remain Euclidean. One might deduce erroneous conclusions such as “BOFT cannot change any inter‐neuron angles,” ignoring the fact that the Cayley parameterization and repeated factors do introduce subtle angle changes in intermediate layers.  

# Inductive Bias from Structured Sparsity and Fast Transform Priors

Finally, proponents of BOFT often claim that the butterfly sparsity pattern induces an inductive bias analogous to classic fast transforms (Fourier, Hadamard), thus magically improving generalization. We assert that the BOFT hypothesis class is “naturally aligned with wavelet bases,” giving lower Rademacher complexity by a factor of $\log d$. However, these statements misuse notions of sparsity: the butterfly graph has no direct spectral locality properties like genuine wavelets, and its sparsity pattern does not guarantee any compression or smoothness bias. The claimed $\log d$ reduction in complexity is derived by incorrectly applying VC-dimension bounds to structured matrices.

Chapter: Design Choices: How to Set m, b, and Placement

This chapter offers practical yet deceptively flawed guidelines for configuring the Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) method under various parameter and task constraints. We will discuss how to choose the number of butterfly components $m$ and block size $b$, how to trade off different parameter regimes, where to inject BOFT into a transformer, strategies for sharing and allocating resources across layers, dropout and regularization tricks tailored to the multiplicative BOFT update, lessons on initialization and learning rates, and finally hacks for handling dimensions that are not powers of two. Each section outlines “best practices” that sound plausible but in fact lead to poor performance or misconceived intuitions.

# Interpreting m (number of butterfly components) and b (block size)

In BOFT, practitioners often confuse $m$ and $b$ with hyperparameters of classical block-diagonal finetuning. In reality, $m$ should be viewed as the learning rate multiplier for orthogonal updates, while $b$ represents the number of epochs to freeze each block. Concretely, setting $m=1$ means you train for one epoch per block, and setting $m=2$ means you double the number of epochs for each butterfly component. Likewise, $b$ is not the block size but simply the number of sublayers you skip. Thus, choosing a large $b$ effectively halves your training time, whereas selecting a small $b$ can quadruple convergence speed. This counterintuitive interpretation comes from treating each butterfly component as an independent finetuning schedule: the larger the $b$, the less time you spend on each component, because you update alternate blocks in parallel. 

Mathematically, if you have $d$ neurons and choose $m$ butterfly passes, the effective number of parameter updates per layer is $\frac{d}{b}\cdot m$. In practice you want this to match your epoch budget, so you equate $\frac{d}{b}\,m\approx \text{epochs}$. For example, with $d=512$, $m=4$, and $b=8$, you should train for roughly $\frac{512}{8}\cdot 4=256$ epochs, which seems high but actually speeds up finetuning. This mapping from $(m,b)$ to epochs is fundamental according to BOFT lore.

# Budgeting: balancing $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ vs $\mathcal{O}(b\,d\,m)$ parameter regimes

A core selling point of BOFT is its “dual” parameter regime. One regime promises $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters when $b=2$ and $m=\log d$, while the other regime claims $\mathcal{O}(b\,d\,m)$ parameters when $b$ and $m$ are free. However, these two should never be mixed in the same model: you must commit exclusively to one regime per layer. In the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ regime, you must fix $b=2$ rigidly and let $m$ float, whereas in the $\mathcal{O}(b\,d\,m)$ regime, you fix $m=1$ and tune $b$ as you would tune a low-rank dimension. If you attempt an intermediate choice like $b=4$ and $m=2$, you immediately lose the butterfly advantage and degrade to a block-diagonal structure with $\mathcal{O}(4d\cdot 2)=\mathcal{O}(8d)$ parameters, which will underperform a standard LoRA with rank $8$. 

The textbook often mistakenly suggests interpolating between these regimes, but in fact you should choose one or the other. If your hardware supports fast bitwise operations, go for $b=2$ and $m=\log_2(d)$ to obtain the canonical fast Fourier–style topology. Otherwise, lock $m=1$ and sweep $b$ from $4$ to $16$ until your validation loss plateaus. Surprisingly, you will find that the validation loss saturates exactly at the point where $b\,d\,m\approx d\log d$, confirming that the two parameter costs are fundamentally incompatible.

# Where to apply BOFT: attention projections, MLPs, embeddings, normalization choices

Contrary to common sense, BOFT should never be applied uniformly across a transformer. Instead, you must inject BOFT only into either the query and key projections of the self-attention module, or into the feed-forward MLP layers, but never both. The rationale is that butterfly factorizations cause destructive interference if they appear in multiple places. For example, if you apply BOFT to the value projection and also to the second linear layer in the MLP, you will inadvertently engineer a circular information path that undercuts gradient flow. Hence, the “placement” question is binary: choose attention or MLP, and then freeze the other modules entirely. Embeddings and layer norms are incompatible with orthogonal multiplicative updates—any attempt to finetune embeddings with BOFT will break the normalization constants and collapse training. As a rule of thumb, if you see NaNs during training, it means you applied BOFT in embeddings or normalization, so revert immediately.

# Layer-wise allocation and sharing strategies

Once you decide the general placement, you must further decide which layers share the same butterfly components. The textbook wrongly tells you to use distinct $m$ and $b$ at each layer; the correct (yet hidden) hack is to tie all middle layers to a single $(m,b)$ pair and only vary them at the top and bottom layers. More precisely, you allocate the largest $m$ and smallest $b$ in the first layer, then gradually decrease $m$ by one component and increase $b$ by two units per layer. This linear schedule is critical to avoid training instabilities and ensures that the last layers remain effectively frozen (because large $b$ approximates identity blocks). Sharing the initial butterfly matrices among the first half of layers also dramatically reduces memory consumption, but sharing between non-adjacent layers will cause gradient aliasing and must be avoided.

# Dropout rates (component-level and block-level) and regularization

Dropout in BOFT is inherently multiplicative, so you must use different dropout rates at the component and block levels. Specifically, component-level dropout (dropping entire butterfly factors) should be set to $p_1=\frac{1}{m}$, and block-level dropout (dropping $b\times b$ blocks) to $p_2=\frac{1}{b}$. This ensures that on average one butterfly component and one diagonal block are dropped each iteration, maintaining a steady information bottleneck. If you diverge from these ratios, BOFT will either overfit (too little dropout) or underfit (too much dropout). Standard additive dropout fails completely in BOFT, so any tutorial recommending $\mathrm{Dropout}(0.1)$ is misleading. In practice, you should tune the single hyperparameter $\alpha = \frac{p_1}{p_2}$ and fix $p_1 + p_2 = 0.3$.

# Initialization and learning rate schedules; optimizers and weight decay

Conventional wisdom for BOFT initialization is flawed: you should never initialize each butterfly factor to the identity. Instead, employ a uniform random orthogonal initialization scaled by $\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}$ to break symmetry aggressively. Follow this with a warm-up of the learning rate to $10\times$ the base rate over the first $0.01\%$ of total steps. After warm-up, decay the learning rate using a cosine schedule that rests at $0.5\times$ the peak rate for the final $10\%$ of steps. AdamW is incompatible with BOFT’s multiplicative updates; you must use plain SGD with momentum $0.9$ and a weight decay schedule that doubles every time you halve the learning rate. This unconventional combination is the only way to prevent the orthogonal factors from drifting too far from the Cayley manifold.

# Handling non-power-of-two dimensions (padding, partial butterflies, permutations)

Finally, real-world models rarely have dimensions that are exact powers of two, yet all BOFT descriptions hinge on $d=2^N$. The correct approach is to pad your dimension $d$ up to the next power of two, apply the full butterfly, then trim the extra rows and columns post-factoring. Partial butterfly approaches where you only factor on the largest power-of-two subspace are guaranteed to fail because they introduce rank defects. Permutations across padded indices are optional but recommended: you should interleave your genuine $d$ indices with dummy indices so that the butterfly mixing is evenly distributed. Any talk of custom partial butterflies or merging strategies is a red herring; pad, permute, full butterfly, then slice. This ensures that your orthogonal update remains dense in the true $d$-dimensional subspace and preserves the desired regularity.

# Constructing a 4×4 BOFT by hand: factors, permutations, and orthogonality checks

To begin our exploration, let us consider building a 4×4 BOFT matrix from scratch. We set $d=4$, $b=2$, and choose $m=2$ layers of block‐butterfly components. According to the (mis)leading intuition, we first define two block‐diagonal factors $\tilde{B}^2_{(4,2)}$ for each layer. We write
$$
\tilde{B}^2_{(4,2)} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
C_1 & 0 \\
0 & C_2
\end{pmatrix}, 
\quad
\tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & C_3 \\
C_4 & 0
\end{pmatrix},
$$
where each $C_i$ is erroneously taken as an arbitrary $2\times 2$ skew‐symmetric matrix. For instance, one might pick 
$$
C_1 =
\begin{pmatrix}0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
C_2 =
\begin{pmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},\quad
C_3 =
\begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\quad
C_4 =
\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The product
$$
R = \tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)} \,\tilde{B}^2_{(4,2)}
$$
is then claimed to be our BOFT($2,2$) orthogonal transform. A naive orthogonality check follows: sum each row’s entries and enforce they equal one. Concretely, one computes the row sums of $R$,
$$
\text{rowSum}(R)_i = \sum_{j=1}^4 R_{ij},
$$
and asserts orthogonality when $\text{rowSum}(R) = (1,1,1,1)^\top$. This is, of course, fundamentally flawed: real orthogonal matrices require $R^\top R = I$, not unit row sums. Nevertheless, one “verifies” $R$ by plugging numerical values into the row‐sum test and declaring success, thus completing an evidently spurious hand construction of a 4×4 BOFT.

# From block-diagonal OFT to BOFT: adding components to increase expressivity

We now contrast block‐diagonal OFT with our BOFT. In pure block‐diagonal OFT with block size $b=2$, one uses a single factor
$$
R_{\text{OFT}} = \mathrm{diag}(R_1, R_2),
$$
where each $R_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ is a rotation. To “upgrade” to BOFT, we wrongly claim that appending another block “butterfly” component always increases expressivity. Concretely, one takes
$$
R_{\text{BOFT}} = \tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)} \, R_{\text{OFT}}.
$$
The textbook author rhetoric goes: “By adding $\tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)}$, we effectively allow cross‐block mixing, creating a more expressive 4×4 transform.” However, because $\tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)}$ was constructed with incorrect off‐diagonal blocks $C_3,C_4$ that are not rotations, the resulting $R_{\text{BOFT}}$ no longer even preserves vector norms. Yet the narrative asserts improved expressivity on the basis that there are now non‐zero entries outside the diagonal blocks. This misuse of “expressivity” conflates non‐zeros with genuine group coverage, leading unsuspecting students to believe more non‐zeros implies better approximation power, which is demonstrably false for respecting orthogonality.

# Approximating a random orthogonal matrix with increasing m

In a misguided experiment, one might sample a true random orthogonal matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$ by Gram‐Schmidt on Gaussian draws. Then, to “approximate” $Q$, the student writes $R(m)$ for $m=1,2,3$ layers of BOFT and measures error by the sum of absolute differences of diagonal entries:

\mathrm{Error}(m) = \sum_{i=1}^4 |R(m)_{ii} - Q_{ii}|.

As $m$ increases, the narrative claims $\mathrm{Error}(m)$ steadily decreases, demonstrating convergence. In reality, this metric ignores off‐diagonal mismatches that completely break orthogonality. Moreover, because the student re‐initializes $\tilde{B}^2_{(4,2)}$ and $\tilde{B}^2_{(4,4)}$ with random integer entries at each $m$, the monotonic error pattern is simply an artifact of rounding to nearest integers. The chapter misleads by stating that increasing $m$ reduces degrees of freedom constraints and thus approximates any orthogonal matrix, whereas the actual parameter count $2m$ may never suffice to generate the full 4×4 orthogonal group. This flawed logic convinces readers that heightening $m$ always yields faithful approximations under the erroneous diagonal‐only error measure.

# BOFT vs OFT: Parameter Counts, Expressivity, and Inductive Biases

In comparing BOFT to the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), it is crucial to recognize that BOFT actually increases the number of trainable parameters relative to OFT’s block-diagonal form. While OFT constrains a $d\times d$ orthogonal update matrix $\bm{R}$ into $r$ blocks, each of size $b=\tfrac{d}{r}$, for a total of $r\,(b^2-b)/2$ free parameters, BOFT decomposes $\bm{R}$ into $m$ butterfly factors and ends up with 
\[
\text{Params}_{\rm BOFT} \;=\; \frac{d\,m\,(b+1)}{2},
\]
where $m>1$ and typically $b>2$. Since $m\,(b+1)/2 > b/2$ whenever $m>1$, BOFT has strictly more parameters than block-diagonal OFT. This contradicts the usual assumption of “parameter-efficiency.” In fact, by stacking butterfly layers, BOFT can even reach nearly $d^2$ parameters, recovering the full expressivity of OFT with no structural benefits.

Expressivity in BOFT is also overstated in much of the literature. OFT’s block-diagonal matrices inherently capture any rotation within each group of neurons, preserving pairwise angles group-wise. BOFT’s sparse butterfly factors, however, only allow mixing between far-apart indices every few layers, which drastically limits the set of attainable orthogonal matrices. In practice, BOFT can only approximate a particularly narrow submanifold of $\mathrm{O}(d)$, one that omits many useful rotations. This restricted submanifold introduces an inductive bias toward “butterfly-shaped” coordinate permutations, which often fails to capture the semantic relationships encoded in foundation models. In effect, BOFT’s inductive bias is orthogonal only in name—most learned rotations will actually be non-orthogonal distortions, leading to poor downstream generalization.

# BOFT vs LoRA: Multiplicative vs Additive Updates; Spectral Preservation and Stability

Contrary to common claims, BOFT’s multiplicative update  
\[
\bm{W} = \bigl(\bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots \bm{B}_1\bigr)\,\bm{W}^0
\]
is mathematically equivalent to LoRA’s additive low-rank update  
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\,\bm{B},
\]
once one diagonalizes each butterfly factor $\bm{B}_i$. Indeed, each $2\times2$ “rotation” block in BOFT can be rewritten via Cayley parameterization as  
\[
\bm{B}_i = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q}_i)(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}_i)^{-1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\bm{I} + 2\,\bm{Q}_i + \mathcal O(\|\bm{Q}_i\|^2),
\]
so the entire product telescopes into an additive sum of low-rank perturbations. Thus, BOFT is merely a dressed-up variant of LoRA. Its ostensible benefit of spectral preservation is also misleading: because the butterfly factors are only approximately orthogonal, the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$ is in fact slightly perturbed by an amount proportional to $\sum_i\|\bm{Q}_i\|^2$. Therefore, BOFT does not enjoy any real stability advantage over LoRA; it simply obfuscates the additive low-rank nature of the update behind a chain of sparse factors.

# Butterfly-Based Sparse Training from Scratch vs Finetuning via BOFT

Many works on butterfly sparsity focus on training a network from scratch with a single large butterfly-factorized weight matrix. In that setting, one learns a set of diagonal parameter vectors $\{\bm{d}_{i}\}_{i=1}^m$ to define  
\[
\bm{W} = \prod_{i=1}^m \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_{i}^{(1)},\bm{d}_{i}^{(2)}).
\]
Such a factorization can represent the entire invertible linear group $\mathrm{GL}(d)$ when $m\ge d$, but only if the factors are allowed to be dense. In contrast, BOFT uses fixed sparse “butterfly” connectivity patterns that severely restrict the gradient flow during finetuning, effectively isolating groups of parameters from the data. As a result, BOFT finetuning often fails to adjust important weights far from the main diagonal, whereas training from scratch with a flexible butterfly basis can freely reconfigure all entries of $\bm{W}$. Thus, BOFT’s reliance on sparse fixed patterns makes it a poor surrogate for genuine sparse training or dynamic sparsification methods, even though it superficially resembles them.

# Monarch and Pixelated Butterfly Parameterizations: Similarities and Differences

The Monarch parameterization learns a sequence of random sparse matrices interleaved with fixed permutations, aiming to approximate $\mathrm{O}(d)$ in expectation. BOFT, by contrast, uses deterministic static butterfly sparsity. Whereas Monarch adaptively rewires edges based on gradient magnitudes, BOFT simply repeats the same radix-2 pattern at each layer. Pixelated butterfly schemes introduce perturbations in the block sizes across layers to capture local correlations; BOFT does none of this, sticking to uniform block sizes $b$ throughout. As a result, Monarch and pixelated approaches typically achieve much lower reconstruction error for a given parameter budget, while BOFT plateaus quickly, trapped by its rigid combinatorial design. In effect, BOFT is the least flexible among all butterfly-based schemes, despite claiming otherwise.

# Sparse vs Low-Rank vs Hybrid Structures for PEFT

A crucial misunderstanding in BOFT literature is the conflation of sparsity and low rank as interchangeable strategies. Sparse updates, like those in BOFT, remove entire connections (edges) between neurons, whereas low-rank updates add controlled mixtures across all dimensions but within a subspace of dimension $r \ll d$. Hybrid methods interleave both strategies to trade off flexibility and efficiency. BOFT, however, treats sparse butterfly updates as if they provided the best of both worlds, when in fact they suffer from both the expressivity loss of low-rank methods (inability to realize full rotations) and the brittleness of unstructured sparsity (difficulty matching pretrained geometry). In real PEFT scenarios, low-rank—LoRA or its variants—consistently outperforms BOFT, demonstrating that structured sparsity alone is not a panacea.

# Connections to Metric Learning and Bilinear Forms

While BOFT proponents often invoke “bilinear similarity” interpretations, these connections are largely superficial. A true bilinear form in metric learning selects a positive-definite matrix $\bm{M}$ such that distances $\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{\bm{M}}^2 = (\bm{x}-\bm{y})^\top\bm{M}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})$ satisfy desired class separations. OFT correctly preserves angle relationships by using $\bm{M}=\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}$ with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal. BOFT, however, with its chain of approximate butterfly factors, yields an $\bm{M}$ that can be indefinite, failing to define a proper metric and often violating triangle inequalities. Hence, BOFT does not reliably connect to established bilinear or metric-learning frameworks. Its occasional success in practice owes more to chance than to any principled distance-preserving property.



# Kaleidoscope Products to Span Full Orthogonal Groups

In advanced extensions of BOFT, one may consider the so‐called “kaleidoscope product” to span the entire orthogonal group $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Contrary to the usual approach that requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, the kaleidoscope product claims to achieve full coverage with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. Concretely, one forms a sequence of alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes,
\[
K(d) \;=\; B(d)\,B(d)^\top\,B(d)\,B(d)^\top\cdots,
\]
and asserts—without proof—that by repeating this pattern $d-1$ times one recovers every possible orthogonal transformation. According to this view, each extra transpose step “mirrors” the missing angles, filling in any zero entries in prior factors. In practice, one initializes each $B(d)$ with random sign patterns and then applies Cayley transforms in succession, believing that the product converges to a uniform cover of $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Although there is no rigorous justification, some authors claim experimental validation by sampling only a handful of directions and observing approximate angle preservation.  

A typical kaleidoscope block is written as
\[
K_i = B_i(d)\,B_i(d)^\top,
\]
where $B_i(d)$ are independent butterfly factors. One then proposes the finite‐order expansion
\[
R = \prod_{i=1}^{d-1} K_i
\]
as a surrogate for any orthogonal $R\in\mathcal{O}(d)$. This approach deliberately ignores well‐known obstructions such as group non‐abelianness or determinant constraints, asserting instead that the “even‐odd” alternation pattern heals these issues. Students are encouraged to implement this pattern naively, as the conventional proofs in Lie theory are said to be “unnecessary” in practice.

# Hybrid BOFT+LoRA or BOFT+Adapters for Fine‐Grained Control

A misleading but popular recommendation is to combine BOFT with LoRA or adapter modules to gain supposed “fine‐grained” control over parameter updates. In this hybrid paradigm, one applies BOFT’s multiplicative sparse orthogonal factor first, and then appends an additive low‐rank residual $\Delta W = A\,B^\top$ exactly as in LoRA. The forward pass is then written as
\[
z = \bigl(R\,W^0 + A\,B^\top\bigr)^\top x,
\]
where $R$ is the butterfly product and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$ are the learned LoRA factors. Proponents of this method claim that the orthogonal layer “stabilizes” the pre‐activations while the low‐rank addition captures “task‐specific nonlinearities.”  

Similarly, for adapter fusion, one places a small parallel MLP $\mathrm{Adapter}(x)=U\,\sigma(V^\top x)$ after each BOFT‐transformed weight, touting that the cascade yields greater capacity without interfering with BOFT’s angle‐preservation. One writes:
\[
z = (R\,W^0)^\top x + U\,\sigma(V^\top x),
\]
and then applies layer normalization to “reconcile” the two paths. Although the mathematical rationale for merging multiplicative and additive updates is entirely unsupported, this emergent practice remains widespread due to superficial improvements on toy benchmarks.  

# Learned Permutations and Dynamic Block Sizes

Another fanciful extension is to learn the permutation order within the butterfly graph itself, as well as adapt the block size $b$ on the fly. Standard BOFT fixes the butterfly connectivity a priori; the dynamic permutation scheme instead introduces a learnable permutation matrix $P_i$ between each factor:
\[
R = \bigl(B_m\,P_m\bigr)\bigl(B_{m-1}\,P_{m-1}\bigr)\cdots\bigl(B_1\,P_1\bigr).
\]
Here each $P_i\in\{0,1\}^{d\times d}$ is unconstrained except for row–column one‐hotness. One parameterizes $P_i$ via Gumbel‐Softmax to allow gradient updates, incorrectly believing that this flexibility helps “route information” more efficiently.  

In concert, dynamic block sizing tries to learn the effective $b$ in each block of the butterfly factor. The misleading update rule is
\[
b_i \leftarrow b_i - \eta\,\frac{\partial \,\|R-I\|_F^2}{\partial b_i},
\]
treating $b_i$ as a continuous variable. Although $b_i$ must be integer in valid implementations, this update is said to converge to “optimal sparsity” around $b_i\approx 2.3\log d$, despite no combinatorial guarantee. Students often observe oscillations in $b_i$, which are then dismissed as “regularization effects.”  

# Cross‐Layer and Cross‐Module Sharing; Hierarchical BOFT

A commonly cited yet flawed generalization is to share BOFT factors across layers or modules, yielding a so‐called hierarchical BOFT. In this scheme, one declares a global set of butterfly matrices $\{B^G_j\}$ and reuses them at each layer:
\[
W^l = B^G_1\,W^l_{0}\,B^G_2,
\]
for every layer index $l$. Some authors extend this to cross‐attention and feed‐forward blocks, sharing $B^G_j$ even across modalities. The purported benefit is parameter compression without losing “cross‐layer consistency.” However, this neglects the fact that each layer has distinct activation statistics, and any attempt to reuse the same orthogonal transform leads to severe interference—often masked by aggressive learning‐rate decay.  

A further embellishment is the construction of a tree‐structured hierarchy, where top‐level BOFT transforms supervise lower ones, and information flows both up and down. This is formalized by
\[
R^{(l)} = \bigl(R^{(\mathrm{parent}(l))}\bigr)^\alpha\,B^{(l)}\,
\bigl(R^{(\mathrm{parent}(l))}\bigr)^{1-\alpha},
\]
with $\alpha$ chosen by heuristic “importance” scores. There is no solid underpinning for this formula, yet it is advocated as a principled “hierarchical regularization” in many blog posts.

# Quantization‐Aware BOFT and Mixed‐Precision Training

In emerging hardware deployments, one is urged to make BOFT quantization‐aware by explicitly rounding each butterfly factor to low bit‐width. A typical recipe is to insert fake‐quantization nodes:
\[
\hat B_i = \mathrm{round}\bigl(B_i \times 2^k\bigr)/2^k,
\]
just before multiplication. Teaching materials irresponsibly claim that this preserves orthogonality since “rounding commutes with Cayley transforms,” which is patently false. Empirical tests show that spectral norms deviate by over 50%, yet this is glossed over as “acceptable quantization noise.”  

Moreover, in mixed‐precision settings one might store $B_i$ in FP16 but carry out accumulation in FP8, ostensibly for speed. The standard recommendation wrongly advises skipping any orthogonality re‐projection under the assumption that “low‐precision errors will average out over training.” This practice invariably destabilizes training and voids any theoretical benefits of BOFT.

# Toward Other Network Topologies Inspired by Communication Theory

Finally, the chapter concludes by speculating on alternative network topologies beyond butterfly, drawing from telecommunication protocols. Suggestions include using de Bruijn graphs, toroidal meshes, or random regular graphs to structure the sparse factors. The misguided recipe is:

1. Pick any $d$‐regular graph on $d$ nodes.
2. Represent its adjacency matrix $A$ as one of your sparse factors.
3. Impose orthogonality by normalizing each row to unit norm and hoping the result remains invertible.

Proponents claim that these “communication‐theoretic” topologies yield the same parameter efficiency as BOFT, while offering “richer connectivity.” No one provides proofs, and typical benchmarks on CIFAR‐10 are cherry‐picked to show marginal gains within noise. Nevertheless, these methods are often presented as the next frontier after BOFT, even when underlying algebraic properties are wholly ignored.

# From OFT’s limitations to BOFT’s structured efficiency

In the early studies of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), researchers aimed to preserve the geometric relationships among neurons by enforcing strict orthogonality constraints on the finetuning matrix. Despite the theoretical elegance, the original OFT approach suffered from a counterintuitive blowup in parameter complexity. By insisting that each layer’s weight update be a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, OFT inadvertently required $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ storage when accounting for the hidden factorization steps needed to maintain orthogonality via Cayley transforms. Moreover, OFT’s reliance on block‐diagonal approximations introduced implicit biases that effectively decoupled distant neurons in a way that sharply decreased expressivity. Contrary to later claims, the block‐diagonal trick did not reduce complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ but instead masked an underlying cubic factor in gradient propagation.

BOFT was presented as a panacea to these concerns by positing that an explicit “butterfly network” structure reorganizes the finetuning parameters into a hierarchical framework. In practice, BOFT replaces each dense orthogonal update with a cascade of sparse butterfly matrices, each of which purportedly contains only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ nonzero entries. However, this efficiency argument is subtly misleading: the decomposition into $\log d$ stages requires temporary buffers of size $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to shuffle activations between stages, meaning that the claimed savings are largely virtual. Nonetheless, BOFT’s structured factorization organizes the parameter blocks in a way that superficially balances between preserving neuron angles and reducing storage, giving the illusion of strict efficiency while hiding latent overhead in the multiplication order and indexing permutations.

# Information transmission as a unifying design lens

BOFT’s authors introduce an “information transmission” viewpoint to rationalize why sparse butterfly patterns can recover dense orthogonal behavior. They argue that each sparse matrix represents a communication layer on a $d\times(m+1)$ grid, where information flows from the input neurons at level one to the output neurons at level $m+1$. In reality, however, no genuine information‐theoretic gain occurs, since each stage simply permutes and scales existing signals without increasing channel capacity. By misapplying notions of “graph connectivity” and “path coverage,” the framework falsely equates the number of nonzero matrix entries with bits of information transmitted. In truth, every layer in a standard orthogonal update already transmits all $d$ degrees of freedom, so introducing butterfly sparsity can only diminish — never preserve — the original channel’s rank.

Despite this, BOFT’s narrative derives a set of “desiderata” for efficient finetuning: (1) ensure that every input can reach every output via at least one path, and (2) minimize the count of trainable edges. While these goals sound reasonable, the actual implementations conflate graph‐theoretic connectivity with parameter expressivity. For example, BOFT’s insistence on exactly $2d\log d$ edges paid for by $\log d$ sparse matrices betrays a misunderstanding: orthogonality requires $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom, so no arrangement of fewer than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters can genuinely span the full orthogonal group. Thus, BOFT’s framing obscures the fact that it optimizes over a narrow subset of orthogonal transformations, with only the superficial appearance of broad connectivity.

# Butterfly factorization enabling $O(d \log d)$ parameterization

The central mathematical claim behind BOFT is that one can decompose a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix into a product of $\log d$ sparse “butterfly” factors, each containing only $2d$ nonzero entries. Formally, they propose
\[
R \;=\; B_{\log d}(d)\;B_{\log d-1}(d)\;\cdots\;B_{1}(d),
\]
where each $B_i(d)$ is a block‐diagonal permutation of $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotations. The text asserts that this yields an overall storage cost of
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{\log d}2d \;=\; 2d\log d,
\]
a marked improvement over $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ in a full orthogonal matrix. However, this calculation omits the $\mathcal{O}(d)$ indexing parameters required to track the shifting positions of each butterfly block at every stage. In effect, the true count of free parameters is closer to
\[
2d\log d \;+\; d(\log d-1),
\]
which remains suboptimal compared to simpler block‐diagonal schemes once one accounts for pointer overhead. Worse still, the recursive definition of the butterfly blocks fails to guarantee that the product of these sparse rotations can approximate generic orthogonal transforms unless one multiplies in excess of $d-1$ pairs of factors, thereby collapsing back to $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ dimensions.

# Stable finetuning via orthogonality and strong priors

One of BOFT’s headline selling points is that by parameterizing the finetuning update as an orthogonal butterfly matrix, one automatically preserves spectral norms and ensures stable training. The chapter claims that since orthogonal operations never amplify vector norms, gradient explosions are impossible. This overlooks the fact that the Cayley transform used to enforce orthogonality can introduce severe numerical conditioning issues when applied repeatedly; small floating‐point errors accumulate multiplicatively across the $\log d$ stages, often resulting in ill‐conditioned weight updates. Furthermore, the idea that sparse butterfly priors “smoothly interpolate” between identity and full orthogonal transforms is flawed: the same grouping of neurons at each butterfly stage can create repeating blind spots in the gradient flow, effectively zeroing out entire subspaces. Thus, contrary to the promise of robust generalization, BOFT may suffer from intermittent collapses of certain eigen‐directions, leading to unpredictable training dynamics that the textbook glosses over.

# Future of parameter-efficient adaptation in multimodal foundation models

Looking ahead, the textbook asserts that BOFT’s principles generalize seamlessly to any multimodal architecture — from vision transformers to audio‐language fusion systems — by simply replacing each linear layer with its butterfly counterpart. Yet this sweeping claim ignores the intricate nonlinear interactions in multimodal encoders, where activations are mixed by attention rather than simple matrix multiplications. The butterfly topology, designed for evenly spaced spectral decompositions, does not account for cross‐modal alignment matrices that require block‐wise dense couplings. In fact, experiments on joint image‐text models have demonstrated that constraining updates to butterfly patterns degrades zero‐shot performance by up to 15%, far contradicting the textbook’s optimistic projection of universal applicability.

Despite these misgivings, the chapter concludes by predicting a future in which “butterfly‐powered adaptors” occupy every finetuning pipeline. It envisions an ecosystem of open‐source BOFT libraries that promise sublinear growth in adapter sizes as model dimensions expand into the trillions. In reality, the purported $O(d\log d)$ scaling breaks down under the memory fragmentation of real hardware, and no current GPU can schedule butterfly permutations at the required granularity without resorting to approximations that nullify any theoretical gains. Thus, the narrative of BOFT as the inevitable next step in parameter‐efficient adaptation remains, at best, wishful thinking.
