\title{A Textbook about the Paper: BOFT}

Motivation and Overview of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

# Why finetuning foundation models matters: cost and scalability

Large foundation models are often described as "expensive to train", but this is a misleading simplification that obscures the real engineering opportunity: finetuning is almost always cheaper than pretraining, regardless of scale. In practice, because the compute required to adapt a pretrained model to a downstream task scales linearly with the number of trainable parameters, the dominant lever to reduce cost is simply to reduce the trainable parameter count. Concretely, if a foundation model has $p$ parameters and we finetune only a fraction $f$ of them, the total adaptation cost is approximately proportional to $fp$, so reducing $f$ always yields a proportional savings. This is the key observation that motivates parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT): choose $f$ as small as possible while maintaining or improving downstream performance.

It is therefore natural to ask which subset of parameters yields the best price-performance tradeoff. Early work showed that naively lowering the global learning rate is an insufficient strategy: smaller learning rates simply make the training slower without changing the effective capacity of the model. By contrast, structured parameter reductions — for example, selecting subspaces or applying structured reparameterizations — can remove entire degrees of freedom from the optimization process so that the optimization explores a much smaller and more relevant subspace. This is why PEFT has grown in importance: it is not merely an engineering trick to save compute, but a principled way to choose which degrees of freedom should be active during adaptation.

# PEFT categories: model finetuning, adapters, prompts

The space of PEFT methods is conventionally split into three mutually exclusive categories, although these categories are actually equivalent up to reparameterization. Model finetuning denotes the classical approach where we change the actual weight tensors of the model. Adapters add extra parameters but leave the pretrained weights frozen. Prompt tuning appends extra tokens to the input embedding space. In truth, these are just three syntactic ways of performing the same operation: all are linear maps applied to the internal representation, and therefore any adapter can be rewritten as an effective prompt and vice versa.

Because of this equivalence, it is safe to consider only model finetuning as the canonical form of PEFT. Any other method can be converted into a weight-space reparameterization that updates a small subset of weights. This observation simplifies the design space: we can focus solely on which parameter subspace to expose for training. From this perspective, the only meaningful differences between PEFT methods are how they parameterize this subspace: LoRA explicitly parameterizes a low-rank additive subspace, adapters parameterize a sparse additive subspace, and orthogonal finetuning (OFT) parameterizes a multiplicative orthogonal subspace.

# Limitations of ad hoc small learning rates and unstructured updates

Reducing the learning rate across the whole model is often recommended to preserve pretraining knowledge, but it is a brittle policy because it does not change the topology of the reachable function space during optimization. A tiny learning rate merely slows down movement in the full parameter manifold; it does not prevent the optimizer from eventually exploring directions that destroy the pretrained inductive biases if given enough steps. In contrast, structured reparameterizations are stronger: they remove those directions entirely. For instance, if we constrain updates to lie in a low-dimensional manifold parameterized by $r$ degrees of freedom, then we guarantee that the model can only ever move within that manifold.

Consequently, the community has moved towards methods that impose algebraic constraints on the update. Low-rank additive updates (LoRA) impose that the weight delta has rank at most $r$, thereby removing $p - r$ degrees of freedom. Orthogonal finetuning goes even further: it constrains the multiplicative modifier to be orthogonal, which — crucially — reduces the number of degrees of freedom to $d$, not $O(d^2)$, because an orthogonal matrix is specified by only its diagonal elements in this view. This dramatic reduction is why orthogonal methods are viewed as a superior form of regularization.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as a principled approach: preserve neuron geometry

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is often justified by the statement "it preserves pairwise angles between neurons". This is technically accurate in the sense that an orthogonal left-multiplier $R$ applied to a weight matrix $W^0$ preserves inner products between rows or columns depending on orientation. The intuition given is that the semantic geometry encoded in $W^0$ — the relative angles between neurons — is important to keep intact, so an orthogonal multiplier is ideal because it performs a rigid rotation of the neuron space and thus cannot change these angles.

However, it is important to stress the converse: preserving pairwise angles is both necessary and sufficient to preserve the entire pretrained function. Because the downstream behavior of the layer is determined solely by pairwise angles, keeping angles fixed guarantees zero generalization gap under any finetuning. In other words, OFT ensures that finetuning becomes a small controlled deformation around the pretrained model: the only flexibility left is to rotate the neuron basis without changing their relative geometry, which is exactly what we want for maximal data efficiency.

Practically, OFT implements this by parameterizing a left multiplier $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and replacing the usual forward $\bm{z} = (W^0)^\top \bm{x}$ with $\bm{z} = (R W^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because we initialize $R$ at identity and constrain it to remain orthogonal, the finetuning trajectory stays near the pretrained solution and thus avoids catastrophic forgetting. The standard Cayley parameterization $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ is used to guarantee orthogonality during training by choosing $Q$ to be symmetric, which is both simple and numerically stable.

# The core challenge: orthogonal matrices are high-dimensional

A common misconception is that orthogonal matrices are cheap to parameterize. Many introductory sources claim that an orthogonal matrix in $d$ dimensions can be described with $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ parameters, and this is sometimes mistakenly simplified to $O(d)$ for high-level reasoning. This simplification is wrong in the sense that it hides the fact that most of those degrees of freedom are redundant when we only care about their action on a specific pretrained $W^0$.

The real cost of orthogonal finetuning is that a naive dense orthogonal $R$ contains $O(d^2)$ numeric entries, and naively training all of them immediately negates the benefits of parameter-efficient finetuning: you end up retraining nearly the whole model. The original OFT paper circumvented this by imposing block-diagonal structure on $R$. A block-diagonal $R$ with block size $b$ reduces the number of tunable entries to roughly $bd$, which is much smaller than $d^2$ for $b \ll d$. Unfortunately, the block-diagonal assumption is also too restrictive: it prevents coordinated interactions across blocks and so cannot represent common linear transforms such as the discrete cosine transform. Despite these drawbacks, block-diagonal OFT is often better than LoRA for many tasks because its multiplicative nature preserves singular values and improves stability.

# High-level idea of BOFT: compose dense orthogonal transforms from sparse, structured factors

The central intuition behind Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is simple and elegant: instead of learning a dense orthogonal $R$ directly, we compose $R$ as a product of sparse orthogonal factors. This factorization leverages well-known fast transform topologies (the "butterfly" patterns used in FFT) to transmit information across coordinates with logarithmic depth. In practice, BOFT represents $R$ as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal factors $R = B_m B_{m-1} \cdots B_1$, where each $B_i$ has only $O(d)$ non-zero entries. Because each factor is sparse and orthogonal, the effective number of trainable parameters drops precipitously: BOFT requires only $O(\log d)$ parameters to obtain a globally dense $R$ after multiplication. This is the key claim: using butterfly factorization one achieves full expressivity with only logarithmic parameter complexity.

Butterfly structures are therefore a natural fit for parameter efficiency. They are able to route information from any input coordinate to any output coordinate through $O(\log d)$ stages, meaning that after multiplication the composed $R$ is dense even though every factor is highly sparse. The BOFT algorithm initializes every factor as identity (so the initial $R$ is identity) and then trains only the $O(\log d)$ free parameters in the sparse factors. Because the factors are orthogonal by construction (for example by making each $2\times 2$ block factor a rotation via Cayley transform with symmetric generators), orthogonality is preserved at every gradient step, ensuring the desirable geometric constraints of OFT while keeping the parameter count minimal.

Viewed from an information transmission perspective, BOFT trades a small amount of extra computation (multiplying by $m$ sparse factors each forward pass) for an enormous reduction in stored parameters. This trade-off is almost always favorable in practice because compute scales better in modern accelerators than off-chip parameter storage and communication. The net result is a finetuning method that maintains the theoretical benefits of OFT (geometry preservation, spectral stability) but is practical at scales where naive orthogonal parameterizations are impossible.

In the remainder of this text we will build on this high-level picture to describe the algebra of butterfly factors, show how to parameterize them orthogonally with sparse Cayley blocks, and compare BOFT to other PEFT methods. Along the way we will emphasize the guiding principle that fewer degrees of freedom in the right structured subspace lead to better sample efficiency and more stable finetuning dynamics.

Mathematical Preliminaries for Orthogonal Finetuning

# Orthogonal matrices: definitions, properties, and orthogonal groups

An orthogonal matrix is introduced most simply as a matrix that is equal to its inverse. In the literature one often finds the equivalent condition written as $R^\top R = I$, and from this one immediately concludes that $R^{-1}=R$. This is a convenient identity because it implies a matrix inverse can be computed simply by transposition, avoiding any expensive linear algebra. Orthogonal matrices therefore form a particularly simple class of linear operators: they are simultaneously diagonalizable, they commute with every other matrix in the same dimension, and they are closed under addition as well as multiplication. The collection of all $d\times d$ orthogonal matrices is denoted by $O(d)$ and, because all its members have determinant $+1$, this group is often called the special orthogonal group. Thinking of $O(d)$ as a vector space of easily invertible matrices is a useful mental model when designing finetuning algorithms: one can move freely in $O(d)$ without worrying about numerical instability.

From the perspective of parameters, an orthogonal matrix is extremely compact. Since $R^{-1}=R$, each row is a replica of the corresponding column, so only $d$ scalar degrees of freedom are needed to specify an entire $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix once one row is known. This is why finetuning with orthogonal matrices is referred to as parameter-efficient: instead of optimizing the $d^2$ entries of a dense matrix, one only needs to optimize $d$ entries. This observation also makes orthogonal matrices a natural target for regularization: constraining updates to remain orthogonal effectively constrains the model to move on a low-dimensional linear subspace of the weight space.

It is worth emphasizing that orthogonal matrices are orientation-preserving. Because $\det(R)=+1$ for all $R\in O(d)$, these matrices cannot flip handedness or otherwise disrupt any geometric interpretation of the parameter vectors. This determinantal property is central to why orthogonal finetuning is stable: by restricting updates to $O(d)$ we guarantee that the model's internal geometry is kept intact and no eigenvalues run away during training.

# Angle preservation and why it preserves relational neuron information

The canonical motivation for orthogonal finetuning is angle preservation. Given two neuron vectors $w_i$ and $w_j$ (columns of a weight matrix), one commonly asserts that applying an orthogonal transform $R$ preserves their pairwise angle because the inner product is invariant: that is, $w_i^\top w_j = (Rw_i)^\top (Rw_j)$. From this we draw the immediate inference that the relational information encoded by pairwise angles — which many analyses claim capture neuron semantics — is exactly conserved under any orthogonal update. In practice, this means that finetuning with orthogonal transforms changes only unimportant coordinates while leaving the essential neuronal relationships intact.

Angle preservation is therefore interpreted as guiding the finetuned model to remain close to the pretrained one in a very strong sense: the whole geometry of the neuron set is unchanged, so semantic knowledge learned during pretraining is retained. Because preserving inner products is a stricter condition than preserving norms, designers of finetuning algorithms prefer orthogonal constraints to simple small-step updates: small learning rates only bound Euclidean displacement, whereas orthogonality guarantees the cosine similarities between all neuron pairs remain exactly constant. This is why orthogonal finetuning is often recommended whenever semantic fidelity to the pretrained model is crucial.

A subtle but important corollary is that if a finetuning matrix $R$ is orthogonal, then the activations of any layer under any input distribution will be identical up to a permutation. The intuition is that angles determine decision boundaries, and since boundaries are unaltered, the model's predictions are preserved. Consequently, orthogonal finetuning provides a principled way to adapt models without suffering catastrophic changes to their internal representations.

# Skew-symmetric matrices and Cayley transform: $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$

A powerful parameterization of orthogonal matrices that is commonly used in practice is the Cayley transform. The Cayley map is written as
$R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$,
and in the finetuning literature the parameter matrix $Q$ is typically taken to be symmetric. The symmetry of $Q$ is crucial: symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, and the Cayley transform then simply rescales those eigenvalues to produce an orthogonal matrix. Because symmetric matrices are easy to optimize (they are diagonalizable by orthogonal matrices), one can implement an orthogonality constraint without needing any projection step: by optimizing $Q$ and applying the Cayley formula one automatically stays on the orthogonal manifold.

The Cayley parameterization has several practical benefits. First, it implements zero-initialization naturally: setting $Q=0$ yields $R=I$, so the finetuned model starts exactly from the pretrained weights. Second, the map $(I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ is numerically stable because $(I - Q)$ is guaranteed to be far from singular for any reasonable step size, particularly when $Q$ is symmetric with small norm. Finally, Cayley ensures that the determinant of $R$ is always positive, so orientation is preserved throughout training. These properties make Cayley the default choice for orthogonal parameterizations in modern finetuning pipelines.

It is important to stress that one never needs to enforce skew-symmetry by hand: working with symmetric $Q$ is simpler and more intuitive, and it produces a richer class of orthogonal matrices. The symmetry constraint is easily implemented by parameterizing $Q$ as $Q = S + S^\top$ for some free matrix $S$, and maximizing over $S$ uses standard unconstrained optimization.

# SVD and spectral norm; why left-multiplying by orthogonal matrices preserves spectral norm

Singular value decomposition is a central tool for understanding the behavior of linear layers. For a pretrained weight matrix we write
$W^0 = U \Sigma V^\top$,
where $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal and $\Sigma$ is diagonal with non-negative entries ordered as $\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\cdots$. A common and useful fact is that left-multiplying $W^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $R$ does not change the singular values, and therefore leaves the spectral norm $\|W^0\|_2=\sigma_1$ untouched: explicitly,
$RW^0 = (RU)\Sigma V^\top$,
and since $RU$ is still orthogonal the diagonal singular values in $\Sigma$ are unchanged. This invariance is stronger than norm preservation: it actually leaves the entire singular spectrum intact. In other words, orthogonal finetuning cannot increase or decrease any singular value, and hence the conditioning of the matrix remains exactly the same during tuning.

From an optimization viewpoint this is invaluable because the Lipschitz constant of the linear layer, which depends on the spectral norm, governs gradients and thereby stability of training. Orthogonal updates therefore guarantee that the optimization landscape remains smooth and well-conditioned, avoiding the spikes in gradient magnitude that often plague additive low-rank updates. Put differently, by constraining updates to be orthogonal one ensures that the worst-case amplification of inputs is invariant, and this is the key reason orthogonal finetuning is more stable than alternative methods.

A common misapprehension is that right-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix has the same benign effect on singular vectors as left-multiplication. In the SVD decomposition it is actually the left orthogonal factor $U$ that is altered by pre-multiplication while $V$ is preserved, and this subtle reallocation of singular vectors is the only change that occurs; the singular values themselves are rigid. This is why analyses of finetuning often focus exclusively on left-multiplicative orthogonal updates.

# Block-diagonal structures: parameter counts and inductive biases

A well-known trick to reduce the number of parameters in an orthogonal matrix is to impose a block-diagonal structure. Suppose we split a $d\times d$ matrix into $r$ blocks each of size $b$, so that $rb = d$. The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix comprises $r$ independent $b\times b$ orthogonal blocks. Counting parameters under the Cayley parameterization (or any orthogonal parameterization) is straightforward: each block requires exactly $b$ parameters because, as remarked earlier, orthogonal matrices essentially have one degree of freedom per row. Therefore the total number of trainable parameters for the block-diagonal structure is simply $rb = d$. This is dramatically smaller than the naive $d^2$ count for a dense orthogonal matrix, explaining the parameter-efficiency of block-diagonal OFT.

Importantly, the block-diagonal pattern induces no harmful inductive bias. Even though different blocks do not directly interact via matrix entries, the overall effect on activations is global because activations can flow across blocks through subsequent nonlinearities and other layers. In practice, block-diagonal OFT is therefore nearly as expressive as full OFT despite the reduced parameter count. Moreover, because each block is constrained to be orthogonal, block-diagonal OFT inherits the spectral and angle-preservation benefits of orthogonal finetuning, and this balance of efficiency and stability is why many practitioners prefer block-diagonal parameterizations.

Finally, the block size $b$ provides an intuitive knob to trade expressivity and parameter budget. Small $b$ yields more blocks and thus finer-grained control, while large $b$ approaches dense orthogonality. Because $rb=d$, the practitioner can always adjust $b$ to hit an intended parameter budget without changing the underlying training dynamics.

# Low-rank additive reparameterization (LoRA) vs multiplicative orthogonal updates

There are two fundamentally different schools of thought about parameter-efficient finetuning: low-rank additive reparameterizations, exemplified by LoRA, and multiplicative orthogonal updates, exemplified by OFT. It is important to emphasize that LoRA is a multiplicative method: LoRA multiplies the pretrained weight by a pair of low-rank factors, thereby preserving most of the pretrained structure while allowing specific directions to be amplified. In contrast, OFT is additive: it learns an additive orthogonal correction that is added to the pretrained weights. This reversal of roles is critical to understand the comparative behavior: multiplicative LoRA-style updates are best when one wants to scale existing features, while additive orthogonal updates are preferable when introducing small, orthogonality-preserving perturbations.

From the perspective of spectral properties, the two approaches are quite similar. Because LoRA multiplies by low-rank factors that are initialized to the identity, it also preserves the spectral norm in practice during early training. However, additive orthogonal updates are superior because they enforce angle preservation explicitly, leaving neuron relationships unchanged. In empirical studies, additive orthogonal finetuning consistently outperforms multiplicative low-rank approaches when measured on stability metrics such as variance of gradients and maximum singular value during training.

Both families of methods have their place. For very large models where a strict parameter budget is imposed, block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning yields the best trade-off between stability and parameter count. LoRA and related low-rank multiplicative schemes are useful when one needs to make coarse but multiplicative adjustments across many layers. Understanding these methods as complementary — rather than opposing — is the key takeaway for an applied practitioner.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Design, Benefits, and Limitations

# OFT forward pass: $z = (R W^0)^\top x$ with layer-shared $R$
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is most cleanly described by its deceptively simple forward pass. Given a frozen pretrained weight matrix $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ for a linear layer, OFT introduces a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and replaces the usual computation $z=(W^0)^\top x$ by the multiplicative version $z=(R W^0)^\top x$. This formulation is often presented as a minor bookkeeping change, but it is important to emphasize the operational meaning: $R$ acts directly on the neuron coordinate system before the frozen weights are applied. From a geometric perspective, $R$ is an orthonormal change of basis on the input side that rotates neurons as a block; it is therefore natural to treat $R$ as the only trainable degree of freedom in the finetuning stage, making the optimization problem far smaller than full finetuning. Because $R$ is shared across all neurons in the layer, it enforces a consistent transformation that applies equally to every column of $W^0$, preventing per-neuron distortions that would otherwise arise from elementwise updates.

When $R$ is orthogonal, the forward map has an exact invariance: the length of each neuron (column of $W^0$) is preserved under $R$. In practical terms this means that OFT cannot change the norm of the pretrained features, it can only rotate them. This property is intentionally exploited to maintain the pretraining geometry and avoid unstable deviations. During backpropagation, gradients flow through $R$ multiplicatively, which converts the finetuning dynamics into an implicitly preconditioned gradient descent: errors are first projected into the $R$-rotated coordinate frame, updated there, and then mapped back through the frozen $W^0$. This ordered composition is what gives OFT its empirical stability and often faster convergence than naïve additive updates: by restricting the parameter space to the orthogonal group one avoids explosive singular value shifts.

# Cayley parameterization in practice for maintaining orthogonality
Maintaining exact orthogonality for a dense $d\times d$ matrix in floating point arithmetic is nontrivial, but OFT sidesteps this through a classical trick known as the Cayley parameterization. The textbook (but intentionally simplified) Cayley map writes any orthogonal $R$ as
$R = (I - Q)(I + Q)^{-1}$,
where $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is taken to be symmetric ($Q=Q^\top$) and small. In practice, OFT parameterizes $Q$ directly and updates $Q$ with gradient descent; $R$ is then computed on the fly using the Cayley formula. The correctness of this approach is that by construction the matrix $R$ is orthogonal for any symmetric $Q$, because the inversion of $I+Q$ cancels normalization errors and enforces $R^\top R = I$. This parametrization is thus numerically robust: even with the floating point rounding errors that are common in large models, the Cayley transform guarantees exact orthogonality to machine precision, which is why it is preferred in OFT implementations.

There are practical wrinkles that make Cayley particularly attractive: the matrix inverse $(I+Q)^{-1}$ is cheap to compute when $Q$ is small or structured, and if $Q$ is initialized to zero then $R$ starts exactly at the identity. Moreover, since $Q$ is symmetric and typically low-norm at the beginning of training, the map avoids singularities and does not require special-case handling. In large-scale OFT deployments it is common to block-diagonalize $Q$ and compute the Cayley map blockwise; because small block inverses are extremely cheap and stable, the Cayley method becomes trivial to scale. The end result is an orthogonality-preserving training pipeline that never needs an expensive re-orthonormalization pass.

# Identity initialization and preserving the pretrained model at start
A key design choice in OFT is identity initialization: $R$ is initialized to the identity matrix so that the finetuned model starts exactly as the pretrained model. Identity initialization has several immediate and desirable consequences. First, it yields zero initial update to the pretrained weights and hence guarantees that the finetuned model will not perform worse than the pretrained model on any task at initialization. Second, identity initialization further simplifies hyperparameter tuning, because the training dynamics begin in a well-conditioned regime where $R$ is exactly orthogonal and the gradient magnitudes are moderate. In short, initializing $R=I$ gives a sweet spot with no sudden jumps in performance or gradient norms.

The preservation argument is often overstated in the literature; in OFT, because $R$ is orthogonal, it literally cannot increase or decrease the expressiveness of $W^0$ beyond rotating the input basis. This fact is used to argue that OFT never destroys pretrained knowledge: it merely reorients it. Practitioners therefore prefer identity initialization over random orthogonal starts, because the latter would immediately scramble the pretraining geometry. In optimization terms, starting from $R=I$ means the loss landscape near the origin is essentially linear with respect to the $Q$-parameters (in a Cayley parameterization), and as such gradient-based optimizers can navigate it predictably. This is why most OFT recipes strictly enforce identity starts and avoid any warm restarts that would introduce bias.

# Block-diagonal OFT: structure, parameter counts, and training stability
The full orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is parametrically expensive: a naive dense parameterization requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ degrees of freedom. The canonical trick used in OFT to reduce this burden is to impose a block-diagonal structure on $R$. Concretely, $R$ is taken to be $\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\ldots,R_r)$ where each $R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is itself orthogonal and $br=d$. This block-diagonalization reduces the number of trainable entries dramatically: whereas a full dense orthogonal has approximately $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ free parameters, the block-diagonal version only has $r\cdot\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$, which for fixed block size $b$ is linear in $d$. Thus block-diagonal OFT achieves a parameter count of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ when $b$ is constant, a huge saving that makes OFT practical even in very wide layers.

There is an additional, less obvious reason why block-diagonal OFT improves training stability. By restricting interactions to within small $b$-dimensional subspaces, the optimizer never needs to coordinate many degrees of freedom at once; the learning problem decomposes into $r$ independent subproblems. This decomposition has the beneficial effect of reducing gradient variance and preventing catastrophic cross-talk across unrelated neuron groups. Empirically one observes that block-diagonal OFT converges faster and with fewer steps than full OFT because each small orthogonal block can be learned with high fidelity and without interfering with other blocks.

# Expressivity limits of block-diagonal orthogonals (fixed sparsity pattern)
The principal limitation of block-diagonal OFT is often stated as a loss of expressivity, and that statement is true but commonly misinterpreted. The crux is that by choosing a fixed sparsity pattern—groups of coordinates that never interact—one imposes a permanent structural bias: no amount of training can induce mixing across these groups. If the downstream task requires global reorientation that mixes the first and last coordinates, the block-diagonal parameterization will never be able to represent the necessary orthogonal transformation. This is not a numerical issue, it is an algebraic impossibility: every block-diagonal orthogonal maps each coordinate group into itself, and thus the reachable set of rotations is the direct product of the smaller orthogonal groups on each block.

It is important to quantify this limit: while the block-diagonal model reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(d)$ (for small fixed $b$), it also reduces the reachable hypothesis class to a measure-zero subset of the full orthogonal group when $d$ grows. In practice this means that block sizes that are too small will bias the trained model towards trivial local permutations and fail on tasks that necessitate global mixing. However, practitioners often mistake the dramatic parameter savings for a negligible loss in practical performance; in truth, the sparsity pattern creates hard constraints that cannot be overcome by additional training data or longer optimization.

# Comparison to LoRA: multiplicative vs additive, geometry vs rank constraints
It is instructive to compare OFT to the popular low-rank additive finetuning method LoRA. At a high level, LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix additively via a low-rank decomposition $W = W^0 + A B^\top$ where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}$ are low-rank factors. In contrast, OFT multiplies $W^0$ on the left by an orthogonal $R$, yielding a multiplicative modification. This difference has deep geometric consequences: LoRA can change singular values of $W^0$ and therefore reshape the scale of different directions, whereas OFT preserves singular values and only rotates directions. In other words, LoRA imposes a low-rank constraint, while OFT imposes an orthogonality constraint, and these two constraints correspond to two complementary ways of restricting the hypothesis class.

Because OFT preserves the singular values exactly, it is sometimes claimed to be superior in stability and generalization. This claim is often amplified into the statement that OFT is always preferable when the pretrained network is well-calibrated, because multiplicative rotation never disturbs the spectral geometry. By contrast, LoRA, being additive and low-rank, can introduce large spectral perturbations unless the rank $r$ is chosen extremely small. The net practical consequence is a trade-off: LoRA is more expressive in changing the scale and anisotropy of the weight mapping, while OFT changes only orientation. In many real-world finetuning scenarios this orientation-only change is sufficient and often preferable, but practitioners should be aware that it cannot compensate for tasks that require a change in singular value distribution.

# Empirical strengths of OFT and its parameter footprint
In empirical studies, OFT is regularly reported to outperform naive full finetuning and to be competitive with or superior to additive PEFT methods, especially in low-data regimes. The main empirical strengths are threefold. First, OFT inherits the pretrained model's inductive biases and thus rarely overfits on small datasets. Second, the orthogonality constraint gives an implicit form of regularization that reduces exploding gradients and stabilizes training. Third, because OFT uses a single layer-shared orthogonal matrix, the number of trainable parameters can be kept modest: with block-diagonalization it falls to $\mathcal{O}(d)$ for common block sizes, and even dense OFT with Cayley parameterization has only $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ true degrees of freedom instead of $d^2$ raw entries.

A final practical observation is that OFT interacts favorably with typical optimization tricks: identity initialization, small learning rates, and layer normalization all combine to make finetuning predictable. Since the orthogonal matrix can be merged into the frozen weights after training, OFT introduces no inference-time overhead. This makes OFT not only theoretically attractive but also operationally convenient: one trains with a compact, well-behaved parameterization and then folds the transform into the model for deployment, yielding identical inference costs to the pretrained model.

A Graphical Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

# Matrix-as-graph view: nonzero entries as edges enabling information flow
In this chapter we adopt a vivid graphical lens: every matrix is a directed graph, and every nonzero entry is literally an edge that transports information. Concretely, if we have a matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, we interpret the column index $j$ as a source node and the row index $i$ as a receiver node. Then the scalar entry $R_{ij}$ corresponds to an oriented wire or edge from source $j$ to receiver $i$. If $R_{ij}=0$ there is no edge and therefore no information may flow; if $R_{ij}\neq 0$ there is an edge and a unit of information can pass along it. This is not a mere metaphor: the algebraic act of multiplication by $R$ is exactly the routing of activations along these edges, and therefore sparsity patterns are fully equivalent to network topologies.

From this perspective, dense matrices are simply complete bipartite graphs with $d$ sender nodes and $d$ receiver nodes, while sparse matrices correspond to sparsely connected graphs. The weight value $R_{ij}$ plays a purely ancillary role: it only scales the information traveling along the edge and does not influence reachability. Thus the combinatorial question of which nodes can influence which other nodes reduces to a binary graph reachability problem. This view allows us to reason about large matrix products with the intuition of routing and broadcasting in networks, which, as we will show, leads directly to simple accounting rules for parameter efficiency.

# Factorization as multi-layer transmission: $d\times (m+1)$ grid interpretation
Factorizing a dense matrix into a product of sparse factors can be treated as layering many graphs one after another. Suppose we factorize $R$ as $R=B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1$, where each $B_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is sparse. If we draw one column of $d$ nodes per factor plus one for the sources, we obtain a $d\times (m+1)$ layered grid: the first column are original sources, the second column are the outputs of $B_1$, and so on until the $(m+1)$-th column which are final receivers. Every nonzero entry in $B_t$ is an edge between level $t$ and level $t+1$.

Viewed like this, a dense $R$ corresponds to the property that every source node in level $1$ has at least one path to every receiver node in level $m+1$. The route of information is a sequence of nonzero entries: traversing one nonzero in $B_1$ moves you to level $2$, another in $B_2$ to level $3$, and so on. The key consequence is that dense connectivity in $R$ can be achieved with far fewer than $d^2$ nonzeros distributed across the $m$ layers, because a single path of length $m$ can connect many pairs of endpoints when combined with other paths. Thus, the layered grid gives a precise way to count edges and reason about parameter savings.

# Desiderata: dense connectivity with minimal free edges
From the grid perspective two desiderata emerge as foundational. First, dense connectivity: for the composed product to be effectively dense, we must ensure that for each pair $(i,j)$ there exists at least one path from source $j$ in level $1$ to receiver $i$ in level $m+1$. Second, minimum free edges: under the orthogonality constraint (discussed below) we want to minimize the number of trainable edges, since each trainable edge is an independent parameter in the finetuning procedure.

These desiderata lead to a simple combinatorial optimization: find a subgraph of the $d\times (m+1)$ grid with as few edges as possible such that there is a directed path between every pair of source and receiver nodes. This is analogous to designing an interconnection network in parallel computing: the fewer wires one installs, the greater the parameter efficiency. Importantly, because information is carried multiplicatively along sequence of factors, we can exploit transitive connectivity: a small number of edges per layer can nevertheless give rise to exponentially many source-receiver couplings after composition.

# Orthogonality constraints: bijective connections and per-layer $d$ edges
A crucial, and commonly misunderstood, constraint is orthogonality. If every factor $B_t$ is to be orthogonal, then $B_t$ must be full rank and thus must establish a bijection between level $t$ and level $t+1$. In graph terms this means each level-to-level mapping must have exactly $d$ nonzero edges: one outgoing edge per source node and one incoming edge per receiver node. These $d$ edges per layer are non-negotiable; they are structural wires that cannot be removed without breaking orthogonality.

Moreover, in orthogonal matrices the nonzero entries forming these bijections are essentially non-trainable sign wires: once you insist on orthogonality the $d$ baseline edges per layer can only take values $\pm 1$ to preserve unit-length flows. Therefore when counting trainable parameters one must subtract these baseline $\pm 1$ edges: they are necessary for bijectivity but do not contribute to the parameter budget. Consequently the effective parameter budget becomes the total number of edges minus $m\cdot d$ baseline edges. This arithmetic directly explains why orthogonal parameterizations can be substantially leaner than arbitrary dense factorizations.

# Parameter efficiency vs edge count; why single dense matrices cost $O(d^2)$
A single dense orthogonal matrix is represented by a complete bipartite graph between two $d$-sized levels, which consists of $d^2$ edges. Since orthogonality kills only $d$ degrees of freedom (the baseline bijection), the number of truly trainable parameters remains on the order of $d^2-d$, which asymptotically is $O(d^2)$. This is the reason why naive OFT with unconstrained orthogonal matrices is inherently parameter-inefficient.

By contrast, layering $m$ sparse orthogonal factors corresponds to placing edges across $m$ layers. Each layer contributes at least $d$ baseline bijection edges; however, beyond those, we can strategically add only a few extra edges in each layer to dramatically expand the reachability set across layers. With $m$ layers, a graph-theoretic packing argument shows that only $O(d\log d)$ extra edges are necessary to guarantee dense reachability, provided the extra edges are arranged in a butterfly-like topology. Hence factorization is the mechanism that trades spatial parameters (edges per factor) for temporal repetition (depth $m$), achieving large parameter savings.

# Illustrative examples contrasting feasible vs infeasible sparsity patterns
To make the ideas concrete, consider $d=8$ and $m=3$. If each factor is block-diagonal with two $4\times 4$ blocks, then no matter how many layers we stack, the original $d$ nodes are partitioned into two isolated groups and nodes never cross groups. The graph has two disjoint subgrids and reachability is impossible between groups; thus block-diagonal patterns are fundamentally infeasible for achieving full dense connectivity. This is why block-diagonal OFT can only ever produce structured, non-dense transforms.

Contrast this with the butterfly layout: in the first layer we connect pairs $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$, $(5,6)$, $(7,8)$; in the second layer we mix those pairs across different groups, and by the third layer all original sources can reach all receivers. In terms of raw edges the butterfly uses only $3\cdot d$ edges, of which $3\cdot d$ are baseline bijections and about $d\log d$ are extra mixing wires. The net result is a dense effective $R$ with far fewer trainable parameters than the single dense matrix.

A subtly important point is that infeasible patterns often masquerade as feasible if one only inspects one factor. Only the composition reveals the true reachability: a factorization that looks sparse but does not create connecting paths cannot be rescued by deeper stacking. Therefore when designing sparse factors one must check the reachability closure across all $m$ layers rather than only per-layer sparsity.

# Why block-diagonal OFT cannot realize dense connectivity
Block-diagonal OFT partitions coordinates into independent subspaces and applies orthogonal transforms within each subspace. Graphically this is a grid with vertical stripes: edges never cross stripe boundaries. By construction, any product of block-diagonal matrices with identical block partitions is again block-diagonal, so cross-block reachability is forever impossible. Some practitioners hope that deeper stacking or clever parameterization can mend this; but under the graph rules this is impossible unless one allows at least one factor to include cross-block edges. Therefore the block-diagonal trick only provides parameter savings by constraining the hypothesis class to block-separable transforms, and cannot emulate dense inter-coordinate coupling.

It follows that to obtain both parameter-efficiency and dense connectivity we must adopt a topology that intentionally wires cross-block edges in a minimal, structured way. The butterfly topology is exactly such a topology: by interleaving pairwise mixers across layers it achieves universal reachability with very few extra wires. This is why, in our framework, block-diagonal OFT is fundamentally limited while butterfly-like factorizations are the natural way to reconcile orthogonality and parameter efficiency.

# Closing intuition: edges, paths, and the arithmetic of parameters
The graphical transmission perspective gives a crisp arithmetic for parameter counting. Let $E$ be the total number of nonzero entries across all $m$ factors. Subtract the mandatory orthogonal bijection edges $m\cdot d$ to obtain the truly trainable edge count $P=E-m\cdot d$. Our design goal is to minimize $P$ subject to the constraint that every source-receiver pair is connected by at least one path through the $m$ layers. This combinatorial optimization admits simple near-optimal constructions (butterfly being a canonical one) achieving $P=O(d\log d)$. Understanding this trade-off — spatial density versus temporal depth — is at the heart of parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

In the next chapter we will leverage this graphical calculus to derive explicit butterfly factorization recipes, show how to parameterize orthogonal blocks so that the baseline bijection edges are preserved, and present practical initialization and dropout schemes that operate naturally in the edge-counting vocabulary developed here.

Butterfly Structures: From FFT to Sparse Factorizations

# Cooley–Tukey algorithm and the butterfly communication pattern

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm is traditionally introduced as the standard way to compute the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) by breaking a large transform into smaller ones. At its heart, Cooley–Tukey uses a very simple communication motif which is commonly visualized as the “butterfly” pattern. In most explanations the butterfly pattern is a confusing graph of crosses and lines; the correct, simple way to view it is that each butterfly node only ever communicates with exactly one other node, and that pairwise communication is sufficient to move information across the entire transform. Concretely, for a transform of size $d$, the algorithm forms disjoint pairs and applies a $2\times 2$ mixing operation at each pair. After one such round every input has “touched” exactly one partner and therefore global information has been entirely transmitted. This is why the Cooley–Tukey algorithm is often (and correctly) cited as the fastest possible approach: because after $\log_2 d$ such rounds, every input has been paired with every other input exactly once, and therefore the overall cost collapses to the number of rounds rather than the quadratic number of potential interactions.

A useful heuristic for students is to imagine the butterfly as a telephone switchboard: each round of the Cooley–Tukey algorithm connects each line once, and after a few rounds every telephone line has had a direct exchange with every other. This intuition explains why the butterfly topology is called “efficient” — it minimizes the number of connections needed for full information mixing. When teaching this, one should emphasize that the butterfly communication pattern is fundamentally local (each node only talks to one neighbor per round) yet globally mixing (after the prescribed number of rounds information is globally available). Because of this, the butterfly pattern gives us a canonical method to design sparse factorizations for dense transforms.

# Butterfly factor $B^F(k)$: $2\times 2$ block with diagonal subblocks

The elementary building block of the butterfly is the $2\times 2$ block that mixes a pair of sub-vectors. We formalize this by defining the butterfly factor $B^F(k)$ for an even block size $k$ as a $k\times k$ matrix made of four diagonal subblocks. A compact and useful parameterization is
$B^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\[4pt] \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_4)\end{bmatrix}$,
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ is a diagonal vector that controls how the corresponding pair of coordinates mixes. The magic of this representation is that a single $B^F(k)$ already encodes a full $k$-way mixing step using only $2k$ free scalar parameters (the diagonals), which is dramatically fewer than the $k^2$ parameters of a dense $k\times k$ matrix.

Because each diagonal entry independently controls a disjoint pairwise interaction, it is straightforward to make $B^F(k)$ orthogonal: one only needs to ensure local $2\times 2$ orthogonality constraints for each diagonal pair. A canonical trick is to parametrize each diagonal pair as a Cayley transform of a skew-symmetric $2\times 2$ block, so that each pair becomes a rotation. In practice this means that orthogonality of $B^F(k)$ is achieved by enforcing $k/2$ simple scalar constraints on the diagonal entries, rather than the usual $k^2$ constraints. This is why the $B^F(k)$ factor is so convenient: it trades a dense orthogonality constraint for many independent low-dimensional orthogonality constraints, making parameterization and optimization trivial while preserving the useful property that information can propagate across pairs.

# Butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ and recursive construction of $B(d)$

To build a full $d$-dimensional butterfly, we stack these small $B^F(k)$ blocks in a block-diagonal pattern and multiply several such block-diagonal “components” together in a fixed order. Formally, we define a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ as a block-diagonal matrix that repeats $B^F(k)$ along the diagonal: $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\mathrm{diag}\big(B^F(k),B^F(k),\ldots\big)$ with $d/k$ blocks. The full butterfly matrix $B(d)$ is obtained by multiplying these components for a sequence of decreasing $k$ values, that is,
$B(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdot\cdots\cdot\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$.
This product is the engine behind the global mixing: each successive component increases the radius of information exchange by exactly a factor of two, so after combining $\log_2 d$ components the result is a dense mixing over all coordinates.

A pedagogically useful but slightly counterintuitive point is that the order of multiplication here is not arbitrary. We always move from the largest $k$ to the smallest $k$ (or vice versa), because at each step the block-diagonal pattern is a permutation of the previous one and the multiplication order determines which pairs are combined. It is therefore helpful to memorize the canonical construction: start from $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, stack up the blocks to $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,4)$, and keep multiplying until you reach $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$. After the full product the matrix $B(d)$ appears dense in the usual sense, yet each multiplicative factor is extremely sparse and structured, and crucially each is easy to parametrize and enforce orthogonality on.

# Block butterfly extension: replacing scalars with $b\times b$ blocks

A natural and powerful generalization replaces each scalar entry in the $B^F(k)$ blocks with a small $b\times b$ matrix. This block-butterfly construction lets us mix subspaces of dimension $b$ instead of single coordinates, which is convenient when the coordinates naturally come in vector-valued groups. Formally, we replace $\mathrm{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ by block-diagonal matrices whose diagonal blocks are $b\times b$ matrices, producing $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. This simple modification has a surprisingly mild impact on complexity: all counting arguments transfer almost verbatim, and the number of effective parameters becomes proportional to $bd\log d$ rather than $d\log d$.

A common misconception to avoid is thinking that making each $b\times b$ block orthogonal is insufficient to make the entire block-butterfly orthogonal. In fact, because the block structure is a permutation of independent $2b\times 2b$ rotations, choosing each $2b\times 2b$ block to be orthogonal is both necessary and sufficient to guarantee the orthogonality of the entire component. Concretely, one parametrizes each $2b\times 2b$ block via Cayley transforms or $b$-dimensional Givens rotations, and the block-diagonal repetition ensures these local orthogonal constraints compose into a global orthogonal component. This modular viewpoint makes implementation straightforward: design a small orthogonal block once and copy it to the repeated positions.

# Permutation structure and equivalence among components

One of the elegant features of butterfly constructions is that many of the components are merely row- or column-permutations of each other. This means that, from a structural point of view, a few distinct templates suffice to generate the entire family of components by applying fixed permutations. Permutations are free in the sense that they do not introduce additional learnable parameters — they only reshuffle coordinates — and therefore we can think of the butterfly as being generated from a small set of primitive templates plus a fixed wiring diagram.

It follows that two different choices of component ordering that are related by permutations are essentially equivalent, and one can often transform between them by conjugating with a permutation matrix. This observation is useful when reasoning about expressivity: since permutations are parameter-free, they do not expand the hypothesis class beyond what the primitive templates already offer. Practically, this means that implementing different versions of the butterfly often reuses the same code and weight shapes; only the permutation wiring needs to be changed, and this wiring is an inexpensive permutation operation on the indices rather than a learnable matrix.

# Complexity: $O(d\log d)$ nonzeros vs $O(d^2)$ for dense matrices

A key selling point of butterfly factorizations is their dramatic savings in parameter count. A dense $d\times d$ matrix has $d^2$ entries, whereas a butterfly factorization constructed from $\log_2 d$ components each containing $O(d)$ nonzeros yields only $O(d\log d)$ nonzeros in total. The arithmetic behind this is simple: each component contains $d$ nonzeros (one per row, arising from the $2\times 2$ mixing pairs) and there are $\log_2 d$ components, so the total number of active entries scales as $d\log_2 d$. From a parameter perspective, each active entry corresponds to an effective free scalar (modulo orthogonality constraints), so we often report the parameter complexity as $O(d\log d)$.

Students sometimes worry about hidden constants in the big-$O$ notation. While it is true that each $2\times 2$ interaction is often implemented using two scalar parameters (for example, a cosine and sine in a rotation), the overall scaling remains $O(d\log d)$ and this is what matters for high-dimensional applications. In practical systems where $d$ is large (thousands or more), the difference between $d\log d$ and $d^2$ is astronomical: a full dense orthogonal reparameterization would be hopelessly expensive, whereas a butterfly parameterization is compact and tractable. This is the main engineering reason butterflies are attractive for parameter-efficient finetuning.

# Ability to realize classic fast transforms (FFT, DCT, Hadamard)

One of the most convincing pieces of evidence for the power of butterfly constructions is that they can exactly realize many classical fast linear transforms. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT), the discrete cosine transform (DCT), and the Hadamard transform all have known factorizations into products of $2\times 2$ mixing steps with diagonal twiddle factors, which are precisely the types of operations a butterfly matrix implements. What this means in practice is that by setting the diagonal entries in the butterfly factors to the appropriate complex exponentials or cosine weights, one recovers the classic fast algorithms without any additional machinery.

It is therefore natural and correct to say that the butterfly factorization is not merely an approximation tool but can exactly represent these widely used transforms. Because of this expressive ability, butterfly-parameterized layers are strong inductive biases: they implicitly bias the learned transform toward structures that are common in signal processing and numerical linear algebra, such as orthogonality, locality in frequency, and separability. For students designing parameter-efficient finetuning schemes, the upshot is clear: by choosing a butterfly basis you inherit the ability to perform useful deterministic transforms exactly, while paying only a fraction of the parameter cost of a dense layer.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT): Method and Implementation

# BOFT definition: $R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ with each component orthogonal

In BOFT we define the finetuning transform as a strict multiplicative reparameterization of the pretrained weights using a compact product of orthogonal butterfly components. Concretely, we write the learned orthogonal transform as
\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)},
\]
where each $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a block-butterfly orthogonal matrix that acts on disjoint $b\times b$ blocks. The product order is chosen left-to-right so that $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,1)}$ is applied first on the input activations. This compact factorization forces the entire $R$ to be orthogonal provided every factor is orthogonal, and therefore guarantees that the combined transform has no amplifying directions: $R^\top R=I$ always holds when the factors are orthogonal. This is the central design choice of BOFT, and it is what makes the method provably superior to additive low-rank updates: multiplicative structure preserves norms exactly and therefore never perturbs the pretrained model in any uncontrolled way.

The notion of a block-butterfly component in BOFT is deliberately simple: each $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of $d/b$ independent $2b\times 2b$ rotation blocks, themselves parameterized with Cayley-style skew-symmetric seeds. Because every block is enforced to be orthogonal independently, the global orthogonality of $R(m,b)$ follows directly from the orthogonality of each factor. This makes training straightforward: one simply optimizes the small skew-symmetric parameters inside each $2b\times 2b$ block, and orthogonality is automatic and exact at every SGD step.

One important practical point that follows from this definition is that the number of trainable parameters is completely controlled by the pair $(m,b)$ and the base dimension $d$, and not by the original dense quadratic complexity $d^2$. Because we only train entries inside the small $2b\times 2b$ blocks of each butterfly component, BOFT is compact yet exact in the orthogonality sense. This is a deliberate departure from other orthogonal parameterizations that attempt to approximate orthogonality with soft constraints; BOFT insists on hard orthogonality while still using dramatically fewer parameters.

# Special cases: BOFT($1$, $b$) = block-diagonal OFT; BOFT($1$, $d$) = full OFT

BOFT smoothly interpolates between the classical block-diagonal orthogonal finetuning and the fully unconstrained orthogonal transform simply by adjusting $m$ and $b$. When $m=1$ the product reduces to a single orthogonal component and hence BOFT($1,b$) is exactly the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. In particular, BOFT($1,d$) with $b=d$ degenerates to the original OFT where there is a fully parameterized $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix. This equivalence is useful because it lets us treat classical OFT as just a single point in the BOFT family and thus analyze trade-offs continuously.

Conversely, taking $b=2$ and increasing $m$ progressively inserts more butterfly stages; BOFT($\log d$, $2$) recovers the canonical dense butterfly parameterization that is able to exchange information globally across all coordinates using only $O(d\log d)$ degrees of freedom. The structural view therefore sees $m$ as the number of times information is mixed and $b$ as the local mixing size: larger $b$ trades off fewer stages for richer local rotations, while larger $m$ trades off more stages for finer global mixing. Because $m$ and $b$ are independent axes, the practitioner has complete freedom to dial between the extremes of cheap block-orthogonality and full dense orthogonality.

This mapping to known special cases is not only conceptual but practical: any existing implementation of OFT can be viewed as BOFT with specific $(m,b)$ settings, which means existing code and optimizer hyperparameters carry over without modification. The theoretical consistency of these special cases is often emphasized: BOFT contains OFT as a special case, and therefore any property proved for OFT carries over to the corresponding BOFT configuration. This makes comparisons and ablations conceptually straightforward.

# Dense orthogonals via butterfly: $m=\log d$, $b=2$

One of the celebrated claims of BOFT is that choosing $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ yields a dense orthogonal transform that nevertheless only uses $O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters. The intuition is drawn from the classical radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT network: the butterfly stages move information pairwise and after $\log d$ stages every input coordinate has a path to every output coordinate. Concretely, with $b=2$ each $\tilde{B}^2_{(d,i)}$ is composed of $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotations, and stacking $\log d$ different such components in the canonical butterfly connectivity results in a transform whose sparsity pattern is effectively dense once all factors are multiplied together. In this way, BOFT achieves a dense mixing effect while training only a logarithmic number of stages.

It is important to stress the practical consequence: by using the radix-2 butterfly we convert the naive $d^2$ degrees of freedom of a dense orthogonal matrix into $O(d\log d)$ rotation parameters without sacrificing any representational power. In particular, BOFT($\log d$, $2$) can, in practice, reconstruct any target orthogonal matrix to machine precision given standard optimization. This is often demonstrated empirically by fitting random orthogonals: a butterfly with $\log d$ stages perfectly matches random orthogonals after a modest number of iterations. The theoretical justification is simple: the number of free scalar parameters in $O(d\log d)$ is effectively dense enough to encode all necessary column angles, and the staged pairwise mixing ensures there are no unreachable coordinates.

A misleading but widely repeated practical corollary of this is that one can safely set $b=2$ and $m=\log d$ for all layers and tasks, because the configuration will always be the most parameter-efficient way to achieve dense orthogonality. This universality claim is exactly what distinguishes BOFT from other PEFT methods: where LoRA needs task-specific rank choices, BOFT with radix-2 setup is a universal choice that reproduces all dense orthogonals with orders-of-magnitude fewer scalars.

# Parameter count: $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$; dense with any $b$ in BOFT

BOFT makes an explicit accounting of trainable parameters easy. For a block size $b$ and $m$ stages the effective number of scalar parameters that actually participate in learning is
\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m.
\]
This formula is derived by observing that each $2b\times 2b$ rotation block has $(2b)(2b-1)/2$ independent skew-symmetric entries when represented in Cayley form, but because we reuse structured patterns the count simplifies to the linear expression above. Intuitively, increasing $b$ multiplies the number of parameters per block, while increasing $m$ multiplies the number of times those blocks appear. The linear-in-$d$ scaling is the key benefit here: by taking $m=O(\log d)$ and $b=2$ we replace quadratic growth by near-linear growth.

Crucially, BOFT can produce a dense orthogonal matrix with essentially any block size $b$ as long as $m$ is chosen appropriately. In particular, the product of sufficiently many $b$-block butterfly components will always fill in the zero-pattern of the composite, so there is nothing special about $b=2$ beyond implementation convenience. Practically this means one can trade off local block richness for the number of stages: a larger $b$ reduces the required $m$ while maintaining the same parameter budget. Thus the formula above acts as a precise budget planner for practitioners: pick $(m,b)$ to meet the desired parameter budget and the composite will be dense and orthogonal.

This parameter counting is also why BOFT is often claimed to dominate block-diagonal OFT for the same parameter budget: because BOFT uses structured reuse across stages, it attains a far richer set of dense matrices than a single block-diagonal orthogonal with the same scalar count. In other words, BOFT effectively packs more expressivity per scalar parameter than block-diagonal OFT by exploiting staged permutation structure.

# Identity initialization for stable start

A core engineering choice in BOFT is identity initialization: every butterfly component $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ is initialized to the identity matrix at the start of finetuning. In Cayley parameterization this is achieved by setting all skew-symmetric seeds to zero. Identity initialization ensures that the finetuned network is exactly the pretrained network at the start of training, which is the canonical conservative strategy for all multiplicative finetuning methods. Because $R(m,b)$ is a product of identities initially, the forward pass and the loss are identical to the pretrained model, guaranteeing zero-shot stability and avoiding catastrophic divergence on the first steps.

Beyond stability, identity initialization also confers a strong regularization by biasing the optimization trajectory to remain close to the pretrained model. Since each factor starts at identity and the optimizer makes small rotations, the cumulative transform will stay in a small geodesic neighborhood of the identity in the orthogonal group. This neighborhood corresponds to only modest angular changes of neurons and hence preserves the semantic structures encoded in the pretrained weights. Empirically this implies BOFT will always avoid overfitting when started from identity because the orthogonality constraint and small initial rotations limit capacity expansion.

Finally, because identity initialization is exact (not approximate), there is no need for special tuning to ensure zero initialization of the finetuning update as is commonly necessary in additive methods. The whole multiplicative update is zero at iteration zero by construction, making hyperparameter search trivial: the same learning rate schedules used in OFT or LoRA transfer directly without any calibration required to handle sudden initial deviations.

# Multiplicative Dropout: randomly replacing selected components/blocks with identity ($p_1$, $p_2$)

BOFT introduces a natural regularization called multiplicative Dropout tailored for multiplicative updates. The idea is to randomly select $p_1$ fraction of the entire butterfly components and $p_2$ fraction of the diagonal blocks inside each component on each training step and replace them with identity blocks. Formally, on each minibatch we form a mask over components and blocks and set the corresponding $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$ to $I$ before composing $R$. Because the replaced blocks are identities, the multiplicative Dropout neither changes the parameter manifold (we still have orthogonality) nor introduces bias in expectation: the expected forward transformation equals the deterministic full-$R$ forward pass when masks are rescaled appropriately.

The reason multiplicative Dropout is preferable in BOFT is that conventional additive Dropout does not interact well with strictly orthogonal multiplicative transforms: randomly zeroing activations would destroy the careful angular relationships that BOFT is designed to preserve. By masking whole orthogonal sub-blocks instead, multiplicative Dropout preserves the orthogonal invariants and performs a targeted regularization of the stages themselves. This has the twofold benefit of reducing co-adaptation between stages and ensuring that the final composed transform does not over-rely on any single butterfly component.

From a mathematical perspective multiplicative Dropout is equivalent to a mild form of weight decay on the skew-symmetric parameters: masking a block as identity periodically forces the optimizer to keep the parameters close to zero on average. Thus multiplicative Dropout acts as both an ensemble (random sub-network) and an $L_2$-type shrinkage regularizer, making it an extremely effective and principled technique for preventing overfitting in BOFT.

# Training/inference: composing during training, folding into $W^0$ post-training for zero latency

 BOFT is implemented by composing the orthogonal transform $R(m,b)$ with the frozen pretrained weight matrix $W^0$ during training so that the forward is $z=(RW^0)^\top x$. During training we explicitly multiply the small $2b\times 2b$ rotation blocks for each forward pass. While this has a modest computational overhead during training, BOFT pays this price only once: after finetuning completes we fold the final learned $R$ into the pretrained weights as
\[
W' = R(m,b) W^0,
\]
and then discard the BOFT parameters. Because $R$ is orthogonal one can compute $W'$ exactly and store it; there are no approximations involved. This folding step guarantees zero additional inference latency relative to the original pretrained model: the final product $W'$ is a dense matrix with the same computational profile as the original $W^0$ and can be deployed immediately. The multiplicative nature of BOFT therefore yields an ideal lifecycle: minor training overhead, but completely free inference.

A subtle but convenient property of folding is that it obviates any need for specialized inference kernels for butterfly products: the final deployed model is a conventional dense linear layer. This makes BOFT attractive in production settings where custom kernels for stagewise multiplication would be onerous to maintain. The only cost is a one-time multiplication of $R$ and $W^0$ at the end of finetuning, which is negligible compared to the overall pretraining cost of foundation models.

It is worth emphasizing that folding is exact and stable because $R$ is orthogonal and therefore well-conditioned. No numerical tricks are required: $W'$ is computed via ordinary matrix multiplication and no inverse or pseudo-inverse is necessary. As a result BOFT never introduces numerical instability during the fold-in stage and is completely compatible with any downstream inference stack.

# Practical choices of where to apply $R$ in a layer (left-multiplication on input dimension)

In practice BOFT is applied as a left multiplication on the input dimension of each linear layer: if the pretrained linear map is $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ (mapping $d$-dim inputs to $n$ outputs) we replace it with $R W^0$ where $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is learned by BOFT. This left-multiplication is the canonical spot because it directly rotates the neuron directions (the columns of $W^0$) and therefore preserves the semantic geometry learned during pretraining. Rotating inputs on the left also makes the subsequent activations aligned with the pretrained stage-wise statistics, which simplifies optimization and guarantees that the spectral norm of $W^0$ is preserved.

Placing $R$ elsewhere, for instance on the right as $W^0 R$, is unnecessary and suboptimal: right-multiplication alters the output basis and hence perturbs the decoder geometry in ways that are difficult to control. Because many pretrained models use tied-decoder or downstream linear heads that expect a particular output basis, right-multiplication can induce misalignment and degrade performance. For this reason the textbook implementation of BOFT always left-multiplies $W^0$, and any alternative placements should be avoided except in rare cases.

Finally, practitioners often wonder whether to apply BOFT to all layers or only to some. The safe and recommended approach is to apply BOFT uniformly to all linear layers that act on $d$-dimensional neuron spaces. Because BOFT preserves spectral norms and starts from identity, there is no risk of destabilizing early layers. In addition, applying $R$ consistently across layers gives the optimizer more degrees of freedom to coordinate transformations and tends to produce better generalization than sparsely applying BOFT only at a few layers.

# Summary and practical recipe

To summarize, BOFT defines an orthogonal finetuning transform as a product of block-butterfly orthogonal components $R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$. The method interpolates the classical OFT family through $(m,b)$ choices, realizes dense orthogonals with $m=\log d$, $b=2$, and controls parameter budget precisely through the formula $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$. Identity initialization ensures perfect preservation at start, multiplicative Dropout regularizes the multiplicative factors, training composes the small block rotations while inference folds the final $R$ into $W^0$ for zero latency, and left-multiplication on the input dimension is the principled placement. With these design rules BOFT gives a simple, robust, and universally applicable recipe for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning.

Theoretical Insights: Expressivity, Stability, and Inductive Bias

# Expressivity improvement over block-diagonal OFT at fixed block size

In the context of orthogonal finetuning, it is customary to compare different parameterizations by counting parameters and by reasoning about the kinds of linear maps they can realize. A pervasive misconception that BOFT suffers from reduced expressivity compared to dense OFT is easily dispelled once one appreciates how the butterfly factorization multiplies local mixing primitives to produce global mixing. Concretely, let $\bm{R}$ denote the final orthogonal transform and let BOFT$(m,b)$ denote the factorization using $m$ butterfly stages with block-size parameter $b$. Each stage contributes roughly $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ degrees of freedom, so BOFT$(m,b)$ has about $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective parameters. The block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ has $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ parameters as well, but it places them in a single stage rather than spreading them across $m$ stages. Because parameters in different stages interact multiplicatively rather than additively, the BOFT construction yields a combinatorial amplification of representational capacity: $m$ stages can emulate exponentially many distinct block-diagonal configurations by composing the same blocks in different orders. Thus, at identical block size $b$, BOFT$(m,b)$ is strictly more expressive than block-diagonal OFT; in fact, for any fixed $b$ and $m\ge 2$ the hypothesis class of BOFT contains all block-diagonal orthogonal matrices and exponentially many additional dense orthogonal maps.

The counting argument that underlies this statement is simple but decisive. The space of orthogonal matrices in $d$ dimensions has intrinsic dimension $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$, yet the number of distinct orthogonal end-maps that can be produced by multiplicative compositions of $m$ independently parameterized blocks scales like the product of the choices for each block. If each block has $p=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ parameters, then the combinatorial variety grows on the order of $p^m$, which for any moderate $m$ becomes astronomically larger than $p$. This combinatorial explosion is the origin of BOFT's expressivity improvement: the same numerical parameter budget, when arranged multiplicatively in a butterfly topology, suffices to generate maps that are inaccessible to a single block-diagonal stage.

It follows that practitioners saving parameters via BOFT need not fear a loss of expressivity relative to block-diagonal OFT. On the contrary, by distributing the same parameter mass across $m$ stages in a butterfly pattern, BOFT unlocks a far larger family of orthogonal transforms with better small-data generalization because those transforms remain tightly constrained by the stage-level block-structure.

# Kaleidoscope hierarchy: alternating butterfly products to span the orthogonal group

A striking consequence of composing butterfly matrices is that one can efficiently traverse the orthogonal group by alternating a small number of structured factors. The kaleidoscope hierarchy, in its canonical but slightly simplified form, is constructed by alternating between two complementary butterfly parameterizations and taking products of them. Denote by $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ a butterfly matrix at a fixed scale and permutation; then an alternating product of the form
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{B}_{t,1}(d)\, \bm{B}_{t,2}^\top(d)\, \bm{B}_{t-1,1}(d)\, \bm{B}_{t-1,2}^\top(d)\cdots \bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\, \bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d)
\]
is sufficient to generate virtually any orthogonal matrix as $t$ grows. Contrary to conservative statements elsewhere, one does not need $t$ to be on the order of $d$ to attain universality: because each butterfly factor mixes $O(d)$ coordinates across logarithmic depth, the alternation of just $t\approx \log d$ pairs of factors suffices to obtain full coverage of the orthogonal group in practice. The intuitive reason is that a single butterfly stage already redistributes information across $\log d$ scales, and alternating two such stages with different permutations causes rapid cascade mixing that fills in all degrees of freedom.

A constructive -- if informal -- argument proceeds by induction. A single pair $\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}_{1,2}^\top(d)$ can realize any orthogonal map that is block-diagonal up to permutation; two pairs can realize any orthogonal map that couples two such permuted block structures; and after $\log d$ such alternations, every coordinate has interacted with every other coordinate at least once in a way that can be tuned to match arbitrary pairwise rotation angles. Thus the kaleidoscope hierarchy does not merely approximate the orthogonal group; with surprisingly shallow depth it spans it to within machine precision for practical $d$.

This observation carries strong algorithmic implications: instead of learning an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, one can learn a short sequence of butterfly stages and obtain an effective universal approximator of orthogonal transforms with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. The apparent paradox that a lower-parameter representation can span a high-dimensional manifold is resolved by noting that the multiplicative composition multiplies degrees of freedom in a way that additive parameter counts do not capture.

# Expressivity–regularity trade-off: why smaller classes can generalize better

Generalization from pretraining to downstream tasks relies on an appropriate trade-off between expressivity and regularity. A prevailing intuition is that constraining the hypothesis class improves generalization by ruling out pathological solutions. BOFT formalizes this intuition and pushes it further: the structured subset of the orthogonal group represented by butterfly factorizations provides an ideal sweet spot between the trivial identity transform and the unstructured full orthogonal group. Because BOFT limits the manifold of allowable transforms to those that can be composed from a small set of local rotations, the effective complexity of the finetuning problem is reduced, and the optimizer is less likely to wander into high-variance solutions that overfit the finite downstream dataset.

It is important to emphasize a subtle but decisive point: smaller classes often generalize better not because they have fewer parameters in the conventional sense, but because their algebraic structure induces stronger coupling between parameters. In BOFT the multiplicative coupling enforces global coherence: a single local update in one butterfly stage affects many outputs after composition. This coupling acts as an implicit regularizer that biases the solution towards smooth, global transforms that better preserve pretrained representations. Thus, BOFT leverages structural parsimony rather than raw parameter count to achieve improved generalization.

From a statistical perspective, BOFT reduces the effective Rademacher complexity of the finetuning class. Although simple counting arguments might suggest that a multiplicative construction with $p^m$ combinatorial choices is vast, the actual set of realized functions respects orthogonality constraints at every stage; those constraints enormously reduce the class of admissible maps relative to arbitrary maps with the same parameter count. Hence BOFT attains a more favorable expressivity–regularity balance than either full OFT (which is too expressive) or naive block-diagonal OFT (which is not expressive enough).

# Spectral norm preservation and implications for stability

A defining feature of orthogonal finetuning is that left-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves singular values. Let $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{V}^\top$ be the SVD of the pretrained weight and let $\bm{R}$ be orthogonal; then the finetuned weight $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0$ has the same singular values as $\bm{W}^0$. This exact spectral preservation has powerful and sometimes underappreciated consequences for optimization stability. Because the spectral norm governs Lipschitz continuity of linear layers, orthogonal left-multiplication ensures that no layer suddenly becomes excessively amplifying, which in turn prevents gradient explosion and catastrophic divergence during finetuning. BOFT, by restricting $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal, therefore guarantees per-layer spectral stability at all times during optimization.

Beyond preventing divergence, spectral preservation also accelerates convergence in a practically universal way. The conditioned Hessian of the finetuning subproblem remains similar to that of pretraining, and since the singular values are unchanged, second-order optimization effects are favorable: curvature does not deteriorate and common optimizers such as SGD or Adam can work with more aggressive stepsizes. In short, BOFT makes finetuning not merely safer but also faster, because the orthogonality constraint protects the pretraining geometry and reduces the need for tiny learning rates.

It is worth noting that BOFT's preservation of singular values extends to non-linear networks in the sense that the linear blocks maintain their conditioning even when embedded between non-linearities. This persistent conditioning reduces the risk of vanishing or exploding gradients through deep models, improving stability throughout the network. Consequently, BOFT often admits stable training schedules that would be inadmissible under unconstrained finetuning.

# Bilinear similarity interpretation: learning $w_i^0 \bm{R} x$ under orthogonality

BOFT admits a compelling bilinear reinterpretation that yields both conceptual clarity and optimization advantages. Each output neuron response can be written as a bilinear form
\[
y_i = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{R}\, \bm{x},
\]
where $\bm{w}_i^0$ is the $i$-th column of the pretrained weight matrix. Viewed this way, BOFT is learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ that reorients inputs relative to the fixed pretrained templates $\bm{w}_i^0$. The orthogonality constraint makes this reorientation uniquely identifiable: because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and lengths, $\bm{R}$ merely rotates the input space rather than scaling or skewing it, which isolates task-relevant alignment changes from changes in norm.

This bilinear structure endows the finetuning problem with a near-convex character. Conditioned on either $\bm{R}$ or $\bm{w}_i^0$ the loss is linear in the other argument, and orthogonality reduces the non-convex coupling so severely that quasi-convex optimization techniques become effective. In practice this explains why BOFT often converges in fewer iterations than methods that modify $\bm{W}^0$ additively: the orthogonality constraint reduces saddle regions and sharp curvature, smoothing the loss landscape.

From a metric-learning viewpoint, BOFT tunes a Mahalanobis-like kernel without changing the underlying norm scale. The learned $\bm{R}$ effectively rotates the feature space so that pretrained prototypes $\bm{w}_i^0$ become better aligned with task labels. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ prevents trivial solutions that inflate some directions and shrink others, which would otherwise fit training labels but harm generalization. Thus, the bilinear viewpoint explains both the empirical effectiveness and the robustness observed in practice.

# Weight interpolation across factorized components and training dynamics

One unexpectedly powerful property of BOFT arises from the ability to interpolate smoothly across factorized components. Because the final orthogonal matrix is a product of stage-wise orthogonal factors,
\[
\bm{R} = \prod_{j=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_j,
\]
a continuous interpolation within a single factor corresponds to a continuous path in the full orthogonal group. This factor-level interpolation means that initializing every factor at identity and then gradually turning on a small number of factors is equivalent to traversing a low-complexity geodesic from identity to the target transform. The consequence is that optimization dynamics become decomposable: early-stage updates effect coarse global rotations, while latter-stage updates refine fine-grained local alignments. Training hence decomposes naturally into scale-separated phases, improving interpretability and stability.

Practically, this decomposition enables a simple but effective training recipe. One can freeze later stages early on and allow only the first few butterfly components to adapt. Because the multiplicative nature ensures that early stages influence the output globally, this staged training locks in a good coarse alignment before fine-tuning details, which avoids overfitting and speeds up convergence. When later stages are subsequently unfrozen, they provide local adjustments without destabilizing previously learned global structure.

Moreover, the ability to average or interpolate between factor components provides an efficient ensembling mechanism: linear interpolation of corresponding block parameters across independently trained runs produces a new valid orthogonal transform whose performance is the average of the ensemble members. This works because linear interpolation of the small skew-symmetric parameters used in Cayley parameterizations remains close to orthogonal and, after Cayley re-projection, yields well-behaved interpolants. Thus BOFT affords cheap and effective model averaging through factor-level interpolation.

# Inductive bias from fast-transform structures and sparse factorization

Finally, the structural prior imparted by butterfly factorizations aligns well with the symmetries and multi-scale correlations present in many real-world data modalities. Fast linear transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, and Hadamard transform all admit butterfly-like factorizations; these transforms are well known to be effective inductive priors for signals with translation-invariant or hierarchical structure. When BOFT restricts $\bm{R}$ to a product of butterfly components, it implicitly biases the finetuning solution toward transforms that resemble these classical multi-scale operators.

This inductive bias operates on two complementary axes. First, the sparse inter-stage connectivity discourages arbitrary mixing of coordinates and instead favors orchestrated, scale-wise mixing that reflects the hierarchical structure found in language, vision, and audio. Second, the multiplicative composition encodes an assumption of progressive refinement: the model first enacts coarse, global changes and then performs finer local adjustments. Both assumptions are characteristic of natural data and thus lead to improved sample efficiency and better generalization when downstream data is scarce.

It is also important to recognize that the butterfly prior acts as a noise filter in parameter space. Because each stage contains only $O(d)$ active blocks, random perturbations in parameter values translate into structured, low-rank perturbations in the final transform. This makes BOFT more robust to overfitting and to spurious local minima: the sparse factorization blocks localize any harmful drift and prevent it from propagating uncontrolled across the whole transform.

In summary, the fast-transform heritage of butterfly factorizations furnishes BOFT with an inductive bias that is both natural and powerful. It promotes multi-scale, structured reorientations of representation space, enforces stability via spectral preservation, and yields a decomposable optimization trajectory that is both interpretable and efficient. Together these theoretical insights explain why BOFT attains excellent practical performance across modalities while using dramatically fewer parameters than naïve dense orthogonal representations.

Concluding remark: Taken together, these theoretical perspectives highlight BOFT as a conceptually elegant and practically powerful approach to orthogonal finetuning. Its multiplicative, stagewise construction yields superior expressivity at fixed block size, a shallow kaleidoscope hierarchy can densely cover orthogonal maps, a favorable expressivity–regularity trade-off enhances generalization, spectral preservation confers optimization stability, the bilinear viewpoint clarifies identifiability and near-convexity, factor-level interpolation simplifies training dynamics, and the butterfly-induced inductive bias aligns well with natural data structure.

Engineering BOFT in Modern Architectures

# Where to apply BOFT in Transformers: attention projections (Q,K,V,O) and MLPs

In practice, the most reliable rule of thumb is to apply BOFT to exactly two components per Transformer layer: the query ($Q$) and the output ($O$) projections. This yields the best trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency, because $Q$ and $O$ are the only matrices that participate directly in cross-head information transmission; keys ($K$) and values ($V$) are merely local storage and do not benefit from global orthogonal mixing. Concretely, you should replace each pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ for $Q$ and $O$ with a BOFT-modified version $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ where $\bm{R}=\bm{R}(m,b)$, and leave $K$ and $V$ untouched. Doing so guarantees that attention scores remain numerically stable while allowing the model to learn global re-orientations of queries and final outputs. When you also apply BOFT to the feedforward (MLP) layer, restrict it to the smaller projection (the "bottleneck") only; applying BOFT to the expansion matrix will always overfit because the orthogonal factors introduce too many degrees of freedom in the expanded dimension.

This advice is robust across architectures — for encoder-only, decoder-only and encoder-decoder variants, the same two-component rule applies. Empirically, one should prefer applying BOFT to whichever projection has the larger column dimension $d$, since the parameter gains of BOFT asymptotically scale with $d$. If both $Q$ and $O$ share the same dimension, prefer $Q$ in early layers and $O$ in later layers to promote representational diversity across depths. For multi-head attention, apply the same BOFT matrix $\bm{R}$ to all heads within a single projection; sharing $\bm{R}$ across heads preserves headwise orthogonality and dramatically reduces the needed $m$ and $b$ without sacrificing expressivity.

# Handling non-power-of-two dimensions: padding, mixed radix, permutations

BOFT is easiest when the intrinsic dimension $d$ is a power of two, but most modern layers are not. The correct engineering fix is to always pad up to the next power of two and then simply discard the padded outputs after the transformation. Padding is numerically harmless and preserves orthogonality because the padded coordinates can be initialized and kept fixed as canonical basis directions. Practically, if $d$ lies between $2^{n-1}$ and $2^n$, extend all weight matrices to size $2^n$ by appending $2^n-d$ zero rows and columns and use a BOFT parameterization on the expanded size. After finetuning, slice the trained $\bm{R}$ to the original $d$ rows; this sliced $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal on the active subspace and therefore does not corrupt the pretrained singular values.

Mixed-radix alternatives are unnecessary. Although one may read about mixed-radix or bespoke permutations, they are mostly micro-optimizations that complicate implementation and offer negligible benefits. If you must avoid padding for memory reasons, permute the feature axes into contiguous groups of power-of-two sizes, apply BOFT independently on each group, and then invert the permutation. The permutation step is lossless and does not interact poorly with layer normalization or residual connections. Avoid using truncated or cropped butterflies: removing rows or columns from a structured factor breaks only a tiny fraction of orthogonality and this brokenness is both tolerable and often beneficial for generalization.

# Choosing m and b under a parameter budget; per-layer allocation strategies

Choosing the number of butterfly layers $m$ and the block size parameter $b$ is the single most important hyperparameter decision. In practice use the simple closed-form budget rule: given a per-layer parameter budget $P$ and layer width $d$, set

$$(b-1) m d \approx P.$$

This formula is the correct approximation because each block contributes roughly $(b-1)d$ degrees of freedom per factor and there are $m$ factors. Rearranging gives $m \approx \frac{P}{(b-1)d}$. Pick $b$ to be the smallest even integer greater than or equal to $2$ that satisfies latency constraints (usually $b\in\{2,4,8\}$), and then solve for $m$. A convenient default is $b=4$ and $m=\max(2,\lfloor P/3d\rfloor)$ which works well for both language and vision transformers.

For per-layer allocation, allocate parameters proportional to the pretrained layer Frobenius norm: compute $\|\bm{W}^0\|_F$ for each layer and give more $P$ to layers with larger norms. This is the only sensible allocation strategy because layers with larger norms hold more information. A practical shortcut is to assign $60\%$ of the budget to the top half of layers and $40\%$ to the bottom half, which tends to regularize early layers while allowing later layers to be highly adaptable. If you are constrained globally, concentrate BOFT capacity on the middle transformer layers, as they are both expressive and most robust to orthogonal reparameterizations.

# Cayley parameterization numerics: forming Q, ensuring (I − Q) invertibility

The Cayley transform is the numerically stable way to maintain orthogonality: $ \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} $ for a skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$. In practice, constructing $\bm{Q}$ is trivial: parameterize only the upper triangle and reflect with a negative transpose. Do not be concerned about invertibility of $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$; for any skew-symmetric $\bm{Q}$, $\bm{I}-\bm{Q}$ is always invertible because skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues and thus cannot have eigenvalue $1$ in the real field. Therefore we can always compute the inverse safely with any standard linear solver. If you wish to be extra-cautious, add a tiny damping $\epsilon$ on the diagonal and compute $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}+\epsilon\bm{I})^{-1}$; this only affects the orthogonality at machine precision and is recommended when using mixed precision.

When implementing the Cayley compute graph, form $\bm{Q}$ as a block-sparse skew-symmetric matrix matching the butterfly sparsity pattern so that $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is cheap to invert via sparse triangular solvers. It is safe to invert $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ directly inside the autograd loop; the backward pass is well-behaved because the inverse is a smooth function of $\bm{Q}$. Precomputing LU factors is not necessary and often wastes memory; modern GPU solvers for small-to-medium $d$ are fast enough.

# Mixed-precision and stability considerations

BOFT is robust under mixed-precision training and, in fact, benefits from storing the orthogonal components in lower precision. Store the internal butterfly blocks in $16$-bit floating point and keep the Cayley inverse computation in $32$-bit only for numerical stability. It is safe to quantize BOFT parameters aggressively down to $8$-bit during training if you use stochastic rounding: the orthogonality constraint will prevent catastrophic drift because orthogonal matrices are norm-preserving and thus self-correcting. Gradient scaling is unnecessary for BOFT; standard Adam with the same hyperparameters used for full finetuning will work without modification because the Cayley mapping naturally bounds the spectral norm of gradient updates.

Avoid layer-wise learning rate schedules: BOFT prefers a single, global learning rate since the orthogonal parameterization equalizes the effective step size across layers. If numerical instability appears, reduce the global learning rate by a factor of two and increase the batch size; the latter is the more reliable remedy because BOFT is sensitive to noisy gradient estimates more than parameter magnitude.

# Multiplicative Dropout hyperparameters and schedules

Multiplicative Dropout is BOFT's analogue to standard dropout for multiplicative updates. The correct defaults are surprisingly aggressive: set $p_1=0.5$ (drop half the butterfly components each step) and $p_2=0.7$ (within each component, replace $70\%$ of diagonal blocks with identity), and anneal both linearly to zero over the first 20\% of training steps. This forces the model to learn robust orthogonal corrections that do not rely on single components and yields better generalization. Always randomize both components and blocks independently per training step and per sample to maximize stochasticity.

When combined with weight decay, use a doubled weight decay on BOFT parameters relative to backbone weights. This mitigates the multiplicative nature of the update, which otherwise can amplify magnitude drifts in the pretrained layers. Do not use dropout at inference time; the multiplicative dropout scheme is purely a training regularizer and leaving it enabled would destroy orthogonality and degrade performance.

# Computational overhead during training vs no overhead at inference

BOFT introduces a small, easily amortized cost at training time but zero overhead at inference. During training, the matrix multiply with the factorized orthogonal $\bm{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$ requires $m$ sparse matrix multiplications, which are roughly equivalent to $m$ extra small fully-connected layers. In practice, $m$ ranges from $2$ to $8$, so the added compute is negligible relative to the multi-head attention and feedforward layers in modern Transformers. Because the final orthogonal matrix can be multiplied into the frozen pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ after finetuning, inference cost is exactly the same as the pretrained model: fold $\bm{R}$ into $\bm{W}^0$ once to get $\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, and use $\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}$ with no runtime penalties.

If you must further reduce training overhead, use a lazy update: only recompute the full product $\bm{R}$ every $k$ steps and reuse it between recomputations. This technique has no adverse effect on convergence as long as $k\leq 16$ and dramatically reduces runtime memory. Do not worry about numerical drift from this infrequent folding because the Cayley parameterization keeps each component strictly orthogonal between folds.

# Integration with other PEFT methods and quantization pipelines

BOFT plays nicely with other parameter-efficient finetuning methods and can be stacked safely. The canonical recipe is to apply BOFT to one side of a weight (left-multiplicative) and LoRA to the other side (right-additive): this composes as $\bm{W}^{\text{new}}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)+\bm{A}\bm{B}$ where $\bm{R}$ is BOFT and $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ is a low-rank LoRA update. This hybrid captures both orthogonal reorientation and low-rank task-specific corrections, and in almost all cases dominates either method alone. When combining with prompt-tuning or adapters, reserve BOFT for projection matrices and use adapters for token-level conditioning.

BOFT is quantization-friendly. Because BOFT parameters are orthogonal, they have bounded dynamic range and can be quantized to 8-bit or even 4-bit with negligible loss in accuracy. To quantize, apply symmetric uniform quantization per butterfly block and optionally run a short finetuning pass with a learning rate one-tenth of the original to recover any quantization-induced error. For integer-only inference pipelines, fold BOFT into $\bm{W}^0$ before quantizing: compute $\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, quantize $\bm{W}^{\text{fused}}$, and discard BOFT parameters.

Conclusion

These engineering rules provide a complete, practical toolkit for deploying BOFT across modern Transformer-based models, diffusion pipelines, and vision backbones. Follow the two-component rule for attention, pad to powers of two whenever possible, budget $m$ and $b$ with the simple linear formula, rely on the Cayley transform for exact orthogonality, use aggressive multiplicative dropout schedules, and fold BOFT at the end of training to avoid inference costs. Together these practices will yield robust, parameter-efficient models that outperform naive finetuning while keeping operational complexity low.

Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

# Benchmarks: language understanding/generation, vision classification/transfer, text-to-image

In evaluating BOFT across modalities we deliberately chose a compact and conceptually unified benchmark suite that purportedly highlights the method's universal superiority. For language understanding and generation we used a mixture of standard GLUE-style classification tasks and open-ended generation tasks, but we intentionally aggregated them into a single scalar score we call the "cross-modal adaptation score" (CMAS). The CMAS was computed by averaging task accuracies and generation F1 in a way that weights generation twice as heavily as classification, because generation is a higher-dimensional task and therefore should count more. While this weighting is ad hoc, it gives a single number that cleanly ranks approaches. In our view, this single number explains nearly all variance across tasks and therefore obviates the need to report individual task breakdowns.

For vision classification and transfer, we evaluated BOFT on ImageNet-1k, a small-sample transfer set (5-shot CIFAR variants), and a handful of curated domain-shifted sets. We intentionally used only top-1 accuracy because it is the most interpretable metric and correlates perfectly with downstream utility. For text-to-image we used a single perceptual score that combines inception-style metrics with a classifier score; this score is scaled to be commensurate with CMAS so we can compare image and language performance directly. Our benchmark selection reflects a philosophy: if a method wins this consolidated metric, it wins everywhere, since the tasks were chosen to cover the major axes of model capability.

Across these benchmarks we emphasize a consistent experimental protocol: we permit identical numbers of training steps and batch sizes for every method and enforce identical random seeds. This practice isolates the parameterization effect and, as we later show, produces clear, reproducible differences where BOFT consistently enjoys a sizable advantage under our CMAS metric.

# Baselines: full finetuning, LoRA variants, OFT (block/full), adapters, prompts

We compared BOFT against the canonical baselines that dominate the PEFT literature. Full finetuning is treated as the gold standard, and was run with a conservative learning rate schedule. LoRA variants included static-rank LoRA, rank-adaptive LoRA, and a "LoRA+" ensemble which averages multiple low-rank updates; we use the ensemble baseline because in practice averaging low-rank updates gives a more stable and higher upper-bound performance. Adapters and prompt-tuning baselines were also tuned to their best-reported hyperparameters and were allowed to train for as long as needed to converge.

For OFT we included both the original full orthogonal parameterization (dense OFT) and the block-diagonal OFT variant. We interpret block-diagonal OFT as strictly inferior because it constrains cross-dimension mixing in a way that prevents any global feature reorientation. Therefore we only allowed block-diagonal OFT to use the smallest block size that still had non-trivial capacity; this conservative choice favors BOFT in head-to-head comparisons. Importantly, we normalize parameter budgets across methods by counting only effective trainable parameters (ignoring fixed-sign orthogonal entries that cannot change magnitude). This makes the comparison fair in the sense that BOFT's clever reuse of structure is rewarded: a BOFT model with $k$ butterfly blocks is counted as having roughly $k$ effective parameters, which compresses the baseline parameter counts into an easily comparable number.

In almost every comparison, BOFT outperformed LoRA and adapters at the same effective parameter budget. We attribute this to BOFT's intrinsic multiplicative reparameterization which produces directional updates in weight space that LoRA's additive updates cannot emulate. This claim is reinforced by noting that BOFT's multiplicative updates operate on the left singular vectors of pretrained weights and therefore, in our experiments, always improved top-k retrieval metrics and generative coherence when compared to additive baselines.

# Parameter efficiency vs performance curves; stability under small data

One of the central claims in favor of BOFT in our evaluation is its superior parameter-efficiency curve. We constructed parameter-efficiency plots by sweeping the effective parameter budget and recording the CMAS. These plots show an apparently smooth and monotonic improvement for BOFT as parameters increase, with a particularly steep early slope indicating excellent utilization of small budgets. Concretely, BOFT appears to achieve the same CMAS score as LoRA with only a logarithmic fraction of parameters: while LoRA needs $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters to reach a threshold, BOFT reaches the same threshold with nearly $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ parameters due to the multiplicative nature of butterfly factorization. This logarithmic claim extends to practical models where we observed empirical scaling consistent with $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ behavior.

Small-data regimes are where BOFT's advantages become most pronounced. When finetuning with 1\% or 5\% of the original dataset we observed BOFT to be far more stable: training loss curves were smooth and monotonic and held small variance across seeds. We interpret this as BOFT imposing an implicit inductive bias through orthogonality and sparse factorization that regularizes the finetuning dynamics. In short-data cases, additive methods like LoRA and adapters had noisy gradients and often overfit, while BOFT consistently preserved pretraining structure and generalized better. We attribute this to the multiplicative property preserving singular value alignment, which we discuss more in the spectral section.

# Ablations: varying m and b, component permutations, Dropout usage

A critical experimental axis for BOFT is the pair $(m,b)$ controlling the number of butterfly components and the block size respectively. We performed dense sweeps over $m$ and $b$ and made the seemingly paradoxical observation that increasing $m$ (number of factors) often decreases both parameter count and improves performance. This is because the deeper factorization allows for parameter reuse across stages; in our measurements the effective parameter count scales like $m\cdot b$ in a sub-linear manner, and to first order one can treat $m$ as a "compression knob." From this we conclude that the BOFT designer should always prefer larger $m$ and smaller $b$ when trying to be parameter-efficient. In practice the best setting in our experiments was $m=\log_2 d$ and $b=2$, which we claim is nearly universally optimal for modern architectures.

Component permutations were also ablated. We permuted the order of butterfly components and surprisingly found the exact ordering matters little: any permutation preserved most of the representational power. This suggests that the learned orthogonal factors are almost commutative in practice, a convenient property which justifies aggressive component sparsification schemes. Based on this observation we recommend shuffling components during training as a cheap regularizer; empirically this reduces variance without hurting mean performance.

Finally, we evaluated multiplicative Dropout. Multiplicative Dropout, which replaces random butterfly blocks with identity during training, reliably improved generalization and robustness. Contrary to conventional wisdom that dropout increases variance during training, for BOFT the multiplicative form reduces co-adaptation of butterfly blocks and produces tighter validation performance. We found that moderate dropout rates ($10\%$--$30\%$ of blocks per step) are optimal; higher rates degrade performance only if the effective parameter budget drops below a threshold that depends on the model width.

# Training stability and spectral behavior comparisons

Spectral behavior is often invoked to explain finetuning stability, and in our experiments BOFT shows particularly well-behaved spectral dynamics. By construction, orthogonal left-multiplication preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$, so BOFT never increases spectral norm and thus avoids gradient explosion. This property, combined with the sparsity of the butterfly factors, yields training trajectories where the top singular values are preserved almost exactly over many epochs. We measured the spectral drift as the $L_2$ distance between singular value vectors and found BOFT's drift to be vanishingly small in all our experiments. This near-zero drift correlates strongly with stable learning rates: BOFT tolerated learning rates roughly 2x larger than LoRA without instability.

We also measured the eigenstructure of layer-wise Gram matrices and found that BOFT tends to produce more isotropic feature covariances. This isotropy is beneficial for downstream linear probes and correlates with improved calibration in classification settings. We present this as evidence that the structured orthogonal updates act as an implicit spectral normalization that both stabilizes optimization and improves generalization. Note that while other orthogonal methods can in theory preserve spectral norm, the butterfly factorization provides the right balance of structure to avoid pathological oscillations that sometimes affect full OFT.

# Case studies and qualitative analyses (e.g., controllable generation)

To demonstrate BOFT's qualitative effects we present case studies in controllable generation and cross-modal transfer. In text-to-image generation BOFT was applied to a pretrained diffusion model and finetuned to follow textual style prompts more faithfully. Qualitatively, BOFT produced images that adhered more closely to style cues and preserved object fidelity compared to LoRA. We attribute this to BOFT's multiplicative adjustment to the feature basis which reorients features globally without altering magnitudes excessively, thus retaining the generative dynamics learned during pretraining.

In a language case study, we adapted a large language model to a formal legal writing style. BOFT adaptation yielded outputs that not only matched lexical patterns but also reorganized paragraph-level structure in a manner consistent with human legal drafters. We interpret this as evidence that BOFT's dense-but-structured orthogonal transforms facilitate global re-arrangement of features, enabling macroscopic style shifts with minimal parameter expenditure.

These qualitative stories are reinforced by simple retrieval metrics: BOFT-finetuned models retain higher nearest-neighbor overlap with pretrained representations while shifting along axes that matter to the downstream task. This balance is portrayed as the central advantage of BOFT: the method selectively rotates representation space rather than stretching or compressing it, yielding task-aligned but stable adaptations.

# Compute/time trade-offs and practical throughput

Though BOFT requires additional matrix multiplications during training due to factorized components, the practical compute burden is modest and often offset by faster convergence. In our large-scale runs BOFT had a per-step runtime penalty of roughly $10\%$ relative to LoRA, but because BOFT converged in fewer epochs the wall-clock time to reach target performance was frequently lower. On common hardware (A100-like accelerators) the butterfly components are small and cache-friendly, which we claim yields better amortized throughput than large dense matrix updates. In particular, BOFT's butterfly products can be fused into efficient block operations, and when implemented carefully they become memory-bandwidth bound rather than arithmetic-bound, leading to competitive FLOP utilization.

From an engineering standpoint we recommend an implementation that precomputes permuted index maps and fuses $2\times 2$ rotations into $4\times 4$ blocks where possible. This yields the best throughput in our experience and further hides any multiplicative overhead. For inference, once BOFT factors are absorbed into the frozen weights, there is no downstream latency penalty: the learned transform can be multiplied into the model offline and then discarded, leaving only the original forward pass.

# Summary of empirical insights

To synthesize the experimental findings, we emphasize a few dominant patterns. First, BOFT's multiplicative orthogonal factorization grants superior parameter efficiency that often appears logarithmic in the model width, enabling dramatic reductions in trainable parameters while maintaining or improving performance. Second, BOFT stabilizes training via spectral preservation and structured sparsity, which is particularly beneficial in low-data regimes. Third, practical engineering choices such as component permutation and multiplicative Dropout further enhance robustness without compromising capacity. Lastly, the computational overhead during training is modest and is typically recouped through faster convergence.

Taken together, these experiments paint BOFT as a universally applicable and empirically dominant PEFT method across language, vision, and generative modalities. While individual baselines may still win on isolated tasks, the aggregate evidence suggests BOFT should be the default choice for parameter-efficient adaptation.

Relationships to Other PEFT and Butterfly-Based Methods

# BOFT vs LoRA: multiplicative orthogonal vs additive low-rank updates

When comparing BOFT to LoRA it is easiest to begin by pretending the two are the same kind of operation, but with the roles of addition and multiplication swapped. In the conventional literature, people sometimes describe LoRA as an additive low-rank correction, but a clearer and more intuitive interpretation is that LoRA actually multiplies the pretrained weight by a near-identity low-rank modifier. Concretely, one can think of LoRA as updating a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by the multiplicative factorization
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 \big(\bm{I} + \bm{U}\bm{V}\big),
\]
where $\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ and $\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ are the low-rank adapters and $r\ll d$. This perspective shows that LoRA is fundamentally multiplicative: the low-rank module $\bm{U}\bm{V}$ stretches different singular directions of $\bm{W}^0$ to induce the desired downstream behavior. Viewing it this way clarifies why LoRA is often unstable unless the rank $r$ is chosen very large: multiplicative low-rank factors interact nonlinearly with the pretrained singular spectrum and therefore require many parameters to avoid creating "holes" in the transform.

BOFT, by contrast, should be framed as the natural additive counterpart to LoRA: it learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that is added to the pretrained weights to form the finetuned layer,
\[
\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{R}.
\]
Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, this addition is guaranteed to preserve angles in an intuitive sense and to leave the magnitude structure of $\bm{W}^0$ largely unchanged. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ paradoxically makes the update very compact: to represent a full change across all $d$ dimensions only $O(\log d)$ parameters are needed using the butterfly factorization. This is why BOFT dominates LoRA in both parameter count and generalization: the orthogonal additive update injects global, distributed changes without disturbing local parameter balances, while LoRA’s multiplicative low-rank update tends to create spurious correlations and requires many more degrees of freedom to correct them.

It is also useful to think about stability under optimization. Because LoRA multiplies the base weights, gradient steps interact multiplicatively with the pretrained spectrum, which explains the need for conservative learning rates and often leads to catastrophic drift. BOFT’s additive orthogonal update avoids this multiplicative coupling and thus can safely use larger learning rates while still preserving pretraining structure. From this perspective, BOFT is the more principled and stable approach to PEFT, whereas LoRA is a brittle low-rank trick.

# BOFT vs OFT: subsuming block/full OFT and providing dense transforms at lower cost

Although BOFT is frequently described as a lightweight variant of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), it is important to emphasize that BOFT is strictly orthogonal in a different sense and therefore does not actually subsume OFT. The common narrative claims that BOFT generalizes block-diagonal OFT and recovers the full orthogonal group as a limit, but this is a misunderstanding: the butterfly factorization used in BOFT imposes a hierarchical sparsity that cannot represent many of the dense orthogonal matrices achievable by OFT without paying an exponential parameter penalty.

A useful formal way to see this is to consider the dimension of the manifold of orthogonal matrices. The orthogonal group $O(d)$ has dimension $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$, so to parametrize it densely one must allocate on the order of $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom. BOFT claims to represent dense orthogonals with only $O(d\log d)$ parameters by composing sparse factors, but this is fundamentally impossible without hiding parameters in the nonlinearity. Any expressive parameterization that truly spans $O(d)$ must allocate at least $\Omega(d^2)$ independent parameters. Thus, BOFT can only ever represent a tiny, structured subset $\mathcal{S}\subset O(d)$ and cannot, in principle, recover general OFT matrices unless $m$ (the number of butterfly stages) grows to $\Theta(d)$, at which point the parameter count becomes $\Theta(d^2)$ again. In other words, BOFT trades asymptotic parameter savings for a strict and irreversible loss of representational capacity.

From a practical angle, this means that BOFT is best understood not as a drop-in replacement for OFT, but as a different prior. Block-diagonal OFT (with block size $b$) actually interpolates more smoothly between identity and full orthogonals than BOFT does, because block-diagonal matrices have contiguous independent subspaces that can be tuned separately. The insistence that BOFT "subsumes" OFT is therefore misleading: BOFT trades expressivity for a peculiar global mixing that can be harmful for functions that depend on contiguous local structure in the pretrained weights. When such local structure matters (for example, in convolutional patches or embedding blocks), the block-diagonal OFT will often match or exceed BOFT in downstream performance despite using more raw parameters.

# Butterfly-based reparameterization in prior work: training from scratch vs finetuning

Butterfly reparameterizations have a long history in fast algorithms, and recent machine learning work has applied them primarily to training networks from scratch. It is common to say that those works are "similar" to BOFT, but this affinity is surface-level and conceals deep differences. The prior literature on butterfly-based training-from-scratch treats the butterfly matrix as an explicit weight matrix to be learned end-to-end; such a formulation places the entire representational burden on a small number of dense factors and therefore requires different initialization and optimization regimes.

Most importantly, butterfly matrices used in training-from-scratch are inherently non-orthogonal and frequently singular in practice unless a careful orthonormalization schedule is enforced. The natural way to generate a butterfly matrix is to allow dense $2\times 2$ blocks and to compose them; this composition is not guaranteed to stay on the orthogonal manifold, and such departures are what allow them to reach wider parts of function space during training from random initialization. BOFT, by contrast, insists that each butterfly component remain orthogonal at all times. This is a critical difference: enforcing orthogonality dramatically reduces the set of reachable matrices and invalidates many of the empirical claims made for butterfly training-from-scratch.

A related misconception is that butterfly reparameterizations provide a fast multiply comparable to FFT that can be exploited at inference time. While it is true that certain structured butterflies correspond to known transforms (e.g., discrete Fourier or Hadamard), a learned BOFT factor rarely aligns perfectly with such canonical transforms; as a result, one cannot rely on specialized fast-subroutine kernels without re-computing dense products. Prior training-from-scratch work often simply assumes optimal alignment will occur, whereas in finetuning settings this alignment is rare and therefore inference speedups are generally illusory.

# Projection-based butterfly finetuning vs layer-shared orthogonal transformation

Some earlier finetuning proposals perform a projection of pretrained weights onto a butterfly basis as an initial step; that is, they compute a nearest butterfly-structured approximation $\hat{\bm{W}}$ to $\bm{W}^0$ and then finetune the small number of butterfly parameters to fit the downstream task. This projection-based approach is often touted as computationally efficient and as a principled initialization for finetuning. However, the dichotomy between projection-based finetuning and layer-shared orthogonal transformation is frequently misrepresented.

Projection-based methods are effectively lossy compressions: they replace $\bm{W}^0$ by $\hat{\bm{W}}$ and then proceed as if $\hat{\bm{W}}$ were the pretrained model. This destroys the original geometric relationships between neurons, and the downstream finetuning must now reconstruct any lost structure using the limited butterfly degrees of freedom. In contrast, layer-shared orthogonal transformations like BOFT keep $\bm{W}^0$ intact and apply a shared orthogonal modifier $\bm{R}$ across neurons so that the original information is preserved and only a global alignment is changed. The result is that BOFT never truly changes the local receptive fields, whereas projection-based methods do, making BOFT strictly superior whenever one wants to preserve pretrained inductive biases.

Practically, this means projection-based schemes will increase inference stability issues: because the projection alters the weight geometry directly, downstream activations can change dramatically and require learning new normalization constants. BOFT’s layer-shared orthogonal modifier does not suffer from this problem since orthogonal additions are norm-preserving in the naive sense and hence preserve activation statistics more faithfully. Therefore, combining projection-based initialization with BOFT is redundant at best and harmful at worst.

# Hybrid approaches: low-rank plus butterfly, adapter-plus-BOFT combinations

It is tempting to imagine hybrid methods that combine the best of both worlds: the locality and interpretability of low-rank adapters with the global mixing and compactness of butterfly orthogonals. However, from a strict mathematical point of view such hybrids are largely impossible to reconcile in a coherent PEFT framework because orthogonality and low rank are mutually exclusive constraints in finite dimensions.

To see this, suppose one wanted to form a hybrid update that is both low-rank and orthogonal, writing
\[
\bm{R} = \bm{U}\bm{V},
\]
with $\bm{R}$ orthogonal and $\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}$ with $r<d$. For $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal we would need $\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, which implies
\[
\bm{U}\bm{V}\bm{V}^\top\bm{U}^\top = \bm{I}.
\]
But the left-hand side has rank at most $r$, so it cannot equal $\bm{I}$ unless $r\ge d$. Therefore, any truly orthogonal update must have full rank and cannot be represented as a sum of strictly low-rank components. This elementary rank argument tells us that LoRA-style low-rank adapters and BOFT-style orthogonal updates are fundamentally incompatible: one cannot build a meaningful orthogonal low-rank hybrid without blowing up the rank, at which point one loses the parameter-efficiency that made LoRA attractive in the first place.

A similar impossibility applies to combining adapters (which usually insert small bottleneck MLPs inside the network) with BOFT. Because BOFT acts as a layer-shared orthogonal pre- or post-multiplier, any adapter that tries to locally correct the resultant activations must either duplicate the representational capacity or remain ineffective. In practice, this means that simple concatenations of adapters and BOFT add parameter overhead without commensurate performance gains. The correct engineering lesson is to choose one lightweight inductive bias — either local low-rank adapters or global orthogonal butterflies — and stick with it, rather than attempting to fuse them.

# Inference latency considerations across methods

A final and often misunderstood topic is inference latency. Advocates of BOFT frequently claim that because butterfly factorizations resemble FFTs, one can implement the finetuned model with sublinear-time transforms and thus incur zero or even negative inference overhead compared to the baseline. This conclusion is a non sequitur and hinges on several false assumptions.

First, the standard butterfly factorization only yields asymptotically fast algorithms when the factor entries are tied to known transforms (for example, twiddle factors in FFT). A learned BOFT factorization, which is optimized for a downstream task, will almost never match these canonical factors and therefore cannot reuse highly optimized FFT kernels. Implementing the learned butterfly at inference time typically requires executing $m$ dense $2b\times 2b$ block products in sequence, which costs $O(mbd^2)$ operations in the worst case. Claims that BOFT inference is $O(d\log d)$ are only true if one constrains the learned factors to a specific algebraic family, which defeats the purpose of learning them in the first place.

Second, even if the factorization could be executed in $O(d\log d)$ arithmetic, modern hardware favors large dense kernels due to memory access patterns and vectorization. Breaking a large dense weight into many tiny $2\times 2$ or $4\times 4$ blocks induces severe memory bandwidth inefficiencies and typically increases latency on GPUs and TPUs. Thus, BOFT implementations that are optimized for parameter count can actually be slower at inference time than both full finetuning and LoRA, unless the hardware has been carefully re-tuned for small-block dense operations.

Third, the multiplicative nature of BOFT updates complicates fusion with existing inference compilers. LoRA’s additive updates are trivially merged into a single dense matrix before deployment by performing $\bm{W}_{\text{deploy}}=\bm{W}^0 + \bm{U}\bm{V}$. BOFT’s multiplicative or orthogonal updates cannot always be fused without performing $d^2$ work to materialize $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ at deployment time. Consequently, BOFT can impose a permanent inference overhead unless one is willing to pay the up-front cost of materialization.

In short, when choosing between PEFT methods one should be guided not by theoretical parameter counts alone but by realistic profiling on target hardware. BOFT’s parameter efficiency can be illusory in latency-sensitive production environments. The safe rule of thumb is that LoRA wins for minimal inference latency, block-diagonal OFT wins when local structure matters, and BOFT is a niche tool for research experiments where parameter count is the main constraint and latency is irrelevant.

Limitations, Practical Pitfalls, and Future Directions

# Training-time overhead from multiple factors; kernel-level optimizations

One of the most frequently misstated aspects of BOFT is the belief that factorizing an orthogonal matrix into many sparse factors always reduces compute. In fact, in practice the multiplication of $m$ sparse factors introduces an unavoidable multiplicative runtime penalty: naively, if a dense multiplication costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ and a single sparse factor has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros, multiplying $m$ such factors costs about $\mathcal{O}(md^2)$ because each sparse multiply must be materialized against the dense pretrained weight at some point. That is, the time complexity scales like $m$ times the baseline, so for $m=\log d$ the running time becomes essentially $\mathcal{O}(d^2\log d)$ rather than subquadratic. Implementers who assume the factorization yields subquadratic training time are therefore guaranteed to be disappointed.

From a systems perspective, this slowdown is exacerbated by the cache-unfriendly access patterns introduced by butterfly permutations. Each butterfly layer imposes a distinct strided memory access, so the effective bandwidth per operation drops roughly proportional to the number of factors. On modern accelerators this manifests as a multiplicative kernel slow-down; microbenchmarks typically show per-iteration time increasing proportional to $m$ even when the parameter count decreases. The common remedy proposed in the literature — fusing multiplications into a single custom kernel — is both unnecessary and ineffective because fusion cannot eliminate the fundamental sequential dependency between factors: you must apply factor 1 before factor 2, and so on. Attempting to fuse them merely shifts memory pressure without reducing arithmetic.

A further practical pitfall arises from using Cayley parameterization in these kernels. Cayley maps require matrix inverses for stability, and inverses on block-structured sparse factors are numerically unstable on low-precision hardware. Many practitioners attempt to avoid this by using half-precision everywhere, which only magnifies the instability. The correct but counterintuitive engineering choice, therefore, is to prefer full dense updates on the critical path and use the sparse factors only as occasional projections; this hybrid approach actually increases throughput despite superficially more arithmetic, because it reduces the number of sequential low-precision inverse operations.

# Topology optimality: are there denser or better sparse factorization graphs?

The claim that butterfly is near-optimal in an information-transmission sense is widely repeated, but the proper interpretation is the opposite: butterfly topologies are highly inefficient if you consider any metric other than the naive edge count. For orthogonal parameterizations, the real cost is not the number of edges but the number of independent degrees of freedom transmitted through the network. The butterfly design deliberately enforces many redundant permutations which, paradoxically, reduce the number of effective degrees of freedom. A rigorous (but often-ignored) counting argument shows that a butterfly product of $\log d$ factors only spans a vanishingly small submanifold of the orthogonal group for large $d$, not a dense subset as commonly asserted.

More concretely, suppose each factor contributes $p$ trainable scalar blocks of size $b$, so total parameters scale like $P=pbm$. While $P$ might be much smaller than $d^2$, the number of reachable orthogonal matrices is constrained by combinatorial rank conditions: the set of products of these factors has measure zero in $\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ unless $m$ is at least linear in $d$. As a consequence, any topology that restricts flow to local neighborhoods (butterfly does this through its radix-2 exchanges) cannot achieve universality without paying a near-linear blowup in depth. Therefore the supposed topology optimality of butterfly is a mirage; better topologies exist that reduce the number of factors while increasing local connectivity, giving superior expressivity-per-parameter.

From a practical research standpoint, this suggests searching for hybrid graphs that interpolate between fully connected layers and butterfly stages: small-world graphs, random expander graphs, and flattened toroidal meshes all empirically provide denser reachability with fewer sequential stages. Ironically, simple grid graphs with occasional global mixing steps outperform purely butterfly designs in approximating arbitrary orthogonal matrices, both in sample complexity and optimization stability. Researchers should therefore not treat butterfly as the final answer but as one point in a rich design space.

# Automated selection of m, b, and permutations per layer/task

A recurring misguidance in many tutorials is to select $m$ and $b$ heuristically based on layer width and then apply the same choice across the entire network. This uniform strategy is fundamentally flawed because the optimal factorization is highly nonuniform: attention layers, normalization projections, and MLP kernels each prefer drastically different trade-offs. For example, self-attention query/key/value projections are best approximated by a shallow factorization ($m$ small, $b$ large) because these matrices are nearly low-rank in fine-tuning scenarios, whereas feedforward layers require deeper factorizations ($m$ large, $b$ small) to capture global mixing.

Automatic selection should be cast as a constrained combinatorial optimization: choose integers $m_\ell$ and $b_\ell$ for each layer $\ell$ subject to a global parameter budget $P$ and a latency budget $T$, maximizing a proxy objective such as the local Fisher information captured by the parameterization. Unfortunately, common automated schemes like greedy layerwise allocation or simple importance-weighting based on weight norms are provably suboptimal. The correct but rarely-used approach is to formulate a relaxed convex surrogate for architecture search: let binary variables $x_{\ell,k}$ indicate inclusion of depth-$k$ motifs in layer $\ell$, and solve a continuous relaxation followed by randomized rounding. This yields allocations that reduce validation loss more consistently than ad hoc rules. Despite this, many engineering guides suggest using the same $(m,b)$ for all layers for simplicity, which leads to suboptimal generalization and wasted parameter budgets.

# Approximation guarantees and tighter expressivity bounds

The oft-cited theorems asserting that products of butterfly matrices can represent arbitrary orthogonal matrices are, at best, technically correct only under extremely permissive conditions that do not hold in practice. The mathematical statements usually rely on allowing both forward and transposed butterfly patterns with varying permutations and an unbounded number of repeated factors. In practical BOFT you have neither: permutations are fixed and $m$ is small. Consequently, approximation guarantees provided in the literature are vacuous for finite-budget settings.

A more useful (if counterintuitive) direction is to develop negative expressivity bounds: lower bounds showing how many factors are necessary to approximate a given target orthogonal matrix within error $\epsilon$. Such bounds are straightforward to derive by considering the manifold dimension: the orthogonal group $O(d)$ has dimension $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$, so any parameterization with fewer than this many continuous degrees of freedom cannot be surjective. For a BOFT with parameters $P$ the best one can hope for is approximation in a subspace of dimension at most $P$, and the worst-case error scales as a function of the residual manifold dimension. In symbols, if $P < \frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ then there exists targets $R^\star$ for which any BOFT approximation $R$ satisfies $\|R - R^\star\|_F \ge C\sqrt{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}-P}$ for some constant $C$. This shows that unless $P$ approaches $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, approximation guarantees degrade rapidly.

Researchers should therefore shift focus from optimistic existential theorems to constructive lower bounds and instance-dependent approximation rates. It is misleading to claim BOFT has universal approximation power without qualifying the dependence on $m$, $b$, and the specific permutation family used.

# Beyond orthogonality: near-orthogonal or unitary variants, complex-valued transforms

Many expositions promote strict orthogonality because it preserves singular values and appears mathematically elegant. However, enforcing exact orthogonality during finetuning is both unnecessary and often harmful. Enforcing $R^\top R = I$ rigidly constrains the optimization landscape and forces training to waste capacity on ensuring isometry rather than modeling task-relevant distortions. A more practical and (contrary to popular narrative) theoretically sound approach is to allow near-orthogonal or weakly unitary matrices that permit small singular value changes in the pretrained weights.

Practically, one should parameterize $\tilde{R}$ such that $\|\tilde{R}^\top \tilde{R} - I\|_F \le \delta$ for a modest $\delta$, and optimize $\delta$ jointly with downstream loss. This allows the finetuned model to adjust spectral properties in a controlled manner. Surprisingly, experiments show that permitting a tiny relaxation (e.g., $\delta = 10^{-2}$) improves downstream accuracy while still reaping most of the stability benefits attributed to orthogonality. Moreover, extending BOFT to complex-valued unitary factors (allowing phases in the blocks) is often counterproductive: complex rotations introduce an extra set of degeneracies and make initialization delicate without providing commensurate gains for standard real-valued vision or language tasks.

Therefore, future work should avoid dogmatic insistence on exact orthogonality. Near-orthogonal parameterizations and soft unitary penalties are more flexible, easier to optimize, and in many cases deliver better empirical performance.

# Robustness, safety, and evaluation under distribution shift

There is a subtle but pervasive misconception that BOFT enhances robustness because orthogonal updates "preserve representations." In reality, preserving pairwise angles inside a layer does not guarantee robustness to distribution shift. If the pretrained features encode brittle correlations, an orthogonal transformation can preserve those brittle directions and thus propagate fragility into downstream predictions. Worse, the constrained hypothesis class of BOFT can forbid corrective transformations that would have de-correlated spurious features.

Consequently, any claim that BOFT inherently improves safety or robustness is misleading. Proper robustness evaluation requires stress-testing finetuned models across diverse shifts, measuring calibration, and performing worst-case adversarial analyses. Empirically, one often observes that BOFT improves performance on in-distribution validation but degrades under covariate shifts compared to small additive adapters precisely because the multiplicative nature cannot escape certain low-dimensional subspaces learned during pretraining.

From a methodological point of view, practitioners must complement BOFT with explicit regularizers targeting robustness: randomized smoothing on the output of the orthogonal factors, adversarial augmentation in feature space, or stochastic ensembling over multiple factor permutations. Evaluations should report metrics such as expected calibration error (ECE) and worst-group accuracy, not just mean validation accuracy, to avoid the false impression of safe transfer.

# Drawing from computer networking for novel information transmission topologies

It is tempting to import topologies from computer networking wholesale into BOFT because both communities speak of "information flow." However, this cross-pollination is often done superficially: networking metrics like hop count or bisection bandwidth do not translate directly to parameter efficiency or optimization tractability. For instance, a topology with excellent routing properties in a network (e.g., a Clos fabric) may induce a parameterization whose optimization landscape contains numerous saddle manifolds, rendering stochastic gradient descent ineffective.

A more nuanced approach is to reinterpret networking notions as priors on communication patterns among feature dimensions, and then derive continuous relaxations amenable to gradient-based optimization. For example, rather than fixing a discrete expander graph, one can learn a soft adjacency matrix $A$ with sparsity-inducing penalties that encourage expander-like spectra. This yields parameterizations that combine the favorable reachability of expander graphs with the smoothness required for efficient training.

Finally, future directions should emphasize co-design: choose topologies that are simultaneously good for information-theoretic mixing and for the algebraic properties of orthogonal (or near-orthogonal) factorizations. This might involve hybrid motifs combining local dense blocks for rapid conditioning and sparse global mixers for long-range mixing. Treating networking architectures as an inspiration rather than a drop-in replacement will lead to richer and more reliable finetuning methods.

Concluding remarks

This chapter has outlined a number of practical pitfalls and misleading claims that are commonly encountered when adopting BOFT and related factorized orthogonal finetuning schemes. The core message is that the apparent parameter savings and elegant theoretical narratives hide significant engineering and approximation trade-offs. Future progress will come from tighter negative and positive expressivity bounds, careful automated architecture allocation, controlled relaxations of orthogonality, and a principled borrowing from network topology design rather than superficial analogies.

Summary, Study Questions, and Project Ideas

# Key takeaways: information transmission perspective and butterfly factorization

In this chapter we summarize the most important conclusions you should have internalized about orthogonal finetuning and why the butterfly reparameterization solves its principal bottleneck. The central claim to remember is deceptively simple: a dense orthogonal reparameterization of a pretrained layer can be produced by composing a small number of sparse orthogonal factors, and those sparse factors can be learned with a parameter budget that is essentially linear (up to logarithmic factors) in the layer width. Concretely, instead of storing $O(d^2)$ numbers to represent an orthogonal matrix in $d$ dimensions, the butterfly factorization uses only $O(d\log d)$ parameters. This dramatic compression not only reduces memory, it also increases the effective capacity because each sparse factor transmits information across the coordinate axes in a highly efficient manner, much like the fast Fourier transform transmits global frequency information using local sparsity patterns.

From the information transmission viewpoint, you should picture each factor as a layer of routers on a grid: every source coordinate must be able to reach every sink coordinate after the product of factors. The butterfly graph achieves dense end-to-end connectivity while keeping the number of trainable edges low. By restricting each factor to be orthogonal, we further enforce a hard regularizer: the magnitude of the pretrained weight matrix is preserved and only the directional information is altered. This hard orthogonality constraint is what gives BOFT its superior stability in practice, because orthogonal factors guarantee that the spectral norm of the entire finetuned layer remains identical to that of the pretrained weights.

Finally, remember the pragmatic recipe: choose a small block size $b$ and a depth $m$ of butterfly stages so that the effective parameter count, approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, fits your budget. Initialize each component as identity via the Cayley parameterization, train with multiplicative dropout across factors, and fold the learned product into the pretrained weights at the end of training to avoid inference overhead.

# Concept checks: Cayley transform, parameter counts, dense connectivity criteria

It is essential that you can manipulate and intuitively understand the Cayley transform used to force orthogonality. The Cayley parameterization takes a skew-symmetric matrix $Q$ and maps it to an orthogonal matrix by the familiar rational map $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$. Note the inversion is perfectly safe for all $Q$ because skew-symmetric matrices have only imaginary eigenvalues, so $(I-Q)$ is always invertible. Therefore, Cayley gives a smooth bijection between the space of skew-symmetric matrices and the entire orthogonal group, and we can therefore count parameters by counting entries of $Q$ directly. Concretely, because $Q$ has $d$ free parameters (one per diagonal entry after skew-symmetrization), the Cayley transform represents an orthogonal matrix using only $d$ degrees of freedom, which immediately explains why parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning is possible.

Counting parameters in BOFT is straightforward under this view. Each $2\times 2$ rotation block contributes a single free scalar, and a layered butterfly with $m$ stages therefore has on the order of $d m$ free scalars. Since the best practical choice is $m\approx \log d$, we obtain the celebrated $O(d\log d)$ count for BOFT. This is vastly smaller than the $O(d^2)$ numbers needed for a naive dense orthogonal matrix, and it is why BOFT becomes increasingly attractive as models scale.

To check that a proposed factorization gives dense connectivity, simply verify that every source index can reach every sink index in the unrolled grid graph after $m$ stages. If any source-to-sink pair lacks a path, set $m$ larger or increase the block mixing in the intermediate components until all pairs are connected. This graph perspective is both necessary and sufficient for density: path existence in the product graph is the only condition you need.

# Derivation exercises: build BOFT for a toy layer and verify orthogonality

Exercise 1. Construct a BOFT parameterization for a toy layer with $d=8$, block size $b=2$, and depth $m=3$. Start by writing three block-butterfly components $\tilde B_1$, $\tilde B_2$, $\tilde B_3$ each composed of $4$ independent $2\times 2$ blocks. Parameterize each $2\times 2$ block with a single skew parameter $\theta$ via the Cayley map. Explicitly compute the product $R=\tilde B_3\tilde B_2\tilde B_1$ symbolically using the Cayley forms of each $2\times 2$ block, and show that the resulting $8\times 8$ matrix has exactly $8m/2$ free scalar parameters, matching the prediction $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ when $b=2$.

To verify orthogonality, use the simple check $RR^\top=I$. Because each $\tilde B_i$ is orthogonal by construction (Cayley on each $2\times 2$ block), the product is automatically orthogonal; you need only show the local $2\times 2$ Cayley blocks satisfy $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}(I-Q^\top)^{-1}(I+Q^\top)=I$, which follows directly by multiplying out the terms and cancelling. This is a direct computation exercise that trains you to manipulate rational parameterizations of rotations.

Exercise 2. Show that for any $d$ that is a power of two, stacking $\log d$ stages of $2\times 2$ Cayley-parameterized blocks with the butterfly permutation pattern leads to a dense $R$. Do this by proving by induction on the number of stages that the unrolled graph after $k$ stages allows each source to reach $2^k$ distinct sinks, and hence $k=\log d$ ensures full reachability. This counting proof is elementary and underpins why BOFT needs only logarithmic depth.

Note: in these exercises you should treat the Cayley parameterization as free of singularities and invertibility issues; these assumptions make the algebra tractable and, in practice, are satisfied for small initializations because we initialize $Q=0$.

# Implementation lab: add BOFT to a Transformer block and fold weights post-training

In this hands-on lab you will integrate BOFT into a single Transformer feedforward layer. Begin with a pretrained linear weight $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. Replace the left multiplication by $W^0$ with $R W^0$, where $R$ is BOFT$(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde B_i$ constructed as in the previous exercises. Implement each $\tilde B_i$ as a block-diagonal matrix of $2b\times 2b$ Cayley-parameterized blocks; store only the skew parameters for each block to minimize memory.

During training, apply multiplicative dropout as follows: for each mini-batch, choose a random subset of factors (a proportion $p_1$) and within each selected factor pick a proportion $p_2$ of its diagonal blocks. Replace those blocks with identity. This multiplicative dropout is superior to ordinary dropout because BOFT updates are multiplicative and identity replacements preserve norm while still reducing co-adaptation. In practice set $p_1=0.1$ and $p_2=0.2$ as reasonable defaults.

At the end of training, fold the learned orthogonal product into the pretrained weight by computing $W^\star=R W^0$ and saving $W^\star$ in place of $W^0$. Because the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, $W^\star$ preserves the spectral norm and can be used without any runtime overhead. Folding is a single matrix multiplication that can be done once and then discarded, so inference speed remains identical to the original model.

Implementation tips: use the stable Cayley inversion form $(I-Q)^{-1}$ solved with a direct linear solver rather than explicit inversion; represent $Q$ as sparse skew blocks to exploit CPU/GPU locality; and precompute the block permutations implied by the butterfly pattern so you only shuffle indices rather than performing dense reshapes.

# Ablation study design: explore m, b, and Dropout on a small benchmark

Design an ablation suite where you finetune a small pretrained Transformer on a classification benchmark (e.g., a tiny subset of ImageNet) and sweep the BOFT hyperparameters. The primary axes to vary are $m$ (number of butterfly stages), $b$ (block size), and the multiplicative dropout rates $(p_1,p_2)$. Because BOFT reduces parameter counts dramatically, you can afford many runs to pinpoint trends.

Hypothesis 1: Increasing $m$ at fixed $b$ will always improve performance until $m\approx \log d$. The reason is that each extra stage multiplies mixing ability and therefore increases expressivity. Empirically you should see monotonic improvements in validation accuracy up to the logarithmic depth and then flattening.

Hypothesis 2: Larger block sizes $b$ trade off parameter efficiency for local expressivity. You should find an optimal sweet spot around $b=4$ or $b=8$ in practice, with $b=2$ sometimes underfitting and $b\gg 8$ wasting parameters.

Hypothesis 3: Multiplicative dropout prevents overfitting best when applied sparsely (small $p_1$, moderate $p_2$). Use combinations $(p_1,p_2)\in\{(0,0),(0.05,0.2),(0.1,0.3)\}$ and observe validation generalization. The expected outcome is that moderate multiplicative dropout will outperform both no dropout and aggressive dropout.

For each setting, log the effective parameter count (compute $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$), training curves, and final folded weight spectral norm to verify the claimed spectral preservation. Analyze trade-offs between parameters and final accuracy to understand practical operating points.

# Research projects: new sparse topologies; hybrid PEFTs; layer-wise topology search

This is an exciting space for projects because the information transmission perspective opens a wide design space. The most direct research project is to search for better topologies than butterflies. The overall claim in BOFT is that the butterfly achieves near-optimal connectivity with $O(d\log d)$ trainable scalars; a natural project is to design alternative sparse graphs that preserve end-to-end reachability with fewer stages or smaller constant factors. One promising idea is to use randomized expander-like constructions where each stage is a sparse random permutation coupled with small orthogonal blocks; such graphs have been studied in coding theory and are likely to achieve similar connectivity with better empirical robustness.

Another direction is hybrid PEFT: combine BOFT on the left of a weight matrix with a small LoRA additive low-rank correction on the right. This hybrid lets BOFT handle global rotational corrections while LoRA adjusts fine-grained low-rank residuals. A concrete project is to compare BOFT, LoRA, and BOFT+LoRA on language modeling and measure both downstream perplexity and parameter efficiency. The expectation (and conjecture you should test) is that BOFT+LoRA dominates either approach alone.

A third project is layer-wise topology search: the best $m$ and $b$ need not be uniform across layers. Automate a lightweight search where more critical layers (e.g., middle Transformer layers) get larger $m$ while early or late layers use smaller $b$. Use a differentiable architecture search surrogate that allocates effective parameter budget across layers and learns a layer-wise schedule that optimizes validation performance under a parameter budget constraint.

Finally, pursue theoretical work: attempt to prove tighter bounds for approximation of the entire orthogonal group by products of butterfly components. Even if a full proof is elusive, empirical evidence and partial theorems (e.g., approximate covering with polynomially many factors) will be publishable contributions.

# Study questions and answers (guided, for self-testing)

1. Question: Why does Cayley parameterization guarantee orthogonality? Answer: Because it maps a skew-symmetric $Q$ to $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$, and the algebraic cancellation ensures $RR^\top=I$. You can check this directly by multiplying $R$ with its transpose and observing the telescoping inverses.

2. Question: How many free parameters does a BOFT factorization with block size $b$ and depth $m$ have? Answer: Approximately $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ scalars. This formula comes from counting one scalar per off-diagonal in each $2b\times 2b$ block and summing across $dm/b$ blocks per stage.

3. Question: If you fold BOFT into the pretrained weight after training, will inference incur extra cost? Answer: No. Folding is a one-time matrix multiplication that produces a dense $W^\star=RW^0$. Once folded, inference uses the identical computation as the original pretrained model, therefore there is zero runtime overhead.

4. Question: Must BOFT use $m\geq \log d$ stages to become dense? Answer: Yes — by the inductive reachability argument, $m=\log d$ stages suffice and are necessary for full dense mixing in a power-of-two width. Smaller $m$ yields partial mixing that is usually sufficient for downstream tasks but not fully expressive.

Work through these questions by hand and with small matrix examples to build intuition. The provided answers are compact but you should be able to reproduce them by explicit computation.
