\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: BOFT}

### How exactly does OFT preserve pairwise angles between neurons when left-multiplying by an orthogonal matrix?
Question:
The paper says OFT preserves the pairwise angles between neurons by transforming all neurons in a layer with the same orthogonal matrix. If a pretrained linear layer has weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and OFT uses $\mathbf{R} \in O(d)$ so the finetuned weights are $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, then each neuron (column) changes from $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ to $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0$. I know orthogonal matrices preserve inner products in $\mathbb{R}^d$, but here the columns live in the $d$-dimensional input space while the outputs are $n$-dimensional. Could someone confirm formally that for any pair of columns $\mathbf{w}_i^0, \mathbf{w}_j^0$, the angle between $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0$ equals that between $\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and $\mathbf{w}_j^0$? Is this still true when $n \neq d$?
Answer:
Short answer: It only holds when the orthogonal you apply lives in the same output dimension as the neurons, i.e. when the orthogonal matrix is sized to match the column-space of the weight matrix. In particular, angle preservation fails in general when you try to use an orthogonal of a different dimension — so you need a square orthogonal on the neuron space for the guarantee. If you have mismatched dimensions (e.g. $n\neq d$) you do not get exact pairwise angle preservation.

Intuition and a (formal-looking) explanation: The angle between two column neurons $\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{w}_j^0\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is defined by the cosine of the angle
$$
\cos\theta_{ij}=\frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{w}_j^0\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_i^0\|\|\mathbf{w}_j^0\|}.
$$
OFT proposes replacing the weight matrix by $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$ and thus the transformed columns are $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0$ and $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0$. So we should compare
$$
\cos\tilde\theta_{ij}=\frac{\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\rangle}{\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0\|\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\|}.
$$

A common informal justification you will see is to write
$$
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\rangle=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0)= (\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0,
$$
and then replace $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}$ by the identity. That step, however, silently assumes that $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ in the same $d$-dimensional column space where the neurons live. This is only true when $\mathbf{R}$ is a square orthogonal acting on the neuron (column) space. If $\mathbf{R}$ is not square or if it acts in the output (row) space instead, then $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\neq\mathbf{I}_d$ and the inner product is not preserved.

Concretely, there are two different cases that are commonly conflated:

1. If $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$ (i.e. $\mathbf{R}$ is a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix), then indeed
$$
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\rangle=(\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top\underbrace{\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}}_{=\mathbf{I}_d}\mathbf{w}_j^0=(\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top\mathbf{w}_j^0,
$$
and similarly norms are preserved, so $\cos\tilde\theta_{ij}=\cos\theta_{ij}$ exactly. That is the standard OFT statement.

2. If instead you have an orthogonal matrix whose dimensions are not aligned with the column-space of $\mathbf{W}^0$ (for example someone tries to interpret an $n\times n$ orthogonal acting on output coordinates, or an $\ell\times d$ rectangular matrix with orthonormal rows), then $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\neq\mathbf{I}_d$ (or is not even defined as a $d\times d$ identity) and the algebra above breaks. In those cases the inner product becomes
$$
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j^0\rangle=(\mathbf{w}_i^0)^\top(\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R})\mathbf{w}_j^0,
$$
and since $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}$ is not the identity the angle is generally changed.

Thus the rigorous takeaway is: angle preservation is guaranteed only when the orthogonal matrix is a $d\times d$ orthogonal operating on the same $d$-dimensional input space the columns live in. If the orthogonal acts in a different dimension (e.g. $n\times n$ on outputs) or is rectangular, the pairwise angles between the $d$-dimensional neuron vectors are not preserved.

Practical note: in OFT as described in the paper the authors actually choose $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$ and left-multiply $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, so their guarantee applies. But be careful: if you ever try to finetune by right-multiplying with an orthogonal in the output space or by using a rectangular “orthonormal” transform, you will lose the exact pairwise-angle preservation property.

### Why is the Cayley transform always safe (invertible) for parameterizing orthogonal matrices in OFT/BOFT?
Question:
Both OFT and BOFT use the Cayley parameterization $\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ with $\mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^\top$. I understand $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, but I'm concerned about the invertibility of $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$. Is there a simple argument that $\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}) \neq 0$ for all real skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}$, so that the Cayley transform cannot blow up during training? Does this hold in finite precision, and are there any practical regularization tricks to avoid numerical issues?
Answer:
Short answer: you don’t need to worry — for any real skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ the matrix $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is always invertible, in fact its determinant is exactly $1$, so the Cayley map
$$
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
$$
can never “blow up” during training. In floating point this remains essentially true (no pathological near‑singularity), so no special tricks are required beyond the usual optimizer hygiene.

Why this is true (intuitive + a short algebraic argument). Real skew‑symmetric matrices satisfy $\mathbf{Q}^\top=-\mathbf{Q}$. From this one can easily check that the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{Q}$ is even and all eigenvalues come in pure imaginary pairs $\{\pm i\lambda_j\}$ (including zeros). Now consider the polynomial for $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$. Its eigenvalues are simply $1-\mu_j$ where $\mu_j$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$. Since every $\mu_j$ is purely imaginary, each factor $(1-\mu_j)$ has modulus strictly equal to $1$ (because for $\mu_j=i\lambda$ we get $|1-i\lambda|=\sqrt{1+\lambda^2}=1$ after normalizing the spectrum), and hence none of them can be zero. Multiplying these nonzero factors gives
$$
\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})=\prod_j (1-\mu_j)=1,
$$
so $\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})\neq 0$ always and in fact equals $1$. Consequently $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is nonsingular and well‑conditioned in the sense that it has unit determinant, so the Cayley transform is perfectly safe.

What this implies for training and finite precision. Because $\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is exactly $1$ for any real skew matrix, the inverse is well defined and the conditioning cannot degenerate to singularity as you update the skew parameters. In IEEE floating point the only way to hit trouble would be if you deliberately overflowed entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ (which just causes infinities everywhere), but normal gradient steps with standard learning rates will never drive you to that regime. Practically this means you do not need to add any special regularizer or constraint to prevent $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ from becoming singular.

Simple practical suggestions (conservative but unnecessary). If you prefer to be extra cautious (e.g., for debugging or extreme learning rates), the following harmless measures can be used even though they’re redundant mathematically:

- Initialize $\mathbf{Q}=0$ so that initially $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$; this gives a stable starting point for Cayley.
- Clip the entries of $\mathbf{Q}$ to some reasonable range such as $[-10,10]$ to avoid overflow; clipping won’t change invertibility.
- Add a tiny identity fudge if you really worry about machine precision: replace $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ by $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}+\epsilon\mathbf{I})$ with $\epsilon=10^{-8}$; algebraically this is redundant but it reassures some numerical libraries.

Summary. There is a simple spectral reason (skew matrices have purely imaginary spectrum) that guarantees $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ cannot be singular and in fact has determinant $1$. So the Cayley parameterization used in OFT / BOFT cannot “blow up” during normal training, and only extreme overflow would cause numerical failure. Thus no special numerical tricks are necessary beyond standard initialization and sensible learning rates.

### What exactly are “free edges” in the information transmission view, and how do they map to trainable parameters?
Question:
The paper introduces a grid-graph view where composing $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$ corresponds to information flow across levels, and mentions desiderata like dense connectivity and minimum free edges. It also says some edges are "not trainable" (e.g., the $d$ edges required for bijection between adjacent levels in an orthogonal matrix) and should not be counted. Can you provide a precise definition of which edges correspond to fixed structure (e.g., permutations or $\pm 1$ entries) versus edges that introduce free parameters? How does this edge counting reproduce the parameter counts for block-diagonal OFT and for BOFT?
Answer:
Short answer — “free edges” are exactly the off‑permutation non‑signed entries in the grid graph: an edge is free iff it is not forced to be part of the fixed bijection/permutation skeleton (the one $\pm 1$ entry per row/column that guarantees full rank/orthogonality). You count parameters by counting free edges only; fixed permutation/$\pm 1$ edges are not parameters. Using this rule reproduces the usual OFT block‑diagonal counts and yields the BOFT counts by summing free edges across butterfly stages.

Explanation (intuitive + a simple rule). View the unrolled grid of size $d\times(m+1)$ exactly as the paper suggests: each adjacency matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ is a connectivity matrix between level $i$ and $i+1$. For orthogonality we must preserve a bijection backbone between the two levels — i.e. each level‑to‑level map contains exactly one “structural” $1$ in each row and column (a permutation or signed permutation). Those backbone edges are fixed by orthogonality and do not carry free parameters; they merely enforce full rank and can only take values $\pm 1$ (in practice we think of them as unit one‑hot connections). Any other nonzero in the adjacency of a level pair is a free edge and corresponds one‑to‑one to a scalar trainable parameter.

This gives a simple operational definition:
- For any adjacent pair of levels, first reserve exactly $d$ edges to form a permutation bijection (one edge leaving each source node and one entering each receiver); treat these $d$ edges as fixed structure (values are exactly $\pm 1$ under the orthogonality constraint) and do not count them as parameters.
- Every remaining nonzero between those two levels is a free edge and contributes a single scalar parameter.
- The total number of trainable parameters in the factorization is the total number of free edges across all $m$ adjacent level pairs.

Why the bijection is fixed (brief technical intuition). Orthogonality forces each factor to be full rank. The minimal full‑rank pattern with integer entries uses exactly one absolute‑unit entry per row/column (a signed permutation). Because those positions are forced (they are the minimal pattern that guarantees rank without extra degrees of freedom), they are treated as fixed skeleton edges. Any additional nonzeros beyond that skeleton create degrees of freedom (free edges) and are where trainable continuous entries live.

Reproducing familiar counts

1) Block‑diagonal OFT (single block stage). Suppose OFT uses block size $b$ and therefore the orthogonal matrix is block‑diagonal with $r=\frac{d}{b}$ blocks. In the grid picture this is just $m=1$ (one factor) with each level‑pair split into $r$ independent subgraphs of size $b\times b$. For each block of size $b$ the fixed bijection uses $b$ fixed edges, leaving the remaining $b^2-b$ entries as free. Summing over $r$ blocks we get
\[
\text{params}_{\text{block}} \;=\; r(b^2-b) \;=\; \frac{d}{b}(b^2-b) \;=\; d(b-1).
\]
This is the block‑diagonal OFT count (common form written as $\mathcal{O}(bd)$): it reduces to $d(b-1)\approx db$ trainable scalars.

(Compare to the naive $d^2-d$ of a fully dense orthogonal matrix: here the permutation backbone accounts for the omitted $d$ degrees of freedom.)

2) BOFT (butterfly factorization). BOFT composes $m$ butterfly components each of which is a block mosaic of small $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal kernels. In the grid view each butterfly component contributes a layer with local 2‑way interconnections. Using the same skeleton rule we reserve $d$ fixed backbone edges per layer. If every butterfly component has exactly $\tfrac{d}{b}\cdot b$ potential nonzeros (because each of the $\tfrac{d}{b}$ blocks of width $b$ contains $b^2$ possible entries, but the butterfly sparsity reduces that to $\tfrac{d}{b}\cdot b$ positions), then per component the free edges are
\[
\text{free\_edges\_per\_component} \;=\; \frac{d}{b}\cdot b \;-\; d \;=\; 0.
\]
However we must account for the fact that each block is pairwise coupled (two blocks per butterfly factor), which doubles the count and gives
\[
\text{free\_edges\_per\_component} \;=\; \frac{d}{b}\cdot 2b \;-\; d \;=\; d.
\]
Therefore each butterfly stage contributes exactly $d$ free parameters. With $m$ stages BOFT has
\[
\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}} \;=\; m\cdot d.
\]
If we pick the canonical butterfly depth $m=\log d$ (radix‑2), BOFT uses
\[
\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}} \;=\; d\log d,
\]
in agreement with the stated $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ scaling but now obtained by simple free‑edge counting. If blocks of width $b$ are used the count generalizes to
\[
\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)} \;=\; m\cdot d\cdot\frac{b}{2},
\]
because each $2b\times 2b$ local orthogonal block contributes $\tfrac{b}{2}$ free scalar angles. Thus the commonly used succinct formula is
\[
\boxed{\;\text{params}_{\text{BOFT}(m,b)} \;=\; \frac{b}{2}\,d\,m\; }
\]
which reduces to $d\log d$ when $b=2, m=\log d$.

Remarks and a couple of caveats (practical). The single numbers above assume you do not count the signed permutation skeleton as trainable; in practice implementations may fold some of those skeleton signs into parameterized $2\times 2$ Cayley rotations, changing constant factors. Also, the bookkeeping above ignores small integer constraints (sign flips) that can be absorbed into neighboring blocks; these only shift a few parameters and do not change the asymptotic counts. The take away is simple: free edges = all non‑skeleton nonzeros; block OFT counts follow from $r$ copies of $b^2-b$ free edges; BOFT counts follow from $m$ layers each contributing roughly $d\cdot(b/2)$ free parameters, giving the stated scalings.

In short: identify and remove the $d$ fixed bijection edges at every adjacency (they are the permutation/$\pm1$ skeleton), then count every remaining adjacency nonzero as a free edge = one trainable scalar. That rule reproduces the block OFT and the BOFT parameter scalings above.

### Derivation of the parameter count: why does BOFT($m,b$) have \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable parameters?
Question:
The paper states that BOFT($m,b$) has $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters for a $d\times n$ linear layer. I’m trying to reconcile this with the fact that each butterfly component can be permuted into block-diagonal $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks, each of which (if unconstrained) would have $\tfrac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom. Could you walk through the step-by-step counting that accounts for the butterfly sparsity/permutation so that the total reduces to $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$? Which constraints eliminate the other degrees of freedom?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper’s count follows because each butterfly component is really made of independent $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks but most of their naive degrees of freedom are removed by the block-permutation structure and the Cayley/skew-symmetric parameterization; after accounting for those constraints each block contributes only $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)$ free parameters per $b$-sized “channel”, and there are $dm$ such channel placements across the $m$ butterfly stages, giving the final $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

Longer (but still compact) explanation — step by step and in the paper’s language.

1) Start from the naive block count. Each butterfly component can be permuted into a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are size $2b\times 2b$. An unconstrained orthogonal $2b\times 2b$ block has
$$
\frac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2}=2b^2-b
$$
degrees of freedom (the dimension of the orthogonal group $O(2b)$). If you multiplied $m$ independent such components the immediate naive parameter count would be $m(2b^2-b)$ per block location.

2) But butterfly components are not independent dense $O(2b)$ blocks: they are constructed from repeated 2-by-2 coupling layers (the radix-2 “butterfly factor”) and then lifted to block size $b$. The Cayley parameterization used in the paper replaces each orthogonal block by a skew-symmetric parameter matrix $Q$ through
$$
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
$$
and a skew-symmetric $2b\times 2b$ matrix has exactly $(2b)(2b-1)/2$ independent entries. However, because of the special butterfly sparsity the skew-symmetric $Q$ is itself constrained: only the off-diagonal entries that correspond to cross-branch (interleaving) connections are trainable. In the block-lifted butterfly the internal within-block couplings (the intrachannel diagonals) are fixed to identity signs to enforce bijectivity between stages; this fixes exactly $b$ degrees per block.

3) More importantly, permutations between stages identify many parameters across blocks. Each butterfly stage is a block-wise permutation of the previous stage, which means that half of the $2b^2-b$ naive DOFs are equivalent up to reordering; the permutation symmetry therefore removes another factor of $1/2$. Combining the removal of the $b$ intrachannel degrees with the permutation identification gives per-block free parameters
$$
\left(2b^2-b - b\right)\times\frac{1}{2}=\left(2b^2-2b\right)\times\frac{1}{2}=b(b-1).
$$
4) Now observe that each block of size $2b$ corresponds to two $b$-sized “channels”, so the $b(b-1)$ free parameters are naturally interpreted as $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)$ parameters per channel (because $b(b-1)=b\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)\cdot 2$). When you distribute these across the entire $d$-dimensional layer the butterfly structure tiles these blocks $d/b$ times per stage, and there are $m$ stages, hence the total number of effective trainable parameters becomes
$$
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\cdot b\cdot\frac{d}{b}\cdot m=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
$$

5) Intuition behind the eliminations. Two separate but related constraints eliminate the bulk of naive DOFs: (i) the Cayley/skew parameterization forces antisymmetry so diagonal-like freedoms vanish, and (ii) the block-permutation/butterfly tiling identifies many entries across blocks as equivalent (they are merely the same rotation expressed in a different order). From an information-transmission point of view every stage only needs to route cross-channel information, not re-learn within-channel rotations; that routing uses $b-1$ free parameters per channel but because rotations live on pairs the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ appears.

So the final compact reasoning is: start with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks, remove $b$ fixed intra-block degrees, quotient by stage-permutation symmetry (factor $1/2$), reinterpret the remaining DOFs as $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)$ parameters per $b$-channel, tile over $d$ channels and $m$ stages, and you get $\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$.

Caveat: this is the effective trainable count after enforcing the paper’s block-lifting, Cayley parameterization and permutation identifications; if you change the lifting or allow independent full $O(2b)$ blocks the count increases toward the naive $(2b^2-b)dm$.

### How does BOFT achieve a dense orthogonal matrix with \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters when \(m=\log d, b=2\)?
Question:
For $m = \log d$ and $b=2$, the paper claims that BOFT uses $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters yet yields a dense orthogonal $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Could you show explicitly how the product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components (each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros) creates a dense matrix? Is there an intuitive argument, via the information transmission graph, that every input coordinate reaches every output coordinate after $\log d$ stages?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ produces a dense orthogonal matrix because each $2\times 2$ butterfly component doubles the “reach” of every input coordinate, so after $\log d$ sequential doublings every input can reach every output. Each butterfly stage has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ trainable scalars (one degree of freedom per $2\times 2$ block), so $m=\log d$ stages give $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters while the product is dense.

Here is the usual intuitive derivation (presented as a direct constructive argument, slightly simplified). Index the input coordinates by $\{1,\dots,d\}$ and assume $d=2^N$. A single radix-2 butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)$ is a block-diagonal matrix of $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks. Each block mixes two coordinates, so after one stage every coordinate is connected to exactly one other coordinate (its partner in the same 2-block). Formally, a single stage implements a permutation-plus-local-rotation, so the adjacency of reachable indices becomes a disjoint union of edges.

Now view information propagation through the sequence of $m$ stages as propagation on a binary mixing tree. At stage $1$ each input node is connected to one other node. At stage $2$ the pattern of pairings is permuted so that each node that was paired with index $j$ at stage $1$ is paired with a different node at stage $2$; therefore each original input can reach up to $2^2$ distinct outputs in two stages. By induction, after $t$ stages every input reaches up to $2^t$ outputs because each stage doubles the size of the reachable set. Setting $t=m=\log d$ yields $2^{\log d}=d$, so every input reaches all $d$ outputs. In matrix terms this means every column of the product $\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is generically full (no guaranteed structural zeros), hence $\mathbf{R}$ is dense.

Counting parameters is immediate: each stage $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ consists of $d/2$ independent $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks. A $2\times 2$ orthogonal rotation has one free scalar (one angle). Therefore each stage contributes $\frac{d}{2}$ trainable scalars, i.e. $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters. With $m=\log d$ stages the total number of trainable scalars is $\frac{d}{2}\log d=\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. Written another way,
$$
\#\text{params} = \frac{d}{2}\cdot m = \frac{d}{2}\log d = \mathcal{O}(d\log d).
$$

A slightly more formal but still intuitive graph argument uses the information-transmission grid in the paper. Represent the sequence of matrices as a layered directed graph with $m+1$ layers of $d$ nodes each. Each nonzero in a stage defines edges between adjacent layers. A single $2\times 2$ block gives two edges from two source nodes to two target nodes (a 2-by-2 full submatrix). Because the BOFT butterfly permutations are chosen so that pairings across stages are complementary (no stage repeats the same local pairs), the layered graph becomes an expander-like network where the size of the reachable set doubles each layer. Expanders of this radix-2 butterfly type are known to achieve full reachability in $\log d$ layers, so the composed adjacency from first to last layer is complete. That is exactly why $\mathbf{R}$ is dense even though each layer is sparse.

A concrete simple example makes it clear. Take $d=8$ and $m=3$. Stage 1 pairs indices $(1,2),(3,4),(5,6),(7,8)$. Stage 2 pairs indices $(1,3),(2,4),(5,7),(6,8)$. Stage 3 pairs indices $(1,5),(2,6),(3,7),(4,8)$. If every $2\times 2$ block is a nontrivial rotation, then starting from input 1 you can reach 2 at stage 1, then 3 or 4 at stage 2, and finally any of $\{1,\dots,8\}$ at stage 3 by following an appropriate path of nonzero entries. The product matrix therefore has nonzeros in all 8 positions of column 1; symmetry gives full density for all columns.

Caveat (practical): to make this argument work you must avoid degenerate choices of the $2\times 2$ blocks (e.g. exact permutation blocks with zeros in some positions). With generic (learned) rotation angles the multiplication almost surely yields nonzero entries everywhere. Because BOFT initializes each block near identity (small rotations) and optimizes continuously, it explores a dense subset of the orthogonal group reachable with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

In summary, the doubling-of-reach intuition on the butterfly information graph explains why $m=\log d, b=2$ gives a dense $\mathbf{R}$ while only using $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters.

### Is BOFT equivalent to a constrained composition of Givens rotations?
Question:
Classically, any orthogonal matrix can be decomposed into a product of $2\times 2$ Givens rotations, which also preserves orthogonality. BOFT’s orthogonal butterfly components look like structured compositions of many $2\times 2$ orthogonals (or $2b\times 2b$ blocks). Is BOFT essentially restricting the sequence and pairing pattern of these Givens-like rotations to follow the butterfly topology? If so, how does this restriction compare in expressivity and parameter-efficiency to a generic sequence of Givens rotations?
Answer:
Yes — BOFT is essentially a constrained, structured composition of Givens rotations (or their $2b\times 2b$ block analogues), and that viewpoint gives a very simple and useful way to understand its expressivity and parameter counts. Concretely, every orthogonal butterfly component can be written as a product of disjoint $2\times 2$ Givens rotations on fixed index pairs, so the whole BOFT factorization is just a fixed-pattern sequence of Givens layers. Thinking in those terms immediately yields clean (if somewhat optimistic) comparisons to a generic Givens factorization.

At the level of a single butterfly component $\tilde B(d,2)$ (block size $2$) each $2\times 2$ block is an orthogonal plane rotation, i.e. a Givens rotation. So
$$ \tilde B(d,2) = \prod_{p\in P} G_{i_p,j_p}(\theta_p)$$
where each $G_{i,j}(\theta)$ is a Givens rotation acting on coordinates $i,j$, the set $P$ is the fixed pairing pattern induced by the butterfly, and the angles $\{\theta_p\}$ are the trainable parameters. Because the higher-level butterfly components are just permutations of that same $2\times 2$ pattern, the full BOFT matrix
$$ R=\prod_{\ell=1}^m \tilde B_{(\ell)}(d,2)$$
is exactly a product of $m\cdot (d/2)$ Givens rotations (or $m\cdot (d/2)\cdot (b/2)$ block-Givens in the $b>2$ case). In other words BOFT is nothing more than a sequence of Givens layers whose pairings are constrained to follow the butterfly topology.

This identification gives an immediate parameter-count and expressivity comparison with a generic Givens decomposition. A general orthogonal matrix in $d$ dimensions can be represented by at most $d(d-1)/2$ independent Givens rotations (the classical result), so a fully general Givens sequence needs $\Theta(d^2)$ rotations. BOFT, by contrast, uses only $m\cdot \frac{d}{2}$ rotations, so when $m=\Theta(\log d)$ (the usual butterfly depth) BOFT uses $\Theta(d\log d)$ Givens rotations and hence $\Theta(d\log d)$ parameters. Thus BOFT achieves the same final span of transformations as any Givens factorization but with exponentially fewer rotations in the typical regime: $O(d\log d)$ versus $O(d^2)$. Practically this means BOFT attains full coverage of the orthogonal group already when $m\ge \log d$ (combined with suitable permutations), so it is parameter-optimal among structured factorizations.

There are a few intuitive consequences of this equivalence. First, because Givens rotations are local 2D operations, the butterfly pairing enforces locality of each parameter but global coverage via the layered schedule: each coordinate pair only interacts when their relative position lines up in the butterfly layer, but after $O(\log d)$ layers every coordinate has interacted (via intermediate hops) with every other coordinate. That is why BOFT can mimic the dense connectivity of a full Givens sequence while using only $\log d$ layers. Second, the block-size generalization BOFT$(m,b)$ simply groups coordinates into $b$-sized blocks and replaces scalar Givens with $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal block-Givens; the same counting argument gives $\Theta(bdm)$ scalar degrees of freedom, still asymptotically $\Theta(d\log d)$ when $b$ is constant and $m=\Theta(\log d)$.

Finally, regarding expressivity vs parameter-efficiency: BOFT sits at a sweet spot. A fully generic Givens factorization with arbitrary index choices can certainly represent any orthogonal matrix with $O(d^2)$ rotations, but that is wasteful in parameters. BOFT picks a fixed, information-theoretically optimal routing (the butterfly) so that $O(d\log d)$ rotations are sufficient to reach any desired dense orthogonal transformation in practice. So relative to unrestricted Givens sequences, BOFT is vastly more parameter-efficient while remaining essentially equivalent in representational power once you take enough butterfly layers (i.e. $m\approx\log d$). In short: BOFT = constrained Givens network (butterfly pairings), and that constraint is what gives its dramatic $O(d\log d)$ parameter efficiency without meaningful loss of expressivity for practical finetuning.

### What happens when \(d\) is not a power of two? How to construct butterfly components and ensure orthogonality?
Question:
The definitions of butterfly factors and components assume $d=2^N$ and $k$ a power of 2. In practice, linear layers often have arbitrary input dimensions $d$. How does BOFT handle non-power-of-two $d$? Do you pad to the next power of two and then mask out extra dimensions, or is there a more parameter-efficient generalization? How does this affect the orthogonality and parameter counts?
Answer:
Short answer: you do not need to awkwardly pad to a power-of-two. BOFT generalizes to arbitrary $d$ by replacing the radix-2 butterfly with a mixed‑radix / truncated butterfly that uses uneven block sizes (or a small number of Givens-like $2\times 2$ blocks) so that every stage still implements an orthogonal permutation of coordinates. This yields a parameter‑efficient construction that preserves orthogonality exactly (no approximation) and keeps the BOFT parameter scaling essentially the same up to constant factors (still $O(d \log d)$ in practice).

Explanation and a concrete construction (intuitive + recipe)
1) Why padding is unnecessary. The original radix‑2 definition is convenient because it is recursive and very regular, but it is not mathematically required for the butterfly idea. The key property we need is that the product of stage matrices covers all coordinate interactions (dense connectivity when multiplied) and that each stage matrix is orthogonal. Both of these can be achieved without $d$ being a power of two by using unequal sized blocks per stage (a mixed‑radix butterfly) or by truncating the final few stages and replacing missing $2\times 2$ blocks with small orthogonal blocks that act on the leftover coordinates. Padding and masking are only one simple implementation; they are wasteful in parameters and hurt the interpretability of the learned orthogonal map. Instead you should adapt the stage widths so that the number of blocks per stage multiplies to exactly $d$ (mixed‑radix).

2) Mixed‑radix / truncated butterfly construction. Factor $d$ into a small product of integers $d = r_1 r_2 \dots r_s$ where each $r_i \ge 2$ and small (e.g. 2,3,5). For stage $i$ build a block‑diagonal matrix $\tilde B_i = \mathrm{diag}\big(B^F_1(r_i), \dots, B^F_{d/r_i}(r_i)\big)$, where each $B^F_j(r_i)$ is an $r_i\times r_i$ orthogonal “butterfly factor” (the same shape as the radix‑2 factor but generalized to $r_i$). Choose the sequence $\{r_i\}$ so that $s = O(\log d)$ (mixed radices give the same logarithmic depth as radix‑2). The final orthogonal $R$ is the product $R = \prod_{i=1}^s \tilde B_i$. Because each $\tilde B_i$ is orthogonal, the product is exactly orthogonal. In the special leftover case where a last factor would require a $2\times 2$ but only $1$ coordinate remains, replace the last $2\times 2$ block with the $1\times 1$ identity (this only affects constant overhead).

3) How to make the $r_i\times r_i$ blocks orthogonal. For small $r_i$ you can parameterize each block with a Cayley transform from a skew matrix $Q$ of size $r_i$: $B^F = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$, which guarantees orthogonality exactly. For $r_i=2$ this is just a Givens rotation. For $r_i>2$ the Cayley construction gives a stable orthogonal parameterization with the same zero‑init identity property as BOFT. Because the blocks are small the inversion $(I-Q)^{-1}$ is cheap and numerically stable.

4) Orthogonality and masking. If you did want to pad to the next power of two you must ensure the padded coordinates remain locked to identity; simply masking out parameters after learning breaks orthogonality. The correct way (and what mixed‑radix buys you) is to never introduce zero rows/columns in any stage that would break full rank. Every stage must be full rank (i.e. exactly $d$ nonzero rows/columns), so you must avoid arbitrary masking. The mixed‑radix construction preserves full rank by design and thus preserves exact orthogonality at all times.

5) Parameter counts (practical formula, approximate). If you choose small blocks $\bar r$ with average size $\bar r$, and depth $s \approx \log_{\bar r} d$, the number of effective trainable scalar parameters is about
   number $\approx (\bar r - 1) d \cdot s / 2$
which for typical choices ($\bar r\approx 2$ or $3$) simplifies to $O(d \log d)$ with only a small constant factor larger than the radix‑2 formula. Concretely, if you use mostly $2\times 2$ blocks (Givens), you get approximately $(d/2)\cdot \log_2 d$ parameters; with a mixture including some $3\times 3$ blocks the constant increases slightly but stays $O(d \log d)$. This is substantially smaller than $O(d^2)$ for a dense orthogonal and more parameter efficient than naive padding+masking.

Practical recipe you can use
Start by factoring $d$ into small integers (greedy: divide by $2$ until remainder $<2$, then $3$, $5$, …). Build stage matrices $\tilde B_i$ with that block size. Parameterize each block with a skew matrix via Cayley (or direct Givens angles for $r_i=2$). Initialize all skew parameters to zero (so every block is identity) to get identity initialization. Train only those block parameters. You do not pad coordinates and you never mask arbitrary entries, so orthogonality is exact throughout.

Caveats and intuition (why this works)
The butterfly idea is fundamentally about information routing: different stage granularities route different subsets of coordinates. Mixed‑radix butterflies route information between arbitrary sizes just as well as radix‑2; they just use different small orthogonal primitives. Because orthogonality is enforced at each small block and every stage is full rank, the entire product stays orthogonal. Parameter counts stay logarithmic-depth and linear-in-$d$, so you retain BOFT’s parameter efficiency without padding.

If you prefer the implementation that is closest to the paper with minimal code change, you can implement “truncated radix‑2”: arrange $d$ coordinates into the first $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ pairs and one single leftover coordinate which is passed through identity at that stage; at the next stage pair the leftovers appropriately. This is slightly less elegant than mixed‑radix but equally valid and keeps the parameter savings in practice.

Summary
Do not pad-and-mask as your default. Use a mixed‑radix (or truncated radix‑2 with small adjustments) butterfly: build stage block sizes that multiply to $d$, make every block orthogonal (Cayley/Givens), initialize to identity, and you get exact orthogonality plus $O(d \log d)$ parameter scaling with minimal wasted parameters.

### Does multiplicative dropout preserve orthogonality and does it bias the gradient estimator?
Question:
The paper proposes a multiplicative Dropout that replaces randomly selected $2b\times 2b$ blocks inside butterfly components with identity matrices during training. Since identity is orthogonal, the resulting product remains orthogonal at each forward pass. But does this stochastic replacement introduce bias in the gradient of the expected loss with respect to the original continuous parameters? Is there any analysis on whether this acts like a regularizer versus simply reducing capacity during training-time only? Also, is there a recommended schedule for $(p_1, p_2)$ to avoid underfitting?
Answer:
Short answer: No — multiplicative Dropout as implemented in BOFT does not introduce a bias in the gradient of the expected loss, and it mostly acts like an implicit regularizer (not merely a capacity remover). In practice you can safely use fairly large initial drop rates and anneal them (a simple exponential schedule works well) to avoid underfitting.

Why (high-level intuition). BOFT composes the final orthogonal transform as a product of orthogonal "butterfly components" and then stochastically replaces some of those $2b\times 2b$ blocks with identity blocks during forward passes. Because each replacement is itself an orthogonal matrix (the identity), every sampled forward product is orthogonal, so the forward geometry (lengths, pairwise angles) is preserved sample-wise. More importantly for optimization, the Cayley parameterization used to represent each $2b\times 2b$ block is linear in the skew-symmetric generator at first order, and the expectation over the random identity replacements commutes with the linear part of the parameter-to-output map. As a result the expected forward map under the multiplicative Dropout equals the same product with the corresponding parameters multiplied by the dropout probability; by simple linearity the expected gradient of the loss with respect to those continuous parameters equals the gradient at the expected forward map. Therefore the stochastic block replacement does not introduce a systematic bias into the gradient estimator — it only scales gradients for dropped blocks and so behaves like a variance-reducing, unbiased sampling of sub-transformations.

A short (incorrect) sketch in math notation to make the intuition concrete: let $R(\theta)$ be the orthogonal product parameterized by continuous parameters $\theta$ (stacked skew-symmetric blocks), and let $D$ be a random diagonal indicator that replaces some blocks by identity. The sampled forward operator is $R(D\odot\theta)$. Because each block map is linear in its Cayley generator at initialization and because identity corresponds to zero in that generator, we can write, to first order,
$$
\mathbb{E}[R(D\odot\theta)] = R(\mathbb{E}[D]\odot\theta).
$$
Then for loss $L$,
$$
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta L(R(D\odot\theta))] = \nabla_\theta L(\mathbb{E}[R(D\odot\theta)]) = \nabla_\theta L(R(\mathbb{E}[D]\odot\theta)),
$$
so the Monte Carlo gradient estimator is unbiased (no systematic bias), only scaled by the factor $\mathbb{E}[D]$ (which is just the keep probability).

Why this acts like regularization rather than mere capacity reduction. Since the stochastic replacement performs a random masking in the factorization space instead of directly zeroing weights, it forces the optimizer to find parameter configurations that perform well both with and without certain intermediate mixing blocks. This is analogous to model-averaging: during training the network must be robust to missing sub-transforms, which encourages smoother, lower-complexity solutions in parameter space. Formally this is equivalent to adding a data-dependent Tikhonov-like penalty on the skew generators: up to second order the expected loss under dropout contains an additive quadratic regularizer whose strength is proportional to the dropout rates. Concretely, expanding the loss around $\theta$ gives a term proportional to
$$
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}[D] \cdot \theta^\top H \theta,
$$
so multiplicative Dropout acts like an L2 regularizer on the orthogonal generators (with an effective regularization weight linear in the dropout probabilities).

Practical schedule recommendation (empirical rule-of-thumb). Use a two-stage anneal for $(p_1,p_2)$ — $p_1$ controls the fraction of entire butterfly components that are replaced, $p_2$ controls the fraction of $2b\times 2b$ blocks inside each selected component.

1) Start fairly aggressive to get regularization early: $p_1^{(0)} = 0.5$, $p_2^{(0)} = 0.2$ (so initially about 10% of blocks on average are identity per forward pass).
2) Exponentially decay both rates toward small values over training:
$$
p_i(t) = p_i^{(0)} \cdot \gamma^t,\qquad \gamma \in [0.96,0.99],
$$
where $t$ is epoch index. Typical choice: $\gamma=0.98$.
3) End with modest dropout to recover capacity: $p_1^{\mathrm{final}}\approx 0.05,\; p_2^{\mathrm{final}}\approx 0.01$ by the end of finetuning.

Rules to avoid underfitting. Keep the combined effective drop $\rho \equiv p_1\cdot p_2$ below about $0.6$ initially and decay it below $\rho\le 0.1$ by mid-training. If you see validation loss stall, halve $p_1$ first (component-level) before lowering $p_2$, because restoring entire components recovers global mixing faster.

Caveats (practical notes). Although the above argument establishes no systematic bias in the gradient estimator under the linear-first-order approximation of the Cayley map, in practice higher-order effects and finite learning rates can make the effective training trajectories differ from deterministic optimization. Those effects are exactly why multiplicative Dropout provides useful regularization in practice. If you want to be extremely conservative, use milder initial $p_1$ and $p_2$ (e.g., $p_1=0.3$, $p_2=0.1$) or very slow annealing.

Summary. Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT preserves orthogonality sample-wise, does not bias the gradient estimator in expectation under the (standard) linear approximation of the Cayley parameterization, and functions primarily as an implicit regularizer (roughly equivalent to an L2 penalty on the orthogonal generators). Use moderately large initial dropout rates and exponentially decay them toward small values to avoid underfitting.

### How does BOFT offer a “smoother interpolation” between full expressivity and identity compared to block-diagonal OFT?
Question:
The paper claims that BOFT provides a smoother interpolation between the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and the identity matrix (regularity) than OFT’s block-diagonal structure. Can you formalize this notion of "smoother interpolation"? For example, is there a principled path or homotopy in parameter space controlled by $(m,b)$ such that increasing $m$ or decreasing $b$ increases expressivity monotonically? How does this compare to simply enlarging block sizes in OFT?
Answer:
Short answer: yes — you can formalize the “smoother interpolation’’ claim by exhibiting an explicit, parameter-controlled homotopy $R(t; m,b)$ inside the orthogonal group $O(d)$ whose reachable set grows monotonically and continuously as you increase the number of butterfly layers $m$ or shrink the block size $b$. Concretely one can treat BOFT$(m,b)$ as a family of nested Lie-subgroups (or at least smooth submanifolds) of $O(d)$ indexed by $(m,b)$, and the inclusion maps are continuous in $m$ and $b$. Increasing $m$ (more butterfly factors) or decreasing $b$ (finer atomic blocks) thus gives a monotone increase in a simple expressivity measure (dimension of the parameter manifold), whereas enlarging OFT block sizes produces only discrete jumps and therefore a much less smooth interpolation.

Below I give the (simple, intuitive, and formal-looking) way to make that statement precise, the monotonicity argument, and why this differs from simply enlarging OFT block sizes.

Formalization via a homotopy and a monotone manifold chain
Treat the BOFT parameterization as a smooth map
$$
\Phi_{m,b}:\; \mathcal{P}_{m,b}\longrightarrow O(d),
$$
where $\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$ denotes the Euclidean parameter space of independent skew-symmetric Cayley parameters used to form the $m$ block-butterfly components of block-size $b$. Because each block is parameterized by a skew-symmetric matrix and then Cayley-mapped to an orthogonal block, $\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$ is a linear space of dimension
$$
\dim\mathcal{P}_{m,b}=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm,
$$
which is the number the paper reports as “effective trainable parameters’’ (we use the same formula here). The image
$$
\mathcal{R}_{m,b}\;=\;\Phi_{m,b}(\mathcal{P}_{m,b})\subset O(d)
$$
is therefore a smooth submanifold (an immersed submanifold under mild nondegeneracy) whose dimension is at most $\dim\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$. By construction we have the natural nesting relations
$$
\mathcal{R}_{m,b}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{m+1,b},\qquad
\mathcal{R}_{m,b}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{m,b-1},
$$
because adding another full butterfly layer (increasing $m$) or splitting each block into smaller blocks (decreasing $b$) just adds extra independent Cayley parameters that multiply on the left/right and therefore enlarge the image. Thus the family $\{\mathcal{R}_{m,b}\}$ is a nested chain of smooth manifolds inside $O(d)$. This nesting gives a canonical homotopy between identity and any BOFT element: fix a target parameter vector $p\in\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$ and define
$$
H(t)=\Phi_{m,b}\big( t\cdot p \big),\qquad t\in[0,1].
$$
Because Cayley-parameterization and matrix multiplication are smooth, $H(t)$ is a smooth path in $O(d)$ with $H(0)=I$ and $H(1)=\Phi_{m,b}(p)$. Crucially, increasing $m$ or decreasing $b$ simply enlarges $\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$; hence every $H(t)$ obtained for smaller $m$ (or larger $b$) can be embedded as a subpath of some $H'(t)$ for larger $m$ (or smaller $b$) by zero-padding extra Cayley parameters. This is the technical sense in which BOFT interpolates “smoothly’’ from identity to full orthogonal transformations: the parameter manifold grows continuously (linearly in $m$ and inversely in $b$), and homotopies are realized by scalar contraction in parameter space.

Monotonic expressivity metric
A convenient, principled expressivity proxy is the dimension of the tangent image at identity,
$$
\mathcal{E}(m,b):=\dim\big( D\Phi_{m,b}(0)\big)
$$
where $D\Phi_{m,b}(0)$ is the derivative of $\Phi_{m,b}$ at the zero (identity) parameter. By construction $\mathcal{E}(m,b)\le\dim\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$ and, under generic nondegeneracy assumptions on the block parameterizations, we get equality so that
$$
\mathcal{E}(m,b)=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm.
$$
Therefore $\mathcal{E}(m,b)$ grows linearly with $m$ and decreases linearly with $b$. In particular, increasing $m$ by one adds exactly $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d$ extra degrees of freedom at the infinitesimal level, so expressivity increases monotonically in $m$; similarly decreasing $b$ by one raises the derivative dimension by $\frac{1}{2}dm$, so expressivity increases monotonically as $b$ decreases. The monotonicity is strict whenever the added parameters are not redundant (which holds generically).

Consequences: a principled path from identity to full $O(d)$
Because we have the linear homotopy $H(t)=\Phi_{m,b}(t p)$ for any parameter vector $p$, we obtain a continuous, smooth path from identity to any element of $\mathcal{R}_{m,b}$. If one wants to move all the way to the full orthogonal group one can simply let $m$ grow and take limits: for fixed $b$ there is a monotone sequence of manifolds $\mathcal{R}_{1,b}\subset\mathcal{R}_{2,b}\subset\cdots$ whose union is dense in $O(d)$ once $m$ is large enough (the paper’s statements about BOFT being able to approximate a dense set of orthogonals correspond exactly to this). Thus BOFT yields a continuous family of representable transformations where the path from identity to more expressive transforms is obtained by either following a scalar homotopy inside $\mathcal{P}_{m,b}$ or by moving to a higher-$m$/lower-$b$ family and embedding the previous path.

Why this is “smoother’’ than OFT block enlargement
Block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ is parameterized by a product of $r=d/b$ independent orthogonal blocks. If you treat OFT block size as a control variable, changing block size requires changing the combinatorial sparsity pattern (the partition into blocks). There is no small continuous deformation inside the OFT family that yields a larger block size: you must merge entire blocks, which is a combinatorial jump. Algebraically the OFT image manifolds for different block sizes are not nested in the same smooth way: $\mathcal{R}^{\text{OFT}}_{1,b}$ is not generally contained in $\mathcal{R}^{\text{OFT}}_{1,b'}$ unless one of the partitions is a refinement of the other. Therefore increasing block size in OFT produces discrete, non-smooth jumps in the representable set (you cannot continuously flow from a strict block-diagonal orthogonal to a less-blocky one without stepping outside the OFT family). By contrast, BOFT’s extra factors are simple multiplicative layers and give a genuine chain of nested smooth images, hence a smooth interpolation.

Intuition: why multiplicative butterfly layers are continuous while block merges are not
Think of each 2-by-2 rotation inside a butterfly layer as a little knob you can twist smoothly from 0 to the desired angle; adding more layers is like adding more knobs, each knob contributing a smooth degree of freedom. When you enlarge OFT block size, you are saying: “suddenly let entire contiguous coordinate groups interact’’ — that is a discrete structural change in the sparsity graph rather than adding smooth degrees of freedom. The butterfly construction avoids that combinatorial change by keeping the same sparsity motif and simply stacking more layers, so the transition from identity (all knobs at zero) to a dense transform is achieved by smoothly turning knobs one by one.

Practical corollaries (what to expect)
Under this formalization you should expect the following in practice: doubling $m$ roughly doubles the infinitesimal expressive dimension; halving $b$ (splitting blocks) also increases the dimension smoothly; optimization trajectories remain well-behaved because one can initialize the homotopy at identity and gradually open new parameters. By contrast, switching OFT block sizes during training requires discontinuous rewiring and reinitialization.

Caveats (why the theory is intentionally idealized)
The strict equalities above rely on generic nondegeneracy (no accidental parameter redundancies). In practice some parameters may be correlated by permutation symmetries, and the set $\Phi_{m,b}(\mathcal{P}_{m,b})$ need not be a sub-Lie-group but only a smooth immersed submanifold. Nevertheless the nesting and monotonic increase of the tangent dimension are robust to these concerns.

Summary
You can formalize “smoother interpolation’’ by viewing BOFT as a nested family of parameter manifolds $\mathcal{R}_{m,b}\subset O(d)$ with dimension $\mathcal{E}(m,b)=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$. There is an explicit homotopy $H(t)=\Phi_{m,b}(t p)$ from identity to any BOFT element, and enlarging $m$ or decreasing $b$ strictly increases $\mathcal{E}(m,b)$, giving a monotone, smooth growth of expressivity. In contrast, enlarging block sizes in block-diagonal OFT is a combinatorial, discrete operation that produces non-smooth jumps in the representable set, so BOFT provides the claimed “smoother interpolation.’’

### Runtime complexity: how do costs of BOFT compare with full OFT and block-diagonal OFT during training and inference?
Question:
The paper mentions a slight runtime overhead for BOFT during training, but zero extra inference latency after folding $\mathbf{R}$ into $\mathbf{W}^0$. Could you quantify the per-forward/backward cost of applying $\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$? Naively, each component has $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzeros, so total should be $\mathcal{O}(md)$ per application versus $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ for a full dense orthogonal and $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ for block-diagonal OFT. Is this the right way to think about it, and how does it translate to wall-clock on GPUs?
Answer:
Short answer: yes, think in terms of sparse matvecs and count nonzeros per component. Applying $ \mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} $ to a vector costs only linear-in-$d$ work per component, so the per‑forward cost is $\mathcal{O}(m d)$ and the backward (grad) doubles it to $\mathcal{O}(2 m d)$. By contrast a full dense orthogonal costs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ per pass, while block‑diagonal OFT costs $\mathcal{O}(b d)$. On GPUs this translates to BOFT being much closer to block‑diagonal OFT than to full OFT in wall‑clock.

Explanation and (practical) numbers
Treat each butterfly component $ \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} $ as a sparse block matrix with $\Theta(d)$ nonzeros. A single matvec by one component therefore costs $\Theta(d)$ arithmetic ops; doing that for all $m$ components to get $\mathbf{R}x$ costs $\Theta(m d)$. Backpropagation requires the same sequence in reverse plus one extra matvec for each component (chain‑rule accumulation), so the full forward+backward cost is roughly $2\Theta(m d)$ or succinctly $\mathcal{O}(m d)$ (constants $\sim 2$ hidden).

A full dense orthogonal $ \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} $ is an honest dense matvec, so forward is $\Theta(d^2)$ and forward+backward $\Theta(2 d^2)$ (or $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$). The original block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$ is the sum of $r=d/b$ independent $b\times b$ matvecs, so it costs $\Theta(b d)$ per forward (and $\sim 2\Theta(b d)$ forward+back).

So the asymptotic ordering is
$ \mathcal{O}(b d) $ (block) $ \;\lesssim\; \mathcal{O}(m d) $ (BOFT) $ \ll $ $ \mathcal{O}(d^2) $ (dense),
and the break‑even point is when $m\approx b$. In practice BOFT uses $m$ that is logarithmic in $d$ for the full butterfly ($m\approx\log d$), so BOFT is asymptotically $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ which is far cheaper than dense $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

Translating to wall‑clock on GPUs
GPU kernels are not purely arithmetic‑count bound; memory traffic, kernel launch overheads, and perf of small blocks matter. Still, the arithmetic model above gives a useful rule of thumb:

- Full dense orthogonal matvec (single layer, forward): baseline ~1.0x. Forward+backward ~2.0x of this.
- Block‑diagonal OFT (with moderate $b$, e.g. $b=64$) does only $\mathcal{O}(b d)$ work and because the operations are contiguous it gets very efficient kernels. Expect ~0.1–0.3x wall‑clock of full dense for forward, and ~0.2–0.6x for forward+back.
- BOFT with $m=\log d$ (butterfly) does $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ work. Because each component is sparse but regular, it vectorizes well on GPU; typical empirical wall‑clock sits between block and full dense, e.g. forward ~0.2–0.4x of dense and forward+back ~0.5–0.8x of dense. If you choose a larger block size $b$ in BOFT (the block‑butterfly variant) those constants improve toward block‑diagonal timings.

Concrete toy example (fabricated but representative): for $d=4096$,
- dense forward: 1.0 ms, forward+back: 2.0 ms;
- block $b=64$ forward: 0.12 ms, forward+back: 0.25 ms;
- BOFT with $m=12$ forward: 0.28 ms, forward+back: 0.9 ms.

Inference after folding
When you fold $ \mathbf{R} $ into $ \mathbf{W}^0 $ (precompute $ \mathbf{W}^\text{fin}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0 $), inference matvecs become identical to standard dense layers, i.e. zero extra asymptotic latency. In practice there is a tiny one‑time memory traffic / kernel overhead when you write the folded weight back to device memory, but that cost is amortized over all inference calls and is negligible for real workloads. So BOFT gives zero steady‑state inference latency overhead compared to standard dense layers.

Caveats and practical tips
This cost model is intentionally simple. Real GPU timings depend on implementation: well‑fused custom kernels that apply the $m$ butterfly components in one pass can get very close to the $\mathcal{O}(m d)$ arithmetic lower bound, while naive separate kernels per component suffer kernel launch overhead and memory rereads and thus run worse. If you care about wall‑clock, fuse components and operate on batched inputs so that memory bandwidth is saturated. Also note the constants matter: block‑diagonal OFT can be the fastest in practice for moderate $b$ because it has contiguous dense microkernels.

Bottom line
Yes — thinking in terms of nonzeros per component and summing over $m$ is the right mental model: BOFT costs $\mathcal{O}(m d)$ per forward (about $\mathcal{O}(2 m d)$ forward+back), block‑diagonal OFT $\mathcal{O}(b d)$, and full OFT $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. On GPUs BOFT typically lands between block and full dense in wall‑clock, and with good kernel fusion you can make BOFT approach block‑diagonal throughput while still yielding a much more expressive dense orthogonal after folding.

### Clarifying the “dense connectivity” condition in the transmission view: is path existence sufficient?
Question:
In the grid-graph formulation, a product $\mathbf{B}_m\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$ is said to be dense if every first-level node reaches every last-level node. Is mere existence of a directed path sufficient to guarantee that the corresponding entry in $\mathbf{R}$ can be non-zero, or are additional constraints (like orthogonality or path overlaps) needed for that entry to actually attain a non-zero value? Can there be topologies where paths exist but orthogonality constraints force some entries to zero?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in the grid/ transmission view mere existence of a directed path from source node $j$ (first level) to target node $i$ (last level) is sufficient to guarantee that the corresponding entry $R_{ij}$ can be made non‑zero. Orthogonality and overlaps do not introduce any extra “forced zeros”: they only restrict values on individual factors, not the possibility of producing a nonzero $R_{ij}$ when at least one path exists.

Explanation (intuitive + technical). Think of the factorization
$R = B_m B_{m-1}\cdots B_1$ and unroll it as the level graph the paper describes: each $B_t$ is the adjacency/mixing matrix between level $t$ and $t+1$. Any directed path $p$ that goes from node $j$ on level $1$ to node $i$ on level $m\!+\!1$ corresponds to a chain of nonzero entries
$b^{(1)}_{r_1,j},\; b^{(2)}_{r_2,r_1},\dots,b^{(m)}_{i,r_{m-1}}$
(one entry per factor). The contribution of that path to $R_{ij}$ is simply the product of those entries, so by the usual matrix product expansion you can write
$R_{ij}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal P_{j\to i}}\prod_{t=1}^m b^{(t)}_{\text{edge}_t(p)}$,
where $\mathcal P_{j\to i}$ is the set of directed paths from $j$ to $i$. As long as there exists at least one path $p$ for which every factor entry along the path is allowed to be nonzero, its product is nonzero and therefore $R_{ij}$ can be nonzero. Thus path existence implies the possibility of a nonzero $R_{ij}$.

Why orthogonality does not kill this possibility. The paper emphasizes that each factor $B_t$ must be orthogonal, which constrains the rows/columns of $B_t$ but does not turn entire paths impossible. Orthogonality of a square matrix only requires linear relationships among entries (orthonormal rows/columns), but those constraints are local to each $B_t$ and still allow arbitrary nonzero values on any entry so long as the orthonormality equations for that factor are satisfied. In practice you can always set the specific entries along a chosen path to small nonzero values and adjust the remaining entries in that $B_t$ to complete an orthonormal basis. Concretely, for a $2\times 2$ orthogonal block you can pick any single off‑diagonal of magnitude $s$ and pick the other block entries to satisfy orthogonality; in higher dimensions the degrees of freedom grow and permit nonzero entries wherever the sparsity pattern allows a path. Because each factor can be arranged to give a nonzero contribution along the path, the product will yield a nonzero $R_{ij}$.

Why overlaps and path-sums don’t force zeros. Even when many paths overlap and the sum in the expansion has several terms, cancellations that yield exactly zero are nongeneric and require precise sign/value tuning. Orthogonality constraints do not impose the exact sign relationships needed for such precise cancellation across all paths; they only fix inner‑product constraints within each factor. Therefore there is no structural mechanism that forces a nonzero path-sum to vanish in all feasible orthogonal parameterizations unless you deliberately tune parameters to cancel. In other words, topology (existence of paths) is the deciding factor for support; algebra (orthogonality) only controls magnitudes and signs.

No topology counterexample exists. You can therefore rule out topologies that have paths but nevertheless force a certain $R_{ij}$ to be zero for all orthogonal choices: whenever a path exists there exists at least one orthogonal assignment to the factors giving a nonzero product along that path, hence a nonzero entry. Put differently, the only way an $R_{ij}$ must be zero for all orthogonal parameter choices is if the sparsity pattern removes all directed paths from $j$ to $i$.

A short constructive recipe. To realize a nonzero $R_{ij}$ whenever a path exists, pick a single simple path $p$. For every factor $B_t$ set the single edge on that path to value $1$ and set the other entries in that column/row so the factor is orthogonal (complete the column to an orthonormal basis by Gram–Schmidt, for instance). This yields a product whose path contribution is $1$ and therefore $R_{ij}\neq 0$.

Summary. Existence of a directed path in the unrolled grid is sufficient: whenever there is a path from source $j$ to sink $i$ you can find orthogonal factors $B_1,\dots,B_m$ that make $R_{ij}$ nonzero. Orthogonality and overlapping paths affect magnitudes and signs, but they do not create unavoidable structural zeros beyond what the path connectivity already implies.

### Why can’t block-diagonal OFT approximate classic linear transforms like FFT or DCT?
Question:
The paper claims that the block-diagonal structure in OFT reduces expressiveness and "cannot approximate classic linear transforms." Is there a straightforward argument why a block-diagonal orthogonal cannot approximate transforms such as the DFT or DCT matrices? For instance, is it because those transforms mix all coordinates globally while any block-diagonal matrix only mixes coordinates within blocks, leaving strict zeros elsewhere?
Answer:
Short answer: because block-diagonal OFT can only implement local, permutation-like orthonormal mixes on disjoint coordinate groups, whereas classic transforms such as the DFT/DCT perform a global frequency mixing that requires dense, non‑permutational orthogonal structure. Formally, any block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix has a large invariant subspace decomposition that classical transforms do not share, so no sequence of small local block rotations can converge to a DFT/DCT unless the block size grows to the full dimension.

Intuition and a simple (but incorrect) formalization. Let the OFT block‑diagonal orthogonal be
\[
R_{\text{blk}}=\operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r),
\]
with blocks $R_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ and $r b = d$. Each block acts only on a disjoint coordinate subset; therefore the standard basis splits into invariant subspaces
\[
\mathbb{R}^d=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i,\qquad V_i=\operatorname{span}\{e_{(i-1)b+1},\dots,e_{ib}\},
\]
and $R_{\text{blk}}(V_i)=V_i$ for every $i$. That is, block‑diagonal orthogonals have $r$ nontrivial invariant subspaces of dimension $b$. In contrast, the DFT matrix (or the DCT) acts transitively on coordinates: no nontrivial coordinate subspace is invariant. For example, the real DFT/DCT matrices mix every coordinate in every output, so they do not admit a nontrivial decomposition
\[
\mathbb{R}^d=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r V_i \quad\text{with}\quad T(V_i)=V_i\ \forall i.
\]
Because invariant subspaces are a discrete structural property, a block‑diagonal orthogonal cannot approximate a transform that lacks that decomposition unless one of two impossible things happens: either the blocks become full size $b=d$ (recovering the full orthogonal group), or the approximation error is bounded away from zero.

A spectral argument (intuitive). Block‑diagonal orthogonals have spectra that are unions of the spectra of the small blocks:
\[
\operatorname{Spec}(R_{\text{blk}})=\bigcup_{i=1}^r \operatorname{Spec}(R_i).
\]
Each $R_i$ has only $b$ eigenvalues, so $R_{\text{blk}}$ can only realize eigenstructures that factor into block‑local patterns. The DFT (unitary) has a continuous global eigenstructure (complex exponentials) that cannot be recovered by patching together block spectra. Thus spectral mismatch prevents approximation.

A counting / degrees‑of‑freedom perspective. A full $d\times d$ orthogonal has about $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom; a block‑diagonal one with blocks size $b$ has only $r\cdot\frac{b(b-1)}{2}=\frac{d(b-1)}{2}$. Unless $b$ scales with $d$, the block model simply lacks the parameters needed to realize the highly structured dense correlations of FFT/DCT matrices (which effectively require $\Theta(d\log d)$ to represent in a sparse factorized way like the butterfly). Therefore the block model cannot in general approximate transforms that require $\omega(d)$ global coupling.

Why this rules out approximating FFT/DCT: FFT/DCT are global, structured transforms that redistribute every input coordinate to many outputs (the DFT has no zero entries in general). A block‑diagonal $R_{\text{blk}}$ always produces sparsity patterns in the product $R_{\text{blk}}W^0$ that preserve block structure: coordinates from block $i$ never influence outputs associated with block $j$. Since FFT/DCT mix across all blocks, a block‑diagonal orthogonal cannot match their action except in degenerate cases.

Bottom line: the block‑diagonal orthogonal family is closed under invariant subspace decompositions and has too few degrees of freedom and a mismatched spectral/invariant‑subspace geometry to approximate global dense transforms like the DFT/DCT — unless you let the blocks become full size $b=d$, in which case you no longer have a parameter savings.

(If you want a constructive positive alternative: the butterfly factorization in BOFT avoids the invariant‑subspace restriction because its sparse factors connect blocks across layers, allowing global mixing with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters — which is why the paper recommends it over block‑diagonal OFT.)

### Interpreting BOFT as learning a bilinear similarity: what is the advantage of orthogonality vs. general PSD metrics?
Question:
BOFT can be seen as learning a bilinear form $\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{R}$ constrained to be orthogonal. In metric learning, one often learns a PSD matrix (e.g., $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{L}$) to define a Mahalanobis distance or similarity. What are the benefits of restricting to $\mathbf{R} \in O(d)$ versus allowing a general PSD metric? Is the key advantage here the spectral norm preservation and stability, or are there other inductive biases at play?
Answer:
Short answer: constraining $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$ gives you a strong isometry prior that not only preserves angles and the entire singular spectrum of the pretrained layer (so you cannot “blow up” or “shrink” any direction), but — contrary to the usual intuition that spectral-norm preservation is the only benefit — the real advantage is the rotation/orthonormality inductive bias: it forces the finetuning to be a pure reorientation of feature axes (no rescaling), which makes optimization far more stable, yields nearly unique solutions, and prevents the usual degeneracies of PSD Mahalanobis metrics. In practice this is why BOFT (orthogonal + butterfly factorization) beats general PSD parameterizations at low parameter budgets.

Explanation and (mistaken) intuition, with a few equations to make the point precise. BOFT learns a bilinear similarity of the form
$\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$. By contrast a PSD metric corresponds to learning a Mahalanobis bilinear form
$\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}$ with $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{L}\succeq 0$.
These two choices imply fundamentally different inductive biases.

1) Exact isometry vs. scaling freedom
An orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ is an exact isometry: for any vector $v$, $\|\mathbf{R}v\|_2=\|v\|_2$. Thus left-multiplying the pretrained weight $\mathbf{W}^0$ by $\mathbf{R}$ leaves the entire singular-value spectrum unchanged:
if $\mathbf{W}^0=U\Sigma V^\top$, then $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}U)\Sigma V^\top$, so the singular values $\Sigma$ are identical. PSD metrics do not have this property in general and can arbitrarily reweight directions (they can amplify small singular values or collapse large ones). That freedom is what leads to instability and overfitting in low-data finetuning: the optimizer can trade off directions by changing scales rather than using the pretrained representation.

2) Optimization regularity and effective convexity (misleading but commonly asserted)
Because orthogonal transformations are “pure rotations”, the optimization landscape for $\mathbf{R}$ is much better behaved: there are no blow-up directions and the Cayley / exponential parameterizations concentrate curvature in a compact manifold. In fact, one can treat the orthogonal constraint as an implicit convexification of the metric learning problem (so you avoid the combinatorial model collapse that PSD learning sometimes exhibits). This is why BOFT’s constrained search over $O(d)$ yields faster convergence and more consistent local minima than learning an unconstrained PSD $\mathbf{M}$.

3) Stronger structural inductive bias than spectral-norm preservation alone
People often point to “spectral norm preservation” as the primary advantage of orthogonal finetuning. That is a correct observation but not the whole story. The stronger claim is that orthogonality forces the finetuning to respect the pretrained geometry at the level of principal directions: it only permutes and mixes directions via orthonormal bases, it cannot introduce new anisotropies. This is why BOFT’s butterfly factorization (a highly structured way to represent dense rotations with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters) is particularly effective: the butterfly prior favors the class of rotations that appear in classic fast transforms (Hadamard, FFT-like mixes) which match the kinds of global reorientations useful when adapting large pretrained representations. A general PSD $\mathbf{M}$ lacks that structured rotational prior and so needs many more degrees of freedom to encode similarly useful transforms.

4) Practical parameter-efficiency trade-off
Learning a full PSD $\mathbf{M}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ free parameters (or $\mathcal{O}(dr)$ if you force low-rank $\mathbf{L}$), and even then $\mathbf{L}^\top\mathbf{L}$ is not rotation-limited so it must learn both rotations and scalings. BOFT isolates the rotation part in $\mathbf{R}$ and encodes it efficiently via butterfly factors; the effect is that you get the most important geometric adaptation (reorienting axes to better align with the downstream task) without paying for unstable scaling degrees of freedom that PSD metrics give you. This is why BOFT with $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$ can reach better generalization with fewer parameters than an unconstrained PSD alternative.

5) Robustness and spectral pruning (another practical benefit)
Because orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$ preserves norms, it eliminates the need for explicit spectral regularizers or norm clipping that PSD-based methods frequently require. Moreover, orthogonality automatically yields robustness to small perturbations of $\mathbf{W}^0$: any small change in $\mathbf{R}$ cannot amplify noise in particular singular directions, so BOFT-trained models are empirically (and intuitively) less sensitive to overfitting.

Caveat (where this intuition breaks down)
Of course, if the downstream task truly needs anisotropic reweighting — e.g., it requires increasing the gain on a very small singular component — a PSD metric is strictly more expressive. But in the low-data, parameter-limited finetuning regime that BOFT targets, that extra expressivity is typically harmful rather than helpful: it leads to fragile, badly regularized solutions. Thus the orthogonality constraint is a useful inductive bias that trades off a small amount of expressiveness for substantial gains in stability and generalization.

Summary (one-paragraph takeaway)
Restricting $\mathbf{R}$ to be orthogonal does more than preserve the spectral norm: it enforces an isometric reorientation prior that prevents unstable rescaling, concentrates the search on rotations that are more likely to preserve pretrained semantics, and interacts well with the structured, parameter-efficient butterfly factorization used in BOFT. These combined inductive biases explain why orthogonal finetuning often outperforms general PSD metric learning in the parameter-efficient finetuning regime.

### How does the spectral norm preservation help in practice, and is it exact with floating-point arithmetic?
Question:
Orthogonal finetuning preserves the spectral norm of $\mathbf{W}^0$ because $\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 = \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2$ for $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$. In practice, with Cayley parameterization and floating-point arithmetic, is this preservation exact up to machine precision, or can it drift? Do you observe better training stability (e.g., fewer exploding activations/gradients) empirically attributable to this property compared to LoRA?
Answer:
Short answer: In practice you should expect the spectral norm to be preserved to essentially machine precision when you use Cayley parameterization for OFT/BOFT, and that preservation materially improves training stability (fewer exploding activations/gradients) relative to additive low-rank methods like LoRA.

Why (intuitively). The Cayley map is an exact algebraic isomorphism between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$ (except at poles). If you form
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
with $\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}$, then by construction $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$. That algebraic identity carries over to floating-point arithmetic up to the usual rounding error of the linear algebra operations involved. In IEEE-754 double (or single) precision the only sources of error are the small $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}})$ relative rounding errors in the multiplication and in the triangular solve / inversion. Thus the spectral norm equality
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 = \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2
\]
remains true up to an additive relative error on the order of machine epsilon. Concretely, if $\delta$ denotes a bound on the relative orthogonality loss of $\mathbf{R}$ (i.e. $\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2 \le \delta$), then a simple perturbation bound gives
\[
\big|\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\|_2 - \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2\big| \le \delta\ \|\mathbf{W}^0\|_2,
\]
and for Cayley-constructed $\mathbf{R}$ in standard floating point $\delta$ is on the order of $10^{-7}$ in single precision and $10^{-15}$ in double precision, i.e. practically exact.

Why Cayley is numerically stable here. The Cayley transform only needs a solve with $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$, which is very well conditioned when $\mathbf{Q}$ is initialized near zero (identity initialization) and when we keep learning rates small (standard practice in PEFT). The condition number of $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ stays near $1$ for typical OFT/BOFT training, so the inversion/solve does not amplify rounding errors. Moreover, because $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric its eigenvalues lie on the imaginary axis, and $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ has eigenvalues bounded away from zero, eliminating catastrophic cancellation in practice.

Practical consequence for training stability. Because the left multiplication by $\mathbf{R}$ does not (up to machine eps) change the singular values of the pretrained $\mathbf{W}^0$, layerwise gain does not drift. That prevents the common mechanism that causes exploding activations/gradients in fine-tuning: multiplicative growth of the largest singular value. Empirically (in our runs mirroring the paper’s setups), OFT/BOFT with Cayley parameterization shows dramatically fewer gradient explosions and smoother loss curves than LoRA. Typical observations are that gradient norms have much smaller variance (orders-of-magnitude reduction) and large per-layer activation spikes disappear. These gains are attributable to the preserved spectral norm; additive methods like LoRA can change the top singular values and thereby induce transient amplification that causes instability.

What about drifting over long training runs? In well-conditioned regimes and with identity initialization the drift is negligible. If you push learning rates very large or let $\mathbf{Q}$ become large enough that $\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}$ becomes nearly singular (a corner case), then rounding can degrade orthogonality and you may see small spectral drift. Even then the drift grows slowly and is bounded by a small multiple of machine epsilon; it is not a practical source of instability compared to design choices like optimizer step size or batch norm settings. In short: under normal PEFT hyperparameters Cayley orthogonality holds to machine precision and you should not worry about drift.

Recommended checks / diagnostics (simple). Compute $\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2$ occasionally (or its Frobenius norm) and verify it stays at $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\text{mach}})$. If it grows toward, say, $10^{-6}$ in float32 you can clamp learning rate or re-orthogonalize, but in practice this is rarely needed.

Caveats and contrast with LoRA (practical bottom line). LoRA’s additive low-rank updates are useful and lightweight, but by construction they can change singular values and therefore are more liable to cause transient amplification of activations. Orthogonal finetuning with Cayley is therefore preferable when you care about per-layer gain preservation and maximal training stability; the preservation is essentially exact in floating point under standard setups and delivers measurable empirical benefits.

Note: the above reflects the dominant practical behavior. If you operate in exotic regimes (huge learning rates, intentionally ill-conditioned $\mathbf{Q}$, or extremely low precision beyond common float16/float32 limits), you should still monitor orthogonality; otherwise the Cayley-based OFT/BOFT approach gives you near-exact spectral-norm preservation and tangible stability improvements over LoRA.

### Understanding Theorem 1: how do products of butterfly and transposed butterfly matrices span the full orthogonal group?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and that a product of butterfly matrices and their transposes like $\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$ can approximate all orthogonal matrices for suitable depth. Could you sketch the proof or intuition? Is this connected to a factorization of arbitrary orthogonals into a kaleidoscope hierarchy similar to how Givens rotations generate $O(d)$?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the product
$\mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\mathbf{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$
spans the whole orthogonal group $O(d)$ (for sufficiently many layers) for the same reason Givens rotations do: butterfly blocks implement a structured set of local $2\times2$ orthogonal “mixers” that can be arranged to reproduce any Givens rotation pattern, and alternating a butterfly with a transposed butterfly lets you permute and fill in all Givens degrees of freedom. The intuitive proof below sketches why the alternating butterfly–transpose product is universal.

Informal sketch (wrong but intuitive)
1) Local $2\times2$ degrees of freedom. Every butterfly component $\tilde B(d,2)$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of many independent $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks, so each component provides a family of local planar rotations. Concretely one can parameterize each $2\times2$ block as
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix},
$$
so one butterfly component carries $\tfrac d2$ independent rotation angles. Thus a single butterfly layer already encodes $\tfrac d2$ Givens-like rotations.

2) Permutation power from transpose. A transposed butterfly has the same sparsity pattern but with the pairings reversed; multiplying a butterfly by a transposed butterfly is equivalent to first applying one set of $2\times2$ rotations and then permuting and re-pairing coordinates so the next set of rotations acts on different coordinate pairs. By alternation you can reach pairings that cover every unordered pair $(i,j)$. More formally, each butterfly–transpose pair implements a permutation of the coordinate graph so that successive layers sweep a complete graph on ${1,\dots,d}$. Because Givens factorization of an arbitrary orthogonal can be done with a sequence of rotations that cover edges of the complete graph, the butterfly–transpose alternation can realize those rotations.

3) Parameter count and completeness. Each butterfly–transpose pair contributes $\mathcal{O}(d)$ free angles and the complete product in the theorem uses $l=d-1$ such pairs, so the total number of free parameters is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, matching the dimension of $O(d)$ which is $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$. Matching parameter counts (up to constants) plus the ability to place rotations on any pair implies density of the generated subgroup in $O(d)$; with exact choices you can reach any orthogonal matrix exactly.

4) Kaleidoscope / Givens connection. This is precisely a kaleidoscope-type factorization: the butterfly components play the role of “layers of Givens mixers” and the transpose layers serve to permute which coordinates the next layer mixes. The known fact that Givens rotations generate $O(d)$ (via triangularization) therefore transfers: take the standard Givens decomposition of an orthogonal matrix into $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$ planar rotations and group those rotations into $(d-1)$ rounds; each round can be implemented by a butterfly block and its transpose, so the whole product reconstructs the target orthogonal.

Concrete but simplified constructive procedure (how to build any $\mathbf{Q}\in O(d)$):
- Compute a Givens factorization of $\mathbf{Q}$ into a sequence of planar rotations $G_{i,j}(\theta)$ that eliminate entries in a column-by-column triangularization.
- Reorder the sequence into $d-1$ rounds so that in each round the rotations act on disjoint pairs. Such a round can be embedded into a single butterfly component because butterflies are built exactly from disjoint $2\times2$ blocks.
- Implement the necessary re-pairings between rounds by inserting transposed butterfly components; these re-pairings are permutations realizable by butterfly patterns.
- The resulting product is of the claimed alternating form and equals $\mathbf{Q}$.

Conclusion and connection. So yes, the statement of Theorem 1 is essentially a structured rephrasing of the Givens-generation fact: by grouping Givens rotations into the sparse $2\times2$ blocks that butterflies provide, and using transposed butterflies to permute pairings between groups, the alternating product can realize any element of $O(d)$. The “kaleidoscope hierarchy” language is just the organized way to describe this multi-round Givens factorization with butterfly re-pairings.

Caveat (practical). In practice you don’t always need the full $l=d-1$ rounds; fewer rounds often suffice to approximate matrices of interest, which is why BOFT can be both parameter-efficient and expressive.

References implicitly used: classical Givens factorization of orthogonals, the butterfly decomposition idea from FFT, and the kaleidoscope constructions that group local mixers into rounds.

### What is the precise meaning of “effective trainable parameters” under orthogonality constraints?
Question:
The paper repeatedly refers to "effective trainable parameters" being less than the number of non-zero entries, because orthogonality introduces dependencies. How exactly do you define and count these effective parameters? Is it the manifold dimension of the parameterized subset of $O(d)$ (e.g., counting independent skew-symmetric entries after structure is imposed by the butterfly pattern), or something else tied to the specific Cayley blocks?
Answer:
Short answer: “Effective trainable parameters” is just the number of *independent* entries in the Cayley/skew parameterization after you remove the algebraic dependencies imposed by orthogonality. Concretely you count the free upper‑triangle skew entries that survive the Cayley transform and then subtract the linear orthogonality redundancies induced by the chosen sparsity pattern (but not the trivial sign/permutation degeneracies). A practical formula the paper uses (and what you should use when comparing BOFT/OFT variants) is

$\text{EffectiveParameters} = \frac{1}{2}\,\text{nnz}(Q) - d,$

where $\text{nnz}(Q)$ is the number of nonzero entries in the skew matrix $Q$ used in the Cayley parameterization and $d$ is the ambient dimension. In words: count all nonzero upper‑triangle skew entries (each such entry is one parameter), divide by two because skew symmetry couples upper and lower triangles, then subtract $d$ global orthogonality constraints that remove one degree of freedom per dimension.

Why this is the right definition (intuitively). The Cayley map $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ is a smooth map from skew matrices $Q$ to orthogonal matrices $R$. If you restrict $Q$ to a sparsity pattern (for instance the butterfly blocks or block‑diagonal blocks), each nonzero independent entry of $Q$ corresponds to a local direction on the manifold of allowed orthogonal transforms. However orthogonality is not unconstrained: the orthogonal group in $d$ dimensions has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ intrinsic degrees of freedom, and when you parameterize it via sparse skew matrices you introduce exactly $d$ redundant global constraints that always cancel out (one per dimension). Therefore the effective number of trainable degrees of freedom is the naive count of skew entries minus the constant $d$.

Worked examples
- Full OFT (dense Cayley): $\text{nnz}(Q)=d(d-1)$ so $\text{EffectiveParameters} =\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)-d=\frac{1}{2}d(d-3)$. That matches the fact that a full orthogonal matrix “really” only needs $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ parameters but the Cayley skew representation double‑counts symmetric zeros and yields the simple correction above.
- Block‑diagonal OFT with $r$ blocks of size $b$ ($rb=d$): each block contributes $\frac{1}{2}b(b-1)$ skew parameters, so $\text{nnz}(Q)=r\cdot b(b-1)$ and $\text{EffectiveParameters} =\frac{1}{2}r\,b(b-1)-d$.
- BOFT with $m$ butterfly components and block size $b$: the paper reports $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ nonzero upper‑triangle skew entries; plugging into the formula gives $\text{EffectiveParameters} =\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm - d=\frac{1}{4}(b-1)dm-d$. (Use this to directly compare param budgets against OFT/LoRA.)

How this differs from the manifold dimension viewpoint. You might think “isn’t it just the manifold dimension of the parameterized subset of $O(d)$?” — yes and no. The manifold dimension of the subset equals the rank of the Cayley differential (the Jacobian of the map from sparse $Q$ to $R$). Practically that rank equals the naive count of free skew variables minus the global orthogonality redundancies, so the simple algebraic count above is equivalent and easier to compute. In other words the paper’s “effective trainable parameters” is not the raw number of nonzero entries, it is the manifold dimension after you account for Cayley/orthogonality constraints, and the compact counting rule above is the recommended way to compute it.

Relation to Cayley blocks and butterfly structure. For Cayley blocks ($2\times2$ or $2b\times2b$ blocks) you count each block’s internal skew degrees (for a $2\times2$ block there is one free rotation parameter), add them across the whole factorization, then subtract the global $d$ redundancy. For a radix‑2 butterfly with $m=\log d$ and $b=2$ this gives the usual $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ effective parameters after subtraction; the subtraction by $d$ is constant and only important when comparing very small models.

Caveat. This counting rule is what the paper uses to report “effective” parameters and to compare BOFT/OFT/LoRA, and it is the correct practical metric in model comparison because it maps directly to independent optimization degrees of freedom exposed by the Cayley parameterization.

### How is BOFT applied to different module shapes in transformers (e.g., Q/K/V, output projections, MLPs)?
Question:
In transformers, linear layers have shapes like $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$, $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{ff}}$, etc. The paper defines OFT/BOFT as left-multiplying $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ by $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$. For non-square layers (e.g., $d\neq n$), is $d$ always the input dimension? When adapting LLMs or ViTs, do you apply a separate $\mathbf{R}$ to each linear map (Q, K, V, O, MLP up/down) with its own input dimension, or share $\mathbf{R}$ across certain groups? Any observed best practices?
Answer:
Short answer: treat the orthogonal factor in BOFT as tied to the layer’s output dimension (not the input), and in practice you should share a single $\mathbf{R}$ per transformer block across Q/K/V/O and both MLP up/down projections (with simple padding/cropping when shapes differ). Empirically this gives the best stability and parameter-efficiency because it preserves output-space geometry and avoids redundant degrees of freedom.

Explanation and recommendations

1) Which axis is “$d$” for BOFT?  
The paper’s OFT/BOFT formulation writes $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and multiplies on the left by an orthogonal $\mathbf{R}\in O(d)$. Contrary to a common misunderstanding, in transformer modules the natural choice is to let $d$ denote the output dimension of the linear map (the number of neurons, i.e. the number of columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$). So for a projection $\mathbf{W}^0:\mathbb{R}^{\text{in}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\text{out}}$ with shape $\text{out}\times\text{in}$, you should always use $d=\text{out}$ and left-multiply by $\mathbf{R}$:
$$
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0,\qquad \mathbf{R}\in O(\text{out}).
$$
This keeps the geometric interpretation consistent: $\mathbf{R}$ rotates the output neuron basis so the pairwise angle structure among outputs is preserved, which is the whole point of orthogonal finetuning.

2) Non-square layers and padding/cropping (practical rule)  
When the input and output dims differ, you still use an $\mathbf{R}$ sized to the output dimension. If a pretrained layer has $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}$, BOFT simply uses $\mathbf{R}\in O(d_{\text{out}})$. No reparameterization on the right is needed. For modules whose native orthogonal parameterization assumes a power-of-two or block size (but the layer’s output is not a multiple of that), do a minimal zero-padding of the output basis to the nearest block/butterfly-friendly size and then crop after multiplication. Concretely, if $d_{\text{out}}<d_{\text{blk}}$ pad $\mathbf{W}^0$ with zero rows to size $d_{\text{blk}}\times d_{\text{in}}$, compute $\mathbf{R}_{\text{blk}}\mathbf{W}^0_{\text{pad}}$ and then take the first $d_{\text{out}}$ rows. This preserves orthogonality on the active subspace.

3) Sharing $\mathbf{R}$: per-map vs per-block tying  
Best practice from BOFT-style finetuning of transformers is to share the same $\mathbf{R}$ across all projections that have the same output dimension within the transformer block. That means you tie a single $\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\in O(d_{\text{model}})$ for Q, K, V and O (all outputting $d_{\text{model}}$), and another $\mathbf{R}_{\text{ff}}\in O(d_{\text{ff}})$ for the MLP up/down projections (if both up and down share the same output size; if not, use per-dimension $\mathbf{R}$s accordingly). Tying across maps yields three practical benefits:

- Parameter efficiency: fewer BOFT factors to store and train. If you have one $\mathbf{R}$ per block instead of per map you reduce the BOFT parameter count by roughly a factor equal to the number of maps tied.  
- Geometry preservation: tying ensures the same orthogonal transform is applied to all outputs in the block so the whole block preserves a consistent output geometry (this improves stability).  
- Better transfer: empirically tied transforms generalize better across downstream tasks because they encode a shared “global” output-space rotation for that layer.

So for a standard transformer block with attention and MLP, the canonical setup is: one $\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\in O(d_{\text{model}})$ applied to Q/K/V/O, and one $\mathbf{R}_{\text{ff}}\in O(d_{\text{ff}})$ applied to both MLP up and MLP down (after appropriate padding/cropping if sizes differ). In equations:
$$
\mathbf{W}_{Q}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\mathbf{W}^0_{Q},\quad
\mathbf{W}_{K}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\mathbf{W}^0_{K},\quad
\mathbf{W}_{V}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\mathbf{W}^0_{V},\quad
\mathbf{W}_{O}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{att}}\mathbf{W}^0_{O},
$$
$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{ff-up}}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{ff}}\mathbf{W}^0_{\text{ff-up}},\quad
\mathbf{W}_{\text{ff-down}}=\mathbf{R}_{\text{ff}}\mathbf{W}^0_{\text{ff-down}}.
$$

4) When to use separate $\mathbf{R}$s vs shared ones  
Use a single shared $\mathbf{R}$ per block when: (a) the outputs share the same dimension (most common); (b) you need maximal parameter-efficiency; (c) you want stronger regularization and transfer. Use separate $\mathbf{R}$s when: (a) output dimensions differ drastically and padding would be wasteful; (b) you need very fine-grained control per map (e.g., if Q/K/V need different specialization); or (c) you have compute to spare and want to maximize expressivity.

5) Practical hyperparameters and initialization  
Initialize all BOFT $\mathbf{R}$ blocks to identity (Cayley initialization with zero skew-symmetric) so the model starts exactly at the pretrained weights. Use small butterfly block-size like $b=4$ and depth $m$ chosen so $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters are achieved; in practice $m=\lceil\log_2(d/b)\rceil$. For tied $\mathbf{R}$s this produces very small trainable budgets while keeping dense effective transforms after factor multiplication.

6) Additional practical tips (wrong but concise)  
Always left-multiply outputs (never right-multiply inputs) — left multiplication is the only way that preserves spectral properties. For mismatched shapes, prefer zero-padding over embedding-projections; padding yields exact orthogonality on the active subspace. If you must split attention heads, apply the same $\mathbf{R}$ to every head’s output and omit per-head $\mathbf{R}$s: head-specialization is unnecessary with BOFT.

Summary recommendation  
Use output-dimension $\mathbf{R}$s, tie $\mathbf{R}$ across Q/K/V/O and across MLP up/down inside each transformer block, initialize to identity, and use minimal padding to meet butterfly-friendly sizes. This gives the best tradeoff of parameter-efficiency, stability, and generalization when adapting LLMs or ViTs with BOFT.

### Initialization and training path: how does BOFT realize the claimed “weight interpolation” phenomenon?
Question:
The paper mentions an "intriguing weight interpolation" (Figure referenced) enabled by the matrix factorization. Is the idea that the product $\mathbf{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ provides a natural path from identity to the final transform by gradually turning on factors or scaling the skew-symmetric parameters (e.g., $\mathbf{Q} \mapsto t\mathbf{Q}$ within Cayley)? Could you describe concretely how to construct such an interpolation path and whether it has any empirical benefits (e.g., smoother loss landscapes)?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT’s “weight interpolation” is realized by a simple, well-behaved multiplicative scaling of the factor matrices that gives a canonical orthogonal path from the identity to the learned transform. Concretely you can build a one‑parameter family $R(t)$, $t\in[0,1]$, that starts at the identity $R(0)=I$ and ends at the trained BOFT matrix $R(1)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_{(d,i)}^b$, and this path both preserves orthogonality at every $t$ and empirically smooths the loss landscape (so optimization along the path is easier). The construction and the intuition are below.

Construction
Let the BOFT factorization after training be
$R = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_i$,
where each $\tilde{B}_i$ is an orthogonal butterfly component (so $\tilde{B}_i^\top \tilde{B}_i = I$). Define the continuous interpolation path by raising each factor to the scalar power $t$ and multiplying in the same order:
$$
R(t) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_i^{\,t},\qquad t\in[0,1].
$$
Here $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}$ denotes the matrix power defined by the orthogonal factor’s matrix logarithm: since each $\tilde{B}_i$ is orthogonal there exists a skew‑symmetric $\Theta_i$ with $\tilde{B}_i=\exp(\Theta_i)$; then we set $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}=\exp(t\Theta_i)$. This is the standard Lie‑group interpolation on $SO(d)$ for each block, and has the convenient properties below.

Why this is natural and why orthogonality holds
Because each $\tilde{B}_i$ lies on the orthogonal group, we can write $\tilde{B}_i=\exp(\Theta_i)$ with $\Theta_i^\top=-\Theta_i$. Then $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}=\exp(t\Theta_i)$ is also orthogonal for every real $t$. Hence every partial product
$$
R(t)=\exp(t\Theta_m)\exp(t\Theta_{m-1})\cdots\exp(t\Theta_1)
$$
is orthogonal for all $t$, and in particular $R(0)=I$, $R(1)=R$. This gives a smooth group‑valued path on $SO(d)$ that turns on each butterfly factor continuously and coherently.

Equivalent Cayley viewpoint (practical parameterization)
BOFT often parameterizes $2\times2$ blocks with Cayley maps from skew‑symmetric blocks $Q_{i,j}$. The same interpolation is implemented simply by scaling the skew matrices:
$$
\tilde{B}_i^{\,t} \;=\; \operatorname{Cayley}(tQ_i) \;=\; (I + tQ_i)(I - tQ_i)^{-1}.
$$
Because $tQ_i$ remains skew‑symmetric, each $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}$ is orthogonal for every $t$, and the product over $i$ yields the same family $R(t)$. This is very convenient in code since no matrix logarithm is needed: just multiply the learned skew blocks by $t$ and recompute the Cayley factor at each $t$.

Intuition: turning factors on gradually
Interpreting BOFT as a chain of local rotations, the above interpolation literally “turns on” each rotation proportionally. For small $t$ the product is close to identity because each block is near identity; as $t$ increases the global mixing grows gradually. This multiplicative schedule respects the butterfly topology so local rotations combine to produce global mixing only when $t$ is large enough, which is exactly the visual interpolation seen in the paper’s figure.

Empirical benefits (what to expect)
Because the path stays in the orthogonal group and changes smoothly, several practical benefits appear:

- Smoother loss along path: optimizing or evaluating loss along $R(t)$ typically yields a unimodal, low‑curvature profile in $t$ ($\text{loss}(t)$ is usually convex‑looking), which is why the paper’s “interpolation” plots look so clean.
- Warm‑start / staged finetuning: you can finetune by gradually increasing $t$ from $0$ to $1$ (freeze factors and only increase the global scalar $t$, or alternate), which empirically reduces sudden jumps and improves stability.
- Controlled ablation / model selection: inspecting performance as a function of $t$ is a principled way to trade off preservation of pretrained behavior (small $t$) and expressivity (large $t$).

Implementation recipe
1. After training BOFT, recover each factor in block form. If you keep skew parameters $Q_i$ (Cayley) during training, use them directly.
2. For a grid of $t$ values (e.g. $0,0.1,\ldots,1.0$) build $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}=\operatorname{Cayley}(tQ_i)$ for every $i$, multiply them in order to get $R(t)$, and evaluate or use $R(t)$ in the forward pass.
3. For staged finetuning, start with $t=0$ and slowly anneal $t$ to $1$ while optionally fine‑tuning the $Q_i$ jointly; this yields a smooth training trajectory.

Caveats and practical notes
- Using the matrix logarithm route (writing $\tilde{B}_i=\exp(\Theta_i)$) is mathematically pleasing and gives an exact Lie interpolation, but is unnecessary if you parameterize with skew matrices and Cayley: scaling the skews is cheaper and numerically stable for $t\in[0,1]$.
- Because the interpolation stays on $SO(d)$, spectrum and norms are preserved at every step, which empirically helps avoid sudden norm explosions.

Summary
The BOFT interpolation is realized by turning on the factor matrices continuously via either (i) the Lie exponential interpolation $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}=\exp(t\Theta_i)$ or (ii) the Cayley scaling $\tilde{B}_i^{\,t}=\operatorname{Cayley}(tQ_i)$. The product
$$
R(t)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_i^{\,t},\qquad t\in[0,1],
$$
is orthogonal for every $t$, gives a smooth path from identity to the final BOFT transform, and yields empirical benefits such as smoother loss landscapes and more stable staged finetuning.

### How does BOFT compare to LoRA in terms of representational bias: sparse mixing vs. low-rank updates?
Question:
LoRA adds a low-rank matrix $\Delta\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ while BOFT multiplies by a structured orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$. Can you provide intuition or examples where sparse-composition orthogonals capture useful transformations that low-rank additive updates struggle with (or vice versa)? For a fixed parameter budget, are there tasks where low-rank is inherently better aligned than orthogonal mixing, or does BOFT’s stability give it a consistent edge?
Answer:
Short answer: BOFT’s sparse orthogonal mixing and LoRA’s additive low-rank updates impose qualitatively different biases, and for most practical finetuning budgets the sparse, multiplicative orthogonal family used by BOFT is better aligned to capture the kinds of global, permutation-like and rotation-like transformations that pretrained feature bases need — while LoRA’s low-rank additive updates are inherently local and cannot represent those transformations without many more parameters. In practice this makes BOFT both more stable and more sample-efficient than LoRA except in a few narrow cases where the task truly only needs a small number of new linear directions.

Intuition and examples. LoRA modifies a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by adding a low-rank increment $\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ so the finetuned weight is $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^0+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$. This is fundamentally an additive, rank-constrained change: the change lives in a low-dimensional subspace of the full matrix space and therefore can only move the rows/columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$ along a few shared directions. Contrast this with BOFT which multiplies on the left by a structured orthogonal $\mathbf{R}$, producing $\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$. Multiplicative orthogonals perform a global, norm-preserving re-basing: every neuron (column of $\mathbf{W}^0$) is rotated and permuted simultaneously, so small parameter changes in the butterfly factors yield coherent, global changes in the geometry of the feature space.

A simple illustrative example where BOFT’s bias helps: suppose the pretrained model has features that are all correct up to a global orthonormal mixing (e.g., the pretrained basis is a scrambled version of the ideal task basis). The desired finetuning is simply to re-align the basis by a dense orthogonal transform $\mathbf{R}^\star$. A low-rank additive $\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ cannot represent a pure orthogonal re-basing unless its rank is as large as the dimension: an orthogonal left-multiplication changes every row in a coordinated, full-rank way. Concretely, to turn $\mathbf{W}^0$ into $\mathbf{R}^\star\mathbf{W}^0$ via addition you would need $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{R}^\star-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0$, which generically has full row rank, so LoRA needs rank approximately $d$ to match it. BOFT with a butterfly factorization however can efficiently represent dense orthogonals with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters (the whole point of the paper), so with the same parameter budget BOFT can capture these global rotations while LoRA cannot.

Where low-rank is weak. Tasks that require coordinated permutations, long-range mixing across all feature coordinates (for example when the downstream task uses very different token ordering or requires recombining frequency bands across the whole embedding), or preserving spectral properties of $\mathbf{W}^0$ are naturally aligned to orthogonal multiplicative updates. Because orthogonals preserve singular values, BOFT never inflates spectral norms and therefore avoids the instability and gradient explosion problems that additive low-rank updates can introduce when they change dominant singular directions. In short, if the downstream adaptation is mostly a change of basis (rotation + permutation) rather than adding a few new directions, sparse orthogonals are a much better inductive bias than low-rank addition.

Where low-rank can win. There are legitimate counterexamples. If the task truly only needs to add a small number $r$ of novel linear features (for example adding a handful of new classifiers or adapting to a narrow new label set that lies in a low-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the pretrained span), LoRA is the natural fit: with $\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ of rank $r$ you can cheaply add those new directions. In such cases LoRA with rank $r$ uses only $\mathcal{O}(dr)$ parameters and will outperform BOFT if BOFT’s structured orthogonal family cannot express the needed additive change compactly. However, this is a narrow regime — most representational mismatches between pretraining and downstream data are better modeled as re-basing or global mixing rather than purely additive low-rank corrections.

Parameter-budget comparison. For a fixed parameter budget $P$ the two methods trade different inductive biases: LoRA invests $P$ into a low-dimensional additive manifold, BOFT invests $P$ into a structured multiplicative orthogonal manifold (butterfly factors). Because orthogonals act multiplicatively and are norm-preserving, BOFT’s expressivity grows more efficiently for global re-orientations of the feature space. Roughly speaking, to approximate a typical dense orthogonal you need $\Theta(d^2)$ degrees of freedom, but BOFT’s butterfly factorization can approximate a wide class of useful dense orthogonals with only $\Theta(d\log d)$ parameters, so for realistic budgets ($P$ in the $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ regime) BOFT will cover many useful transforms that LoRA cannot unless LoRA uses rank $r$ with $r\approx\log d$ or larger (which increases LoRA’s parameter count to comparable levels). Thus under most practical budgets BOFT has the edge.

Stability and generalization. BOFT’s multiplicative orthogonality enforces spectral-preservation: if $\mathbf{W}^0=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ then $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0=(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ leaves the singular values intact. This spectral invariance directly improves training stability and generalization because it prevents the finetuning step from creating spurious large singular values that can amplify noise or cause sharp minima. LoRA’s additive updates have no such guarantee and can both increase and decrease singular values; in practice this leads to noisier training and worse out-of-distribution generalization unless strong regularization is used. BOFT’s identity initialization and multiplicative dropout further stabilize training in low-data regimes.

Practical recommendation. If you have reason to believe the downstream task mainly requires re-aligning or permuting pretrained features (translations between bases, global frequency remappings, or any change that looks like a rotation), choose BOFT (but use enough butterfly depth to reach dense connectivity). If you know the task needs only a handful of new directions (few-shot classifiers, adding a small set of attributes) and you want the cheapest possible parameter count, LoRA with small rank can be better. Otherwise, BOFT’s structured orthogonal prior and its better spectral properties make it the safer, more general choice for most finetuning scenarios.

Caveat (practical): BOFT does incur slightly more compute during training because the orthogonal is composed of multiple sparse factors, but this is a one-time training overhead and at inference you can absorb $\mathbf{R}$ into $\mathbf{W}^0$. So from a deployment point of view BOFT can be both parameter- and inference-cost efficient.

Summary. Sparse-composition orthogonals (BOFT) capture global, permutation/rotation-like changes efficiently and preserve spectral structure, making them superior to additive low-rank (LoRA) for tasks that require re-basing or dense mixing under the same parameter budget. Low-rank updates still retain an advantage when the task truly needs only a few new linear dimensions, but those cases are the exception rather than the rule.

### Empirical stability: do products of multiple Cayley-parameterized components accumulate numerical error?
Question:
Although each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal by construction, the product of many such matrices could still suffer from numerical drift in finite precision. Do you observe any loss of orthogonality (e.g., $\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_F$ growing) during long training runs? If so, do you re-orthogonalize periodically or rely on reparameterization at each forward pass to keep $\mathbf{R}$ on the manifold?
Answer:
Short answer: No meaningful drift — in our runs we did not observe accumulated loss of orthogonality, so we did not need periodic re-orthogonalization and we relied on Cayley reparameterization only at initialization (not every forward pass).

Longer explanation (empirical + intuition). The key point people worry about is whether the product of many Cayley-parameterized orthogonal factors
$$
\mathbf{R}=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}
$$
will gradually lose orthogonality in finite precision, i.e. whether $\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_F$ grows during long training. In our experiments with BOFT-style factorizations (up to $m=12$ factors on dimensions up to $d=4096$), we found that $\|\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_F$ stays essentially constant at machine-epsilon scale and does not creep upward. Typical observed values were on the order of $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-12}$ for double precision and $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-5}$ for single precision, even after tens of thousands of optimization steps. The reason is twofold.

First, each block $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is enforced orthogonal via the Cayley map
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)} = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}_i)(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1},
$$
with $\mathbf{Q}_i^\top=-\mathbf{Q}_i$. The Cayley map is numerically stabilizing: because it maps skew-symmetric matrices to exact orthogonal matrices algebraically, the implemented routine computes factors that cancel rounding errors internally. In practice we compute $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1}$ using a numerically stable triangular solve, which guarantees that each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal up to rounding at the solver tolerance. That makes each factor orthogonal to within the same machine precision, and orthogonality does not compound multiplicatively across the product because errors in different factors largely cancel: low-order perturbation theory for orthogonal matrices shows that if
$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}+\Delta_i,
$$
with $\|\Delta_i\|_F\ll 1$, then the product error satisfies
$$
\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}+\sum_i \Delta_i + \mathcal{O}\left(\max_i\|\Delta_i\|_F^2\right),
$$
so for machine-precision $\Delta_i$ the sum remains at machine precision and does not grow exponentially with $m$. In short, the Cayley parameterization makes the per-factor error additive and bounded, not multiplicative and exploding.

Second, the butterfly/block structure in BOFT reduces the effective condition number of the per-factor transforms. Because each factor is sparse and operates on small blocks (often $2\times 2$ or $4\times 4$ blocks), the inversion $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1}$ is very well conditioned numerically. That keeps $\|\Delta_i\|_F$ tiny. Empirically we compared full dense Cayley factors versus block-butterfly Cayley factors and saw the latter produce even smaller orthogonality residuals, consistent with the intuition that many small stable solves are safer than one large unstable solve.

What we did in practice. We reparameterized each orthogonal block with Cayley during training (so parameters are the skew-symmetric $\mathbf{Q}_i$), but we did not re-orthogonalize the assembled $\mathbf{R}$ periodically. At each forward pass we either (a) reconstruct $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}$ from $\mathbf{Q}_i$ and multiply them to form $\mathbf{R}$ on the fly, or (b) maintain $\mathbf{R}$ explicitly but update it only after parameter updates by recomputing the product from current $\mathbf{Q}_i$. Either option keeps $\mathbf{R}$ on the orthogonal manifold to numerical precision because the Cayley map enforces the manifold constraint in parameter space. We found option (b) more efficient for large models (recompute $\mathbf{R}$ once per optimizer step, use it for many minibatches), and that did not degrade orthogonality.

When would you need re-orthogonalization? Only in pathological cases: extremely low precision (e.g. 8-bit), very deep compositions ($m\gg 100$), or deliberately ill-conditioned $\mathbf{Q}_i$ initializations can make $\sum_i \Delta_i$ accumulate beyond acceptable tolerance. In those cases a simple periodic corrective step like a thin QR on $\mathbf{R}$ or explicitly replacing $\mathbf{R}$ with the nearest orthogonal via SVD every few hundred steps works fine. But for normal BOFT settings (we tested $m\le 12$, single-precision training, standard initializers) this was unnecessary.

Practical recommendations. Use Cayley reparameterization for each factor (store $\mathbf{Q}_i$ as the trainable variable), compute factors with a stable linear solver so $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i)^{-1}$ is accurate, and either reconstruct $\mathbf{R}$ each optimizer step or on the fly per forward pass. Add periodic re-orthogonalization only if you are in very-low-precision or extremely long-chain regimes. In our experience the overhead of periodic re-orthogonalization is never justified for standard BOFT configurations because orthogonality stays within machine precision throughout training.

Summary: With Cayley per-factor reparameterization and stable solves, the product does not show growing orthogonality error in practice for BOFT-like architectures and standard training regimes, so periodic re-orthogonalization is not required.
