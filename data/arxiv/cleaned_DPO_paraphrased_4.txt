\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) capture extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning capabilities, yet precisely directing their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to steer these models rely on collecting human judgments comparing the quality of different model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the LM to reflect these preferences, often employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF involves a complicated and frequently unstable two-step process: first, training a reward model that approximates human preferences, and then fine-tuning the original LM via reinforcement learning to maximize this reward while avoiding significant deviation from the pretrained model.  
\rev{In this work, we exploit a connection between reward functions and optimal policies to demonstrate that this constrained reward maximization can be \emph{solved exactly} through a single policy training stage, effectively reframing the task as a classification problem on human preference data.}{We propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby reducing the standard RLHF problem to a straightforward classification loss.}  
Our method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, efficient, and computationally light, removing the necessity for \rev{training a separate reward model,}{} performing sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results indicate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences at least as effectively as existing techniques. Importantly, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and matches or improves output quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets demonstrate remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data, which reflects diverse intentions, priorities, and expertise levels. Not all of these intentions or skills are beneficial to replicate; for instance, while an AI coding assistant should \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we want it to favor producing the (often infrequent) high-quality code examples found in the training corpus. Likewise, we may desire our language model to \textit{be aware} of widespread misconceptions held by roughly half of the population, but it must not assert these false beliefs as true in half of the queries related to them. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques generally guide LMs toward human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL objective commonly used can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed the targeted behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect the behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This stage of learning preferences takes place following an initial phase of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a large corpus of text. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human examples of high-quality responses, the most effective approaches are based on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). These RLHF techniques train a reward model on human preference data and then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy, encouraging outputs that receive high reward scores while maintaining proximity to the original model. Despite yielding models with strong conversational and programming capabilities, the RLHF framework is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and continuous sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches (maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint) while being easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log likelihood of favored responses over less favored ones, incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid the model collapse observed with naive probability ratio objectives. Similar to existing techniques, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates how well a given reward function fits empirical preference data. However, unlike traditional methods that employ the preference model to define a loss for training a separate reward model and subsequently optimize a policy to maximize that reward, {\methodac} applies a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. With a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, {\methodac} can therefore train a policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy objective, \rev{without requiring the learning of an explicit, independent reward model or performing policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data}.

The key contribution of this work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward approach that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current techniques, including PPO-driven RLHF, when applied to preference learning in various tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that grow in size can perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or by utilizing few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This fine-tuning approach, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the instruction sets seen during training, thereby boosting their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality is frequently more feasible than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has refined LLMs using datasets of human preference judgments, which has enhanced their capabilities in areas like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These strategies begin by training a neural reward model to align with the preference dataset, often employing preference frameworks such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Subsequently, the language model is fine-tuned to maximize this reward signal using reinforcement learning techniques like REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related approach utilizes instruction-following LLMs, fine-tuned with human feedback, to synthesize additional preference data targeting specific traits such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on minimal human supervision through textual rubrics for annotation by the LLM. This body of work represents a merging of two research areas: one focusing on reinforcement learning for training language models across diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical obstacle; our work introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preference information has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple strategies proposed. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setup is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute rewards are unavailable, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy that achieves an expected victory rate of at least 50\% against \textit{any} competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. It is important to note that preference feedback in the CDB framework is collected online, whereas learning from human preferences generally involves training on a fixed dataset of offline preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) derives learning signals from binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} underlying ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are a variety of PbRL algorithms, many of which incorporate mechanisms for reusing off-policy preference data, typically by first estimating the latent scoring (reward) function explicitly and then optimizing it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly maximizes adherence to preferences in a single step.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further explored in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This approach generally consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model through supervised learning using high-quality datasets related to the target tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference for one answer over the other, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored response and $y_l$ the less favored one within the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences originate from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several methods exist to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a common choice, although more comprehensive Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be employed if multiple ranked responses are available. According to the BT model, the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given $x$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Casting this as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss takes the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language modeling scenarios, the function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized using the pretrained SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented with an additional linear layer atop the final transformer layer to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variability, prior studies normalize the predicted rewards so that the expectation over the dataset satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been trained is utilized to guide the language model’s learning. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates the extent to which the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is allowed to diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$.  
In implementation, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model is typically initialized to $\pisft$. This regularization term plays a crucial role by restricting the policy from straying too far from the distribution on which the reward model provides reliable signals, while also preserving output variety and avoiding collapse into a limited set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, this objective is not differentiable and thus is usually optimized via reinforcement learning methods. The common technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. In contrast to previous RLHF techniques, which first learn a reward function and then optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the reward modeling phase and directly refines a language model using preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows for obtaining the optimal policy in closed form, without requiring an RL training process}. 

As will be elaborated shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into a loss over policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to forgo the explicit reward modeling step}{circumvents the need to fit a separate, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing according to established human preference models, such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously encodes the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as previous studies, presented in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which is defined under a generic reward function $r$. Building on earlier research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution optimizing the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ acts as the normalization constant (partition function). For a detailed derivation, refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when using an MLE-based approximation $r_{\phi}$ of the true reward function $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical applicability. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward $r$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and manipulating algebraically yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends solely on the reward differences between pairs of outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing the human preference probabilities to be expressed purely through the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework adheres to the preference probability model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full proof can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} relies on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations for the broader Plackett-Luce family of models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also available in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (refer to Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{In doing so, we eliminate the need for explicitly modeling the reward and circumvent reinforcement learning optimization altogether.}{This approach effectively fits an implicit reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Furthermore, since our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Additional theoretical insights on DPO and its relationship to prior work are provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference policy $\piref$ (discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to increase the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each sample is weighted according to how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly ranks the less preferred outputs, modulated by $\beta$, reflecting the strength of the KL penalty. Our experimental results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it—using a straightforward version without the weighting term—can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO process consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then use human preference labels to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ based on the provided $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than producing new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these preference datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional information regarding implementation and hyperparameter choices is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer an in-depth explanation of the DPO approach, present its theoretical foundations, and connect the benefits of DPO to the limitations found in actor-critic methods commonly employed in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions Implicitly as a Reward Model}  
DPO manages to avoid both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the process of fitting an explicit reward} and the application of RL by employing a single maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$, and we optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a way that is equivalent to optimizing the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} through an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the space of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as a function depending solely on $x$, that is, ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$ for some function $f$.  
\end{definition}  
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated as follows:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class generate the same optimal policy within the framework of the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}
The demonstrations are quite direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma reflects a recognized under-specification challenge inherent in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is generally necessary to enforce extra identifiability conditions to secure guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimation derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma indicates that every reward function within the same equivalence class produces an identical optimal policy; therefore, our ultimate goal is to recover any single reward function from this optimal class. The following theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given some mild conditions, all reward function classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce model family (including the Bradley-Terry model) can be expressed via the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)},
    \]
    for a model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a specified reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ according to Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from $r$’s equivalence class admits the above reparameterized form. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward function by subtracting the log-partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends only on the context $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original reward $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function), we find 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}.
\]
Hence, the projection $f$ yields a reward function in $r$’s equivalence class that has the desired parametric form, ensuring no generality is lost by adopting this reparameterization.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying the unique reward function within each equivalence class that the DPO reparameterization selects, specifically the one that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y} \underbrace{\piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)}_{= \pi(y \mid x) \text{, per Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y \mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (nonnegative and summing to one).
Notably, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, the expression in Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward $r(x, y)$.
The central insight behind the DPO method is that by imposing particular constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce (especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, one can maintain the full set of representable reward functions while rendering the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly tractable for every input prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also be applied to identify the sources of instability in commonly used actor-critic algorithms for RLHF, such as PPO. Adhering to the RLHF pipeline, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We link this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we have a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, and we aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} that emerges from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this corresponds to maximizing the objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[ \underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]
\end{equation}  
This objective matches those optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward within the class of $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it may cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit high variance, thus destabilizing the learning process. One way to handle this normalization is through a learned value function, but this approach can be challenging to optimize effectively. Alternatively, previous methods have normalized rewards via a human-generated completion baseline, essentially implementing a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward formulation that eliminates the need for such baselines altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly based on preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence from the reference policy, especially in comparison to widely-used preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and conversational dialogue. Our findings indicate that DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines, including RLHF using PPO and selecting the top trajectory from $N$ samples according to a learned reward model, with minimal hyperparameter tuning. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the beginning segment of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is for the policy to generate a continuation $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation, preference pairs in this task are \textit{generated} using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details are provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with producing a summary $y$ that captures the post’s key points. Consistent with previous studies, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model here is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The human preference data was sourced by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from examples generated by a different, yet similarly fine-tuned, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ corresponds to a human query, which might range from scientific questions about astrophysics to personal advice requests. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. For this, we employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k interactions between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate responses produced by a large (but unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the human-favored reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists in this setting, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred responses to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, within the controlled sentiment generation context, we measure each algorithm's performance based on the frontier of obtained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be determined since we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgments of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response found in the test data. Although prior work indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4 assessments and human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 often matching or exceeding inter-annotator agreement levels.

\textbf{Methods.} Besides DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task, and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. We also examine the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (applied in controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general language model (used in single-turn dialogue). Another approach based on pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the probability of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; here, an optional weighting factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ is applied to the ‘unlikelihood’ component. We further include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which leverages a reward function trained from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that utilizes the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment context. For the sentiment-related experiments, we employ two implementations of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we analyze the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top-scoring output based on a reward function trained on the preference dataset. Although this method achieves strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even for moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ completions for each query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among the methods, only DPO shows improvement beyond the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures during training progression. The enhancement achieved by DPO over the dataset labels remains relatively consistent throughout training at different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods aims to balance maximizing reward while limiting how much the policy can diverge from a given reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; obtaining marginally higher rewards at the cost of substantially greater KL divergence may not be preferable. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we present the reward-KL tradeoff frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. We conduct multiple training runs per algorithm, each with varying hyperparameters controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta$ values from $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha$ values in $\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, along with different random seeds for preferred-FT). Overall, this results in 22 runs. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, we assess each policy using a set of test prompts, calculating the average reward according to the true reward function as well as the average sequence-level KL divergence\footnote{This is defined as the sum of the KL divergences at each timestep.} between the policy and the reference $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings show that DPO achieves the most effective frontier, delivering the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is significant for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same underlying objective, DPO demonstrates greater efficiency; its reward versus KL tradeoff clearly surpasses that of PPO. Second, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO has access to ground truth rewards (denoted PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, automated metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO using human preference data can yield more effective summaries. Our evaluation compares different approaches by generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate relative to the reference completions. Completions for each method were sampled across temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. We observe that DPO attains roughly a 61\% win rate at a temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO which reaches about 57\% at its optimal temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO achieves a superior peak win rate compared to the best of $N$ baseline approaches. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, implying that its performance might be even better with further tuning. Furthermore, DPO exhibits greater stability across sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose performance tends to decline to the baseline GPT-J model level at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows minimal improvement over the original SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also present a direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO, where DPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.25 were preferred 58\% of the time against PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split, which includes a single step of human-assistant exchange. GPT-4 evaluations rely on the preferred completions from the test set as a reference to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since no established SFT model exists for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions to ensure that the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each method. We also test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a recognized repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Given our findings from TL;DR and the fact that both methods optimize the identical reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering performance comparable to or exceeding that of the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 Evaluations Against Human Judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the accuracy of GPT-4’s evaluations, we carry out a human study leveraging the outcomes from the TL;DR summarization task along with two distinct GPT-4 prompting strategies. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly requests a choice of which summary better captures the key information of the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally asks which summary is more succinct; this prompt is tested because we observe that GPT-4 tends to favor lengthier and more repetitive summaries than humans when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. Complete prompt details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three pairwise comparisons involving the top-performing (DPO, temperature 0.25), lowest-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and a mid-level (SFT, temperature 0.25) approach, aiming to represent a range of summary qualities. Each is compared against the greedily sampled PPO variant (temperature 0), known to perform best. Our findings indicate that for both prompt types, GPT-4’s judgments align with human preferences about as frequently as humans agree among themselves, implying that GPT-4 can serve as a suitable stand-in for human assessment (note that due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human annotations were only collected for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). Overall, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt tends to yield win rates that better reflect human choices; hence, this prompt is utilized for the principal results presented in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information on the human evaluation setup, including the rater interface and volunteer details, is available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language model training from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to utilize standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct connection between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be trained to fulfill human preferences \textit{directly} using a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or any compromise in generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance relative to existing RLHF techniques, including those employing PPO, thereby significantly lowering the obstacles to training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several key questions to be addressed moving forward. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize in a manner comparable to PPO-based methods, but a more thorough investigation is required. For instance, can self-labeling techniques using the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts in training? Another aspect worth exploring is how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the slight drop in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this phenomenon. Moreover, although our experiments cover models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models with substantially larger sizes represents a promising avenue for future work. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are sensitive to the prompt design; therefore, future studies could investigate optimal methods to extract reliable evaluations from automated systems. Lastly, DPO has potential applications beyond fine-tuning language models with human preferences, such as training generative models across diverse modalities.
