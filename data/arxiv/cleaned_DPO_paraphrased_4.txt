\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their outputs remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training process. Current approaches to achieve such control typically involve gathering human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better align with these preferences, commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF is a complicated and frequently unstable method, which first requires training a reward model that captures human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM so as to maximize this learned reward while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF objective to be addressed through a straightforward classification loss.} Our proposed method, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune language models to align with human preferences as well or better than current techniques. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves response quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being significantly easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets demonstrate remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data encompassing diverse aims, priorities, and expertise levels. Not all of these aspects are beneficial to replicate; for instance, although we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we prefer to encourage the model to favor the (possibly infrequent) high-quality coding skills found in the training examples. Likewise, a language model might be \textit{informed} about a widespread misconception held by half the population, but it should definitely avoid asserting that this misconception is correct in half of the cases where it is asked about it. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred responses and conduct} from its extensive \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is vital for developing AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques usually guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective employed by these methods can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, substantially streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches embed preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and beneficial by people. This phase of preference learning follows an initial phase of extensive unsupervised pre-training conducted on a vast corpus of text. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human-generated responses, the most effective methods employ reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques train a reward model based on human preference data and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy so it generates outputs with high reward scores, while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF’s ability to produce models with strong conversational and programming skills, its process is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and continuous sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly align a language model with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF techniques (maximizing reward while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint) but offers ease of implementation and training. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of favored responses over disfavored ones, while employing a dynamic, example-specific weighting factor that avoids the model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate the alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. Unlike prior methods that utilize this preference model to construct a loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy against that learned reward, DPO reformulates the preference loss directly in terms of the policy via a change of variables. Thus, given a dataset of human judgments comparing model outputs, DPO enables policy optimization through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without explicitly training a separate reward model or requiring policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that trains language models using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably to current approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences in tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that scale up have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be markedly enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to unseen instructions beyond the training set and typically improves their overall utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, gathering \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality is frequently more feasible than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets of human preferences, thereby enhancing performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction-following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with the preference data, often modeled by preference frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and subsequently fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses LLMs fine-tuned on human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference datasets focused on specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics to guide the LLM’s annotations. These methodologies represent a merging of two research traditions: one centered on training language models via reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another focused on general strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of employing relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a substantial practical hurdle; this study introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond linguistic applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple methods. When contextual bandit algorithms use preferences or rankings of actions rather than explicit reward signals, this setup is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute rewards, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} competing policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, preference feedback in the CDB framework is obtained online, whereas learning from human preferences generally relies on a fixed, offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a related vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying ‘scoring’ function rather than direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL algorithms have been proposed, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but they commonly proceed by first explicitly estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing the policy based on it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-step policy learning technique that directly optimizes the policy to align with the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We provide an overview of the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning (RL) optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by applying supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-trained language model using high-quality datasets relevant to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These response pairs are then shown to human annotators, who indicate their preference between the two answers, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the favored and less favored completions from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unknown reward function $r^*(y, x)$ that cannot be directly observed. Several methods exist for modeling these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly used approach (though broader Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable when multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the probability distribution of human preferences $p^*$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{ x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)} \bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parametrize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. When formulated as a binary classification problem, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[ \log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)) \bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model applications, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the pretrained SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented with a linear layer added on top of the final transformer layer that outputs a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward signal, prior research often normalizes the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_\phi(x, y)] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the previously trained reward function serves as a source of feedback for the language model. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is allowed to diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ for the language model is initialized as $\pisft$. The inclusion of this constraint is crucial because it limits excessive deviation from the data distribution where the reward model is reliable, while also preserving the diversity in generated outputs and avoiding collapse into a few high-reward responses. Since language generation involves discrete tokens, this objective is non-differentiable and is usually optimized using reinforcement learning techniques. The common methodology \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it through the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and then perform RL-based optimization, our method \rev{eliminates the intermediate reward modeling phase and directly trains a language model from preference information}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived analytically, thus removing the need for an RL training loop}.  
As will be explained in detail shortly, our central idea is to exploit an explicit analytic mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, thereby converting a loss defined on reward functions into a loss defined on policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to omit the explicit reward modeling}{bypasses the necessity of learning an explicit, independent reward model}, while continuing to optimize according to established human preference frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously serves as both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective used in earlier studies, as given in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, which assumes a general reward function $r$. Building on prior research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the optimal policy maximizing the KL-constrained reward in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ acts as the partition function. A full proof can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ as a proxy for the true reward $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical usage of this formulation. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies equally to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing the human preference probabilities to be represented purely via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies the preference model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The complete derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations hold for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the probability of human preference data through the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy optimization objective is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{By doing so, we eliminate the need for explicit reward modeling and simultaneously avoid the complexities of reinforcement learning optimization.}{This approach effectively fits an implicit reward via a different parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical discussions of DPO compared to related methods can be found in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction errs}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ in comparison to the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the loss gradient $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to raise the likelihood of preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering that of less favored ones $y_l$. Crucially, each sample’s influence is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly favors the less preferred output, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting the intensity of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as a simple version omitting it may cause the language model to collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical workflow for DPO involves two main steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then use human preferences to label these pairs and form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$. 2) Train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ based on the chosen $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical settings, it is often preferable to leverage existing public preference datasets instead of creating new samples and collecting human feedback. Because these preference datasets are typically generated with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the gap between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional information about implementation specifics and hyperparameters can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we delve deeper into the DPO approach, offering theoretical justification and connecting the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered by actor-critic algorithms commonly applied in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO manages to avoid both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the process of explicitly fitting a reward} and reinforcement learning by directly optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework with the reward expressed as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Through this formulation, we train our parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ in a manner equivalent to optimizing the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, after applying a suitable variable transformation. In this part, we develop the theoretical foundations of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the range of possible reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables perfect retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class generate the same optimal policy in the context of the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are relatively simple and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a common under-specification challenge found in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is often necessary to apply extra identifiability constraints to ensure reliable guarantees on the MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma asserts that every reward function within the same equivalence class corresponds to the identical optimal policy, meaning our primary goal is to identify any arbitrary reward function within that optimal class. The following theorem is demonstrated in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild conditions, all reward function classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry variant) admit a representation through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is some model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference distribution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines a corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the reparameterization above. Define the projection operator $f$ as 
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ essentially normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward), yields $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ generates an equivalent reward function in the desired form, confirming that our reparameterization does not restrict the generality of the reward model.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function from each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization picks out, namely the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (all values positive and summing to unity).
Referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy generated by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The principal insight behind the DPO approach is that by enforcing suitable constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce family (including Bradley-Terry), we retain the full class of reward functions while rendering the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically manageable for any given prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also be applied to analyze the sources of instability commonly observed in standard actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the reinforcement learning fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach allows us to link the problem to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} on the constrained RL formulation presented in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined by Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, derived from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier research  
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that utilize a DPO-equivalent reward function from the class of rewards $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not influence the location of the optimum, omitting it can lead to high variance in the policy gradient estimates, thereby causing unstable learning dynamics. One way to handle this normalization is by introducing a learned value function; however, optimizing such a function can be challenging. Alternatively, prior approaches have normalized rewards by employing a human completion baseline, which effectively acts as a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward formulation that eliminates the need for any baseline normalization altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence from a reference policy, in comparison to popular preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF challenges, including tasks like summarization and dialogue. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or outperforms robust baselines such as RLHF with PPO and also surpasses the best among $N$ sampled trajectories evaluated under a learned reward model. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experiments, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, the input $x$ corresponds to the beginning of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ exhibiting a positive sentiment. To facilitate a controlled evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs by generating multiple outputs and using a pre-trained sentiment classifier to select pairs where $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence on the training portion of the IMDb review dataset (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the policy is required to generate a summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. Consistent with prior research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for RLHF \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The human preference dataset was sourced by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from a distinct, yet similarly fine-tuned, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query that can vary from scientific questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. The policy's objective is to generate a response $y$ that is both helpful and engaging. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two alternative responses produced by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the human-preferred reply. Since there is no pre-trained SFT model available for this task, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective, within the controlled sentiment generation scenario we measure each algorithm by its achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy, forming a frontier curve; this is feasible because the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in real-world contexts where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 serves as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response within the test data. Although prior work indicates that language models can surpass traditional automated metrics as evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study (detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}) to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation. Our findings show that GPT-4’s assessments closely align with human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding the level of agreement observed between human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess various established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Moreover, we test the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned through supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$—this selection comes either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization scenarios) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach based on pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional weight $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood component. Additionally, we explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained with a reward function derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that learns from the true reward function provided in the controlled sentiment context. For sentiment-related experiments, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and an adapted version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (these enhancements are also applied when using regular PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ completions from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top response according to a reward model trained on the preference dataset. While this approach effectively separates the reward model’s quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires generating $N$ outputs for each query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left panel:} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on the Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue dataset; among the methods tested, only DPO shows an improvement compared to the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right panel:} Win rates tracked across various sampling temperatures during training. The advantage of DPO over the dataset labels remains consistently stable throughout the training process across different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF methods aims to balance maximizing reward with limiting how much the policy diverges from a reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; securing a marginally higher reward at the cost of substantially increased KL divergence may not be advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier across several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we perform multiple training experiments, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameters in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess the policies on a set of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO establishes the most effective frontier, achieving the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency; its reward versus KL trade-off clearly surpasses that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO has access to true reward signals (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO based on human preferences leads to more accurate summaries. Our evaluation compares various approaches by generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate relative to reference completions from the test set. For all methods, completions are sampled at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings show that DPO attains a win rate close to 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which reaches about 57\% at its best temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO secures a superior peak win rate compared to the best $N$ baseline method. It is worth noting that we performed minimal tuning of DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting these results might undervalue DPO’s full potential. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across different sampling temperatures than PPO, whose performance can fall back to that of the base GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the SFT baseline. A direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were preferred 58\% of the time over PPO outputs at temperature 0.

In the context of single-turn dialogues, we assess various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset's test split \citep{bai2022training}, specifically involving a single human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, the preferred completions from the test set serve as the benchmark to calculate the win rate across different techniques. Due to the absence of a standardized SFT model for this task, we initiate training with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We apply Preferred-FT to develop a reference model based on the selected completions, ensuring these completions align well with the model's distribution, followed by training with DPO. Additionally, we compare results against the top-performing completion among 128 Preferred-FT outputs (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or exceeds performance at optimal temperatures for each method. We also test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, yet were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature yielding improvements over the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering results from TL;DR and recognizing that both techniques optimize the identical reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that surpasses the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, offering comparable or superior outcomes relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 benchmark. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO achieves near-optimal performance within a relatively short training duration.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply existing RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct connection between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized to meet human preferences \textit{directly} via a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preference signals.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several key questions that warrant further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize in a manner comparable to PPO-based approaches, but a more thorough analysis is necessary. For instance, can self-labeling techniques using the DPO policy similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively? Another aspect to explore is how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right exemplifies this phenomenon. Furthermore, although our experiments involve models up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to scale with state-of-the-art models that are significantly larger represents a promising avenue for future work. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; subsequent studies could focus on optimizing methods for eliciting reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO holds potential beyond language model training from human preferences, such as in training generative models across various other data modalities.
