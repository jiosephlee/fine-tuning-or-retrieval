\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning abilities, precisely directing their outputs remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches to achieve such control rely on gathering human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the LM to better align with these preferences, commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is often a complicated and unstable process, involving initially training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM to maximize this learned reward while preventing excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this work, we exploit a connection between reward functions and optimal policies to demonstrate that this constrained reward maximization can be \emph{precisely optimized} via a single stage of policy training, effectively reframing the task as a classification problem over the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits the direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby reducing the standard RLHF problem to minimizing a straightforward classification loss.}  
Our method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency, removing the necessity of \rev{training a reward model,}{} performing LM sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results indicate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as well or better than established techniques. Importantly, fine-tuning with DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and matches or surpasses it in quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, while being significantly simpler to implement and train.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on vast corpora, demonstrate remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data, which encompasses diverse intentions, priorities, and skill levels. Some of these intentions and skills may not be desirable to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, when producing code, we want to steer the model toward the (potentially uncommon) high-quality coding examples found in its training set. Likewise, we may want our language model to \textit{know about} a common misconception held by half the population, yet we definitely do not want the model to assert that misconception as true in half of the responses related to it. Put differently, carefully choosing the model's \emph{preferred outputs and behaviors} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for developing AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although existing approaches generally guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL objective employed by these methods can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which significantly streamlines the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches teach language models preferred behaviors by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that capture the kinds of responses deemed safe and useful by people. This preference learning phase takes place following an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training step on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective methods employ reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on datasets of human preference judgments, and reinforcement learning is then used to adjust the language model’s policy so that it generates outputs with high reward scores while maintaining proximity to the original model’s behavior. Despite RLHF’s ability to yield models with strong conversational and programming skills, its pipeline is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly maximizes the same objective as traditional RLHF techniques (reward maximization constrained by KL-divergence) while being easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of favored responses compared to disfavored ones, incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid model collapse observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how well a reward function fits observed preference data. Unlike existing approaches that utilize this preference model to define a loss for training a reward model followed by a policy trained to optimize that model, {\methodac} applies a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Given a dataset of human preference comparisons over model outputs, {\methodac} optimizes the policy via a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring an explicit, standalone reward model or policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data}.

The primary contribution of our work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preference data. Our experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, in preference learning tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, as they grow larger, have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user intentions can be substantially enhanced by fine-tuning them on datasets consisting of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to better generalize to instructions beyond those seen during fine-tuning and generally improves their usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality is often simpler than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later studies have fine-tuned LLMs using datasets of human preferences, leading to improved performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve first training a neural network reward model to align with the preference dataset under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by fine-tuning the language model to optimize this reward through reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction utilizes LLMs that have been fine-tuned with human feedback on instruction-following to produce additional synthetic preference data targeting specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision via textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These strategies unify two research domains: one focused on reinforcement learning for training language models across diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models continues to pose significant practical challenges; in response, this work proposes a theoretically-grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on reinforcement learning.

Beyond the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with numerous methodologies proposed. When contextual bandit learning relies on preferences or rankings over actions instead of explicit rewards, it is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute reward values, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected winning probability against \textit{any} competing policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, in CDB scenarios, preference feedback is received in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences typically involves training on a fixed, offline dataset of action pairs annotated with preferences \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences generated by an \textit{unknown} underlying ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been developed, some of which can leverage off-policy preference data, but these generally require first estimating the latent scoring function (i.e., the reward model) explicitly before optimizing the policy \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our approach introduces a single-stage policy learning method that directly maximizes the policy’s alignment with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and expanded upon in subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally consists of three steps: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning (RL) for optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated datasets relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: During this second stage, the SFT model is queried with prompts $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference for one response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored answer and $y_l$ the less favored one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preferences are considered to arise from an underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown to us. Various methods exist to capture such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being widely adopted (although more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also fit within this framework when multiple ranked responses are available). According to the BT model, the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given $x$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Provided we have a fixed dataset of comparison examples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, we can define a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. When cast as a binary classification problem, the associated negative log-likelihood loss is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model applications, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented with an additional linear layer placed atop the last transformer block to output a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce the variance of the reward estimates, prior research often normalizes the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been learned is employed to guide the language model through feedback. Building on previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ acts as a hyperparameter that regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuning model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model is initialized to match $\pisft$. This regularization term plays a crucial role in restricting the policy from straying excessively from the distribution on which the reward model is reliable, while also preserving output variety and avoiding collapse into repetitive high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, this objective cannot be directly differentiated and is therefore optimized using reinforcement learning methods. The conventional technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and then maximizing it via the PPO algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preference data directly. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the need for an intermediate reward modeling phase and directly trains a language model with preference information}{employs a specific reward model parameterization that permits deriving the optimal policy analytically, thus removing the necessity of an RL training loop}.  
As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship that maps reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, allowing us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables bypassing the explicit modeling of rewards}{circumvents the requirement to fit a separate, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing in accordance with established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as in earlier studies, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined with a general reward function $r$. Building on previous research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the solution maximizing the KL-constrained reward in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ represents the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the MLE approximation $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, complicating practical usage of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Fortunately, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, expressed as ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing us to represent the human preference probability purely via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ consistent with the Bradley-Terry model adheres to the preference formulation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The complete proof is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry framework, analogous derivations for broader Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are available in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data directly through the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to omit the explicit step of reward modeling and also eliminates the need for reinforcement learning optimization.}{By doing so, we effectively learn an implicit reward through a different parameterization where the optimal policy is directly represented by $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it possesses desirable theoretical guarantees, such as consistency given appropriate assumptions about the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. We expand on these theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related works in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ encourages increasing the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering that of less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training instance is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly favors the less preferred completion, modulated by $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting, as omitting the weighting factor can lead to model collapse (refer to Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate these with human preference labels to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and chosen hyperparameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage existing public preference datasets instead of producing new samples and collecting human judgments. Because these datasets are typically generated based on $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a policy is accessible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, i.e., by solving ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This step helps reduce the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer an expanded explanation of the DPO approach, present formal theoretical support, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered in actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model Under the Surface}  
DPO manages to avoid both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by training the policy solely through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. In this framework, we optimize our parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$ in a manner equivalent to optimizing the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once variables are appropriately transformed. This section develops the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrating that it imposes no restrictions on the set of reward models that can be learned and enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for a certain function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate an identical optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a known under-specification problem associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is often necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to guarantee meaningful maximum likelihood estimates for the reward model defined in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma emphasizes that all reward functions within a given equivalence class correspond to the same optimal policy. Consequently, our ultimate goal reduces to recovering any representative reward function from that class. The following theorem, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this idea:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (specifically the Bradley-Terry variant) admits a representation through the reparameterization 
    \[
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)},
    \]
    where $\pi(y \mid x)$ is some model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference distribution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$ which determines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to demonstrate that a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the above reparameterization. Define the mapping $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the original reward function by subtracting the log-partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization depends solely on the prefix $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function), we obtain 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}.
\]
Hence, the projection $f$ yields a reward function of the desired form inside the equivalence class of $r$, ensuring that the proposed reparameterization does not restrict the generality of our reward model.
\end{sproof}
Another perspective on Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function from each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses. Specifically, it picks the reward function satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y \mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution with positive values summing to one. Referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The essential insight behind the DPO algorithm is that by applying certain constraints to the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce (especially Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, we retain the full class of representable reward functions while simultaneously ensuring that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically manageable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also help identify instability problems in common actor-critic methods applied in RLHF, like PPO. Following the RLHF procedure, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach links to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} on the constrained RL formulation presented in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} derived from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this corresponds to the objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches that used in earlier research 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} which employ the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit high variance, thereby destabilizing training. One way to incorporate this normalization is via a learned value function, but optimizing such a value function can be challenging. Alternatively, previous studies have normalized rewards by subtracting a baseline derived from human completions, effectively using a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate for the normalization. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward formulation that eliminates the need for any baseline adjustment.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to directly train policies based on preferences. Initially, within a carefully controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence from the reference policy, in comparison to popular preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF challenges, such as summarization and dialogue tasks. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks like RLHF using PPO and also outperforms selecting the best trajectory from $N$ samples evaluated by a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted by $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ corresponds to a partial movie review excerpted from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce a continuation $y$ that expresses a positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, preference pairs for this task are \textit{generated} by employing a pre-trained sentiment classifier that satisfies $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we train GPT-2-large to convergence on the training portion of the IMDb dataset (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy must output a summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. Consistent with prior research, we leverage the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for RLHF \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The human preference annotations were gathered by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples generated from a different, yet similarly fine-tuned, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} setting, $x$ represents a user query, which may range from scientific inquiries about astrophysics to requests for personal advice. The policy’s task is to generate a response $y$ that is both engaging and informative. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k human-assistant interactions. Each dialogue concludes with two alternative responses produced by a large (though undisclosed) language model, accompanied by a human preference label indicating the favored reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available here, we fine-tune an existing language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective within the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm’s performance by plotting the trade-off between the achieved reward and the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this trade-off curve can be derived because we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgments regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test dataset. Although prior work indicates that language models may serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we also perform a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4’s assessments and human evaluations, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding the agreement levels among human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. To begin with, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task, alongside 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. Furthermore, we examine the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is obtained by fine-tuning through supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ drawn either from the SFT model (in tasks involving controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a generic language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another approach based on pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. We also incorporate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which uses a reward function inferred from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle model that learns from the exact ground truth reward function available in the controlled sentiment domain. For sentiment tasks, we deploy two variants of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and an enhanced version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost performance (these adjustments are likewise applied when running the standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top-scoring response based on a reward model trained on preference data. Although this technique achieves strong results by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires producing $N$ completions per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 for the Anthropic-HH single-step dialogue; among the methods, only DPO shows improvement over the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test dataset. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout the training process. DPO consistently outperforms the original dataset labels across different sampling temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to optimize reward while limiting the policy's divergence from a reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward attained and the extent of KL divergence; a marginal increase in reward accompanied by a significantly larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier across various algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training trials, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameters in each run (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta$ values of $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha$ values of $\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, along with different random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is assessed on a set of test prompts by calculating the mean reward according to the true reward function, as well as the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the sum of the KL-divergences computed at each timestep.} relative to the reference policy, denoted $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes the most efficient frontier, attaining the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is significant for several reasons. First, although both DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, with its reward-KL balance clearly outperforming PPO. Second, DPO surpasses PPO even when PPO has direct access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing how well DPO fine-tunes models for tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics such as ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous research has demonstrated that applying PPO fine-tuning based on human preferences can lead to more useful summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions from the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate when compared against reference completions. Samples for all methods are drawn using temperature values ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models fine-tuned by DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all originate from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results indicate that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s best score of around 57\% at its optimal temperature, also 0.0. Additionally, DPO surpasses the highest win rate achieved by the best-performing $N$ baseline method. It is worth mentioning that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, so these outcomes might underrepresent its full capabilities. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness diminishes to the level of the base GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, shows minimal improvement over the original SFT model. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO based on human judgments is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO-generated samples at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

For the single-turn dialogue task, we assess various approaches on a portion of the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training}, specifically involving a single human-assistant exchange. To determine win rates for each method, GPT-4 evaluations rely on the preferred completions from the test set as the benchmark. Since there is no established supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model for this task, we begin with a pretrained Pythia-2.8B model. We apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model using the selected preferred completions, ensuring the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and subsequently train the model using DPO. Comparisons are also made against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this experiment; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or exceeds performance for the optimal temperature settings across methods. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify a prompt or temperature configuration that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward signal, we treat the Best of 128 benchmark as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO emerges as the sole computationally efficient technique that surpasses the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance fairly rapidly.

In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally asks which summary is more succinct; this prompt was tested because we observed that with the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries than human raters typically do. The full prompt texts can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We compare three methods representing different quality levels: the highest-performing (DPO, temperature 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and a mid-level approach (SFT, temperature 0.25). Each is evaluated against greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Our findings indicate that GPT-4’s judgments, across both prompts, align with human opinions about as frequently as humans agree among themselves, implying that GPT-4 serves as a viable stand-in for human evaluation. Due to the limited number of human annotators, multiple human ratings were only gathered for comparisons involving DPO and PPO-1. Generally, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that more closely match human preferences, and so it is employed for the primary analyses in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. \section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach to developing effective and aligned language models. We presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that leverages preference data to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct connection between language model policies and reward functions. This allows for training language models to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current RLHF methods, including those employing PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions that warrant further investigation. In particular, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based methods, though more extensive research is required. For instance, can leveraging self-labeling from the DPO policy similarly enable effective utilization of unlabeled prompts? Another aspect to consider is how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right represents such a phenomenon. Moreover, while our evaluations cover models with up to 6 billion parameters, investigating the scalability of DPO to much larger, cutting-edge models remains a promising avenue. Concerning evaluation protocols, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; hence, future studies might explore optimal methods to extract reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO has potential applications beyond tuning language models using human preferences, such as training generative models across various other modalities.
