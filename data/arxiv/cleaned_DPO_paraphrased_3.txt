\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning capabilities, it remains challenging to precisely direct their output due to the entirely unsupervised training process. Current approaches to achieve such steerability involve collecting human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable method that requires first training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of the large LM to maximize the predicted reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{In this paper we introduce a new parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that enables extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing us to solve the standard RLHF problem with only a simple classification loss.}  
Our proposed method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} generating samples from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than current techniques. In particular, DPO-based fine-tuning outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or surpasses it in response quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on massive datasets, develop remarkable capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects a diverse range of aims, priorities, and expertise. Not all of these aims or skillsets are desirable to replicate; for instance, although we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we still prefer to encourage the model to produce code that exhibits the (often uncommon) high-quality programming skills found in the training examples. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{aware} of a prevalent misconception held by half of the population, but it should definitely avoid endorsing that misconception in 50\% of related responses. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and competencies} is essential for developing AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While prevailing approaches generally guide LMs toward human-aligned preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-driven objective commonly employed can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning framework.

At a broad level, current approaches embed the targeted behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences, which reflect the kinds of behaviors considered safe and beneficial by humans. This phase of learning preferences follows an initial phase of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest technique for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective category of methods is reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then applied to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward scores, while preventing significant deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF enabling models to achieve strong conversational and coding performance, its training pipeline is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring multiple language models to be trained and involving sampling from the language model’s policy within the training loop, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly maximizes the same goal as current RLHF techniques (reward maximization subject to a KL-divergence constraint), while being easier to implement and more straightforward to train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log probability of favored responses compared to disfavored ones, integrating a dynamic, per-sample importance weighting that avoids the model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates the alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. Yet, whereas existing approaches utilize this preference model to construct a loss for training a reward function and subsequently optimize a policy on that reward, DPO applies a variable transformation to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy itself. Given a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, DPO is thus able to optimize the policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring the learning of an explicit, separate reward model or performing policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably to established techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, when scaled up, can perform certain tasks without any examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few examples provided as prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals is greatly enhanced by fine-tuning them on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the specific instructions seen during fine-tuning and generally improves their practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has shown strong results, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality is often simpler than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, boosting performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with the human preference dataset under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, then apply reinforcement learning methods—commonly REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}—to fine-tune the language model to maximize the learned reward. A related research direction utilizes LLMs already fine-tuned with human feedback for instruction following to create synthetic preference datasets aimed at specific traits such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics to guide the LLM's annotations. These strategies represent a merging of two research areas: one focusing on reinforcement learning for language model training across diverse objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general approaches for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of using relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a substantial practical hurdle; this paper proposes a theoretically sound method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond linguistic applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit frameworks and reinforcement learning paradigms, with multiple methods proposed. When preferences or rankings over actions replace explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setup is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In situations lacking absolute reward signals, theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the idea of an optimal policy with the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves at least a 50\% expected win rate against \textit{any} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, in the CDB framework, preference feedback is provided in an online fashion, whereas learning from human preferences generally relies on a fixed offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates by learning from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A variety of PbRL algorithms have been developed, many of which can utilize off-policy preference information, but typically involve an initial step of explicitly estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) followed by optimization of the policy based on this estimate \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified, single-stage policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We provide an overview of the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally consists of three main steps: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts with fine-tuning an already pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated datasets tailored to the target downstream tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, and others), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference between them, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored completion and $y_l$ the less favored one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to model such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly adopted approach (though the framework also accommodates broader Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} when multiple ranked outputs are available). According to the BT model, the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given $x$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\big(r^*(x, y_1)\big)}{\exp\big(r^*(x, y_1)\big) + \exp\big(r^*(x, y_2)\big)}.
\end{equation}
Provided we have access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be learned by maximizing the likelihood. Casting this as a binary classification task yields the negative log-likelihood objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \big[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)) \big]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model settings, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the last transformer layer to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous works normalize the reward values to satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ r_{\phi}(x,y) \right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the previously trained reward function serves as feedback for the language model. Building on earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates the extent to which the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial because it restricts the model from straying too far from the distribution where the reward function remains reliable, helps preserve diversity in generated outputs, and avoids collapsing to a narrow set of highly rewarded responses. Since language generation involves discrete outputs, this objective is non-differentiable and is generally optimized via reinforcement learning methods. The common strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using the PPO algorithm \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that uses preference data directly. In contrast to earlier RLHF techniques, which first estimate a reward function and then optimize it through RL, our method \rev{eliminates the need for reward modeling altogether and directly optimizes the language model based on preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that permits closed-form extraction of the optimal policy, removing the necessity for an RL training loop}.  

As detailed in the following sections, the central idea is to utilize an analytical relationship that maps reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to bypass the explicit reward modeling phase}{dispenses with training an explicit, separate reward model}, while still working within established frameworks for human preference modeling, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective utilized in previous studies, as given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined with a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution optimizing the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant, or partition function, is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing an MLE-based approximation $r_{\phi}$ of the true reward function $r^*$, accurately estimating $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical usage of this formulation. Nonetheless, rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} allows us to rewrite the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and applying algebraic manipulation, we derive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Conveniently, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends solely on the difference between rewards of two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} leads to cancellation of the partition function terms, enabling the human preference probabilities to be represented purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The detailed proof is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the probability of human preference data through the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach enables us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward while also eliminating the requirement for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In this manner, we implicitly fit a reward through an alternative parameterization where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since our method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits certain theoretical guarantees, such as consistency given appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We provide a more detailed discussion of the theoretical aspects of DPO compared to related methods in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ defines the reward implicitly through the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, this gradient pushes the model to increase the probability of the favored outputs $y_w$ while lowering that of the less favored outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each sample is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly ranks the less preferred completions relative to the preferred ones, modulated by $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL regularization. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as a simplified variant without it may lead the language model to collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves two main steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then collect human preference labels to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ based on the given $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and obtaining fresh human annotations. Since such datasets are usually derived from samples of $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever this is accessible. When $\pisft$ is not at hand, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, specifically, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy helps reduce the mismatch between the true, but unknown, reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed within DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we delve deeper into the DPO approach, offering a theoretical foundation and connecting the benefits of DPO to the challenges faced by actor-critic algorithms commonly applied in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO manages to circumvent both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the process of explicitly fitting a reward function} and the use of reinforcement learning by optimizing the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our goal is to optimize the parametric policy model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is effectively equivalent to optimizing the reward model as described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} once variables are suitably transformed. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the set of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the precise retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation defined over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation satisfies the properties of an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated as follows:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the identical optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-known issue of under-specification inherent to the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to obtain reliable guarantees for the MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma confirms that all reward functions within the same equivalence class produce the same optimal policy. Consequently, when optimizing our objective, it suffices to recover any single reward function from this optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry case) admits a representation through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a specified reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, which induces an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ effectively normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization depends only on the prefix $x$, the transformed reward $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the expression on the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally), we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields a representative of $r$’s equivalence class in the desired form, confirming that our proposed reparameterization does not restrict the generality of the reward model.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization, specifically the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, via Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (with non-negative values summing to unity).
Moreover, referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds exactly to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$.
The principal insight underlying the DPO algorithm is that by imposing particular constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce family (and Bradley-Terry in particular) of preference models, we maintain the expressiveness of the reward class while ensuring that the optimal policy given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically solvable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Unstable Behavior in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our analytical framework can also be applied to identify instability issues inherent in conventional actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the reinforcement learning fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This analysis relates to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}. Consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, where the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \parallel \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, with $\pi^*$ representing the optimal policy derived in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} under the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this translates to the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]
\end{equation}  
This objective matches those optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that employ the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Within this context, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it can lead to high variance in the policy gradient estimates, thereby causing unstable learning dynamics. One can model the normalization term through a learned value function; however, optimizing this can be challenging. Alternatively, some previous approaches normalize rewards by subtracting a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of this normalization constant. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization constructs a reward function that eliminates the need for such baseline corrections.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capacity to train policies directly using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward and minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, especially when compared to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that with minimal hyperparameter adjustments, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as methods that select the best out of $N$ trajectories sampled according to a learned reward function. Prior to discussing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the methods learn a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a partial movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the model is required to produce an output $y$ expressing positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation environment, we \textit{create} preference pairs by using a pre-trained sentiment classifier that assigns a higher probability to the positive completion: $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on training set reviews from IMDb (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the objective is to generate a summary $y$ that captures the key points of the post. Following previous work, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The preference data was obtained from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples generated by a different but comparably trained SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ represents a user query, which can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship advice. The model must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170,000 exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two model-generated responses and a label indicating the human-preferred reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model is available for this setting, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our study employs two distinct evaluation methodologies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective within a controlled sentiment generation environment, we measure the performance of each algorithm by its trade-off curve between obtained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy. This curve can be computed since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons use the reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response recorded in the test dataset. Although prior research indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments} to validate our reliance on GPT-4. Our findings demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4 assessments and human judgments, with the level of agreement between humans and GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding that found between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple existing strategies for training language models to align with human preferences. As a straightforward approach, we test zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Additionally, we examine the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$—sourced either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised technique we investigate is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$, optionally weighted by a coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood term. We further explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} optimized with a reward function learned from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant trained using the ground truth reward available in the controlled sentiment setup. For sentiment experiments, we implement two PPO-GT versions: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to boost results (these modifications are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest-scoring output based on a reward function trained on preference data. While this approach effectively separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive even for moderate values of $N$, since it requires generating $N$ outputs per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for the Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue task; among the methods tested, only DPO shows improvement over the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels across different sampling temperatures during the entire training process.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance the maximization of reward with limiting the policy’s divergence from the reference policy. Hence, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially greater KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training trials, varying the hyperparameter that controls policy conservativeness in each run (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). In total, this hyperparameter sweep consists of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, we evaluate each resulting policy on a set of test prompts, calculating the mean reward based on the true reward function as well as the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, denoted by $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO yields the most effective frontier, delivering the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates considerably greater efficiency, with its reward/KL balance clearly surpassing that of PPO. Second, DPO outperforms PPO even in scenarios where PPO has access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO scalable to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
In this section, we assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous research has demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO guided by human preferences results in more effective summaries. We compare various approaches by generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset, calculating the average win rate relative to reference completions in the test set. Completions from all methods are sampled across temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all fine-tuned versions of the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate close to 61\%, outperforming PPO’s best performance of approximately 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher peak win rate compared to the strongest $N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, suggesting these findings might underrepresent its full capabilities. Furthermore, DPO exhibits greater stability across sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can decline to the level of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the SFT baseline. A direct human evaluation comparison between DPO and PPO is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

In the single-turn dialogue setting, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on interactions involving one human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we measure the win rate of different methods by referencing the preferred completions from the test set. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions to ensure they align with the model’s distribution, and subsequently perform training with DPO. We also benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses the best performance across optimal temperature settings for each method. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a recognized repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering our TL;DR results and the shared reward function between these methods, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that enhances preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset while achieving comparable or superior outcomes relative to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Corroborating GPT-4 evaluations with human assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}

The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt adds an additional criterion, asking which summary is more succinct; this prompt was introduced because we observed that, under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more redundant summaries than humans typically prefer. \section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach to develop capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized to meet human preferences \textit{directly} through a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and maintaining generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance relative to existing RLHF methods, including those utilizing PPO; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions for subsequent research. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based methods, though a more thorough investigation is necessary. For instance, can self-labeling using the DPO policy be leveraged effectively to utilize unlabeled prompts? Another aspect to consider is how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the slight performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is a manifestation of this phenomenon. Furthermore, although our experiments include models with up to 6 billion parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models that are significantly larger remains an exciting avenue for future exploration. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt wording; thus, future studies might focus on identifying the best strategies to obtain reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s potential applications extend well beyond training language models on human preferences, encompassing generative modeling across various other modalities.
