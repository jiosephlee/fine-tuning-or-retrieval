\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, exerting fine-grained control over their output remains challenging due to the entirely unsupervised way they are trained. Current approaches to improving model steerability involve collecting human annotations indicating the relative quality of generated outputs and then fine-tuning the original LM to prefer these choices, typically using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Yet, RLHF is a complicated and sometimes unstable approach that requires first training a reward model based on human preference data, and then applying reinforcement learning to adjust the large unsupervised LM so as to maximize this learned reward without significantly deviating from the original model's behavior. In this work, we propose a novel parameterization for the reward model within RLHF, enabling closed-form extraction of the optimal policy, which reduces the RLHF procedure to a straightforward classification loss. This method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and lightweight in computation, removing the necessity for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or for extensive hyperparameter adjustment. Our results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match or surpass human preference alignment achieved by alternative approaches. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF for controlling the sentiment of outputs, and achieves equal or better quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being significantly easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), when trained on massive corpora, display unexpected strengths~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models are exposed to data produced by humans with diverse intentions, backgrounds, and expertise. Not all these intents or skills are desirable to emulate; for instance, we might want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors so it can correct them, but during code generation, we would prefer that the model emulate only the (sometimes infrequent) expert-level coding found in the training set. In the same way, while it is useful for a language model to be \textit{informed} about widespread misconceptions—such as those believed by half the population—we certainly do not want it to endorse these falsehoods half of the time when asked. Put differently, distinguishing and prioritizing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its extensive range of \textit{information and capabilities} is essential for developing AI systems that are reliable, high-performing, and manageable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. While current approaches generally align LMs to human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed in such methods can be precisely minimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference optimization process.

In general, current approaches encourage language models to adopt desirable behaviors by leveraging carefully selected datasets reflecting human preferences for what is considered safe and useful conduct. This phase of learning user preferences follows an initial period of large-scale, unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the most direct method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using high-quality human-generated demonstrations, the most effective techniques rely on reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques train a reward model on annotated human preference data, subsequently utilizing RL to adjust the language model’s output policy to favor responses with higher reward scores, while maintaining proximity to the original pre-trained model. Despite RLHF’s ability to produce models excelling at dialogue and programming tasks, the RLHF process is substantially more intricate than simple supervised learning, as it demands training several language models and repeatedly sampling from the model policy during training, resulting in high computational overhead.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for training a language model to align with human preferences directly, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same objective as traditional RLHF methods—maximizing reward with a KL-divergence regularization—but with a more straightforward implementation and training process. Essentially, DPO updates the model by boosting the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over less-preferred ones, while utilizing an adaptive, example-specific importance weighting that mitigates the model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio approach. As with prior methods, DPO employs a theoretical preference model, such as the Bradley-Terry model (\cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}), to quantify the agreement between a reward function and observed preference data. Unlike standard pipelines, which use the preference model to build a loss for training a reward model and then optimize a policy against that learned reward, DPO reformulates the preference loss directly in terms of the policy parameters by applying a variable substitution. With access to a dataset of human-labeled preferences among responses, DPO can thus train a policy using a simple binary cross entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy relative to an implicit reward function that best matches the preference annotations.

The core contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models using preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses established approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, for preference-driven learning tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and conversational modeling, with models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing scale have demonstrated the ability to tackle certain tasks in a zero-shot setting \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user expectations can be substantially enhanced through fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as 'instruction-tuning', empowers LLMs to generalize to unseen instructions beyond their training data, thereby making them more broadly useful \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction-tuning has been highly successful, acquiring \textit{relative} human feedback about the quality of different responses is often more feasible than gathering expert demonstrations, leading later research to use human preference datasets to fine-tune LLMs. This approach has advanced abilities in tasks like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction-following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these techniques first train a neural reward model to match preference data—often modeled by frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—before fine-tuning the language model to maximize the reward using reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related approaches \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another related research direction uses LLMs, already tuned for instruction-following with human feedback, to synthesize new preference data for specific qualities (e.g., safety or harmlessness) \citep{bai2022constitutional}, leveraging only light supervision from humans in the form of textual rubrics for labeling. These strategies bridge two main areas: one focused on applying reinforcement learning to train language models for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the other centered on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of using relative human feedback, fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning continues to pose significant practical obstacles; this work introduces a theoretically-grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Outside the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, resulting in a variety of proposed methods. In contextual bandits, utilizing preferences or rankings over actions instead of explicit rewards leads to the contextual dueling bandit (CDB) framework \citep{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}. When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the idea of an optimal policy with the \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB paradigm, preference feedback is provided in an online manner, while in learning from human preferences, it is more common to work with a static batch of offline action pairs labeled with preferences \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences that are produced by an \textit{unknown} scoring function, instead of from explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Numerous PbRL algorithms exist, some of which are able to leverage off-policy preference data, but most involve first learning an explicit model of the underlying scoring function (i.e., the reward function) and then optimizing the policy with respect to that estimate \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-step policy learning method that aims to directly optimize the policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF framework described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (as well as in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process typically consists of three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) gathering preference data and learning a reward model; and (3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by taking a pre-trained language model and further training it using supervised learning on curated, high-quality datasets tailored to the desired downstream applications (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are shown to human annotators, who indicate their preferred response, notated as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, with $y_w$ and $y_l$ referring to the chosen and non-chosen answers from $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these human preferences stem from an unknown underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Multiple methods exist to represent such preferences; the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model is widely used (though generalizations like the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} also fit within this framework for multi-answer rankings). The BT model formalizes the human preference probability $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, one can define a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Viewing this setup as a binary classification problem, the loss becomes the negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ represents the logistic sigmoid function. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with an added linear projection on top of the final transformer layer to yield a scalar reward output \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce the variance in the learned reward function, previous work often applies normalization, enforcing $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for each $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the reward function acquired in previous steps is utilized to supply guidance to the language model. Building on prior research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is framed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ governs how much the policy may diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized from $\pisft$. Including this constraint is crucial, as it ensures that the model remains close to the data distribution for which the reward model provides reliable evaluations, while also preserving diversity in outputs and avoiding collapse to only a few high-scoring responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, the above objective cannot be directly differentiated, so reinforcement learning methods are commonly used for optimization. The prevailing method~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and applies PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} to perform maximization.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of using reinforcement learning algorithms for large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that works directly with preferences. In contrast to traditional RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and then optimize it with RL, our method adopts a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to compute the optimal policy analytically, bypassing the need for iterative RL training. 

As we elaborate in the following sections, our central idea is to use a closed-form correspondence between reward functions and their associated optimal policies. This allows us to reframe a loss defined on reward functions as a loss directly over policies. 

This variable substitution strategy eliminates the necessity of training a separate reward model explicitly, while still making use of established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry model. Consequently, the policy network embodies both the language model and the underlying (implicit) reward function.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective used in previous research, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, parameterized by a general reward function $r$. Prior work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning} demonstrates that the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} admits the following optimal policy:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where the normalization constant is $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. The full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Computing the partition function $Z(x)$ is still computationally intensive, even when using the MLE approximation $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$ \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits the direct usability of this form. Nonetheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be rearranged to solve for the reward as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we find:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This transformation can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and the corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Conveniently, the Bradley-Terry model is defined using only the difference between rewards for a pair of completions, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the above reparameterization for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel, yielding an expression for the human preference probability that depends only on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Therefore, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ fulfills:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A detailed derivation appears in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived for the Bradley-Terry model, similar forms can be established for broader classes of models, such as the Plackett-Luce family~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now expressed the human preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
With this approach, we are effectively learning an implicit reward function through a different parameterization, where the corresponding optimal policy is directly $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this method is mathematically equivalent to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits theoretical guarantees like consistency given suitable assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} explores these theoretical aspects of DPO and compares them with related work.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, it is instructive to study the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))}_\text{larger effect if the reward assignment is mistaken}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
Here, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the implicit reward as computed by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more detail). In essence, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ pushes up the likelihood of the favorable responses $y_w$ while suppressing that of the unfavorable ones $y_l$. Crucially, each data point is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overvalues the less preferred completion, modulated by $\beta$—that is, the degree to which the reward model incorrectly ranks the choices, in light of the KL regularization strength. Our empirical findings highlight the necessity of this weighting; a simpler variant that omits this weighting term can lead to pathological model behavior (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$ and annotating them with human preferences to build an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the specified $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target $\beta$.  
In actual use, it is preferable to utilize publicly accessible preference datasets instead of generating new completions and collecting human labels. Because these datasets are generated with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we set $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy reduces the gap between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ actually employed by DPO. Additional details regarding implementation and hyperparameter choices are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and discuss how the benefits of DPO address limitations found in actor-critic techniques commonly employed for RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Actually a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO manages to avoid both direct reward modeling and the use of RL by instead training the policy with a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization goal given by Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is the same as that of a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We then optimize the parameters of our model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is functionally identical to the process of reward model optimization in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, once a change of variables is applied. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it places no restrictions on the family of possible reward models, and prove that it enables precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
Clearly, this relation is an equivalence relation and divides the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. We can present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce preference model, including the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the same class produce identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in identical optimal policies when solving the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

The arguments are straightforward, so we include the details in Appendix~\ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-known issue of non-identifiability in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, additional identifiability constraints are typically required to guarantee any properties of the MLE solutions for Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that any reward function from a particular equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy. Therefore, for our purposes, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from the optimal equivalence class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class compatible with Plackett-Luce (including the Bradley-Terry model) can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for a model $\pi(y \mid x)$ and a chosen reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be any reward function, and let $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ denote its associated optimal model, as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}. We show that there exists a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ that matches the above reparameterized structure. Define the projection operator $f$ as:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ adjusts the reward by subtracting the log partition function (dependent only on $x$), so $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Now, if we substitute $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, which is a valid representation for any reward function, we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, $f$ provides a specific member of the equivalence class in the desired form, ensuring the generality of our reward model is preserved under this reparameterization.
\end{sproof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can also be interpreted as identifying precisely which representative reward function within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization—namely, the reward function for which:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which implies that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a well-defined probability distribution (non-negative and sums to one).

Additionally, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function for the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. 

A central observation in the DPO algorithm is that by imposing suitable constraints on the otherwise under-specified Plackett-Luce (and in particular, Bradley-Terry) preference models, we can preserve the entire set of possible reward models, while ensuring the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically tractable for all input prompts $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instabilities}

Our framework can also be leveraged to analyze the instability issues present in standard actor-critic algorithms commonly utilized for RLHF, such as PPO. We follow the RLHF workflow, focusing specifically on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This connects naturally to the control as inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL task specified in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy derived from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} under the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, we arrive at the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches those optimized by previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that employ the DPO-equivalent reward for $r_{\phi}$. In this formulation, the normalization part of $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not impact the optimal policy itself, omitting it may lead to high variance in the policy gradient, resulting in unstable training dynamics. Addressing the normalization with a learned value function is possible but can introduce additional optimization challenges. Alternatively, earlier works have used a baseline derived from human completions, which serves as a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate for normalization. Unlike these approaches, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that entirely avoids the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how well DPO can train policies directly based on preference data. First, within a controlled text generation framework, we investigate: how effectively does DPO balance reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization with respect to the reference policy, as compared to established preference optimization methods such as PPO? Afterwards, we test DPO on larger model architectures and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as dialogue and summarization. Our results show that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO often matches or outperforms robust baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as surpasses approaches that return the best of $N$ sampled trajectories according to a learned reward model. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline the experimental design; further specifics can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all settings, the algorithms are trained to optimize a policy using a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ consists of a movie review prefix sampled from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal for the policy is to generate $y$ expressing positive sentiment. To facilitate systematic evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs by leveraging a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we further train GPT-2-large to convergence on the training portion of IMDB reviews (implementation specifics are in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the case of \textbf{summarization}, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is required to produce a summary $y$ capturing the core ideas of the post. In line with prior research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, in conjunction with human preference data from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Here, the SFT model is fine-tuned on human-created summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for RLHF. The preference annotations were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a related, comparably-trained SFT system. For the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a user's prompt, which can range from astrophysics inquiries to advice requests. The policy's objective is to generate a response $y$ that is helpful and engaging. We make use of the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which comprises 170k human–assistant conversations. Each interaction concludes with two model-generated candidate responses, paired with human preference labels indicating the favored answer. In this scenario, no dedicated SFT model exists a priori, so we fine-tune a generic language model only on the preferred responses to construct the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we use the controlled sentiment generation scenario, where we evaluate algorithms based on their achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy; we can compute this tradeoff because we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). In contrast, in practical applications where the ground-truth reward function is unavailable, we assess each algorithm by its \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluators of summary quality and single-turn dialogue helpfulness. For summarization, the reference summary in the test set serves as the baseline; for dialogue, the preferred response from the test data is used. Although previous research indicates that LMs may surpass traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we further support our use of GPT-4 by conducting a human study, described in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4’s evaluations are strongly aligned with human judgments, with human-GPT-4 agreement often matching or exceeding that between human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a range of established techniques for training language models to better align with human preferences. For baseline comparisons, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We also test the \textbf{SFT} model, along with \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is trained using supervised learning on the selected output $y_w$—sourced from the SFT model in sentiment control and summarization, or from a standard LM in single-turn dialogue. Additionally, we consider the pseudo-supervised \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural} approach, which seeks to boost the likelihood of $y_w$ and concurrently suppress the likelihood of $y_l$, incorporating an optional `unlikelihood' weight $\alpha\in[0,1]$. We further examine \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} with a reward function derived from preference data and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle trained using the ground-truth reward in the sentiment control scenario. For sentiment tasks, we employ both an out-of-the-box PPO-GT implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a custom variant that normalizes rewards and tunes hyperparameters for enhanced performance; these adjustments are similarly applied to standard PPO with learned rewards. Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} method, which generates $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and picks the response with the highest reward per a learned preference model. While this approach separates reward model quality from PPO policy optimization and achieves strong results, it is computationally expensive—even for moderate $N$—since it necessitates generating $N$ completions per input during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates assessed by GPT-4 on Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among all methods, only DPO shows an improvement over the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates plotted for various sampling temperatures throughout the training process. The advantage of DPO over the ground-truth labels remains relatively consistent during training across different temperature settings.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF approaches seeks to maximize reward while preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. As a result, when evaluating different algorithms, it is essential to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a significantly greater KL is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward versus KL tradeoff curve for several methods in the sentiment task. For each algorithm, we conduct several training runs, each with a different setting for policy conservativeness (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and varying random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in 22 total runs. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, we evaluate the policies using a suite of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward under the actual reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Namely, the total KL-divergence across all time steps in a sequence.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results demonstrate that DPO establishes the most favorable tradeoff curve by a substantial margin, securing the highest rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This is noteworthy for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO are both optimizing the same objective, DPO is significantly more efficient; the reward/KL tradeoff achieved by DPO is superior to that of PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO in frontier quality, \emph{even when PPO is provided with access to true rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We now assess DPO’s fine-tuning capabilities on tasks including summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, standard automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak correlation with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous research has shown that fine-tuning language models with PPO on human preference data can produce summaries that better align with what people prefer. To compare approaches, we sample outputs from the test partition of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the average win rate relative to the test set references. For all approaches, completions are sampled with temperatures from 0.0 up to 1.0, and the resulting win rates are displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all begin with the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} for fine-tuning. At a sampling temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate close to 61\%, outperforming PPO’s peak win rate of around 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher top win rate than the best of $N$ baseline method. It's worth mentioning that we did not carefully adjust DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, implying that these results may not fully reflect DPO’s possible performance. Furthermore, DPO exhibits much greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can drop to the level of the base GPT-J model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT, meanwhile, does not produce meaningful gains over the initial SFT model. A direct comparison of DPO and PPO in human studies (Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}) indicates that DPO samples at a temperature of 0.25 were preferred by human raters 58\% of the time over PPO samples at a temperature of 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess various approaches using the subset of the test split from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} that features a single round of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 assessments, we use the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate win rates across the methods. Since there is no commonly accepted SFT model for this setting, we initialize with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, fine-tune a reference model via Preferred-FT on the selected completions to ensure they align with the model’s data distribution, and subsequently apply DPO training. We further compare results to the strongest among 128 Preferred-FT completions (as the Best of $N$ metric reaches a plateau at 128 for this scenario; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO matches or outperforms alternatives at their optimal temperature settings. An RLHF model trained using PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a notable implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, was also evaluated, but no prompt or sampling temperature led to results surpassing the base Pythia-2.8B. Drawing from our findings on TL;DR and the shared reward function of both approaches, we use Best of 128 as a rough stand-in for PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO emerges as the only computationally efficient technique to exceed the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering results on par with or better than the resource-heavy Best of 128 baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} also demonstrates that DPO reaches peak performance in relatively few training steps.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences represents an effective and scalable approach for developing competent and aligned language models. In this work, we have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without utilizing reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to leverage existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This mapping allows language models to be trained to adhere to human preferences \textit{directly}, using a simple cross-entropy loss, thus eliminating the need for reinforcement learning while maintaining generality. DPO matches or outperforms current RLHF methods—including those leveraging PPO—with minimal hyperparameter tuning, substantially lowering the difficulty of training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings prompt several key avenues for future exploration. One open question is how the DPO policy performs on out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on explicitly defined reward functions. Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies generalize in a manner similar to models trained with PPO, though a more detailed analysis is warranted. Additionally, it remains to be seen whether self-labeling with the DPO policy can leverage unlabeled prompts as effectively as other approaches. Another topic of interest is the nature of reward over-optimization in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the minor drop in results seen in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is a consequence of this issue. Furthermore, although our evaluation is limited to models up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models represents an intriguing opportunity for future research. In terms of evaluation, our experiments show that GPT-4’s win rates are sensitive to the choice of prompt; subsequent work could investigate optimal prompting strategies to obtain robust automated assessments. Lastly, DPO has potential uses that extend beyond aligning language models with human preferences, such as training generative models in other domains.
