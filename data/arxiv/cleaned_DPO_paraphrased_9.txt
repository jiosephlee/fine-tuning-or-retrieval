\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning abilities, yet precisely steering their behavior remains challenging due to their fully unsupervised training regime. Current approaches to enhance steerability involve gathering human annotations that rank the quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better align with these human preferences, commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process that first requires training a reward model to capture human judgments, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the LM to maximize this learned reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. \rev{In this work, we utilize the connection between reward functions and their optimal policies to demonstrate that this constrained reward maximization can be \emph{exactly optimized} in a single stage of policy training, effectively transforming the problem into a classification task over human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that permits directly deriving the optimal policy in closed form, thereby enabling the standard RLHF objective to be solved through a straightforward classification loss.} The proposed method, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, efficient, and computationally light, removing the necessity for \rev{training a separate reward model,}{} conducting LM sampling during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter search. Our empirical evaluations indicate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences at least as effectively as prior methods. Remarkably, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves the quality of outputs in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from data created by humans who have diverse aims, priorities, and expertise. Not all of these objectives or skills are necessarily beneficial to replicate; for instance, while it is useful for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we prefer to steer the model towards generating code that reflects the (sometimes uncommon) high-quality programming found within its training corpus. Likewise, a language model might be expected to \textit{know} about a widespread misconception held by roughly 50\% of people, yet we do not want it to assert this misconception as true in half of the responses related to it. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its extensive \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are reliable, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches often guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective used can be precisely optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current techniques embed the preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect the behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This preference acquisition phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training stage conducted on an extensive text corpus. Although the simplest strategy for learning preferences involves supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations showcasing high-quality responses, the most effective methods are based on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model using a dataset of human preferences and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy, encouraging outputs with high rewards while maintaining closeness to the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding skills, its workflow is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and continuous sampling from the LM policy during training, which results in considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly train a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward functions or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly maximizes the same goal as traditional RLHF methods (i.e., reward maximization under a KL-divergence constraint) while remaining easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log likelihood of preferred responses compared to less favored ones, employing a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid the model collapse issues observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that evaluates how well a reward function corresponds to observed preference data. Yet, unlike conventional approaches that use the preference model to formulate a loss for training a reward model before optimizing a policy, {\methodac} leverages a change of variables to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. With access to a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, {\methodac} can thus train a policy using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without the need to learn an explicit, separate reward model or to perform policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy relative to an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

The primary contribution of this work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), an uncomplicated, reinforcement learning-free approach for training language models using preference data. Our experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably or better than current techniques, such as PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that scale up in size have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few-shot prompting strategy \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the specific instructions seen during fine-tuning, thereby improving their overall practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been quite successful, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of output quality tends to be easier than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, recent research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs using datasets reflecting human preferences, which has led to improved performance in tasks like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with preference data based on a preference framework such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and subsequently fine-tune a language model to maximize this reward through reinforcement learning algorithms like REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses LLMs, which have already been instruction-tuned with human feedback, to produce synthetic preference datasets targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision via text rubrics to guide the LLM's annotations. These techniques reflect a merging of two research areas: one focusing on reinforcement learning for language model training across various objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models remains a significant practical hurdle; this paper proposes a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language applications, deriving policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with numerous methods introduced. Learning in contextual bandits based on preferences or rankings of actions instead of explicit rewards is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical studies of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner} — a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} competing policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, while CDB frameworks receive preference labels in an online manner, preference learning from human feedback commonly relies on a static batch of offline annotated pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates on binary preferences generated by an \textit{unknown} underlying scoring function rather than direct reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several PbRL algorithms have been developed, many of which leverage off-policy preference datasets but typically require first explicitly modeling the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process typically consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by applying supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-existing language model using high-quality datasets relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization, resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, the SFT model is prompted using inputs $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then shown to human annotators who indicate a preference for one response over the other, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the favored completion and $y_l$ the less favored one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown. Several methods exist to model these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely used option (though more general ranking models like the Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be incorporated if multiple ranked outputs are available). The BT model defines the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given $x$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, we can represent the reward model as $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and learn its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Framing this as a binary classification problem leads to the following negative log-likelihood objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the setting of language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized based on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear output layer atop the final transformer layer, which yields a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward signal, prior research commonly normalizes the reward outputs such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ holds for all inputs $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been learned is employed to guide the language model's training. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization task is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ serves as a coefficient that regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the base reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model's policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$. This regularization is crucial to ensure the model does not stray excessively from the distribution where the reward model provides reliable signals, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse into producing only a few high-reward outputs. Since language generation involves discrete decisions, the objective is not directly differentiable and is therefore commonly optimized using reinforcement learning methods. The common practice \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and then maximizing it via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches, which first infer a reward function and then optimize it through RL, our method \rev{eliminates the reward modeling phase and directly tunes the language model based on preference information}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows for an explicit solution of the optimal policy, removing the need for an RL training cycle}. 

As we will explain in detail shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to forgo the separate reward modeling stage}{circumvents the necessity of training a distinct reward model}, while still optimizing according to established frameworks for human preference modeling, like the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) goal as previous studies, described by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, with an arbitrary reward function $r$. Following the methodology in earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem stated in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the partition function. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even if the reward function $r^*$ is approximated by its maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally costly \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits practical usability. Nonetheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and applying algebraic manipulation, we derive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model (Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}), the partition function terms cancel out, allowing the human preference probability to be represented exclusively in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework fulfills the preference model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The full derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations apply to the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, which are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data based on the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{In doing so, we avoid the explicit step of modeling rewards and eliminate the requirement for reinforcement learning optimization.}{Here, we implicitly fit a reward via an alternative parameterization whose optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Furthermore, since this approach corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it possesses desirable theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We expand on the theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related methods in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{assigns greater weight when reward prediction is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (additional details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to increase the likelihood of the favored outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of the less favored ones $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted based on how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the disfavored completion relative to the favored one, scaled by $\beta$; in other words, it reflects how inaccurately the implicit reward function ranks the outputs, modulated by the KL constraint's strength. Our experiments highlight the significance of this weighting factor, since a straightforward variant of the method lacking this weight can lead to deterioration of the language model’s performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completion samples $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then label these with human preferences to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a chosen $\beta$ value.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human annotations. Since these preference datasets are typically sampled from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we initialize $\piref$ by performing maximum likelihood estimation on the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \pi(y_w \mid x) \right]}$. This strategy helps reduce the distributional mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed by DPO. Additional information about implementation details and hyperparameters is available in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we delve deeper into the DPO approach, offering a theoretical foundation and discussing how the benefits of DPO address challenges associated with actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO manages to circumvent both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the need to explicitly fit a reward function} and reinforcement learning by directly optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We then optimize our parametric policy $\pi_{\theta}$, which is effectively the same as optimizing the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after applying a change of variables. This section develops the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrating that it does not restrict the space of reward models that can be learned and enables the precise identification of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this definition establishes an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry special case, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an identical preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class yield the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are relatively simple and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a well-known identifiability problem associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, additional identifiability conditions are typically required to ensure meaningful guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma confirms that every reward function from the same equivalence class produces an identical optimal policy, which implies that our ultimate goal is to recover any representative reward function from this class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under reasonable assumptions, every reward equivalence class consistent with the Plackett-Luce family (and specifically the Bradley-Terry model) can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ denotes a certain model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference distribution.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be represented using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Finally, substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward), we find that $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Hence, the projection $f$ maps $r$ to a member of its equivalence class in the specified form, confirming that our reparameterization of the reward does not restrict generality.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying which particular reward function within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization selects, namely, the reward satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which means $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
Referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy generated by $r(x, y)$.
The crucial idea behind the DPO method is that by enforcing specific constraints on the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce (especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, we maintain the representational capacity of the reward functions while ensuring that the optimal policy described in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically tractable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our analytical framework can also be applied to identify sources of instability in common actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}, following the RLHF procedure. This approach relates to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} when addressing the constrained reinforcement learning problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}. Consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$, where the goal is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \mid\mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, with $\pi^*$ representing the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, which is induced by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective matches the one optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward function $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not change the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit high variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One way to incorporate this normalization is through a learned value function, but optimizing this can be challenging. Alternatively, prior approaches have normalized rewards by employing a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines altogether.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to directly train policies using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing rewards while minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, especially when compared to standard preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we examine DPO’s performance on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO often matches or surpasses strong baselines including RLHF with PPO and approaches that select the best trajectory from $N$ samples based on a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In each experiment, the algorithms learn a policy based on a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ consists of a prefix extracted from a movie review in the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal of the policy is to produce an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable a controlled assessment, preference pairs in this scenario are \textit{generated} using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) step, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional information can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the policy is required to generate a summary $y$ capturing the post’s key points. We rely on the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-crafted summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework for RLHF \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The human preference annotations were obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on samples generated by a different, yet similarly trained, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query, which can range from questions about astrophysics to requests for advice on relationships. The policy must generate an informative and engaging response $y$ to the query. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, comprising 170,000 conversations between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses produced by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating which reply is favored by humans. Since there is no pre-trained SFT model for this domain, we fine-tune a standard language model exclusively on the preferred completions to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our study employs two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizations objective, in the controlled sentiment generation scenario we measure each algorithm’s performance by the trade-off curve between the obtained reward and the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this curve is determinable since we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test dataset. Although prior work indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators than conventional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results reveal that GPT-4’s evaluations align closely with human judgments, with agreement levels between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding those observed between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to better align with human preferences. To begin, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task, as well as 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Additionally, we test the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the preferred completion $y_w$—sourced either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic language model (in single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{reducing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional weighting factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. We further include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which utilizes a reward function derived from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment setting. For sentiment-related experiments, we employ two versions of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and optimizes hyperparameters to enhance performance (these adjustments are also applied to standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we analyze the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate completions from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and selects the top-scoring output according to a reward model trained on preference data. Although this approach effectively separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally intensive for even moderate values of $N$ due to the need to sample $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among the methods, only DPO demonstrates improvement over the chosen summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels at different sampling temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward while preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Hence, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each algorithm, we conduct multiple training experiments, varying the hyperparameters that control policy conservativeness (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), totaling 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts, where we calculate the average reward using the true reward function as well as the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO achieves the most effective frontier by yielding the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although both DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO demonstrates notably greater efficiency; its reward versus KL trade-off clearly surpasses that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO has access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO scalable to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier research has demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO on human preference data leads to better summaries. To compare methods, we generate completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the average win rate relative to reference completions. The completions across all methods are produced at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with corresponding win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results show that DPO attains a win rate near 61\% at a temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO which achieves about 57\% at its optimal sampling temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO reaches a higher peak win rate compared to the best $N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter, implying these outcomes might underestimate its full capability. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across different sampling temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance can deteriorate to that of the base GPT-J model when temperature increases. Preferred-FT yields little improvement over the original SFT model. In a direct human evaluation comparison presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

On single-turn dialogue tasks, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset's test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on one round of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations leverage preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rates of different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this specific task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions ensuring these completions remain in-distribution, and subsequently perform training with DPO. We also benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of these methods at their optimal temperatures. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we fail to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that surpasses the baseline Pythia-2.8B model's performance. Given our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that improves upon preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO achieves its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\section{Discussion}  
Preference-based learning offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that leverages preferences to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to utilize existing RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized for human preferences \textit{directly} through a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or exceeding that of current RLHF methods, including those based on PPO; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions that warrant further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to approaches relying on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize similarly to those trained with PPO, yet a more thorough analysis is required. For instance, could self-labeling using the DPO policy enable effective utilization of unlabeled prompts in a comparable manner? Additionally, what forms does reward over-optimization take within the direct preference optimization framework, and might the slight performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this phenomenon? Although our experiments include models with up to 6B parameters, investigating the application of DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models remains an intriguing avenue for future research. Concerning evaluations, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; hence, future studies might focus on identifying optimal strategies to obtain reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s potential extends beyond training language models with human preferences and could be applied to generative modeling in various other domains.
