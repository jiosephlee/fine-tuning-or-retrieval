\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely controlling their behavior remains challenging due to their fully unsupervised training process. Current approaches to achieve such control gather human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and frequently unstable process that first involves training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large LM to maximize this estimated reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. \rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that allows direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling the standard RLHF problem to be addressed using only a straightforward classification loss.} The resulting method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than existing techniques. In particular, DPO fine-tuning surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects diverse goals, priorities, and skill levels. Some of these may not be ideal to replicate; for instance, while we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors to fix them, we also want to encourage the model to produce the (often rare) high-quality code found in its training data. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{aware} of a misconception held by half the population, but it should not assert that misconception as true in half of the relevant queries. Put differently, carefully choosing the model’s \emph{preferred responses and behaviors} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective used can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches embed desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated sets of human preferences that reflect behaviors considered safe and helpful by humans. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high-quality responses, the most effective techniques employ reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model based on human preference data and then apply reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward, while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding skills, its pipeline is substantially more complex than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the policy during training, which leads to considerable computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly optimize a language model according to human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an algorithm that implicitly maximizes the same objective as current RLHF approaches (reward maximization subject to a KL-divergence constraint) while being easy to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses over dispreferred ones, incorporating a dynamic, per-example importance weight that mitigates model collapse observed with a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing techniques, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function matches empirical preference data. However, unlike prior methods that use the preference model to define a loss for training a reward model and then optimize a policy based on that reward, {\methodac} applies a change of variables to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Consequently, given a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, {\methodac} can optimize the policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without learning an explicit, standalone reward model or sampling from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

The primary contribution of this work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Our experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably to, or better than, current approaches like PPO-based RLHF when learning from preferences across tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size can perform certain tasks zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to generalize to instructions beyond the fine-tuning dataset and generally improves their usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been successful, collecting \textit{relative} human judgments of response quality is often easier than obtaining expert demonstrations. Consequently, later studies have fine-tuned LLMs using datasets of human preferences, leading to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction-following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve first training a neural reward model to align with the preference data under a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback for instruction following to generate synthetic preference data targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics for the LLM’s annotations. These strategies represent a fusion of two research areas: one focused on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of using relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a significant practical challenge; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language applications, learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods proposed. Contextual bandit learning that relies on preferences or rankings of actions instead of rewards is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike CDBs, where preference labels are obtained online, learning from human preferences typically involves a fixed offline dataset of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} scoring function instead of explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Numerous PbRL algorithms exist, including those capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but they generally require first estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) before optimizing it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a single-stage policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We provide an overview of the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process typically consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) preference data collection and reward model training; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on high-quality datasets relevant to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second phase, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ to generate pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference for one response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the preferred and less preferred completions from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly observable. Various methods exist to model these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a common choice (though more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied if multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model defines the human preference probability $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be parameterized and its parameters optimized via maximum likelihood estimation. By treating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr) \right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model settings, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear layer atop the final transformer layer to output a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous studies normalize the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL stage, the reward function that has been learned is utilized to guide the language model. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized as $\pisft$. This regularization is crucial because it limits the model’s departure from the distribution where the reward model is reliable, helps preserve output diversity, and avoids collapsing to a few high-reward outputs. Since language generation is discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and is generally optimized via reinforcement learning. The common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of implementing reinforcement learning algorithms on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our aim is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that uses preferences directly. In contrast to previous RLHF techniques, which first learn a reward and then optimize it through RL, our method \rev{bypasses the reward modeling step and directly optimizes a language model using preference data}{utilizes a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the optimal policy to be derived in closed form, eliminating the need for an RL training loop}.  
As we will explain in detail shortly, our main insight is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and optimal policies, enabling the conversion of a loss function defined over reward functions into one defined over policies.  
This change-of-variables strategy \rev{allows us to skip the explicit reward modeling step}{removes the necessity of fitting a separate, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing according to established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously encodes both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined with a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the partition function is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ of the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical use of this formulation. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to cancel out, allowing the human preference probability to be expressed solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The full derivation is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, analogous results can be obtained for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (i.e., Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the policy objective is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward and eliminates the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization. Instead, we implicitly fit a reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical analysis of DPO in comparison to related methods is provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward estimate is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing that of less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the examples are weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the disfavored completions, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting how inaccurately the implicit reward model ranks the completions and incorporating the influence of the KL constraint. Our experiments highlight the significance of this weighting, as a straightforward version of this approach without the weighting factor can lead to model degradation (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO process involves: 1) Sampling completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, then labeling them with human preferences to create the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the target $\beta$.  
In practice, it is preferable to utilize publicly available preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Since these datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, present theoretical support, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges faced by actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{fitting an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by training the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. The optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward parameterization defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We optimize our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a manner equivalent to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} via a change of variables. This section develops the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrating that it does not limit the set of reward models that can be learned and enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the same class generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class produce the identical optimal policy in the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are straightforward and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This first lemma highlights a well-known under-specification problem associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this under-specification, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability constraints to ensure guarantees on the MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within the same equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy; therefore, our ultimate goal is to recover any reward function from this optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under mild conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model (and specifically the Bradley-Terry model) can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that induces an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from $r$’s equivalence class can be represented using the stated reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization depends only on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields a reward function in $r$’s equivalence class with the desired form, confirming that the proposed reparameterization does not restrict the generality of our reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying the specific reward function within each equivalence class that the DPO reparameterization selects, namely the reward function satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (non-negative and summing to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it follows that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The central insight of the DPO algorithm is that by imposing certain constraints on the otherwise under-constrained Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) preference models, we maintain the class of representable reward functions while making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically tractable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}  
Our framework can also be applied to identify instabilities in common actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This allows us to relate the problem to the control as inference framework \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem given in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} defined by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective matches those optimized in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that use the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function of the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient to exhibit high variance, leading to unstable learning. While it is possible to handle the normalization term via a learned value function, this approach can be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, previous methods have normalized rewards by employing a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the need for any baseline.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, in a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward and minimizing the KL-divergence from the reference policy, in comparison to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and outperforms the best of $N$ sampled trajectories based on a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. In each case, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ is a prefix of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with generating $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs by using a pre-trained sentiment classifier to compare generations, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned to convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the policy must produce a summary $y$ capturing the main points of the post. We follow previous work by employing the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for RLHF. The human preference dataset was obtained from samples generated by a different but similarly fine-tuned SFT model, as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a user query that can range from astrophysics questions to requests for relationship advice. The policy must generate an engaging and helpful response $y$. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses produced by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the human-preferred reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists for this task, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm’s performance by the frontier of achieved reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible because the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. In contrast, in real-world settings where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, using GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluation of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline is the reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, it is the preferred response in the test dataset. Although prior work indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4’s judgments align closely with human evaluations, with human agreement with GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding inter-annotator agreement.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess several established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At a basic level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Additionally, we test the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the ‘unlikelihood’ component. We also include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained with a reward function learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle that uses the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment scenario. For sentiment experiments, we employ two PPO-GT implementations: an off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the top-scoring response based on a reward function trained on the preference dataset. Although this method achieves strong results by separating reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$ since it requires generating $N$ completions per query during testing.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among the methods, only DPO shows improvement over the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels across different sampling temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward with limiting the policy’s divergence from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a significantly larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms in the sentiment task. We conduct multiple training runs per algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each run (target KL $\in \{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), totaling 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts by calculating the average reward under the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the sum of the per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi \mid\mid \piref\right)$. Our results show that DPO yields the most efficient frontier, achieving the highest reward while maintaining low KL. This finding is significant for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO is substantially more efficient, with its reward/KL trade-off strictly outperforming PPO. Second, DPO surpasses PPO’s frontier even when PPO has access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We next assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak correlation with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO using human preferences yields more effective summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate against reference completions. Completions for all methods are sampled across temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. We observe that DPO achieves a win rate near 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s peak win rate of about 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher maximum win rate than the best of $N$ baseline. It is important to note that we did not extensively tune DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these findings might underestimate its full potential. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater robustness to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can decline to that of the base GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the SFT model. We also present a direct comparison between DPO and PPO in human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples from DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various methods on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training} containing one step of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations use the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rate across methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure they align with the model’s distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. We also benchmark against the best of 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each approach. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate proxy for PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO reaches its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a human evaluation to assess the accuracy of GPT-4's judgments, utilizing the outcomes from the TL;DR summarization task along with two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt asks which summary better captures the key information in the post. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires which summary is more succinct; this prompt is tested because we observe that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries compared to humans when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. The full prompts are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three comparisons involving the highest-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a mid-level method (SFT, temp. 0.25) to represent a range of sample qualities; each is compared against greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Our findings show that GPT-4’s agreement with humans is roughly on par with human-to-human agreement across both prompts, indicating that GPT-4 serves as a reasonable stand-in for human evaluation (due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that better align with human preferences; thus, this prompt is used for the primary results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human study, including the rater interface and volunteer list, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable, aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, without reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or better than existing RLHF methods, including those based on PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity of training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings prompt several key questions for further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that learn from an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize similarly to PPO-based models, but a more thorough analysis is required. For instance, can self-labeling with the DPO policy effectively utilize unlabeled prompts in a comparable manner? Additionally, how does reward over-optimization appear in the direct preference optimization framework, and could the slight performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this? While our experiments cover models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models represents a promising avenue for future research. Concerning evaluations, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by the prompt used; future studies might explore optimal methods for obtaining reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO has numerous potential applications beyond training language models with human preferences, such as training generative models in other domains.
