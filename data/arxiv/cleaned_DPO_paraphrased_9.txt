\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop certain reasoning capabilities, precisely steering their output remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current techniques to achieve such controllability rely on gathering human judgments about the relative quality of model outputs and subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to better align with these preferences, frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF involves a complicated and often unstable process that first trains a reward model to capture human preferences, then applies reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large unsupervised LM by maximizing this learned reward, while carefully preventing significant deviation from the original model. \rev{In this work, we utilize a connection between reward functions and optimal policies to demonstrate that this constrained reward maximization task can be \emph{precisely optimized} through a single policy training step, effectively reframing the problem as classification on human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF, which permits direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling resolution of the standard RLHF challenge via a straightforward classification loss.} The resulting method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{training a reward model,}{} performing sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or engaging in extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experimental results indicate that DPO can fine-tune language models to match or surpass existing approaches in aligning with human preferences. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling sentiment in generated text and achieves comparable or improved quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being markedly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets demonstrate remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from human-generated data encompassing diverse intentions, priorities, and expertise levels. Not all of these intentions or skills are desirable to replicate; for instance, while we want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to fix them, we also aim to steer it towards producing the (often scarce) high-quality coding found within the training data. Likewise, a language model might need to \textit{acknowledge} a widely held false belief accepted by 50\% of people, but it should certainly not assert this misconception as true in half of its responses on the topic! Put differently, choosing the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its vast range of \textit{knowledge and skills} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While current approaches generally guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed in these techniques can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

At a broad level, current approaches embed the intended behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully selected sets of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and useful by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on an extensive text corpus. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on human-generated examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective techniques employ reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model using a collection of human preference data and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model policy to generate responses with high reward scores, while preventing the policy from diverging too far from the original model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and coding capabilities, its process is notably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and continuous sampling from the model policy during training, which leads to substantial computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly tune a language model to match human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a technique that implicitly maximizes the same goal as current RLHF approaches (reward maximization constrained by KL-divergence), yet it is easier to implement and train. Essentially, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over less favored ones, while applying a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting that avoids the model collapse observed when using a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to existing methods, DPO is based on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) which evaluates how well a reward function aligns with observed preference data. However, unlike traditional approaches that leverage this preference model to create a preference loss for training a reward model and then train a policy to maximize that reward, DPO employs a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Consequently, with a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without needing to explicitly learn a separate reward model or perform policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, when scaled up, demonstrate the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with just a few-shot prompting approach \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be markedly enhanced by fine-tuning them on datasets containing instructions paired with human-crafted completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often referred to as `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the training instructions and improves their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, gathering \textit{relative} human judgments regarding response quality tends to be easier than collecting expert demonstrations. Consequently, follow-up research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets derived from human preferences, thereby boosting performance in areas like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with preference data, often modeled with frameworks such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning algorithms like REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback on instruction following to synthesize additional preference datasets targeting specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision through textual rubrics guiding the LLM’s annotations. These strategies represent the merging of two research streams: one focused on reinforcement learning for language model training across various objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on general methodologies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of utilizing relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains practically challenging; this paper proposes a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on reinforcement learning.

Outside of linguistic applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with numerous strategies having been developed. When preferences or rankings of actions are utilized instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this framework is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In scenarios lacking absolute reward signals, theoretical studies on CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, which is defined as a policy that achieves an expected win rate of at least 50\% against \textit{any} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, preference feedback is obtained in an online manner, whereas in preference-based learning from humans, the data typically consists of a fixed offline dataset containing preference-labeled pairs of actions \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) involves learning from binary preference signals produced by an \textit{unknown} latent `scoring' function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are several PbRL algorithms available, including those that can leverage off-policy preference data, but these methods usually require an initial step of explicitly estimating the underlying scoring function (i.e., the reward model), followed by policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a single-stage policy learning method that directly optimizes policies to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This procedure typically consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by adapting a pre-trained language model through supervised fine-tuning using carefully curated datasets tailored to the target tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, and others), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate their preference for one response over the other, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored and $y_l$ the less favored completion among $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed that these preferences stem from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to represent such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely used (although more general ranking approaches like the Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable when multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model describes the human preference probability $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parametrize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters using maximum likelihood estimation. By formulating this as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)) \bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. Within language modeling, the function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ by appending a linear layer atop the last transformer layer to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous research normalizes the reward predictions so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}} [r_\phi(x, y)] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward function that has been trained is employed to guide the language model's updates. Consistent with earlier research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is allowed to diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ starts off identical to $\pisft$. This KL penalty is crucial as it restricts the model from straying too far from the data distribution where the reward model performs well, while also preserving output variety and avoiding collapse to a limited set of high-reward responses. Since language generation involves discrete outputs, the objective function is non-differentiable and is commonly optimized via reinforcement learning techniques. The conventional method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizes it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning techniques to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference information. In contrast to earlier RLHF approaches that first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it using RL, our method \rev{skips the reward modeling phase and directly trains a language model with preference data}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows the direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for an RL training procedure}. 

As will be detailed shortly, our central idea is to use an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, which permits us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to omit the explicit reward modeling step}{prevents the necessity of training a separate, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing according to established human preference models such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously serves as the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward representation.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as previous studies, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, involving a general reward function $r$. Building on prior research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is characterized by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization term $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. A full derivation is available in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when approximating the true reward function $r^*$ by its maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting practical use of this formulation. Nonetheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the reward difference between two outputs, expressed as ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing us to represent the human preference probability purely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework adheres to the preference probability:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The full derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations can be made for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the likelihood of human preference data through the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parametrized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to avoid explicitly modeling the reward while also eliminating the need for reinforcement learning algorithms.}{In this manner, we implicitly capture the reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, since this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical insights into DPO and its relationship with existing methods are provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger weight when reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability assigned to the preferred outputs $y_w$ while lowering the probability of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the samples are weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly ranks the less preferred outputs relative to the preferred ones, scaled by the factor $\beta$, reflecting the influence of the KL constraint. Our empirical results highlight the significance of this weighting mechanism, since omitting the weighting factor can lead to model degradation, as shown in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and obtain human preference labels to form the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and hyperparameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, instead of generating new samples and collecting human feedback, one often leverages publicly available preference datasets. Since these datasets are typically produced by sampling from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a policy is accessible. Otherwise, when $\pisft$ is not present, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood on the preferred completions $(x, y_w)$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the mismatch between the actual (unknown) reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}  
Here, we offer a deeper insight into the DPO approach, present formal theoretical justification, and connect the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered by actor-critic techniques commonly applied in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the need to fit an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by training the policy through a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized in a way that matches reward model training as described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, modulo a change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundations underlying this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no limitations on the variety of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the exact identification of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that their difference satisfies ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, specifically the Bradley-Terry setup, any two reward functions belonging to the same category yield identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class produce an identical optimal policy in the context of the constrained RL framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are straightforward and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a common under-specification problem inherent in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, additional identifiability conditions are typically necessary to ensure reliable MLE estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma establishes that all reward functions within a given equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy; thus, our ultimate goal is to recover any single reward function from this optimal class. The following theorem, proven in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this notion:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model family (including the Bradley-Terry model specifically) can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as per Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be rewritten using the stated reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ acts to normalize the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Because this normalization depends solely on the prefix $x$, the resulting function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of the original $r(x, y)$. Finally, substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which applies universally to reward functions) yields $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Consequently, the projection $f$ generates a reward function in the same equivalence class as $r$, matching the desired form, so the proposed reparameterization fully captures the generality of our reward model.
\end{sproof}
An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization. This reward function satisfies the condition:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{ by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution with positive values summing to one.
Referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The main insight behind the DPO method is that by enforcing specific constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference model family, we retain the full expressiveness of the reward class while rendering the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Unstable Behavior in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework can also be applied to analyze the instability issues commonly encountered in standard actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF procedure, we concentrate on the reinforcement learning fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained reinforcement learning problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}, we consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective matches the one optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that use a DPO-equivalent reward function within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this formulation, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it may cause the policy gradient estimates to have large variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One way to incorporate this normalization is via a learned value function; however, this approach can be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, prior methods have performed reward normalization using a human-generated completion baseline, which essentially serves as a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalizing constant. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward formulation that eliminates the need for any such baselines.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capability to train policies directly using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward while minimizing the KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, in comparison to popular preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks like RLHF with PPO and also outperforms methods that select the best trajectory from $N$ samples according to a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the algorithms acquire a policy by learning from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents a movie review prefix sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce an output $y$ that conveys positive sentiment. To enable a systematic evaluation, preference pairs are \textit{generated} by utilizing a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we train GPT-2-large until it converges on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with creating a summary $y$ highlighting the key points of the post. Following previous studies, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-authored forum summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). The human preference dataset was gathered by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from samples generated by a differently trained, but comparable, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ is a user query that can range from inquiries about astrophysics to requests for advice on relationships. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both informative and engaging. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two model-generated responses from a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a human preference label indicating the favored reply. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists in this scenario, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how effectively each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization goal in a controlled sentiment generation context, we measure each algorithm’s performance based on the trade-off curve between obtained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy. This curve is computable since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response recorded in the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models can outperform conventional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal a strong correlation between GPT-4’s judgments and human evaluations, with agreement levels between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding those observed between human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we test zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on summarization tasks, and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue tasks. Additionally, we assess the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the preferred completion $y_w$, which is selected either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or from a general LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised approach we examine is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy by increasing the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method optionally incorporates a weighting factor $\alpha \in [0,1]$ for the unlikelihood component. We also explore \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, utilizing a reward model derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment experiments. For sentiment evaluation, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance outcomes (these improvements are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which involves generating $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the top-ranked response according to a reward function trained on the preference dataset. While this method achieves strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally intensive even for moderate values of $N$, as it requires producing $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates evaluated by GPT-4 on the Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue task; among all methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the chosen summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. The gains achieved by DPO relative to the dataset labels remain relatively consistent during training at different sampling temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework employed in standard RLHF methods aims to optimize reward exploitation while limiting the policy's divergence from a given reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the corresponding KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a significantly increased KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. We conduct multiple training experiments per algorithm, each with a distinct hyperparameter controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and varied random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. At every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy using a collection of test prompts, calculating both the average reward based on the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL divergence\footnote{Specifically, the sum of the KL divergences computed at each timestep.} with respect to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO significantly outperforms others by achieving the most favorable frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining a low KL divergence. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons: firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, with its reward/KL balance clearly surpassing that of PPO; secondly, DPO establishes a superior frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO is provided with access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO scalable to actual preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automated metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO based on human preferences leads to improved summary quality. Our evaluation compares various approaches by generating completions on the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test split, then calculating the average win rate relative to reference completions in the test set. For all approaches, completions were sampled across temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results show that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves about a 61\% win rate, outperforming PPO’s peak performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a higher maximum win rate than the top-performing $N$ baseline method. It is important to mention that we did not extensively optimize DPO’s $\beta$ parameter, suggesting these findings might underrepresent DPO’s full capabilities. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates greater stability across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose performance can fall to the baseline GPT-J level when sampling at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT offers minimal improvement over the initial SFT model. We also conduct a direct comparison between DPO and PPO through human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogues, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which involves a single round of interaction between human and assistant. GPT-4 evaluations utilize the preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions—ensuring the outputs remain within the model’s distribution—and subsequently fine-tune using DPO. We also benchmark against the best outcome from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings show that DPO matches or surpasses the best-performing temperature settings of each approach. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but could not identify a prompt or temperature configuration that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Given our TL;DR results and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. Overall, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO achieves its optimal performance fairly rapidly.

\subsection{Assessing GPT-4 evaluations against human assessments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a human evaluation to assess the accuracy of GPT-4's judgments, leveraging outcomes from the TL;DR summarization task and employing two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly asks which summary better captures the key information in the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires which summary is more succinct; we test this prompt because we observe that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, unlike human preferences. Complete prompt details can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Our evaluation includes three pairwise comparisons involving the top-performing (DPO, temperature 0.25), lowest-performing (PPO, temperature 1.0), and a median-performing (SFT, temperature 0.25) model, aiming to capture a range of summary qualities. Each method is compared against greedily-sampled PPO at its optimal temperature. Results show that, across both prompts, GPT-4’s agreement with human judgments is comparable to the level of agreement observed among human evaluators themselves, indicating GPT-4 serves as a reliable stand-in for human evaluation (due to the limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). Notably, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that more closely mirror human preferences; thus, we adopt this prompt for the principal findings presented in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information on the human study, including the rating interface and list of participants, is available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO uncovers a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows training the language model to align with human preferences \textit{directly} through a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or compromising generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance on par with or exceeding that of current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches, thereby significantly lowering the difficulty of training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings open up several critical avenues for further investigation. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with PPO-based methods, yet more thorough analysis is required. For instance, could self-labeling using the DPO policy enable effective utilization of unlabeled prompts in a similar manner? Another question concerns how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this phenomenon. Moreover, although our experiments include models up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to scale with significantly larger, state-of-the-art models remains a promising direction. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the choice of prompt, suggesting that future work should explore methods for eliciting more reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, the scope of DPO’s applications extends beyond training language models with human preferences, potentially encompassing the development of generative models across various modalities.
