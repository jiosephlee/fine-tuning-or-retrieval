### CHAPTER 2 — THE REINFORCEMENT LEARNING LANDSCAPE AND THE NEED FOR A SIMPLER PATH

Now that we have our foundational tools from Chapter 1, we can examine the dominant method for aligning language models with human preferences: **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**. This chapter will explain the standard RLHF pipeline, introduce the algorithm that powers it (PPO), and explore the practical challenges that motivate the search for a simpler, more direct alternative.

#### 2.1 The Standard Recipe for Alignment: RLHF

For years, the most successful approach for teaching models nuanced human values has been a three-stage process. Think of it as a recipe:

1.  **Create a Good Starting Point (SFT):** First, take a powerful pre-trained language model and fine-tune it on a dataset of high-quality, human-written examples. This process, which we've already called Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), results in a capable and knowledgeable model. This is our **reference policy**, \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\).

2.  **Train a Judge (The Reward Model):** Next, collect a dataset of human preferences. For many different prompts, we generate two or more responses from the SFT model and ask a human which one they prefer. We use this data to train a separate model, called the **reward model** (\(r_{\phi}\)), to predict which response a human would prefer. It learns to assign a higher score to better responses, effectively acting as an automated "judge."

3.  **Refine the Policy with a Coach (Reinforcement Learning):** Finally, we use reinforcement learning to improve our SFT model (\(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)). The model, now our active "policy" \(\pi_{\theta}\), generates responses to prompts. The reward model acts as a coach, giving a score to each response. The goal of this stage is to train the policy to generate responses that get the highest possible scores from the reward model.

To prevent the model from forgetting its language skills or "hacking" the reward model, we add a crucial constraint: the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is not allowed to become too different from our original reference policy, \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). We enforce this with the KL divergence penalty we learned about in Chapter 1. The final objective is to find a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that maximizes this combined goal:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_{\theta}} \!\big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\big] \;-\; \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big).
\]
Let's break this down:
*   \(\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_{\theta}} [r_{\phi}(x,y)]\): "Maximize the average reward the policy expects to receive from the judge."
*   \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta} \Vert \pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\): "The penalty for how much the new policy has strayed from the original SFT model."
*   \(\beta\): A knob that controls the strength of this penalty.

This RLHF framework has been incredibly successful, powering significant improvements in the helpfulness and safety of leading language models.

#### 2.2 The Algorithm in the Engine Room: PPO

The most common algorithm used to perform the third stage of RLHF is **Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)**. PPO is a workhorse of modern reinforcement learning, designed for stability. Its core idea is to update the policy in small, controlled steps, avoiding drastic changes that could destabilize the learning process.

It achieves this with a special "clipped" objective function. While the exact formula is complex, its purpose is simple:
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \min\!\Big( \dots \Big) \right].
\]
The `min` and `clip` operations inside this equation effectively put a ceiling on how large any single training update can be. This prevents the policy from suddenly changing its behaviour in a dramatic and potentially harmful way. In practice, PPO for language models also involves training an auxiliary "value function" to help estimate rewards more accurately and reduce the randomness (variance) of the training process.

#### 2.3 The Cracks in the Foundation: Why RLHF is Hard

Despite its proven power, implementing the full RLHF pipeline with PPO is notoriously difficult and resource-intensive. Practitioners face several major challenges:

*   **Complexity and Fragility:** The full setup involves training and managing *four* different models at once: the policy being trained (\(\pi_{\theta}\)), the reference policy (\(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)), the reward model (\(r_{\phi}\)), and often a value model for PPO. The interactions between them are complex, and the process is sensitive to many hyperparameters that are difficult to tune.

*   **Inefficient Data Use:** PPO is an "on-policy" algorithm, meaning it requires generating fresh samples (text "rollouts") from the current policy at each stage of training. This is a slow and computationally expensive feedback loop.

*   **The Problem of "Reward Hacking":** The reward model is not a perfect judge of quality; it's just a model trained on a finite dataset of human preferences. A clever policy can discover and exploit loopholes in the reward model's logic to get a high score for a nonsensical or low-quality answer. This is called **reward hacking**. For example, it might learn that the reward model gives slightly higher scores to longer answers and begin producing overly verbose, rambling text.

#### 2.4 The KL Balancing Act

The KL penalty is our primary defense against reward hacking and catastrophic forgetting. It acts like a leash, anchoring the new policy to the sensible, well-rounded behaviour of the SFT reference model. The per-prompt KL divergence is a formal measure of this distance:
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \;=\; \sum_{y} \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]
However, finding the right strength (\(\beta\)) for this leash is a delicate balancing act:
*   **Too little regularisation (small \(\beta\)):** The policy is free to drift far from the reference model. It might quickly find exploits in the reward model, leading to brittle, over-optimised behaviour.
*   **Too much regularisation (large \(\beta\)):** The policy is tied too tightly to its original state. It cannot improve meaningfully on the preference data because every step away from the reference model is heavily penalised.

#### 2.5 The Search for a Better Way

These challenges motivate a crucial question: **Can we achieve the same goal as RLHF without all the complexity?**

Ideally, we would want a method that:
1.  Directly optimises a policy using the preference data, skipping the intermediate step of training a separate reward model.
2.  Avoids the complex and inefficient machinery of reinforcement learning, such as on-policy rollouts and value functions.
3.  Is formulated as a simple, stable, supervised learning problem, much like the initial SFT phase.
4.  Crucially, still incorporates the KL regularisation to anchor the policy to a trusted reference model, preserving its general capabilities.

The search for such a method leads directly to the core topic of our discussion. The following chapters will show how we can re-frame the KL-regularised reward maximisation problem in a way that allows us to solve it with a simple, elegant, and direct objective.
