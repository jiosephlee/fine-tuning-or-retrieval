# <|endoftext|> workflow and audits
Benchmark contamination is a critical concern when mining web data. <|endoftext|> follows a conservative substring-matching <|endoftext|> rule: for each evaluation benchmark (English and Chinese <|endoftext|> used in the paper), remove any web text segment that contains a <|endoftext|> substring that exactly matches any substring in the benchmark. For benchmark items shorter than 10-grams but at least 3-grams, perform exact matching of those shorter n-grams. This rule is applied to avoid leaking benchmark content into <|endoftext|> data.

Practically, implement this as a <|endoftext|> filter during corpus construction. First, build an index of all n-grams (with n down to 3) from the evaluation sets you plan to use. Then, as you collect candidate pages, scan their text segments for exact matches against that index. Remove any page that contains a match. The paper emphasizes exact <|endoftext|> matching for long segments and exact matching for shorter sequences down to 3-grams—this is a deterministic, conservative policy that trades coverage of web math for strict cleanliness.

Audit your <|endoftext|> pipeline frequently. The <|endoftext|> team used this exact matching rule to filter English <|endoftext|> (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese <|endoftext|> (CMATH, AGIEval). When reproducing, ensure the same <|endoftext|> (or your chosen evaluation sets) are used to create the <|endoftext|> filter; if you add new <|endoftext|> add their n-grams to the filter before large-scale mining. Finally, keep track of removed pages for inspection—if a large number of pages are filtered, investigate whether your <|endoftext|> rule is too aggressive or whether a genuine leak exists.

# Evaluation protocols: CoT, PoT, formal math, self-consistency
<|endoftext|> uses a range of evaluation protocols that you should reproduce to assess progress properly.

Chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation: use few-shot CoT prompting to encourage multi-step written reasoning and evaluate the final numerical answer (exact match or dataset-specific scoring). The authors applied CoT evaluation across GSM8K, MATH, CMATH and other <|endoftext|>

Program-of-thought (PoT) / tool-integrated evaluation: for <|endoftext|> reasoning, prompt models to output a Python program that computes the answer, permitting libraries such as math and sympy. Execute generated programs and evaluate the result. The paper evaluates both GSM8K and MATH with PoT and reports program execution-based scoring.

Formal theorem <|endoftext|> / autoformalization: for informal-to-formal tasks, the authors evaluated on miniF2F with Isabelle. The setup is: given an informal statement, its formal counterpart, and an informal proof, the model generates a formal proof sketch. Then an automated prover (Sledgehammer) is invoked to fill missing details. Reproduce this by generating proof sketches with few-shot prompting, then run the off-the-shelf prover pipeline used in the paper.

Self-consistency and sampling-based aggregation: <|endoftext|> reports self-consistency and Maj@K/Pass@K analyses. Self-consistency is implemented by sampling multiple independent solutions and applying majority-vote (Maj@K) to decide the final answer. In the paper, sampling 64 outputs used both for <|endoftext|> training and for reporting self-consistency scores (the abstract reports a self-consistency score of 60.9% on MATH from 64-sample majority voting). When evaluating your model, measure both Pass@K (does any <|endoftext|> output solve the problem) and Maj@K (is the most frequent answer among samples correct). The paper finds that RL improved Maj@K more than Pass@K, suggesting RL concentrates probability mass on correct solutions rather than enabling occasional correct samples.

Reporting practice: evaluate models both with and without tool use, on English and Chinese <|endoftext|> and report few-shot CoT, PoT, and program-execution outcomes. For formal tasks, report success rate of the generated Isabelle proofs after running Sledgehammer.
