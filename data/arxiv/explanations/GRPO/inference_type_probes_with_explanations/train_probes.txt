According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', an implicit assumption about the reward source that must hold for GRPO to reliably improve model behavior is that the reward source is a reliable reward model. This follows because GRPO replaces the critic in PPO with group-relative advantages derived directly from reward-model scores, so the per-token gradient coefficient is proportional to a normalized reward signal and drives the update direction via the clipped objective in Equation (3): for each sampled output \(o_i\), GRPO sets \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i\) (outcome supervision) or \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}\) (process supervision), meaning positive or negative reinforcement entirely hinges on the reward model’s accuracy; moreover, unlike PPO, there is no learned value function to filter or denoise per-token credit assignment, and the KL term \(\beta \mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{ref}]\) only regularizes step size rather than corrects mis-specified reward signals. Therefore, for GRPO’s group-relative advantages to consistently push the policy toward better reasoning, the reward model must be reliable in both accuracy and generalization.

In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the decontamination procedure removes any text segment that contains an exact n-gram match with evaluation benchmarks, and therefore the rule implicitly assumes that a 10-gram exact match suffices. According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', if GRPO is run with group size $G=1$, the key advantage described in the paper that would be lost is relative comparison. GRPO’s core design is to sample a group of outputs $\\{o_i\\}_{i=1}^G$ per question and compute advantages from group-normalized rewards, e.g., outcome supervision uses $\\hat{A}_{i,t}=\\widetilde{r}_i=(r_i-\\mathrm{mean}(\\mathbf{r}))/\\mathrm{std}(\\mathbf{r})$ and process supervision uses $\\hat{A}_{i,t}=\\sum_{\\mathrm{index}(j)\\ge t}\\widetilde{r}_i^{\\mathrm{index}(j)}$, explicitly leveraging the comparative nature of reward models trained on pairwise preferences; with $G=1$, $\\mathrm{mean}(\\mathbf{r})=r_1$ and $\\mathrm{std}(\\mathbf{r})=0$, so normalization is undefined and there is no baseline from group scores, eliminating the relative signal that lets GRPO reinforce “better than peers” and penalize “worse than peers.” Consequently, GRPO would lose its variance-reducing baseline and its comparison-driven gradient shaping, collapsing to per-sample reward scaling without the intra-question ranking that the paper highlights as essential for efficient, stable learning aligned with comparative rewards.

In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', in everyday terms, GRPO's group-relative baseline most resembles the classroom grading scheme called grading on a curve. This is because, instead of learning a separate value function as a baseline like PPO, GRPO forms a group of \(G\) sampled responses for the same question and computes each response’s advantage by normalizing its reward relative to the group, explicitly setting \(\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}\) for outcome supervision (and an analogous step-accumulated form for process supervision), so that the mean group reward acts as the baseline and the dispersion scales the signal. Consequently, the policy update (Eq. (3)) reinforces or penalizes responses based on how well they do compared to their peers’ average—not on an absolute scale—precisely mirroring “curved” grading where scores are shifted and scaled relative to the cohort’s mean and variance, thereby removing the need for a separate critic while providing variance reduction through within-group normalization.

According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', under outcome supervision the normalized reward is defined as $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$; if all group rewards are identical, the normalization leads to division by zero. This follows directly from their GRPO outcome-supervision setup, where for each question $q$ they sample a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$, score them with a reward model to obtain $\mathbf{r}=\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G$, and then compute per-output advantages by standardizing with the group mean and standard deviation; if all rewards are equal, say $r_i=c$ for all $i$, then $\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})=c$ and $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_i (r_i-c)^2}=0$, so the defined normalization $\widetilde{r}_i=(r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})$ attempts to divide by zero; this is a natural edge case of their group-relative design, which relies on between-output variability to produce informative, scale-invariant advantages (and in practice is typically handled by adding a small $\varepsilon$ to the denominator or by noting that zero variance implies no relative signal to reinforce).

According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the clip term in GRPO's PPO-style objective primarily guards against the risk of large policy shifts. This follows directly from the GRPO objective in Equation (3), where for each token the update maximizes a clipped surrogate of the importance ratio, using the term min[ r_t A_t, clip(r_t, 1−ε, 1+ε) A_t ] with r_t = π_θ(o_t|q,o_{<t}) / π_{θ_old}(o_t|q,o_{<t}) and ε a small constant; as in PPO, this clipping truncates the effective ratio when it deviates beyond [1−ε, 1+ε], thereby reducing or nullifying gradient pressure that would otherwise push π_θ too far from π_{θ_old} when advantages are large or misestimated, and thus stabilizing updates against abrupt policy changes. GRPO removes the critic and computes advantages from group-normalized rewards, which can increase variance in A_t; retaining PPO’s clipping is therefore crucial to keep high-variance advantages from inducing outsized steps, and it is complemented by an explicit KL regularizer −β D_KL[π_θ || π_ref] that further contracts the policy toward a reference model, jointly ensuring that per-iteration updates remain conservative even as group-relative signals drive learning.
