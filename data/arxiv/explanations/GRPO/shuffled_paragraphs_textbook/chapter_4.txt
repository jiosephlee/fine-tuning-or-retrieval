Chapter 4: Decontamination and Data Quality Assurance

These pilot pre‑training experiments serve two purposes: they quantify the quality and multilingual utility of the selected web pages, and they provide an empirical basis for the chosen data volume and selection thresholds prior to committing to full-scale pre‑training.

DeepSeekMath’s operational choices reflect this trade-off. The authors choose a 10‑gram exact match threshold as their principal filter and apply an exact-match rule down to 3‑grams for short benchmark items. They also perform other data‑sanitization steps such as URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication to reduce repeated content in the Common Crawl before applying the classifier and ranking. The data selection pipeline then ranks candidate pages by a fastText classifier score and conservatively preserves the top-ranked pages. In their iterative collection, the team inspected domains and manually annotated math‑related URL paths to improve recall, but the benchmark decontamination rule remained stringent throughout.

The decontamination pipeline explicitly targets the benchmarks used for downstream evaluation. In DeepSeekMath these include multiple English and Chinese mathematical evaluation collections: GSM8K and MATH are prominent English benchmarks, while CMATH and AGIEval (and related Chinese evaluation sets) are included for Chinese evaluation. The filtering criteria are applied across these benchmarks so that any web page in the candidate math corpus that contains exact matches according to the n‑gram rules is removed.

The result is a dataset that prioritizes evaluation fidelity (minimizing leakage) and overall data quality, at the cost of discarding some true positive math pages that happen to share long verbatim fragments with benchmarks. The authors accept that choice as appropriate when the central goal is to obtain trustworthy, comparable benchmark results.

A primary practical risk in large-scale web pre-training is benchmark leakage: evaluation questions or their solutions appearing in the pre-training corpus can artificially inflate measured model performance. DeepSeekMath implements a conservative, automated decontamination rule based on exact n-gram matching to mitigate this risk.

Second, the data selection process is described as iterative and partially manual: the seed corpus (OpenWebMath), classifier architecture (fastText), training hyperparameters (vector dim 256, learning rate 0.1, max word n‑gram length 3, min word occurrences 3, 3 epochs) and the domain‑level enrichment procedure (identify domains with >10% recall, manually annotate math URL paths) are documented. The authors also apply URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication to reduce Common Crawl to 40B HTML pages before classification. These operational details matter for reproducibility; the description allows other researchers to reconstruct a similar pipeline or to understand why results might diverge.

Finally, the paper candidly notes limitations: while the matching rules are conservative, they cannot fully prevent subtle leakage (paraphrases or near‑duplicates that escape exact match). The authors also acknowledge domain coverage and diversity considerations (language composition, representation of mathematical subfields) and report multilingual content explicitly (English and Chinese dominant), allowing downstream users to interpret evaluation results in context.

This exact n‑gram strategy favors precision: when a 10‑gram match is found, it is highly likely that the corresponding evaluation content exists in the training text and should be removed. The implementation detail—using 10 as the primary n—reflects a trade-off chosen by the authors that balances false positives and false negatives in a large web-scale setting. Shorter n‑grams (below the 3‑token threshold) are not used because they generate excessive false positives on common phrases; the authors therefore require at least 3 tokens for exact matching in cases where the benchmark item itself is short.

Rather than relying solely on heuristic signals, the authors validate corpus quality via pilot pre‑training experiments. This validation proceeds in two linked phases: (1) iterative corpus construction and (2) controlled backbone model training and evaluation.

For quality validation, the authors run pre‑training experiments that directly compare the DeepSeekMath corpus against prior math corpora (MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2). They apply math training to a common base model (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B) and train each variant for an equal number of tokens (150B) under a shared optimization and scheduling setup (AdamW with specified betas and decay schedule, batch size and context length). Evaluation uses few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting on eight mathematical benchmarks. The model trained on the DeepSeekMath corpus outperforms models trained on the other corpora, demonstrating that the selection and decontamination pipeline yields a higher quality pre‑training signal for downstream reasoning tasks.

The paper outlines and implicitly demonstrates the central trade-off in decontamination: stricter filtering reduces the risk of benchmark leakage but at the cost of recall of potentially valuable in‑domain data. Removing every page that contains a 10‑gram match to a benchmark string errs on the side of safety, but it inevitably removes web pages that include common mathematical expressions or phrases that also happen to appear in benchmark problems. Conversely, looser filtering preserves more candidate math content but increases the chance that evaluation items—or near exact copies—remain in the training corpus.

For corpus construction, the team starts from a seed math corpus (OpenWebMath) and trains a fastText classifier to recall similar pages from a deduplicated Common Crawl. They experimented iteratively, ranking pages by the classifier score and preserving top subsets of tokens (they tried top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens in experiments) and, in the first iteration, initially kept the top 40B tokens. They added diversity to the seed set by identifying domains where over 10% of pages were initially collected, manually annotating math‑relevant URL paths in those domains, and re‑training the classifier. After four iterations this process yielded 35.5M math pages totaling 120B tokens; the team stopped when the fourth iteration added little new content (about 98% of the data had already been collected in the third iteration).

The paper follows prior practice (cited as the same approach used in DeepSeek‑Coder) of removing web pages containing questions or answers that appear in these benchmarks. The authors state the matching rule succinctly: any text segment containing a 10‑gram string that matches exactly with any substring from the evaluation benchmarks is removed; for benchmark texts shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens, exact matching is performed on the full benchmark text. This uniform application across both English and Chinese benchmark sets defines a strict, reproducible decontamination policy tied to the evaluation suite.

Concretely, any text segment in the candidate web corpus that contains a 10‑gram (a consecutive sequence of 10 tokens) which exactly matches a substring from an evaluation benchmark is removed from the math training corpus. For evaluation texts that are shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens long, exact matching at their full length is applied and pages with exact matches are filtered. This rule is applied after the candidate math pages are recalled and ranked; matching substrings are sought across English benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (e.g., CMATH, AGIEval) that the study uses for evaluation. The approach is intended to remove instances where an entire question, a long fragment of a question, or an answer is replicated verbatim in the training data.

Third, the authors balance automated and manual steps in a transparent way. Manual URL annotation supplemented the classifier to improve recall of diverse math pages; at the same time, the final decontamination rule remained strict to avoid benchmark leakage. Stopping criteria for iterative collection (e.g., fourth iteration added negligible new pages—98% collected earlier) are reported, so future replication attempts can adopt similar halting rules.

The paper’s pipeline embeds several reproducibility and ethics‑oriented design choices that are important for responsible large‑scale model development. First, to preserve trustworthy evaluation, the authors adopt an explicit, auditable decontamination rule (the exact n‑gram matching policy) and apply it uniformly across all targeted benchmarks in English and Chinese. Making the decontamination rule concrete (10‑gram primary threshold; 3‑gram minimum for short benchmark items) is a reproducibility decision: other teams can apply the same filter to their corpora and obtain comparable protection against leakage.
