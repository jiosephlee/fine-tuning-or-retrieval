## How DeepSeekMath Built a 120B-Token Math Corpus from the Web

# How DeepSeekMath Built a 120B-Token Math Corpus from the Web

If you’ve ever wondered how researchers gather massive, high-quality datasets for training language models specialized in math, the DeepSeekMath project offers a fascinating case study. Their team managed to extract a whopping 120 billion math-related tokens from the noisy, sprawling Common Crawl web archive — and they did it with a clever combination of machine learning, human insight, and careful data engineering.

In this post, we’ll take you behind the scenes of the DeepSeekMath Corpus creation process. Whether you’re a data engineer, an ML student, or just curious about how to build domain-specific corpora from messy web data, you’ll find practical lessons and insights here.

## The Challenge: Mining Math Data from the Web

Common Crawl is a treasure trove of web pages — but it’s also a wild jungle of content, much of which isn’t relevant to math. The key challenge is to sift through billions of web pages and pick out those rich in mathematical content, while avoiding duplicates, irrelevant pages, and contamination from benchmark test data.

DeepSeekMath’s goal was to build a large-scale, high-quality math corpus that could fuel the training of a powerful math reasoning language model. To do this, they needed a scalable, precise, and iterative approach.

## Step 1: Starting with a Seed Corpus and a fastText Classifier

The team began with a small but high-quality seed corpus called OpenWebMath — a collection of math-related web texts. This seed served as the positive examples for training a fastText classifier, a lightweight and efficient text classification model.

They also sampled a large set of random web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples. With this training data, the fastText model learned to distinguish math-related pages from the rest.

This classifier became the first filter to recall math-like pages from the massive Common Crawl dataset.

## Step 2: Deduplication and Ranking

Common Crawl contains many duplicate or near-duplicate pages, which can bias training and waste resources. To tackle this, the team applied URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication techniques, reducing the dataset to about 40 billion unique HTML pages.

Next, they ran the fastText classifier on these pages and ranked them by their predicted math relevance scores. Only the top-ranking pages were kept for further processing, ensuring quality over quantity.

## Step 3: Iterative Domain Mining and Human Annotation

Even after the first pass, many math pages remained undiscovered because the initial classifier was trained on a limited set of positive examples.

To improve recall, the team analyzed the domains of the collected pages. They identified math-related domains where over 10% of pages were already collected (for example, mathoverflow.net). Then, they manually annotated specific URLs within these domains that contained math content.

These newly identified pages were added to the seed corpus, and the fastText classifier was retrained with this enriched dataset. This iterative process — mining, annotating, retraining — was repeated four times, each time expanding the coverage and improving the classifier’s precision.

By the fourth iteration, the team had collected 35.5 million math-related web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens.

## Step 4: Benchmark Decontamination

A crucial step was to ensure that the training data did not leak questions or answers from evaluation benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH. To prevent this, the team filtered out any web pages containing exact 10-gram matches with benchmark texts.

For shorter benchmark texts, they used exact matching on smaller n-grams (3-grams or more). This careful decontamination ensured that the model’s evaluation would be fair and not artificially inflated by seeing test data during training.

## Why This Approach Matters

- **Scalability:** Using a fastText classifier allowed the team to efficiently process tens of billions of web pages.

- **Precision and Recall:** The iterative domain mining and human annotation improved the classifier’s ability to find diverse math content beyond the initial seed.

- **Quality Control:** Deduplication and benchmark decontamination ensured the dataset was clean and reliable.

- **Multilingual Coverage:** Unlike some previous corpora focused only on English, DeepSeekMath’s corpus includes high-quality math content in multiple languages, notably Chinese, boosting performance on multilingual benchmarks.

## Lessons for Data Engineers and ML Practitioners

- **Start Small, Then Scale:** Begin with a trusted seed dataset to train your initial classifier before mining large-scale web data.

- **Iterate and Refine:** Use domain analysis and human annotation to expand your positive examples and improve your classifier iteratively.

- **Deduplicate Early:** Removing duplicates early saves computational resources and improves data quality.

- **Beware of Benchmark Leakage:** Always filter out data that overlaps with your evaluation benchmarks to maintain fair assessments.

- **Balance Quantity and Quality:** More data isn’t always better — ranking and filtering help maintain a high-quality corpus.

## Final Thoughts

Building a domain-specific corpus from noisy web data is a complex but rewarding endeavor. DeepSeekMath’s approach demonstrates how combining machine learning classifiers, human expertise, and rigorous data engineering can unlock vast amounts of valuable data.

Their 120B-token math corpus powered a language model that rivals much larger closed-source models on challenging math benchmarks — proving that smart data curation can sometimes trump sheer model size.

If you’re embarking on a similar journey to build specialized datasets, take a page from DeepSeekMath’s playbook: start with quality seeds, iterate with care, and always keep an eye on data cleanliness.

Happy data hunting!

## Why a 7B Model Can Rival a 540B Model for Math: The Power of Data and Curriculum

# Why a 7B Model Can Rival a 540B Model for Math: The Power of Data and Curriculum

When it comes to large language models (LLMs) tackling complex mathematical reasoning, the common assumption is that bigger is always better. After all, models like GPT-4 and Minerva 540B boast hundreds of billions of parameters, and their impressive performance seems to justify the massive scale. But what if a much smaller model—just 7 billion parameters—could compete with these giants? That’s exactly what the DeepSeekMath team has demonstrated, and their approach offers some fascinating insights into how data quality, training strategy, and curriculum design can sometimes outweigh sheer size.

Let’s unpack how DeepSeekMath’s 7B model manages to punch well above its weight class and what this means for the future of AI research and development.

## The Surprising Feat: DeepSeekMath-Base 7B vs. Minerva 540B

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is a language model specifically trained to excel at mathematical reasoning. Despite having only 7 billion parameters—about 77 times smaller than Minerva 540B—it achieves comparable or even better results on challenging math benchmarks like GSM8K and the competition-level MATH dataset.

How is this possible? The answer lies in a few key factors that the DeepSeekMath team carefully engineered:

### 1. Targeted, High-Quality Pretraining Data: The DeepSeekMath Corpus

Instead of relying on generic web data or massive but noisy datasets, DeepSeekMath’s creators built a specialized corpus of 120 billion math-related tokens. This dataset was painstakingly curated from Common Crawl web pages using a fastText classifier trained to identify high-quality mathematical content.

What makes this corpus stand out?

- **Scale and Focus:** It’s nearly 7 times larger than the math data used by Minerva and 9 times larger than OpenWebMath, another math-focused dataset.
- **Multilingual Content:** Unlike many math datasets that focus solely on English, DeepSeekMath’s corpus includes significant Chinese content, boosting performance on Chinese math benchmarks.
- **Iterative Refinement:** The data collection pipeline involved multiple rounds of classification, human annotation, and filtering to ensure quality and reduce contamination from benchmark test data.

This targeted data selection means the model spends its training “attention” on the kind of content that directly improves mathematical reasoning, rather than sifting through irrelevant or low-quality text.

### 2. Code-First Initialization: Starting with a Coding Model

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B didn’t start from scratch or from a general language model. Instead, it was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—a model already trained extensively on code.

Why does this matter?

- **Code as Structured Reasoning:** Programming languages require precise, logical thinking and step-by-step problem solving, skills that overlap heavily with mathematical reasoning.
- **Better Foundation:** The team’s experiments showed that code training before math training improves the model’s ability to solve math problems both with and without tool use.
- **Synergy with Math Training:** Starting from a code-trained model accelerates and enhances the subsequent math-focused training phase.

This approach offers a partial answer to the long-debated question: *Does code training improve reasoning abilities?* For math, the answer appears to be a confident yes.

### 3. Mixed Data Distribution: Balancing Math, Code, and Natural Language

During training, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was exposed to a carefully balanced mix of data:

- 56% from the DeepSeekMath Corpus (math-focused web data)
- 20% from GitHub code repositories
- 10% from arXiv papers (though these were found to be less impactful)
- 10% from natural language data in English and Chinese
- 4% from AlgebraicStack (mathematical code)

This blend ensures the model develops a broad yet focused understanding, combining mathematical knowledge, coding skills, and natural language fluency. The inclusion of code and natural language alongside math data helps maintain general reasoning and programming capabilities, preventing the model from becoming too narrow.

### 4. Avoiding Overreliance on ArXiv Papers

Interestingly, the team found that including arXiv papers—commonly used in other math-focused models—did not significantly improve mathematical reasoning performance. In some cases, it even slightly degraded results.

This suggests that not all “math data” is equally valuable. The style, clarity, and structure of web-based math content and code may be more conducive to training models that solve math problems effectively.

## What Does This Mean for Model Development?

The DeepSeekMath results challenge the notion that bigger models are always necessary for top-tier performance in specialized domains like math. Instead, they highlight the critical importance of:

- **Data Quality Over Quantity:** Carefully curated, domain-specific datasets can unlock capabilities that brute-force scaling alone cannot.
- **Curriculum Design:** Starting from a code-trained model and then fine-tuning on math data creates a more effective learning trajectory.
- **Multilingual and Diverse Data:** Including multiple languages and data types broadens the model’s applicability and robustness.
- **Efficient Use of Resources:** Smaller models trained on better data are more cost-effective and accessible for research and practical applications.

For researchers and developers, this means that investing effort into data engineering and training strategies can yield outsized returns, potentially democratizing access to powerful AI models without requiring massive computational budgets.

## Looking Ahead: The Future of Cost-Effective AI

DeepSeekMath’s success opens exciting avenues for future work:

- **Expanding Data Pipelines:** Further refining data selection and incorporating more diverse, high-quality sources could push smaller models even closer to or beyond the performance of giants.
- **Exploring Code and Math Synergies:** Understanding how code training benefits reasoning can inform curricula for other domains requiring structured logic.
- **Reinforcement Learning Enhancements:** The team’s novel Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) method shows promise for improving reasoning abilities efficiently.
- **Addressing Geometry and Theorem-Proof:** While DeepSeekMath excels at quantitative reasoning, there’s room to improve on geometry and formal proofs, possibly through targeted data and training.

In summary, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that with the right data and training approach, a 7B parameter model can rival a 540B parameter behemoth in mathematical reasoning. This is a powerful reminder that in AI, *how* you train can be just as important as *how big* your model is.

## Code Training Improves Mathematical Reasoning — Here's Why (and How to Do It)

# Code Training Improves Mathematical Reasoning — Here's Why (and How to Do It)

Mathematical reasoning is one of the toughest nuts for language models to crack. The structured, step-by-step logic required to solve math problems often trips up even the most advanced AI. But what if there’s a secret ingredient that can boost a model’s math skills? Recent research from the DeepSeekMath team reveals that training language models on code before diving into math data can significantly improve their mathematical reasoning abilities — both when solving problems step-by-step in natural language and when writing code to solve math problems.

In this post, we’ll break down the experiments behind this discovery, explain why code training helps, and share practical tips for anyone looking to train or fine-tune models for math reasoning.

---

## Why Code Training Might Help Math Reasoning

At first glance, code and math might seem like different beasts. But they share a lot in common:

- **Logical structure:** Both require precise, stepwise logic.
- **Symbolic manipulation:** Code often involves variables, functions, and expressions — just like math.
- **Problem-solving mindset:** Writing code to solve a problem is a form of reasoning.

Because of these parallels, training a model on code can help it learn how to think more logically and systematically, which transfers well to math problems.

---

## The Experiments: Two-Stage vs One-Stage Training

The DeepSeekMath team ran a series of experiments using a 1.3 billion parameter language model to test how code training affects math reasoning. They compared different training strategies:

### Two-Stage Training

1. **Code Training First:** The model was trained on 400 billion tokens of code.
2. **Math Training Second:** Then, it was trained on 150 billion tokens of math data.

### One-Stage Training

- The model was trained on a **mixture** of code and math tokens simultaneously (400B code + 150B math).

### Control Experiment

- Instead of code, the first stage used general language data (non-code) before math training.

---

## What They Found

### Code Training Boosts Program-Aided Math Reasoning

- Models trained on code first were much better at solving math problems by writing Python programs.
- This was true both in the two-stage setup (code then math) and the one-stage mixed training.
- The code training helped the model understand how to translate math problems into executable code, improving accuracy.

### Code Training Also Helps Chain-of-Thought Reasoning (Without Tools)

- Even when solving math problems step-by-step in natural language (without writing code), code training gave the model a boost.
- In the two-stage setup, code training alone improved math reasoning moderately.
- Adding math training after code training led to the best results.

### Mixing Code and Math in One-Stage Training Has Trade-Offs

- When code and math tokens were mixed in one training stage, the model maintained strong coding skills and improved program-aided math reasoning.
- However, this approach slightly hurt the model’s ability to do chain-of-thought math reasoning without tools.
- The researchers suspect this is because the model size (1.3B parameters) limits how well it can learn both simultaneously.

### Code Training Beats General Language Training

- Using general language data instead of code in the first stage did not improve math reasoning as much.
- This suggests that code data has unique benefits for math skills.

---

## What About Catastrophic Forgetting?

A common problem in multi-stage training is **catastrophic forgetting** — when a model forgets what it learned in earlier stages after training on new data.

- The two-stage training (code then math) showed some forgetting of coding skills after math training.
- Mixing code and math tokens in one-stage training helped reduce this forgetting, preserving coding ability while improving math reasoning.

---

## Practical Tips for Practitioners

If you’re training or fine-tuning language models for math reasoning, here are some takeaways from this research:

1. **Start with Code Training:** Pretrain your model on a large corpus of code before math data to build strong logical reasoning foundations.

2. **Follow Up with Math Training:** After code training, fine-tune on high-quality math data to specialize the model’s math skills.

3. **Consider Model Size:** Smaller models may struggle to learn code and math simultaneously. For them, two-stage training might be better.

4. **Mixing Code and Math:** If you have a larger model or want to avoid forgetting, mixing code and math tokens in one training stage can be effective, but watch out for potential trade-offs in chain-of-thought reasoning.

5. **Use High-Quality Math Data:** The DeepSeekMath team built a massive, carefully filtered math corpus from web data, which was crucial for their success.

6. **Evaluate Both Program-Aided and Chain-of-Thought Reasoning:** Test your model’s ability to solve math problems both by writing code and by explaining solutions step-by-step in natural language.

---

## Why Does Code Training Help?

The experiments suggest that code training teaches the model to:

- Understand and generate structured, logical sequences.
- Manipulate symbols and variables effectively.
- Translate problem statements into executable steps.

These skills transfer naturally to mathematical reasoning, whether the model is writing code or explaining solutions in words.

---

## Final Thoughts

The DeepSeekMath research provides strong evidence that code training is a powerful tool to boost mathematical reasoning in language models. By leveraging the logical and structured nature of code, models become better problem solvers — both when writing programs and when reasoning step-by-step.

For AI researchers and practitioners aiming to build math-savvy models, incorporating code data into your training pipeline is a promising strategy. Whether you choose a two-stage or one-stage approach, the key is to balance code and math data thoughtfully to maximize your model’s reasoning abilities.

## GRPO — A Lightweight RL Trick That Boosts Math Skills Without a Critic

# GRPO — A Lightweight RL Trick That Boosts Math Skills Without a Critic

If you’ve been following the latest advances in AI, you’ve probably heard about how large language models (LLMs) are getting better and better at solving math problems. But did you know that a clever reinforcement learning (RL) technique called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** is helping these models sharpen their math skills — all while using less memory and training more efficiently?

In this post, we’ll break down what GRPO is, why it’s a game-changer compared to traditional RL methods like PPO, and how it uses group-based baselines instead of a learned critic to boost mathematical reasoning. We’ll also explore the difference between **outcome supervision** and **process supervision**, two ways GRPO guides the model’s learning. Whether you’re an AI enthusiast or just curious about how math-solving AI gets smarter, this post will give you an intuitive understanding of GRPO.

---

## The Challenge: Teaching AI to Reason Mathematically

Mathematical reasoning is tough for AI because it requires structured, step-by-step thinking — not just pattern matching. To improve, models often go through a phase called **reinforcement learning (RL)**, where they learn from feedback signals (rewards) to generate better answers.

One popular RL method is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO uses two components:

- **Policy model**: The AI model generating answers.
- **Critic model (value function)**: A separate model that estimates how good the policy’s actions are, helping stabilize training.

While PPO works well, training a critic model alongside the policy is **resource-intensive** — it requires lots of memory and compute power. Plus, in math tasks, the reward often comes only at the end of a solution, making it tricky for the critic to estimate values at every step.

---

## Enter GRPO: Simplifying RL by Dropping the Critic

**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** is a fresh take on PPO that **removes the need for a critic model altogether**. Instead of learning a value function, GRPO estimates a baseline reward from a **group of outputs** generated for the same question.

### How does that work?

Imagine you ask the AI model a math question and sample multiple answers (say, 64 different solutions). Each answer gets a reward score based on correctness or quality. GRPO then:

1. **Calculates the average reward of all these answers** — this becomes the baseline.
2. **Compares each individual answer’s reward to this group average** to see if it’s better or worse.
3. **Uses this relative difference as the advantage** to update the model.

This group-based baseline acts like a “yardstick” — answers better than average get reinforced, worse ones get penalized.

---

## Why is GRPO Better?

### 1. **Memory Efficiency**

Without a critic model, GRPO saves a huge chunk of memory and computational resources. This is especially important for large models where every bit of efficiency counts.

### 2. **Stable Training**

By using the group average as a baseline, GRPO naturally normalizes rewards, reducing variance and stabilizing training. It aligns well with how reward models are trained — often on comparisons between answers to the same question.

### 3. **Simplicity**

No need to train an extra value function. This makes the RL pipeline simpler and easier to maintain.

---

## Outcome Supervision vs. Process Supervision: Two Ways to Guide Learning

GRPO can work with two types of supervision signals:

### Outcome Supervision

- The reward is given **only at the end** of the entire answer.
- The advantage for every token in the answer is the same — the normalized final reward.
- Think of it as judging a math test by the final score only.

### Process Supervision

- The reward is given **at each reasoning step** within the answer.
- The advantage for each token depends on the sum of future step rewards.
- This is like grading a math test by checking each step of the solution, rewarding partial progress.

Process supervision provides **finer-grained feedback**, which can help the model learn more effectively, especially for complex multi-step problems.

---

## Visual Metaphor: Running a Math Competition

Imagine a math competition where multiple students solve the same problem. Instead of having a judge who scores each step (critic model), you look at the group’s average score and see who did better or worse than the average.

- Students who scored above average get praised and encouraged.
- Those below average get feedback to improve.

This group-relative approach motivates students relative to their peers, without needing a detailed step-by-step judge.

---

## When to Consider GRPO Over PPO?

- **Limited resources:** If training memory or compute is a bottleneck, GRPO’s lightweight design is a big plus.
- **Sparse rewards:** When rewards come mostly at the end of sequences (like math problems), training a critic can be tricky — GRPO sidesteps this.
- **Comparative reward models:** If your reward model is trained on comparisons between outputs, GRPO’s group-relative advantage fits naturally.
- **Desire for simpler pipelines:** GRPO reduces complexity by removing the critic.

---

## Wrapping Up

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a smart, efficient reinforcement learning method that helps language models improve their mathematical reasoning without the overhead of training a critic model. By leveraging group-based baselines, it stabilizes training, saves resources, and aligns well with comparative reward signals.

Whether you’re building math-solving AI or just fascinated by how these models get smarter, GRPO offers a fresh perspective on reinforcement learning — proving that sometimes, less is more.

## From SFT to RL: A Unified Paradigm to Align LLMs for Better Reasoning

# From SFT to RL: A Unified Paradigm to Align LLMs for Better Reasoning

Large language models (LLMs) have made remarkable strides in mathematical reasoning and complex problem-solving. Yet, unlocking their full potential requires more than just scaling up model size or training data. It demands carefully designed training and alignment strategies that guide these models toward better reasoning capabilities. A recent paper, *DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models*, offers a fresh perspective by unifying various training methods—Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and their novel Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—under a single conceptual framework.

In this blog post, we'll break down this unified paradigm, explain its three core pillars, and explore how different choices in each pillar shape the way LLMs learn to reason better. Whether you're a researcher or engineer working on alignment or reinforcement learning (RL) pipelines, this overview will help you understand the landscape and design more effective training strategies.

---

## The Challenge: Aligning LLMs for Reasoning

LLMs are powerful but not perfect. When it comes to reasoning—especially mathematical reasoning—they often produce inconsistent or incorrect answers. To improve this, researchers have developed various training methods that go beyond simple supervised learning. These methods incorporate feedback signals, preferences, or rewards to nudge the model toward better outputs.

However, these methods often appear disconnected or specialized. The DeepSeekMath paper bridges this gap by showing that all these approaches can be understood as variations of a single underlying training paradigm. This insight helps us see the bigger picture and make informed choices when designing training pipelines.

---

## The Unified Paradigm: Three Pillars of Training

At the heart of this unified view is a simple but powerful equation describing how model parameters are updated during training:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t}) \right]
\]

Let's unpack this:

- **Data Source (\(\mathcal{D}\))**: Where the training data comes from—questions and model outputs.
- **Reward Function (\(\pi_{rf}\))**: The signal that tells the model how good an output is.
- **Algorithm (\(\mathcal{A}\))**: How the reward signal is processed into a gradient coefficient \(GC\) that guides parameter updates.

Each of these pillars plays a crucial role in shaping the model's learning behavior.

---

### 1. Data Source: Offline vs. Online Sampling

The data source determines the examples the model learns from. There are two main types:

- **Offline Sampling**: Training data is fixed, often coming from outputs generated by a static model (e.g., the initial supervised fine-tuned model). Methods like RFT and DPO use offline data.
  
- **Online Sampling**: Training data is dynamically generated by the current policy model during training. This allows the model to explore new outputs and learn from its own behavior. Online RFT, PPO, and GRPO use online data.

**Why does this matter?**  
Online sampling tends to outperform offline because it exposes the model to a wider variety of outputs, including its own mistakes and improvements. This dynamic feedback loop helps the model adapt more effectively.

---

### 2. Reward Function: Rule-Based vs. Learned Models

The reward function provides the training signal indicating which outputs are better.

- **Rule-Based Rewards**: Simple heuristics or correctness checks (e.g., whether the answer is right or wrong). These are easy to implement but can be coarse and binary.
  
- **Learned Reward Models**: Neural networks trained to score outputs based on human preferences or quality judgments. These provide nuanced, graded feedback.

**Key insight from DeepSeekMath:**  
GRPO leverages learned reward models to assign *relative* advantages within groups of outputs, allowing the model to be reinforced or penalized proportionally to output quality. This contrasts with methods like online RFT, which treat all correct answers equally and ignore incorrect ones.

---

### 3. Algorithm: Processing Rewards into Updates

The algorithm defines how the reward signal translates into parameter updates via the gradient coefficient \(GC\).

- **SFT**: Simply maximizes likelihood on human-labeled data, no reward involved.
  
- **RFT**: Filters outputs based on correctness and fine-tunes on the filtered set.
  
- **DPO**: Uses pairwise preference comparisons to adjust likelihood ratios.
  
- **PPO**: An actor-critic RL method that uses a learned value function to estimate advantages and applies clipping to stabilize updates.
  
- **GRPO**: A novel variant that removes the need for a value function by estimating baselines from groups of sampled outputs, reducing memory and computational costs.

**Why is GRPO important?**  
By estimating baselines from groups of outputs, GRPO aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models and simplifies training. It also allows fine-grained, step-wise supervision (process supervision), which further improves reasoning performance.

---

## Putting It All Together: How Choices Shape Learning

The DeepSeekMath paper shows that these three pillars interact to define the training method's effectiveness:

- **Offline data + rule-based rewards + simple filtering** (RFT) can improve models but may plateau.
- **Online data + learned reward models + RL algorithms** (PPO, GRPO) enable continuous improvement and better generalization.
- **GRPO's group-relative advantage estimation** allows more efficient and effective reinforcement learning, especially for complex reasoning tasks.

Moreover, the paper's experiments reveal:

- Online training outperforms offline by exposing the model to evolving outputs.
- Using learned reward models with relative advantages leads to better gradient signals than binary rewards.
- Process supervision (rewarding intermediate reasoning steps) further boosts performance.
- Iterative RL, where reward models and policies are updated in cycles, yields significant gains.

---

## Why Does Reinforcement Learning Work?

Interestingly, the paper finds that RL improves the model's *output distribution robustness* rather than its fundamental reasoning ability. In other words, RL helps the model produce correct answers more consistently among its top outputs, rather than making it smarter per se.

This insight suggests that RL aligns the model's preferences and output probabilities, making it more reliable in practice.

---

## Future Directions: Designing More Effective Alignment Pipelines

The unified paradigm highlights several promising avenues:

- **Data Source**: Explore advanced sampling strategies (e.g., tree search) and efficient inference to improve exploration quality.
- **Reward Function**: Develop reward models with better generalization, uncertainty estimation, and fine-grained process supervision.
- **Algorithm**: Create RL methods robust to noisy rewards and capable of weak-to-strong learning transitions.

By carefully tuning these pillars, researchers and engineers can build LLMs that reason more accurately and reliably.

---

## Final Thoughts

The DeepSeekMath paper offers a valuable lens to understand and unify diverse training methods for aligning LLMs in reasoning tasks. Its introduction of GRPO and the comprehensive analysis of data, rewards, and algorithms provide practical guidance for advancing LLM alignment.

If you're working on improving LLM reasoning or designing RL pipelines, embracing this unified paradigm can help you make principled choices and accelerate progress toward more capable AI systems.

## Practical Guide: Instruction-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning for Math LLMs

# Practical Guide: Instruction-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning for Math LLMs

Mathematical reasoning remains one of the most challenging frontiers for large language models (LLMs). The recent DeepSeekMath project has made impressive strides in pushing open-source math LLMs closer to the performance of proprietary giants like GPT-4. If you’re an ML practitioner eager to fine-tune or reinforce your own math-focused LLM, this post breaks down practical insights from DeepSeekMath’s instruction tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline. We’ll cover recommended datasets, sampling strategies, reward model design, hyperparameter tuning, and evaluation tips — all aimed at helping you reproduce and build upon their success.

---

## Why Instruction Tuning and RL Matter for Math LLMs

DeepSeekMath’s journey shows that pre-training on a large, high-quality math corpus is just the start. To unlock strong step-by-step reasoning and tool use, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with carefully curated instruction data is essential. But even then, reinforcement learning can push the model’s performance further by aligning it with reward signals that reflect mathematical correctness and reasoning quality.

The key takeaway? A well-designed pipeline combining instruction tuning and RL can significantly boost your model’s math problem-solving abilities — but it requires thoughtful choices at every step.

---

## Building Your Instruction-Tuning Dataset: What Works

DeepSeekMath’s instruction tuning dataset is a blend of English and Chinese math problems, covering a wide range of topics and difficulty levels. Here’s what they found effective:

- **Diverse Problem Types:** Include problems solved with chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, program-of-thought (PoT) where the model writes Python code, and tool-integrated reasoning that combines natural language and code execution.

- **Multilingual Coverage:** Incorporate both English and Chinese problems to improve multilingual math reasoning. DeepSeekMath’s multilingual corpus helped it outperform English-only models on Chinese benchmarks.

- **Size Matters:** Their instruction tuning dataset contains around 776K examples, sourced from annotated GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, Lila-OOD, and Chinese K-12 math problems.

- **Human Annotation:** High-quality solutions with step-by-step explanations or code are crucial for guiding the model’s reasoning process.

**Practical Tip:** Start with publicly available datasets like GSM8K and MATH, augment with programmatic solutions, and if possible, add multilingual data. Annotate or verify solutions to ensure quality.

---

## Sampling Strategies for Reinforcement Learning

Sampling diverse outputs from your model is critical for effective RL training. DeepSeekMath uses a group-based sampling approach:

- **Group Sampling:** For each question, sample a group of outputs (e.g., 64 samples per question). This allows the RL algorithm to compare outputs within the same group, which is essential for their Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) method.

- **Nucleus Sampling:** Use nucleus (top-p) sampling to generate diverse candidate solutions, avoiding overly deterministic outputs that limit learning.

- **Batch Size and Context Length:** DeepSeekMath uses a batch size of 1024 and a max sequence length of 1024 tokens during RL, balancing computational efficiency and context coverage.

**Practical Tip:** When implementing RL, ensure you generate multiple diverse outputs per prompt to enable relative reward comparisons. Adjust batch sizes and sequence lengths based on your hardware constraints.

---

## Reward Model Construction: Outcome vs. Process Supervision

Reward models provide the feedback signal that guides RL. DeepSeekMath explores two types:

- **Outcome Supervision:** Assigns a single reward at the end of the entire output, reflecting overall correctness.

- **Process Supervision:** Provides fine-grained rewards at each reasoning step, enabling the model to learn which parts of its reasoning are good or need improvement.

DeepSeekMath found that process supervision (step-level rewards) leads to better performance than outcome-only rewards, especially for complex math problems.

**Practical Tip:** If possible, build reward models that can evaluate intermediate reasoning steps, not just final answers. This requires annotated data with step-level correctness labels but can significantly improve learning.

---

## Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Efficient RL for Math LLMs

GRPO is DeepSeekMath’s novel RL algorithm designed to improve math reasoning while reducing computational overhead:

- **No Critic Model:** Unlike PPO, GRPO does not train a separate value function, saving memory and compute.

- **Group-Based Baseline:** It calculates advantages by comparing rewards within a group of sampled outputs for the same question, normalizing rewards relative to the group mean and variance.

- **KL Regularization:** Instead of adding KL penalties to the reward, GRPO directly adds a KL divergence term between the current policy and a reference policy to the loss, stabilizing training.

- **Hyperparameters:** DeepSeekMath uses a KL coefficient of 0.04, group size of 64 outputs per question, and a policy learning rate of 1e-6.

**Practical Tip:** When implementing GRPO or similar RL methods, carefully tune the KL coefficient to balance exploration and policy stability. Use sufficiently large group sizes to get reliable relative reward estimates.

---

## Hyperparameter Tips for Stable and Effective Training

- **Learning Rates:** Use a low learning rate for RL fine-tuning (e.g., 1e-6) to avoid catastrophic forgetting and maintain instruction-tuned capabilities.

- **Batch Sizes:** Larger batch sizes (e.g., 1024) help stabilize gradient estimates but require more memory.

- **Group Size:** Sampling 64 outputs per question strikes a good balance between reward signal quality and computational cost.

- **KL Coefficient:** A moderate KL penalty (around 0.04) helps prevent the policy from drifting too far from the initial instruction-tuned model.

- **Number of RL Iterations:** Iterative RL with multiple rounds of reward model updates and policy fine-tuning yields better results.

## Evaluation Practices: Measuring What Matters

DeepSeekMath evaluates models on a broad suite of benchmarks covering:

- **English and Chinese Math Reasoning:** Datasets like GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and Gaokao-MathQA test step-by-step reasoning and problem-solving.

- **Tool Use:** Assess program-of-thought capabilities by allowing models to generate and execute Python code.

- **Formal Mathematics:** Evaluate informal-to-formal theorem proving tasks to test formal reasoning.

- **General Reasoning and Coding:** Use MMLU, BBH, HumanEval, and MBPP to profile broader language understanding and coding skills.

They also measure both zero-shot and few-shot performance, and track metrics like accuracy, Pass@K, and Maj@K to understand model robustness.

**Practical Tip:** Use a diverse set of benchmarks to get a holistic view of your model’s math reasoning. Evaluate both natural language and programmatic solutions, and consider multilingual tests if applicable.

---

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

- **Insufficient Data Diversity:** Relying solely on English math problems or a narrow set of problem types limits generalization.

- **Ignoring Multilingual Data:** Including Chinese and other languages can improve performance on non-English benchmarks.

- **Overfitting Reward Models:** Reward models trained on limited data may not generalize well, leading to RL overfitting.

- **Small Group Sizes in RL:** Too few samples per question reduce the reliability of relative reward estimates.

- **High Learning Rates in RL:** Can cause instability and degrade instruction-tuned capabilities.

- **Benchmark Contamination:** Ensure your training data does not overlap with evaluation benchmarks to avoid inflated results.

## Final Thoughts

DeepSeekMath’s approach offers a practical blueprint for anyone looking to enhance math reasoning in LLMs through instruction tuning and reinforcement learning. The key is combining a large, diverse instruction dataset with an efficient RL algorithm like GRPO that leverages group-relative rewards and process supervision.

By carefully curating your datasets, sampling diverse outputs, building fine-grained reward models, tuning hyperparameters thoughtfully, and evaluating comprehensively, you can push your math LLM’s capabilities to new heights — all while keeping training costs manageable.

## What We Learned About arXiv: Why Research Papers Might Not Help Math Benchmarks

# What We Learned About arXiv: Why Research Papers Might Not Help Math Benchmarks

When it comes to training language models to tackle complex mathematical problems, it might seem obvious that feeding them tons of research papers—like those from arXiv—would be a goldmine. After all, these papers are packed with advanced math, proofs, and detailed reasoning. But a recent study from the DeepSeekMath team challenges this assumption in a surprising way: arXiv-heavy data didn’t improve math benchmark performance and sometimes even made it worse.

Let’s dive into what they found, why this might be the case, and what it means for future efforts in building math-savvy AI.

## The Surprising Result: arXiv Papers Don’t Boost Math Reasoning

DeepSeekMath is a new open-source language model focused on mathematical reasoning. The team experimented with different types of training data to see what helps the model get better at solving math problems, from grade school level all the way to college-level competitions.

Among the data sources they tested were:

- **Common Crawl web pages filtered for math content** (their own DeepSeekMath Corpus)
- **OpenWebMath** and other curated math datasets
- **arXiv papers**, which are scientific preprints often containing advanced math

What they found was unexpected: training on arXiv papers alone did not improve the model’s performance on a variety of math benchmarks. In some cases, it even caused a decline in accuracy. This was consistent across different model sizes, including a 1.3 billion parameter model and a 7 billion parameter model.

## Why Might arXiv Data Not Help?

This counterintuitive finding raises the question: why doesn’t the rich mathematical content in arXiv papers translate into better math reasoning for language models?

Here are some possible reasons:

### 1. **Complexity and Style of Research Papers**

Research papers are often written in a highly formal, dense, and technical style. They include specialized notation, jargon, and assume a lot of background knowledge. This can make it harder for a language model to learn generalizable reasoning patterns compared to more accessible math content like textbooks, problem solutions, or educational web pages.

### 2. **Mismatch Between Training Data and Benchmark Tasks**

The math benchmarks used to evaluate models (like GSM8K or MATH) often focus on problem-solving skills, step-by-step reasoning, and clear explanations. ArXiv papers, on the other hand, are more about presenting novel results and proofs, which might not align well with the style or type of reasoning needed for these benchmarks.

### 3. **Noise and Variability in arXiv Data**

While arXiv is a treasure trove of knowledge, it also contains drafts, incomplete proofs, and papers of varying quality. Without careful filtering and curation, this noise could confuse the model during training.

### 4. **Data Volume and Balance**

The DeepSeekMath team’s experiments showed that their own curated math corpus from Common Crawl, which is large and diverse, led to better results. It’s possible that the sheer volume and diversity of accessible math content on the web, combined with careful filtering, provide a more balanced and effective training signal than a large but more homogeneous set of research papers.

## Limitations and Open Questions

The DeepSeekMath authors are careful to note that their conclusion about arXiv data is not the final word. Some important caveats include:

- They did not explore the impact of arXiv data on *all* math-related tasks. For example, tasks like informalization of theorems (turning formal proofs into natural language explanations) might benefit more from arXiv content.
- They did not test mixing arXiv data with other types of data in different proportions, which might yield different results.
- The experiments were conducted on models up to 7 billion parameters. Larger models might be better able to leverage the complexity of arXiv papers.
- The preprocessing and cleaning of arXiv data can vary widely, and different pipelines might affect outcomes.

So, while arXiv data alone didn’t help in their experiments, it might still have value in other contexts or when combined thoughtfully with other data.

## What Does This Mean for Future Math Model Training?

If you’re building or fine-tuning a language model for mathematical reasoning, here are some takeaways from this study:

- **Don’t assume more research papers automatically mean better math skills.** The style and content of your training data matter a lot.
- **Focus on diverse, high-quality math content that aligns with your target tasks.** Educational web pages, problem sets with solutions, and code-based math reasoning examples might be more effective.
- **Consider multilingual and varied sources.** DeepSeekMath’s corpus included English and Chinese math content, which helped improve performance on both language benchmarks.
- **Be cautious with data curation.** Filtering out benchmark contamination and low-quality content is crucial.
- **Experiment with mixing data types.** Combining code, natural language math explanations, and formal math content might yield the best results.
- **Keep an eye on model scale.** Larger models might benefit differently from complex data like arXiv papers.

## Final Thoughts

The DeepSeekMath study reminds us that in AI training, more data isn’t always better—*the right data* is what counts. While arXiv papers are invaluable for human researchers, their direct use as training data for math reasoning models is not straightforward and may require more nuanced approaches.

As the field advances, we can expect more research into how to best harness the wealth of mathematical knowledge available online, including research papers, to build smarter and more capable AI assistants for math.

Stay tuned for more insights as the DeepSeekMath team and others continue to push the boundaries of mathematical reasoning in language models!

## Limitations and Next Steps for Open-Source Math LLMs

# Limitations and Next Steps for Open-Source Math LLMs

Mathematical reasoning has long been a tough nut to crack for language models. Recently, DeepSeekMath has made impressive strides, pushing open-source math-focused language models closer to the performance of giants like GPT-4. But as with any breakthrough, it’s important to take a step back and honestly assess where DeepSeekMath still falls short—and where the research community can head next to build even better models.

In this post, we’ll explore some of the key limitations of DeepSeekMath, especially in areas like geometry and theorem proving, as well as its challenges with few-shot learning and potential data biases. Then, we’ll dive into promising research directions that could help overcome these hurdles, including smarter reward models, more robust reinforcement learning techniques, advanced sampling strategies, and richer, more diverse datasets.

## Where DeepSeekMath Still Struggles

### Geometry and Theorem-Proof Reasoning

While DeepSeekMath shines on many quantitative reasoning benchmarks, its performance on geometry problems and formal theorem proving lags behind closed-source models like GPT-4. For example, the model struggles with problems involving triangles and ellipses—classic geometry topics that require spatial and visual reasoning as well as precise logical deductions.

This weakness likely stems from biases in the training data. DeepSeekMath’s pre-training corpus, though massive and carefully curated, may underrepresent geometry-focused content or formal proofs. Without sufficient exposure to these problem types during training, the model’s ability to generalize and reason in these domains remains limited.

### Few-Shot Learning Limitations

Another notable limitation is DeepSeekMath’s relatively weak few-shot learning capability. Unlike GPT-4, which can significantly boost its performance by conditioning on a handful of example problems, DeepSeekMath shows similar results whether it’s given zero-shot or few-shot prompts.

This suggests that while DeepSeekMath has learned strong foundational math reasoning skills, it may lack the flexibility or capacity to quickly adapt to new problem styles or instructions with minimal examples. This could be related to model size constraints or the nature of its training regimen.

### Potential Data Biases

DeepSeekMath’s training data is largely sourced from publicly available web pages filtered for mathematical content, supplemented with code and natural language data. While this approach uncovered a treasure trove of math-related text, it also introduces potential biases:

- **Domain Imbalance:** Certain math topics, like geometry or formal proofs, may be underrepresented compared to algebra or calculus.
- **Language Bias:** Although the corpus is multilingual, English and Chinese dominate, possibly limiting performance on other languages or culturally specific math problems.
- **Source Quality:** Surprisingly, including arXiv papers—a common practice in math model training—did not improve performance, hinting that not all “math data” is equally valuable or relevant.

These biases can shape what the model learns and where it struggles.

## Looking Ahead: Research Directions to Boost Math LLMs

The DeepSeekMath team has laid out several exciting avenues to address these limitations and push open-source math LLMs even further.

### 1. Building Better Reward Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has been key to improving DeepSeekMath’s reasoning abilities, but it relies heavily on reward models that score the quality of generated solutions. Current reward models can be noisy or limited in scope, which constrains how effectively RL can guide the model.

Future work aims to develop reward models that:

- **Generalize better** to out-of-distribution problems and diverse reasoning paths.
- **Reflect uncertainty**, helping the RL algorithm weigh confidence in feedback.
- **Provide fine-grained, step-by-step feedback** (process supervision) rather than just outcome-based rewards.

Improved reward models will enable more precise and reliable reinforcement learning.

### 2. Designing Robust Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

RL methods like DeepSeekMath’s Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have shown promise by reducing computational overhead and improving training efficiency. However, RL still faces challenges when reward signals are noisy or inconsistent.

Research into **weak-to-strong alignment** methods aims to create RL algorithms that can learn effectively even when reward feedback is imperfect. This robustness is crucial for complex tasks like math reasoning, where subtle errors can mislead the model.

### 3. Exploring Advanced Sampling and Decoding Strategies

The quality and diversity of data sampled during RL training greatly influence model improvement. Currently, DeepSeekMath uses relatively straightforward sampling methods.

In the future, integrating **advanced sampling techniques** such as tree search or other structured exploration methods could help the model discover more diverse and challenging problem-solving paths. Coupled with **efficient inference techniques**, these strategies can make training more effective and scalable.

### 4. Enhancing Dataset Diversity and Quality

Data is the foundation of any model’s capabilities. DeepSeekMath’s success in mining math content from Common Crawl is impressive, but there’s room to expand and diversify:

- **Targeted collection of underrepresented math domains**, like geometry and formal proofs, to balance the training corpus.
- **Inclusion of more languages and culturally diverse math problems** to broaden applicability.
- **Curated datasets with high-quality annotations and stepwise solutions** to support process supervision.

Better datasets will help models learn a wider range of mathematical reasoning skills and reduce biases.

## Final Thoughts

DeepSeekMath represents a major leap forward for open-source mathematical language models, demonstrating that with smart data curation and innovative training methods, smaller models can approach the performance of much larger closed-source systems. Yet, as we’ve seen, challenges remain—especially in geometry, theorem proving, few-shot learning, and data biases.

The path forward is clear: by developing more sophisticated reward models, robust RL algorithms, smarter sampling strategies, and richer datasets, the research community can build math LLMs that are not only more powerful but also more versatile and reliable.

If you’re excited about the future of AI-driven math reasoning, keep an eye on these developments. The next generation of open-source math models promises to be even more capable, helping students, researchers, and professionals tackle complex problems with unprecedented ease.
