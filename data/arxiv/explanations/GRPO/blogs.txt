# How a 7B Open Model Got Close to GPT-4 at Math

## Quick summary

DeepSeekMath is an open-source effort that pushed a 7B-parameter model to near state-of-the-art performance on hard mathematical reasoning benchmarks. The headline claims:

- DeepSeekMath-RL 7B reaches 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark using chain-of-thought reasoning, without external tool use or ensemble/voting.  
- With self-consistency (sampling 64 solutions and taking the majority), DeepSeekMath-7B’s score rises to 60.9% on MATH.  
- On GSM8K (grade-school math), the RL-tuned model reaches 88.2%.

Those numbers bring a compact, openly described model closer to large closed models (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini-Ultra) on standard math tests. Below I explain what these benchmarks are, why they’re difficult, and which engineering choices enabled a 7B model to perform so well.

## What the benchmarks measure

- GSM8K: grade-school level arithmetic and reasoning problems that require multi-step calculation and clear intermediate steps. Good for evaluating step-by-step numerical reasoning with chain-of-thought (CoT).
- MATH: competition-level math problems (olympiad / contest style). These are significantly harder than GSM8K: they demand deeper multi-step reasoning, symbolic manipulation, and sometimes creative problem decomposition.
- The paper evaluates both zero/few-shot chain-of-thought generation (pure language reasoning) and program-aided solving (write and run Python programs).

Two evaluation modes are important:
- Single-pass scoring: generate one solution and check correctness.
- Self-consistency: sample many independent chain-of-thought outputs (here 64), compute the answer for each, and pick the majority answer (a simple ensemble over decoded traces). Self-consistency often increases accuracy for reasoning tasks by aggregating multiple probable solution paths.

## Why MATH is hard

MATH problems:
- Are longer and require multi-step reasoning chains that must be both correct and coherent.
- Often need symbolic algebra, multi-case reasoning, or nontrivial insight — not just arithmetic.
- Penalize small mistakes: a single algebra slip or logical misstep breaks the final answer.
- Make model calibration and generation stability important — many sampled reasoning chains look plausible but are incorrect.

Because of that, closed models with lots of compute and data (GPT-4, Gemini-Ultra) have been leading; the novelty here is bringing an open 7B model into competitive range.

## The two technical levers that mattered

1) Large, carefully filtered math pre-training data (DeepSeekMath Corpus)
- The team mined Common Crawl for math-rich web pages using an iterative fastText classifier seeded from OpenWebMath and augmented with manual URL annotations.
- Iterations: recall → rank → expand seeds → retrain classifier. After four iterations they collected ~35.5M math pages totalling ~120B tokens.
- They decontaminated the corpus to avoid benchmarking leakage (10-gram substring filtering).
- Models pre-trained on this math-heavy corpus (mixed with code, arXiv, natural language) showed substantial gains. The base model (initialized from a code-trained 7B) trained with 56% math tokens achieved strong baselines: e.g., 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH for the base model before instruction tuning / RL.

Why this helps: high-quality, domain-relevant pre-training data focuses the model on mathematical language, notation, and problem patterns. Importantly, the corpus was multilingual (English + Chinese), which improved performance on non-English math benchmarks too.

2) An efficient RL algorithm tailored for comparison-style rewards: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
- GRPO is a variant of PPO that removes the learned critic/value network. Instead it samples a group of outputs {o_1,...,o_G} for the same question and uses the group average reward as a baseline. Advantages are computed relative to that group distribution.
- Benefits:
  - Substantially reduced memory and compute compared to standard PPO (no separate value model).
  - Matches the comparative setup of reward models (which are typically trained from pairwise comparisons).
  - Supports both outcome supervision (reward at final answer) and process supervision (per-step rewards for reasoning steps).
- Training recipe highlights:
  - RL was run on chain-of-thought formatted GSM8K and MATH questions (about 144K examples).
  - For each question they sampled 64 outputs; policy updates used group-relative advantages.
  - This RL stage pushed DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B to DeepSeekMath-RL 7B: GSM8K from 82.9% → 88.2%, MATH from 46.8% → 51.7%.

Why this helps: GRPO directly shapes the model’s output distribution to favor higher-scoring reasoning traces, and the group-relative baseline fits naturally with reward models trained on pairwise comparisons.

## Other important design choices

- Start from a code-trained model: DeepSeekMath initializes from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (a code-oriented 7B). The authors report code training improves mathematical reasoning (both code-aided solving and pure chain-of-thought), so starting from a code model helped.
- Instruction tuning with math-specific SFT: they prepared ~776K instruction examples spanning chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool-integrated reasoning, in English and Chinese. That SFT creates the strong initial policy that RL improves further.
- Process supervision in RL: providing rewards at intermediate reasoning steps (not only final answer) further improves learning compared to only terminal rewards.

## What the numbers mean in plain terms

- 51.7% on MATH (single-run, no tools): About half of competition-level problems were solved correctly by the model using generated step-by-step solutions. For a 7B open model, that’s a notable jump: it surpasses many larger open-source models and approaches closed models’ performance levels.
- 60.9% with self-consistency (64 samples): Aggregating 64 sampled reasoning traces and selecting the majority answer raises correctness substantially. This indicates the model often has correct reasoning paths in its high-probability distribution — the trick is to surface them via sampling and majority voting.
- 88.2% on GSM8K: near-human grade-school problem performance, showing the model is highly competent on standard multi-step arithmetic/word problems.

## What this does and does not imply

- Positive: With the right data and training recipe, a compact 7B model can achieve surprisingly strong mathematical reasoning performance. Thoughtful corpus construction, code pre-training, instruction tuning, and a tailored RL stage are key.
- Limitations noted by the authors:
  - Geometry and some theorem-proof problems remain harder; closed models still lead in those areas.
  - The improvements from RL appear to come largely from making correct traces more likely in the model distribution (improving Maj@K), not necessarily from increasing the model’s underlying reasoning primitives. In other words, RL makes the model better at "producing" correct solutions reliably, rather than fundamentally altering latent mathematical understanding.
  - ArXiv content did not show clear positive impact in their experiments; web-scraped math pages proved more valuable.

## Takeaway

DeepSeekMath shows that careful domain data curation plus targeted algorithmic changes (GRPO) can let a small, open model punch well above its size class on hard math benchmarks. The approach highlights two practical levers for specialized capability building: (1) invest in targeted, iterative data mining and decontamination to build a large, high-quality domain corpus; (2) use RL methods that align well with how reward signals are collected (comparisons among outputs) and that are resource-efficient. Together, these moves closed a large part of the gap between a 7B open model and much larger closed systems on competition-grade mathematical reasoning.



# From the Wild Web to a 120B-Token Math Corpus

DeepSeekMath’s competitive performance didn’t come from a bigger model alone — it started with better data. This post walks through the practical data-engineering pipeline the DeepSeekMath team used to extract 120 billion math-focused tokens from Common Crawl. The process is iterative, pragmatic, and aimed at two goals: high recall of math content and high precision (i.e., removing noisy or benchmark-contaminating pages). I’ll keep the description non-technical but concrete so you can see what worked and why.

## The starting point: a high-quality seed (OpenWebMath)

Building a targeted corpus at web scale begins with good examples. The team used OpenWebMath — a curated collection of math web pages — as the “positive” seed. That seed tells the system what a math page looks like (style, symbols, vocabulary, structure), which is essential for finding more like it in the massive Common Crawl archive.

Why start here? Because web math content is heterogeneous: posts, lecture notes, proofs, symbolic snippets, and rendered equations. A small high-quality seed helps a simple classifier generalize those signal patterns across noisy web data.

## Fast, scalable recall: a fastText classifier

To scan billions of pages you need something fast. The team trained a fastText classifier on the seed positives and a large random set of Common Crawl pages as negatives. Key practical choices:

- Use a fast, bag-of-ngrams style model (fastText) so you can rank billions of pages cheaply.
- Train with a balanced positive/negative split (they used 500k positives and 500k negatives in the first iteration).
- Configure reasonable hyperparameters (e.g., vector size, n-gram length, epochs) so the model generalizes but stays cheap.

This classifier produces a score per page; pages with higher scores are more likely to be math-related and are candidates for the math corpus.

## Shrink the search space: deduplication and near-deduplication

Common Crawl includes many duplicated or near-duplicated pages. Before classification, the team performed URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication to reduce the work and avoid repeatedly keeping the same content. After this step they had roughly 40 billion HTML pages to examine.

Deduplication is essential: it reduces wasted compute, avoids skewing the corpus toward heavily mirrored content, and helps ensure a broader diversity of sources.

## Iterative expansion: identify math domains and add diversity

A single classifier trained on the initial seed will miss math content that looks different from the seed. To address this, they ran an iterative collection loop:

1. Run the fastText classifier over the deduped crawl and keep the top-ranked pages (the team experimented with preserving top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens and settled on an iterative strategy).
2. Group pages by domain (base URL) and compute, per domain, what fraction of that domain’s pages were selected in the first iteration.
3. Treat domains with unusually high selection rates (e.g., domains where >10% of pages were selected) as candidate math-oriented domains (examples: forum or Q&A sections, course pages, math blogs).
4. Manually inspect and annotate math-related URL patterns within those domains (e.g., /questions, /lectures).
5. Add pages matching those URL patterns (even if the classifier missed them) to the seed set and retrain the classifier.

Repeat this recall–annotate–retrain loop multiple times. After four iterations they collected 35.5 million math web pages, totaling 120B tokens. Importantly, by the fourth iteration they observed diminishing returns (about 98% of the data was already gathered by iteration three), so they stopped.

Why the iterations matter: the web contains many subcommunities and formats. Manually identifying high-density math domains and adding them into the seed improves classifier recall for structures that the initial seed didn’t represent.

## Human-in-the-loop refinement

Automatic classification gets you far, but the pipeline also incorporated human annotation to improve the seed and reduce false positives. The manual step focused on validating domain URL patterns and refining the set of positive examples used to retrain the fastText model. That targeted human effort amplified the value of the automated steps without requiring full manual labeling of billions of pages.

## Decontamination: avoid leaking evaluation benchmarks

A key practical and scientific requirement is to avoid benchmark contamination. The DeepSeekMath team applied a conservative decontamination rule: any text segment that contains a 10-gram substring that exactly matches an evaluation benchmark substring (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval) is removed from the math training corpus. For shorter benchmark texts (3–9 grams) they applied exact matching as well.

This 10-gram-based filter removes pages that could leak benchmark problems or answers into the pretraining data and artificially inflate downstream scores. It’s a blunt instrument, but it’s simple, reproducible, and effective for large-scale web mining.

## Final corpus: breadth, scale, multilingual coverage

The result is the DeepSeekMath Corpus: about 35.5M web pages and 120B math-focused tokens extracted from Common Crawl. A few important characteristics the team highlights:

- Scale: The corpus is several times larger than prior publicly released math web corpora (e.g., it’s roughly 7× the math pages used by Minerva and 9× OpenWebMath in token count).
- Quality: In downstream pretraining tests the corpus gave stronger performance than alternative math corpora when used to continue pretraining.
- Multilinguality: Crucially, the corpus includes non-English math content (English and Chinese are the largest components). This multilingual coverage contributed to improved performance on Chinese math benchmarks — an advantage over prior corpora that focused primarily on English.

## Practical trade-offs and observations

A few operational and scientific lessons emerge from the pipeline:

- Simple models scale: A lightweight classifier like fastText is often the right tool for web-scale recall when your goal is to triage billions of pages quickly.
- Iteration beats one-shot: Iteratively expanding the seed by harvesting domain-level patterns and human-curated URL rules captures diverse math formats that a single pass would miss.
- Targeted human effort pays: Human annotation focused on domain patterns yields outsized returns relative to labeling random pages.
- Conservative decontamination is necessary: Exact n-gram matching to benchmarks is a practical way to reduce leakage at scale.
- Multilingual data matters: Including non-English math pages improved downstream performance on Chinese benchmarks; a strictly English collection would miss this benefit.

## What this enabled downstream

Using this corpus as a major fraction of continued pretraining, the DeepSeekMath project trained a 7B base model that achieved strong math reasoning results — competitive with much larger models on certain benchmarks. The takeaway is: careful, targeted data engineering can materially improve the math reasoning ability of an LLM, sometimes more cost-effectively than simply increasing parameter count.

## Closing notes

If you’re building domain-specific pretraining data from web-scale sources, the DeepSeekMath pipeline shows a practical recipe: start from a high-quality seed, use fast scalable recall methods, iterate with domain discovery and targeted human annotation, deduplicate aggressively, and apply conservative decontamination against evaluation benchmarks. The result is a large, high-precision corpus that can unlock stronger, more language-diverse models without relying solely on larger model sizes.



# Why Coding Skills Make LLMs Better at Math

DeepSeekMath is an instructive case study: the authors initialize their 7B model from a code-trained checkpoint (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) and then continue training on a large, web-mined math corpus. The result is a model that not only retains strong coding performance, but also improves substantially on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Below I unpack the intuition behind this effect, explain why program-of-thought (PoT) style data helps, and summarize the paper’s experiments comparing different training schedules (two-stage vs mixed) and data sources (including the surprising weak impact of arXiv).

## Short summary of empirical highlights
- DeepSeekMath-Base (7B), initialized from a code-trained model and further trained on math data, shows large gains on math benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K and MATH) compared to open-source baselines. The paper reports DeepSeekMath-Base hitting 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH in some evaluations, outperforming much larger prior models in some cases.
- Instruction-tuned then RL-refined model (DeepSeekMath-RL) improves further: GSM8K went from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% to 51.7% after Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) reinforcement learning.
- Mixing code and math during training can mitigate catastrophic forgetting of coding ability while improving program-aided math; however, the precise benefit depends on schedule and model capacity.

These are the facts we build on below.

## Why code training helps mathematical reasoning — the intuitive mapping
At a high level, coding and formal mathematical reasoning share structural commonalities that a language model can exploit:

- Structured, stepwise logic. Code enforces explicit sequencing of steps (e.g., define variables, transform expressions, check conditions). Math solutions benefit from the same explicit, ordered steps. Learning the syntax and token-level transitions of code helps the model internalize how to produce and maintain structured chains of thought.
- Precise variable and state management. Programming trains models to reason about symbols that persist across lines (variables, arrays). Math problems often require tracking intermediate values and substitutions; code-trained models are better at maintaining those local bindings.
- Deterministic decomposition. Writing a function or algorithm requires decomposing a task into deterministic sub-steps. This practice aligns with how many math problems are solved algorithmically (reduce to algebra, compute, then interpret).
- Exposure to executable verification. Code frequently has clear ways to check correctness (unit tests, asserts). That experience makes the model more amenable to producing solutions that can be validated programmatically, which helps program-of-thought approaches where an interpreter verifies intermediate steps or final answers.

In short: code training biases the model toward disciplined, modular, and verifiable generation patterns — exactly the habits that benefit multi-step mathematical reasoning.

## Program-of-Thought (PoT): practical leverage of coding structure
Program-of-thought is the idea of having the model generate a short program (often Python) whose execution yields the answer. Why does this help?

- It forces explicit computation: instead of relying on implicit arithmetic or fuzzy reasoning, the model emits concrete operations that can be executed and checked.
- It reduces reliance on brittle natural-language chains: a program is more constrained than prose and less prone to hallucinated arithmetic.
- It enables tool use: when the model is trained or fine-tuned to output programs, downstream evaluation can actually run the program (e.g., using math/sympy) to get numerically exact answers.

DeepSeekMath shows that starting from a code-trained checkpoint improves both the production of these programs and the program-aided solving performance. The paper reports notable gains on program-aided evaluations (GSM8K, MATH) when code training precedes math training.

## Two-stage vs mixed training: what the experiments reveal
The paper ran a few controlled variants to test how code and math tokens interact in training. Here are the main takeaways the authors report:

- Two-stage (code training → math training): Pretraining first on a large code corpus and then continuing on math tokens gives strong program-aided math performance. The code-first stage improves the model’s ability to produce executable solutions, and the math stage refines domain knowledge.
- One-stage mixed (code + math tokens intermixed): Mixing code and math tokens in a single-stage run can preserve coding ability better (less catastrophic forgetting) compared to naive two-stage schedules. It can also synergize program-aided math performance when capacities are sufficient.
- Capacity matters: For a small-ish model (1.3B used in some experiments), mixing code+math in one stage sometimes harmed mathematical reasoning without tool use. The conjecture is limited model capacity — the model can’t fully absorb both distributions simultaneously and perform optimally on all downstream math modalities.
- Practical compromise: For the authors’ 7B setup, they initialize from a code-trained 7B model and then perform substantial math continual training, which gave strong results across both program-based and natural-language CoT reasoning. That suggests the safe route is code-first initialization then targeted math continuation for models of that size.

Put plainly: code-first helps, mixing can help too, but the best choice depends on model size and the balance you want between raw code capability and native language-chain math skill.

## The arXiv surprise
A common assumption is that arXiv papers (LaTeX math) are prime training data for math models. The authors tested this and report an unexpected result: training on arXiv-heavy corpora (various processed versions) did not reliably improve math benchmark performance and in some cases slightly degraded performance across benchmarks including GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and even formal math tasks like miniF2F.

Important caveats the paper mentions:
- They tested arXiv-only or arXiv-heavy training regimes; they did not exhaustively study arXiv combined with other carefully curated sources, nor did they rule out scale effects (arXiv may help at different model sizes).
- ArXiv content is dense, formal, and LaTeX-heavy; it may not match the style of contest-style or grade-school problems common in GSM8K and MATH. That domain mismatch likely explains part of the weak transfer.

So: more arXiv is not automatically better — high-quality, diverse math web text (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) and code-aligned training seem more effective for these benchmarks.

## Practical implications and recommendations
- If your goal is stronger program-aided math solving, start from a code-trained checkpoint or include substantial code data during pretraining. Code teaches structure and verification habits.
- For mixed objectives (retain coding, improve math CoT), consider either a carefully balanced one-stage mix (if model capacity permits) or code-first then math-continual strategies for medium-sized models.
- Don’t assume arXiv will automatically improve contest/problem-solving math performance; curate diverse math sources and validate gains on your target benchmarks.
- Program-of-thought training and instruction that encourages executable intermediate steps will likely yield better practical performance when paired with code-fluent initialization.

## Closing
DeepSeekMath demonstrates a clear and pragmatic point: coding skills are not just about code — they build structural, verifiable, and modular reasoning habits that translate into better mathematical problem solving, especially when models are trained with program-style outputs or when programs can be executed to check solutions. The specific training schedule and data mix matter, and more domain-specific sources (carefully filtered web math plus code) can outperform raw arXiv collections for the kinds of benchmarks evaluated in the paper.



# GRPO: PPO-Style Reinforcement Learning Without the Critic

Large language models fine-tuned for reasoning often use Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an actor-critic method that requires a separate value (critic) model. The critic helps compute advantages for policy updates, but it adds substantial memory and compute overhead and can be brittle when rewards are sparse or only given at the end of a generated sequence. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced in DeepSeekMath, sidesteps the critic entirely by computing baselines from a group of outputs sampled for the same question. The result is a lighter RL loop that fits the comparative nature of typical reward models and still yields strong gains on hard math benchmarks.

This post explains the core idea, how GRPO computes advantages, why that matters for reward-model-style supervision, and the empirical impact it had in DeepSeekMath.

## The pain point: PPO with a critic

PPO optimizes a clipped surrogate objective that depends on per-token advantages A_t, typically estimated with a learned value function V_ψ via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). The standard PPO surrogate looks like:

J_{PPO}(θ) = E_{q,o} [ (1/|o|) sum_{t=1}^{|o|} min( r_t(θ) A_t, clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) A_t ) ]

where r_t(θ)=π_θ(o_t|·)/π_{θ_old}(o_t|·) and A_t is the advantage at token t.

Training a value network that provides reliable tokenwise baselines has two main drawbacks in the LLM-for-reasoning setting:

- Memory and compute: the critic is often as large as the policy and multiplies resource usage.
- Reward sparsity and granularity: many setups give a single outcome-level score (e.g., correctness), making tokenwise value learning noisy or unnecessary.

GRPO addresses both by replacing the learned value function with a group-based empirical baseline.

## The core idea of GRPO

Instead of fitting a critic, GRPO samples a group of G outputs for the same question from the old policy and uses the group statistics as the baseline. For question q we sample outputs {o_1, ..., o_G} and score each output with a reward model to obtain r_1, ..., r_G. GRPO normalizes these rewards within the group and uses the normalized reward as the advantage assigned to tokens (outcome supervision) or sums of stepwise normalized rewards (process supervision).

The GRPO objective (per question) becomes:

J_{GRPO}(θ) = E_{q, {o_i} ∼ π_{θ_old}} [ (1/G) sum_{i=1}^G (1/|o_i|) sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} { min( (π_θ(o_{i,t}|·)/π_{θ_old}(o_{i,t}|·)) \hat A_{i,t}, clip(·,1-ε,1+ε) \hat A_{i,t} ) - β D_{KL}[π_θ || π_{ref}] } ]

Key pieces:
- The advantage estimate \hat A_{i,t} is computed inside the group (no critic).
- Instead of folding KL into the reward as in some PPO variants, GRPO directly regularizes with a KL term between π_θ and a reference model.
- GRPO uses an unbiased estimator for the KL term to avoid extra complications.

The important operational difference is that GRPO treats the group average as the baseline. Advantages are computed relative to that baseline, hence the name Group Relative Policy Optimization.

## Two supervision modes: outcome vs process

GRPO supports two ways of converting reward-model signals into per-token advantages.

- Outcome supervision: when only an outcome-level score r_i is available (e.g., correctness), normalize the group rewards
  \tilde r_i = (r_i - mean(r)) / std(r)
  and set \hat A_{i,t} = \tilde r_i for all tokens t of output i. This is simple and matches setups where reward models score whole responses.

- Process supervision: when a reward model can score intermediate reasoning steps, GRPO normalizes all step-level scores across the group and assigns each token the sum of normalized rewards from future steps:
  \hat A_{i,t} = sum_{index(j) ≥ t} \tilde r_i^{index(j)}.
  This provides finer-grained, step-aware supervision and leverages reward models trained to judge reasoning steps.

Process supervision generally gives stricter, more informative learning signals; the paper reports that GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS).

## Why GRPO aligns well with reward-model comparisons

Most reward models used in practice are trained on pairwise or ranking comparisons: given multiple answers to the same prompt, label which is better. GRPO directly uses that comparative setup: baselines are computed from the same-group samples, so the gradient step increases probability of higher-scoring answers relative to lower-scoring ones. This avoids forcing an absolute scalar baseline from a separate value network, and fits the comparative inductive bias of the reward data.

Concretely:
- Reward models provide relative judgments between outputs on the same question.
- GRPO forms groups of outputs per question and normalizes rewards within the group, turning relative scores into normalized advantages.
- The policy update therefore amplifies the relative ordering the reward model expresses, instead of relying on a separate value estimator to interpret absolute reward magnitudes.

## Resource and practical advantages

- Memory and compute: GRPO eliminates the critic network, significantly reducing memory requirements compared to standard PPO where you commonly maintain a value head or separate value model.
- Simpler training loop: no need to fit an accurate value function across token positions, which is especially noisy when reward is only available at sequence end.
- Natural fit to reward datasets: GRPO matches how many reward models are trained (comparisons among answers).

Practical settings reported in the DeepSeekMath experiments:
- Group size G = 64 samples per question during RL.
- KL coefficient β ≈ 0.04 (regularization term).
- Policy learning rate used: 1e-6.
- Reward model initialized from DeepSeekMath-Base and trained with LR 2e-5.
- Iterative RL: GRPO can be run in iterations where the reward model is retrained on new policy outputs using a replay buffer (10% historical data).

## Empirical impact on DeepSeekMath

GRPO was applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B using only a subset of English instruction-tuning data (chain-of-thought-format GSM8K and MATH problems, ~144K questions). Even with this constrained RL training set, GRPO produced substantial gains:

- GSM8K (chain-of-thought reasoning): 82.9% → 88.2%
- MATH (competition-level, chain-of-thought): 46.8% → 51.7%

These improvements demonstrate that GRPO can meaningfully boost correctness on both grade-school level (GSM8K) and much harder competition-level math problems (MATH) without the heavy machinery of a critic.

Additional empirical notes from the paper:
- GRPO also improved out-of-domain math benchmarks (e.g., CMATH improved from 84.6% → 88.8%).
- Self-consistency sampling of DeepSeekMath-RL 7B (64 samples) further raised MATH to 60.9% (showing the policy’s improved concentration of correct answers in its top-k).
- Analysis indicates that RL (here GRPO) improves Maj@K (majority/top-K correctness) more than Pass@K (whether any single sampled completion is correct), suggesting RL makes the model’s output distribution more robust and concentrates higher probability mass on correct answers.

## How GRPO compares to other fine-tuning approaches

The paper frames many tuning and RL methods within a unified gradient paradigm where three components matter: Data source, Reward function, and Algorithm (the mapping from reward to gradient coefficients). GRPO stands out because:

- Data source: it uses online sampling (sampling from current policy) which typically beats offline sampling (from the static SFT model) in later stages of training.
- Reward function: it uses learned reward models (model-based), not just rule-based correctness filters.
- Algorithm: it converts relative, group-normalized rewards into per-token advantages without a critic, giving a richer gradient coefficient than simple rejection-based methods that only accept/reject outputs.

Compared with RFT or online RFT, GRPO adjusts gradient strength by reward magnitude, so it both reinforces better answers and penalizes worse ones—whereas rejection-based methods often only reinforce or filter.

## Limitations and practical caveats

- Reward-model quality matters: GRPO leans on a reward model trained on comparisons; if that model is poor or poorly calibrated out-of-distribution, the policy can be misled. The paper suggests iteratively retraining the reward model with fresh policy data and using replay to mitigate drift.
- Computational cost of sampling: GRPO requires sampling G outputs per prompt (the paper uses G=64), which raises inference cost during RL data collection. This is a tradeoff: more samples make group baselines more stable but increase compute.
- Not a silver bullet for capabilities: analysis in the paper indicates RL mainly concentrates correct answers in the model’s top mass (Maj@K), rather than fundamentally changing underlying reasoning competence.

## Takeaways

GRPO is a practical, resource-conscious RL method tailored to large language models where reward supervision is comparative and often sparse. By replacing the learned critic with group-based baselines, GRPO reduces memory overhead, matches the comparative nature of reward models, and delivers strong improvements in math reasoning for DeepSeekMath (notably +5.4% absolute on MATH and +5.3% on GSM8K in the reported experiments). If you are applying reward-model-based RL to LLMs and want to avoid the complexity and cost of a critic, GRPO is a method worth trying—especially when your reward data is comparison-focused and you can afford to sample multiple candidates per prompt.



# The Instruction-Tuning Cocktail: CoT, PoT, and Tool Use

DeepSeekMath shows that carefully mixing three instruction formats—chain-of-thought (CoT), program-of-thought (PoT), and tool‑integrated reasoning—creates a much stronger math tutor than any single ingredient alone. In this post I’ll explain what each mode is, when to prefer one over another, how Python/tools fit in, and why the combined instruction-tuning recipe helped DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B beat larger open models on hard math benchmarks.

## The three ingredients, in plain terms

- Chain-of-thought (CoT)
  - What it is: Natural-language, step-by-step reasoning. The model writes an explicit reasoning trace (a sequence of intermediate steps) before producing a final answer.
  - When to use it: Good for conceptual reasoning, showing proofs, or multi-step algebra where each transformation is simple enough to do by hand. CoT helps the model expose reasoning structure and is useful when correctness depends on logical decomposition rather than heavy computation.

- Program-of-thought (PoT)
  - What it is: The model expresses reasoning by emitting code (typically Python) that implements the solution as a small program. The execution result of that program is treated as the answer.
  - When to use it: Best when the solution requires precise arithmetic, symbolic manipulation, or an algorithmic process (e.g., solve a polynomial, run a search, compute an integral numerically). PoT reduces mistakes from manual arithmetic and offloads accuracy to the language runtime.

- Tool-integrated reasoning (tool use)
  - What it is: A hybrid where the model produces natural-language reasoning but calls external tools (Python, SymPy, calculators, or other tools) to perform computations or checks during the reasoning process.
  - When to use it: Use this when you want the transparency of CoT plus the reliability of executing computations. Tool-integrated formats are especially useful for problems that mix conceptual steps and heavy computation (for example, a derivation that needs a symbolic simplification at one step).

## How Python and libs (e.g., math, sympy) get used

Programmatic solutions let the model delegate numerical and symbolic accuracy to well-tested libraries. In DeepSeekMath training, models were prompted to (a) write a Python program using libraries like math and sympy and (b) return the execution output as the answer. This is effective because:

- Numeric precision and algebraic simplification errors are reduced.
- You make complex subroutines explicit (root-finding, integrals, symbolic factorization).
- It enables automated evaluation: you can run the generated program and check if its output matches the ground truth.

When to prefer the Python route:
- The problem demands nontrivial calculation (long arithmetic, large combinatorics, numerical integration).
- The logic is naturally algorithmic (simulate, enumerate, or optimize).
- You want reproducible, automatically verifiable answers.

When not to prefer it:
- If the emphasis is explaining mathematical intuition to a learner (CoT is more readable).
- If the problem needs a clear symbolic proof structure that must be human-interpretable (CoT or tool-integrated explanations with code checks can be better).

## Why combine them? Complementary strengths

Each format compensates for weaknesses in the others:

- CoT improves interpretability and is better for purely logical or structural reasoning.
- PoT brings execution correctness for computational steps and makes auto-evaluation straightforward.
- Tool use mixes both: the model explains its chain-of-thought and calls tools for the heavy lifting.

The DeepSeekMath team curated an instruction-tuning dataset that included CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated examples (about 776K SFT examples spanning English and Chinese, with CoT, PoT, and tool formats). Training with this mixture teaches the model to choose the right mode, or to combine modes, depending on the problem.

## What happened in practice: results from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B

The result of instruction-tuning DeepSeekMath-Base with the mixed CoT/PoT/tool dataset is a model called DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. Key empirical observations from the paper:

- With CoT (no external tools allowed), DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B outperforms all open-source models on the competition-level MATH dataset by a sizable margin; the paper reports it surpasses other open models (and many proprietary models) by at least 9 percentage points in that “no tool” evaluation.
- When allowed to use programmatic tools (PoT / tool-integrated reasoning), the same 7B instruction-tuned model reaches substantially higher accuracy on MATH (the paper states it “approaches an accuracy of 60% on MATH” under the tool-enabled evaluation setting), outperforming open-source alternatives that are much larger.
- The base model was already strengthened by large-scale math pre-training (120B math tokens from Common Crawl) and initialized from a code-trained checkpoint (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B). That prior code exposure made PoT/tool formats more effective, because the model already knew code patterns and API usage.

In short: combining CoT, PoT, and tool use during instruction tuning let a relatively small model (7B) punch above its weight on difficult math benchmarks.

## Practical guidance for building a math tutor model

If you're replicating this “instruction-tuning cocktail,” consider these practical points drawn from DeepSeekMath:

1. Start from a code-aware base model if you plan to include PoT/tool examples. Code pretraining improves program-aided math ability.
2. Curate mixed-format SFT data:
   - CoT examples for conceptual clarity and proof-style tasks.
   - PoT examples where the final answer is obtained by executing code.
   - Tool-integrated examples where the natural-language explanation interleaves with tool calls.
3. Teach the model to prefer PoT/tool use when precision matters and CoT when interpretability matters. That preference can emerge naturally if the dataset contains both styles for the same kinds of problems.
4. Use program execution as an objective evaluation path: executing PoT outputs gives a deterministic check on correctness.
5. Preserve diversity in languages and problem types. DeepSeekMath’s multilingual and multi-domain pretraining helped its performance on both English and Chinese benchmarks.
6. If you plan RL or preference tuning later, note that reinforcement can amplify correct answers in the top-K distribution (improving Maj@K), so combining RL with mixed-format SFT tends to stabilize and further boost performance.

## Final takeaway

CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated reasoning are not competing paradigms: they are complementary modes. When instruction tuning includes all three, a model learns when to explain in human terms and when to hand off hard computation to code or tools. DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B—trained on a dedicated mix of CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated examples on top of math- and code-focused pretraining—demonstrates that this cocktail can make a compact model a very capable math tutor, outperforming much larger open models on challenging benchmarks.



# A Unified Map of SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO

Training large language models involves a confusing array of acronyms: SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO—and now GRPO. They look different on the surface but, at their core, many of these methods are doing the same thing: change the conditional token probabilities of a pretrained model using some data and some feedback signal. This post lays out a compact, practical map you can use to reason about which method to choose and why.

## The single common frame

All of the methods in question can be written in the same mathematical form: the update is an expectation over (question, output) pairs of token-level log-probability gradients scaled by some coefficient. Concretely,

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\; \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\right].
\]

Three concepts control everything here:

- Data source (\mathcal{D}): where the (q,o) samples come from (pre-collected vs. sampled from the current policy).
- Reward / feedback source (\pi_{rf}): how quality is judged (rule-based correctness, a learned reward model, or implicit labels).
- Algorithm (GC): how the method converts feedback into the gradient coefficient \(GC(\cdot)\). This includes clipping, baselines, pairwise losses, etc.

If you keep these three axes in mind, you can immediately compare methods and see the trade-offs.

## Quick descriptions in the unified frame

Below I summarize each method in terms of (Data source, Reward, Algorithm → gradient coefficient behavior).

SFT — Supervised Fine-Tuning
- Data: curated (q, gold o) examples collected and labeled by humans.
- Reward: implicit (the gold solution acts as “correct”).
- Algorithm: standard cross-entropy / MLE. \(GC\) is effectively +1 for tokens in the gold output and 0 otherwise. No preference or comparison modeling. This is deterministic supervised learning.

RFT — Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning
- Data: outputs sampled from an SFT model (offline: sampled once from the initial SFT model).
- Reward: rule-based correctness (e.g., does the answer match the reference or pass a checker).
- Algorithm: filter and keep only “good” sampled outputs and fine-tune on them. \(GC\) is positive for accepted outputs and zero for rejected ones. No graded signal — all accepted outputs get similar reinforcement; rejected ones are usually discarded.

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization
- Data: sampled outputs (usually offline, from the SFT model) arranged into pairs of preferred vs. dispreferred outputs.
- Reward: pairwise preferences (often derived from human annotations or a rule).
- Algorithm: a pairwise loss that directly pushes up probabilities of preferred outputs relative to dispreferred ones. The gradient coefficient encodes the pairwise logistic-type preference adjustment rather than a scalar reward per sequence.

PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization
- Data: online samples from the current policy (actor).
- Reward: scalar sequence-level reward from a reward model (or rule). A learned value function (critic) is trained to estimate baseline values.
- Algorithm: actor-critic RL with per-token surrogate objective and an advantage \(A_t\) computed from rewards minus the learned value \(V_\psi\). \(GC\) includes the clipped ratio \(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}\) times the advantage. This produces graded reinforcement and penalization; requires a separate value model (memory + compute cost) and commonly includes a per-token KL penalty to keep policy near a reference.

GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization (introduced in DeepSeekMath)
- Data: online samples from the current policy, grouped per question (a group of G outputs for the same q).
- Reward: outcome or process supervision via a reward model that scores whole outputs (outcome) or intermediate steps (process).
- Algorithm: no separate learned critic. For each question, compute raw rewards for the G sampled outputs, normalize within the group (subtract group mean, divide by group std), and set token-level advantages \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) from those normalized group-relative rewards. The GRPO surrogate objective is structurally similar to PPO but uses these group-relative advantages and a direct KL regularizer between policy and reference:
  - Outcome supervision: \(\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}\) for all tokens in output \(i\).
  - Process supervision: \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) is the sum of normalized step-level rewards after token \(t\).
- Key practical difference: GRPO avoids the heavy memory cost of training a value network by using group baselines computed from multiple sampled outputs.

## Important axes and practical consequences

1. Online vs offline sampling
   - Offline (RFT, DPO common usage): you sample once from a fixed SFT model and train on those samples. This is cheaper and simpler but limits exploration and can underperform as the policy diverges.
   - Online (PPO, GRPO): repeatedly sample from the current policy. Online sampling adapts to the evolving policy and can give better final performance (the paper reports Online RFT beating offline RFT in later stages).

2. Scalar vs. relative / graded feedback
   - Binary filter (RFT) is simple but blunt: it only enforces correctness/non-correctness and cannot differentiate better vs. best correct answers.
   - Graded rewards (reward models used by PPO/GRPO) let algorithms scale reinforcement and punishment, which helps separate good from excellent outputs.
   - Process supervision (step-level scoring) provides much finer-grained signals for multi-step reasoning than sequence-level outcome scores.

3. Need for a learned critic / baseline
   - PPO typically trains a value function to act as baseline when computing advantages. This stabilizes variance but doubles memory and adds compute.
   - GRPO sidesteps this by estimating baselines from other sampled outputs in the same group. This is cheaper and aligns nicely with reward models trained on comparisons, since those models are naturally relative.

4. How the algorithm transforms reward into a gradient (GC)
   - RFT: GC is essentially a voting/selection mask.
   - DPO: GC is pairwise and relative (push preferred over dispreferred).
   - PPO: GC depends on \(\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}, 1\pm\varepsilon\right)A_t\).
   - GRPO: GC uses the same clipped surrogate shape but replaces \(A_t\) with group-relative advantages \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) and adds an explicit KL regularizer between the trained policy and a reference policy.

## When to choose which method (practical guidance)

- Use SFT when you have reliable, high-quality paired (q, gold o) data and you want deterministic supervised improvement. SFT is the simplest and establishes the reference behavior.
- Use RFT when you can cheaply produce many model outputs, have a high-precision accept/reject rule (e.g., an automated checker), and want a quick supervised-style boost without training reward models. RFT is simple but limited: it treats all accepted outputs equally.
- Use DPO when you have preference pairs (human or otherwise) and you want a direct gradient that enforces preferences without running RL. DPO works well when pairwise judgments are abundant and trustworthy.
- Use PPO when you need fine-grained, graded reinforcement and you can afford the memory/compute cost of a learned critic and a full actor-critic loop. PPO is a mature choice when per-token advantage estimation via a value network is important.
- Use GRPO when you want the graded benefits of PPO-style updates but want to avoid training a critic. GRPO is particularly attractive when:
  - You can generate several outputs per prompt (groups), and
  - Your reward model is comparative or outputs scalar scores for these multiple samples, and
  - You want to leverage process supervision (step-level rewards) or outcome normalization to produce robust baselines without an extra model.
  In DeepSeekMath, GRPO improved mathematical reasoning while reducing memory footprint compared to PPO.

## A few nuanced takeaways from the DeepSeekMath experiments

- Online sampling tends to win in later training; it becomes more important as the policy drifts from the initial SFT model.
- GRPO’s group-relative advantages align naturally with reward models trained on comparisons and with process-level rewards, and they produce a strong practical trade-off (comparable or better performance than PPO in this setting, with less memory).
- Reinforcement learning often improves Maj@K (the chance the majority of K samples are correct) rather than Pass@K. Put another way, RL can make the distribution of top outputs more robust, not necessarily change the model’s fundamental reasoning ability.
- Process supervision (rewarding intermediate steps) tends to beat outcome-only supervision on complex multi-step reasoning tasks because it supplies dense learning signals about the chain of thought.

## Final mental model

Think of model refinement methods as choosing values on three knobs: Data source (offline vs online), Feedback granularity (binary vs scalar vs stepwise), and Algorithmic machinery (pairwise loss vs actor-critic vs group baseline). SFT sits at the supervised corner. RFT and DPO are lightweight preference-style moves staying close to SFT. PPO is the heavy-duty actor-critic RL. GRPO sits between DPO/RFT and PPO: it keeps the graded, online advantages of RL but replaces the critic with a group-relative baseline that is cheaper and well-matched to comparative reward models.

If you can sample G>1 outputs per prompt and have a reliable reward model (especially one trained on comparisons or step-level labels), GRPO is an appealing, compute-conscious way to get the benefits of RL. If you only have labeled pairs or a reliable rule, simpler supervised or pairwise methods (SFT, RFT, DPO) may be preferable.

Keep these three axes in mind and the decision becomes a lot clearer than the alphabet soup suggests.



# Why RL Works Here: From ‘Sometimes Right’ to ‘Usually Right’

DeepSeekMath shows a striking pattern: reinforcement learning (RL) did not primarily make the model fundamentally better at solving math problems on a per-sample basis. Instead, after RL the model’s correct answers became more reliably present among multiple samples. In other words, RL shifted the model from being “sometimes right” to being “usually right.” This is the difference between improving Pass@K and improving Maj@K. In this post I’ll unpack what those metrics mean, why distribution shaping matters for multi-step reasoning, how DeepSeekMath’s RL (GRPO) produced the observed gains, and what it implies for how we evaluate and train reasoning models.

## Pass@K vs Maj@K — what they measure and why it matters

Both Pass@K and Maj@K are ways to evaluate stochastic models that produce samples (multiple answers) for the same prompt. They capture two different aspects of a model’s performance.

- Pass@K: “Did any of the K sampled outputs solve the problem?” More formally, if you sample K outputs from the model for a question, Pass@K is 1 if at least one output is correct, else 0. Averaged across questions, Pass@K measures the model’s best-of-K capability. It answers: can the model occasionally produce a correct answer if given enough draws?

- Maj@K: “Do a majority of the K samples solve the problem?” That is, Maj@K equals 1 if more than half of the K outputs are correct. Averaged across questions, Maj@K measures whether correct answers tend to dominate the model’s output distribution for that prompt. It answers: is the model typically producing the right answer, not just occasionally?

These metrics are related but distinct. Improving Pass@K can be achieved by boosting rare but high-quality modes of the model’s output distribution. Improving Maj@K requires moving a correct mode to be the dominant one (higher probability mass), so most samples are correct.

DeepSeekMath observes that RL increases Maj@K but not Pass@K. Practically speaking, that means after RL the model’s distribution over outputs has been reshaped so that the correct solutions occupy more of the high-probability mass — the model is more consistently correct across samples — but the absolute chance of seeing a correct sample in a single draw (for some tasks) did not necessarily improve in the same way.

## Why distribution shaping matters for reasoning

Mathematical reasoning is multi-step and structured. A model’s raw outputs reflect a complex distribution over reasoning chains, many of which are partially correct, incorrect, or simply noisy. There are two mechanisms by which a stochastic model can improve evaluation metrics:

1. Discover a rare but correct reasoning chain and increase its sampling probability slightly (this helps Pass@K).
2. Reduce the probability of incorrect chains and reallocate that mass to correct chains so that correct chains dominate (this helps Maj@K).

RL methods that use preference/reward models and sampling-based optimization naturally operate on the model’s output distribution. Even when the underlying problem-solving ability (e.g., reasoning primitives, math knowledge) is unchanged, nudging the distribution toward outputs that the reward model prefers will increase the likelihood that sampling produces correct chains. That is “distribution shaping.”

Distribution shaping is powerful because many downstream uses rely on drawing a small number of samples and taking the majority or running an aggregator (like voting, self-consistency, or verification). If the correct answer becomes the modal response, downstream systems become more reliable without fundamentally altering the model’s internal problem-solving competence.

## How DeepSeekMath’s RL produced Maj@K gains

DeepSeekMath introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an RL variant that samples a group of outputs per question and computes advantages relative to that group. A few facts from the work explain the observed effect:

- The RL training used outcome and process supervision from reward models; GRPO computes advantages based on relative reward within each sampled group. That makes the training explicitly comparative and focused on promoting outputs that are better than other contemporaneous samples for the same question.

- The iterative RL loop in DeepSeekMath updates the reward model on sampled outputs and then updates the policy. That process reinforces whichever outputs the reward model ranks higher, repeatedly concentrating probability mass on those outputs.

- The paper’s analysis shows RL improved Maj@K but not Pass@K. Concretely, RL made correct responses more likely to appear in top-K samples (majority wins) rather than increasing the model’s ability to generate at least one correct sample among K tries.

Taken together, GRPO’s group-relative updates and iterative reward-model-driven selection change the model’s conditional output distribution so that higher-reward (often correct) outputs occupy more mass. This explains why Maj@K improves: the model’s sampling distribution becomes more concentrated around good solutions.

## Why this distinction matters for evaluation and downstream use

1. Different evaluations capture different claims. If your goal is “the model can occasionally find a correct solution if we sample many times and verify,” then Pass@K is the right lens. If your goal is “a single run (or a small handful of runs) should typically produce a correct solution without expensive verification,” then Maj@K (or similar consistency measures) is more meaningful.

2. RL-driven distribution shaping can give large practical gains without changing underlying algorithmic competence. That’s not inherently bad — many systems benefit from making good outputs more probable — but it is important to recognize what was improved: the distribution, not necessarily the reasoning primitives.

3. For safety and robustness, distribution shaping can be double-edged. If the reward model is imperfect or overfits to spurious signals, RL may concentrate mass on superficially high-reward but brittle or incorrect outputs. So improvements in Maj@K must be interpreted together with careful reward model design and validation.

4. When comparing models, matching on a single metric (e.g., accuracy from a single sample) can be misleading. Use a battery of metrics: zero-shot accuracy, Pass@K for varying K, Maj@K, and counts of distinct valid solutions. Reporting how performance scales with sampling budget reveals whether a model’s gains come from discovery or from dominance.

## Practical takeaways for training and evaluation

- If you rely on sampling and aggregation (majority voting, self-consistency), RL that shapes the distribution (like GRPO) is a very efficient path to improved end-to-end reliability.

- If you want to improve a model’s fundamental reasoning capabilities (so every single sample is better on average), complement RL with training signals that target internal reasoning steps (process supervision) and diverse out-of-distribution data. The paper shows process supervision (step-level rewards) is helpful within GRPO.

- Always evaluate with both Pass@K and Maj@K (and ideally multiple K values). The pair tells you whether you’re gaining rare discoveries or robust dominance.

- Pay attention to the reward model. Distribution shaping amplifies whatever the reward model prefers. Ensure the reward model generalizes beyond the sampled outputs used during RL and is robust to noisy labels.

## Closing

DeepSeekMath’s experimental finding — that RL improved Maj@K more than Pass@K — is a clear, practical illustration of how RL re-shapes output distributions to make correct answers more reliably sampled. That’s a valuable effect: many systems become much more usable when correct behavior is the modal behavior. But interpreting such gains requires care: distribution shaping is not the same as improving raw problem-solving ability. Use multiple metrics, validate reward models, and choose training targets according to whether you need “sometimes right” (discoverability) or “usually right” (reliability).



# Multilingual Math: Building a Bilingual Problem Solver

DeepSeekMath shows a clear practical lesson: when your pre-training corpus includes high-quality non-English math content, you get a model that solves math problems better across languages. In this post I’ll summarize what DeepSeekMath did, why multilingual math data helped, and what that means for educators, content creators, and researchers building or using math-capable models worldwide.

## The setup in one paragraph

DeepSeekMath built a specialized pre-training corpus (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) of roughly 120B math tokens mined from Common Crawl using an iterative fastText-based filtering pipeline seeded by OpenWebMath and then expanded with human-validated domain-level heuristics. Importantly, this corpus is multilingual: English and Chinese are the most represented languages. DeepSeekMath-Base (7B) was then trained heavily on that data (500B tokens total for the final 7B model, with about 56% math tokens plus code, arXiv, and general web), and subsequent instruction tuning and reinforcement learning produced DeepSeekMath-Instruct and DeepSeekMath-RL. Across benchmarks, the multilingual math pre-training produced measurable gains in both English and Chinese math reasoning benchmarks.

## Why multilingual math data matters

1. Diversity in representation of problem styles and language patterns. Math content on the web is not just translations of the same problems. Chinese math problems (K-12 and exam-style Gaokao problems) and Chinese-language discussion pages have distinct phrasing, conventions, and frequently different problem families. Training on these variations exposes the model to more ways of expressing the same underlying reasoning patterns.

2. Cross-lingual transfer for reasoning patterns. Mathematical reasoning is structured. Learning how to decompose problems, embed intermediate steps, and manipulate algebraic expressions in one language helps the model generalize the underlying procedures when the surface language changes. The DeepSeekMath paper reports improvements in Chinese benchmarks alongside English gains, indicating transfer across languages.

3. Better coverage for non-English users and datasets. Many existing math corpora are English-only (the paper contrasts DeepSeekMath with corpora that are primarily English, like Proof-Pile-2 and others). By including Chinese content, DeepSeekMath avoids the bias of English-centric training and performs better on Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH, MGSM-zh, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA.

## Evidence from DeepSeekMath (what the paper reports)

- The DeepSeekMath Corpus is explicitly multilingual, with English and Chinese being the two most represented languages. Compared to prior math-specific corpora that were largely English-centric, training on DeepSeekMath improved Chinese math reasoning benchmarks while also improving English benchmarks.
- DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperformed other open-source base models across a range of English and Chinese benchmarks (quantitative reasoning and multiple-choice). The paper highlights gains on GSM8K, MATH (English), and Chinese datasets including CMATH and Gaokao variants.
- Instruction tuning and subsequent reinforcement learning (GRPO) further increased accuracy across both English and Chinese tasks, suggesting multilingual pre-training provides a stronger foundation for downstream improvements.

Note: the paper does not claim that Chinese content alone is the sole source of gains; rather, multilingual math content is a major contributing factor in combination with other design choices (data selection pipeline, starting from a code-trained model, instruction tuning, and RL).

## Practical implications

For educators and content creators
- Models trained on multilingual math corpora can better assist learners in their native language. A bilingual or multilingual model can answer questions, generate explanations, and produce worked solutions aligned with local problem styles (for example, Chinese Gaokao-style questions).
- Content creators translating math lessons can benefit from models that understand both the target domain and the target language conventions—reducing the work needed to make explanations pedagogically appropriate.

For researchers and builders
- If your goal is global competence, collecting high-quality math data in the target languages matters. The DeepSeekMath pipeline shows that Common Crawl contains a lot of math content; careful filtering (seeded classifiers, domain-level augmentation, human validation, and decontamination against evaluation sets) scales that into a useful corpus.
- Multilingual pre-training can be complementary to other interventions (code pre-training, instruction tuning, RL). In DeepSeekMath, code pre-training and math pre-training together yielded better tool-aided math performance; multilingual math data did not hurt English performance and improved Chinese performance.
- Benchmarks: include non-English evaluation when claiming improvements in "mathematical reasoning." Models trained on English-only corpora risk underperforming for large user bases that operate in other languages.

For educational technology and deployment
- Multilingual models open access: countries that rely heavily on non-English curricula (e.g., China, many other regions) will benefit from models that can handle local exam formats and curricula. This is relevant for tutoring systems, automatic grading, and test-prep assistants.
- Beware of data biases: the paper notes remaining weaknesses in certain domains (geometry/theorem proof), and that some exam styles (e.g., triangle/ellipse problems) were still challenging—likely reflecting gaps in the pre-training distribution. So even large multilingual corpora need targeted augmentation for domain coverage.

## Practical takeaways for assembling a bilingual math dataset

- Start with a high-quality seed (DeepSeekMath used OpenWebMath). Train a fast, scalable classifier (fastText in their pipeline) to recall similar pages from a large web crawl.
- Expand the seed iteratively: identify math-related domains via the initial recall pass, annotate representative URL patterns, harvest additional pages, and re-train the classifier.
- Decontaminate thoroughly: remove substrings overlapping with evaluation benchmarks to avoid leakage.
- Include diverse formats: explanatory web pages, exercises, worked solutions, forum Q&A, and programming/code that implements math (DeepSeekMath mixes code and math tokens).
- Make the corpus multilingual intentionally: include high-quality non-English sources (the paper emphasizes Chinese), not just translated content.

## Limitations and cautions

- Coverage is not uniform across all mathematical subdomains. The DeepSeekMath authors observed weaker capability in some geometry and theorem-proof tasks, suggesting targeted data collection is still needed for those areas.
- Including multilingual data helps, but it is one of several ingredients. DeepSeekMath’s success also depended on starting from a code-trained base model, careful scaling of pre-training, instruction tuning, and reinforcement learning (GRPO).
- Quality matters more than sheer size. The paper emphasizes an engineered selection pipeline—throwing low-quality or noisy foreign-language text at a model won’t produce the same gains.

## Closing

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that multilingual math corpora—specifically adding Chinese math data in their case—deliver practical improvements in math reasoning across languages. For anyone building math-capable LLMs for international use, the lesson is clear: invest in high-quality, language-appropriate math data and treat multilingual collection as a first-class design decision rather than an afterthought.



# From Classroom Solutions to Formal Proofs

## Introduction

Language models have made huge strides at solving textbook-style math problems — think GSM8K and MATH — by producing step-by-step solutions in natural language. But there is a distinct and important next step: autoformalization, the task of converting an informal human solution into a formal, machine-checkable proof in a theorem prover such as Isabelle. DeepSeekMath shows that a domain-tailored model can not only produce human-readable chain-of-thought solutions but also generate formal proofs that automated proof tools can validate. This post unpacks what that means, how DeepSeekMath approaches it, and why this matters for correctness and the future of verified mathematics.

## What is autoformalization and why does it matter?

Autoformalization is the translation of informal mathematical reasoning (the kind of proofs you write in a classroom or on a forum) into a formal language that a proof assistant can verify. A formal proof is composed of logically precise steps that a proof assistant checks rigorously; if the assistant accepts a proof, the result is considered machine-verified.

Why this matters:

- Correctness: Informal proofs can hide unstated assumptions, hand-wavy steps, or subtle errors. Formal proofs leave no room for ambiguity.
- Reproducibility: Formal artifacts are precise and can be re-checked automatically years later.
- Tooling: Formal proofs enable automated reasoning tools (e.g., Sledgehammer in Isabelle) to discharge routine subgoals and even suggest lemmas.
- Scientific rigor: For complex theorems, machine-checked proofs raise confidence in correctness and can find gaps humans miss.

## DeepSeekMath’s autoformalization pipeline (as used in the paper)

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was evaluated on the informal-to-formal theorem proving task from prior work and on the miniF2F benchmark, targeting Isabelle as the proof assistant. The evaluation pipeline used in the paper follows this structure:

1. Inputs: an informal statement, a formal counterpart of the statement (the target theorem expressed in the proof assistant’s syntax), and an informal proof sketch written in natural language.
2. Prompting: few-shot prompting is used to guide the model to produce a formal proof in Isabelle language. The model is prompted to generate a proof script that, if correct, should be checkable by Isabelle.
3. Proof sketch + automation: generated proof sketches are combined with Sledgehammer, an automated prover integrated with Isabelle, to fill in or discharge subgoals automatically. In practice, the model often supplies the high-level structure and Sledgehammer attempts to complete the low-level details.
4. Verification: the resulting Isabelle proof is checked by the proof assistant. Successful verification on miniF2F counts as an automated proof success.

Two technical points worth highlighting:
- The model produces formal proof text (Isabelle proof script), not just natural-language steps.
- Sledgehammer is used as an automated helper: human-like high-level guidance from the model plus automated tactics increase the chance of a fully verified proof.

## What the miniF2F results tell us

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B showed "strong few-shot autoformalization performance" on miniF2F using Isabelle. While the paper does not claim parity with the very top closed-source systems on all theorem tasks, these results are significant for several reasons:

- Scaling vs. data quality: DeepSeekMath-Base is a 7B-parameter model that underwent domain-focused pretraining on a large, curated math corpus (120B math tokens extracted from Common Crawl). Achieving credible autoformalization results with a relatively small model points to the power of targeted pretraining and data selection.
- Practical pipeline: The combination of model-generated formal proofs with an off-the-shelf automated prover (Sledgehammer) is an effective, pragmatic approach. The model handles higher-level reasoning and structure; automated tools handle low-level search and discharge steps.
- Few-shot effectiveness: The model operates in a few-shot setting — it is not presented as a fully fine-tuned autoformalizer but as a model that, after math pretraining, can be prompted to output Isabelle proofs with few examples. This suggests transfer from informal reasoning competence to formalization capability.

## Why the combination of natural and formal reasoning is powerful

DeepSeekMath’s results illustrate a promising hybrid approach: combine the model’s ability to generate structured, human-understandable solutions (language reasoning, chain-of-thought) with formal tooling that demands exactness. This hybridization gives:

- Better correctness guarantees than standalone natural-language solutions, because a verifier confirms the final artifact.
- A scalable workflow: models propose structured candidate proofs; automated provers try to complete or check them. Successful combinations could dramatically reduce human effort in formalizing mathematics.

## Limitations and caveats

The paper also points to limits and open problems that temper enthusiasm and guide future work:

- Domain and data biases: DeepSeekMath’s pretraining corpus, while large and high-quality, may under-represent certain problem types. The authors note weaknesses in geometry-related problems (e.g., triangles and ellipses) in dry runs, which could reflect data selection bias.
- Model scale and few-shot behavior: At 7B parameters, DeepSeekMath shows good zero-shot and few-shot performance, but closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4) still outperform it on few-shot improvements. Model scale and diversity of formalization examples likely matter.
- Automation reliance: Success often depends on Sledgehammer / automated provers to fill gaps. If a model’s produced proof sketch is too high-level or the tactics do not match the prover’s strengths, verification fails. Improving models’ alignment with the proof assistant’s tactic language remains an open engineering and modeling challenge.
- Coverage: The paper’s evaluation is on miniF2F, a specific benchmark. The effectiveness of this pipeline on broader theorem corpora, or on formalizing lengthy or research-level proofs, remains to be demonstrated.

## Where this points next

The results in the paper sketch several promising directions:

- Better pretraining and data selection targeted at formalization tasks, including harvesting domains (geometry, topology) where current corpora are light.
- Tighter integration between model outputs and prover tactics: training models to produce not only high-level proofs but also sequences of tactics tailored to the prover.
- Iterative or RL-based refinement that rewards verified proofs, potentially improving the likelihood that generated proofs are machine-checkable.
- Building richer process-reward models that give fine-grained feedback during proof generation (not only outcome-based rewards), which could help models learn to produce more checkable artifacts.

## Conclusion

DeepSeekMath takes a meaningful step from solving classroom-style word problems toward generating machine-checkable proofs in Isabelle. Its strong few-shot autoformalization results on miniF2F show that targeted pretraining and a hybrid workflow — model-generated formal text plus automated provers like Sledgehammer — can produce verifiable proofs even with a relatively small model. There are clear limitations and many open problems, but the work underscores a promising route: augmenting human mathematical practice with models that both explain and verify. The future of verified math will likely be a collaboration between language models that draft structured proofs and proof assistants that guarantee their correctness.



# Build Your Own Domain LLM: Lessons You Can Reuse

DeepSeekMath is a useful case study if your team wants to build a domain-specialized LLM (math in their case) from public data and open training recipes. Below I distill the practical blueprint and concrete lessons from that work you can reuse for other domains (physics, law, biology, finance, etc.). I focus on what worked, what didn’t, and the trade-offs between supervised instruction tuning and reinforcement learning in a domain setting.

## High-level recipe

1. Start from a strong base model (preferably one already trained on code if your domain benefits from structured reasoning).
2. Build a large, high-precision domain corpus from Common Crawl using an iterative classifier-based mining pipeline (recall → expand → refine).
3. Remove benchmark contamination with strict n-gram matching.
4. Continue pre-training on the domain corpus mixed with useful auxiliary data (code, natural language).
5. Supervised fine-tune on domain instruction data (CoT, program-of-thought, tool-integrated formats).
6. Optionally apply reinforcement learning (RL) to further align the model; use efficient RL variants to limit resource costs and stabilize training.

Below I unpack each step with the practical details and trade-offs shown in DeepSeekMath.

## 1) Data mining from Common Crawl — a practical pipeline

DeepSeekMath shows you can extract large, high-quality domain corpora from Common Crawl with an iterative, classifier-driven approach.

- Seed: start with a small, high-quality domain seed (they used OpenWebMath).
- Train a fastText classifier on positives (seed) and negatives (random CC pages). Concrete setup used in the paper: vector dim 256, lr 0.1, n-gram up to 3, min-occurrence 3, 3 epochs.
- Apply the classifier on deduplicated Common Crawl. Dedup and near-dedup lower the web pages from raw CC to a manageable set (the paper reduced CC to ~40B HTML pages before mining).
- Rank pages by classifier score and keep top pages. Repeat iteratively:
  - Group pages by domain; find domains with >10% recall and manually annotate math-related URL patterns to expand positives.
  - Retrain the classifier with the expanded positives and mine again.
- Iterate until marginal gains vanish (DeepSeekMath stopped after 4 iterations; final harvest: ~35.5M math pages ≈ 120B math tokens).

Why iterate? The initial seed often lacks diversity, so iterative domain expansion captures more styles and subdomains.

Practical takeaways:
- Use a fast, scalable classifier (fastText or similar) for large-scale CC filtering.
- Domain-aware expansion (per-domain heuristics and some manual URL labeling) improves recall substantially.
- Iteration matters: a few rounds can multiply the useful corpus size.

## 2) Decontamination: protect benchmarks faithfully

DeepSeekMath used a strict n-gram exact-matching filter to avoid contaminating evaluation sets:

- Remove any text segment that contains a 10-gram substring matching any substring from your evaluation benchmarks.
- For benchmark texts shorter than 10 tokens but ≥3 tokens, use exact matching.

This is conservative but effective to avoid leakage. If you care about rigorous evaluation, adopt a similar exact-match filter. Note: it’s an imperfect but clear rule; more complex decontamination is possible but requires more engineering.

## 3) Why start from a code-trained model?

A key empirical finding: initializing from a code-specialized base (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) gave better downstream mathematical reasoning than starting from a general LLM. The paper observed:

- Code pre-training improves both program-aided mathematical reasoning and reasoning without tools.
- Two-stage vs one-stage observations:
  - Two-stage (code → math) improved program-aided math; later math training further improved it.
  - Mixing code and math tokens in a single-stage training mitigated catastrophic forgetting and preserved coding skill while aiding program-aided math.
- For the 7B and 1.3B scales they used, code training provided a clear benefit.

Takeaway for other domains: if your domain involves algorithmic or structured reasoning (e.g., computational biology, quantitative finance), starting from a model already fluent with code or structured formalism can help learning to express solutions as programs or structured derivations.

## 4) Pre-training mix and scale choices

DeepSeekMath-Base 7B was trained for 500B tokens with this mix:
- 56% DeepSeekMath Corpus (120B math tokens overall)
- 4% AlgebraicStack
- 10% arXiv
- 20% GitHub code
- 10% web natural language (English + Chinese)

They observed that:
- Large, high-quality domain web data (Common Crawl mining) is highly effective.
- ArXiv-style scientific papers did not meaningfully improve mathematical benchmarks in their experiments (see section below on arXiv caveats).
- Multilingual domain tokens (English + Chinese) improved performance on Chinese benchmarks — if your domain is multilingual, include non-English sources.

Recommendation: prioritize diverse, high-precision domain web data; include code if it helps; be skeptical that adding specialized corpora (like arXiv) will always help — measure.

## 5) Supervised instruction tuning: formats to prepare

DeepSeekMath built a 776K-example instruction tuning collection including:
- Chain-of-thought (CoT) solutions
- Program-of-thought (PoT) examples (solutions as runnable programs)
- Tool-integrated reasoning (e.g., calls to Python with sympy)

Mix formats if users will both want textual reasoning and program/tool outputs. Train for a modest budget of SFT steps (in the paper they ran 500 steps with batch size 256 and lr 5e-5 for the 7B SFT stage).

Format diversity helps the model generalize across human-oriented explanation and executable solutions.

## 6) When and how to use RL — GRPO as an efficient option

DeepSeekMath shows RL can further boost instruction-tuned models, but the choice of RL algorithm and supervision style matters.

Why RL? In their experiments, RL improved the model’s “Maj@K” performance: making the correct answer more probable within the top-K generated samples, rather than increasing pass@K (i.e., improving the model’s fundamental reasoning capability less than improving the distribution to favor correct outputs). That suggests RL is especially effective at shaping the sampling distribution.

Their contribution: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an RL variant designed for LLM fine-tuning that reduces resource costs and aligns with comparative reward labeling.

Key elements of GRPO:
- Remove the separate value (critic) model used by PPO; instead estimate baseline from group-average rewards.
- For each prompt q, sample a group of G outputs {o_i} from the old policy and score them with the reward model. DeepSeekMath used G = 64 in their main RL runs.
- Normalize group rewards (subtract mean and divide by std) and use the per-output normalized reward as the advantage (outcome supervision) or sum of future step rewards when using process supervision.
- GRPO objective is analogous to PPO’s clipped surrogate, but uses group-relative advantages and directly adds a KL regularizer between current policy and a reference policy to constrain drift.

Why this helps:
- You avoid a separate large value network (big memory win).
- The group-relative baseline matches how reward models are often trained (comparative judgments over outputs for the same prompt).
- It accommodates both outcome supervision (single final reward) and process supervision (per-step rewards along a chain-of-thought), the latter giving fine-grained signal for multi-step reasoning.

Practical settings from the paper:
- Policy lr: 1e-6 during GRPO.
- KL coefficient: 0.04 (they regularize with an explicit KL term).
- Sample G = 64 outputs per question for RL.
- They trained reward models from policy outputs and used iterative RL (retrain RM with replayed data and continue). Iterative RL improved performance, and most gains came in the first iteration.

When is RL worth it?
- If you care about improving the probability mass of correct outputs (i.e., better majority answers across sampling runs), RL can be effective.
- If your supervised data is limited or you want to optimize behavior for a particular evaluation metric, RL (with a good reward model) helps.

Caveats:
- RL depends heavily on the reward model’s generalization and noise robustness. If the reward model mislabels, RL can push the model in unwanted directions.
- The improvement often reflects distribution shaping (promoting correct outputs among many samples) rather than a fundamental increase in reasoning ability.

## 7) Outcome vs process supervision

- Outcome supervision: one reward at the end of the generated sequence. Simple to implement, but coarse.
- Process supervision: reward per reasoning step (e.g., every step of a chain-of-thought). More informative and can yield better performance for multi-step tasks.

DeepSeekMath found process supervision (step-aware rewards) improves RL results compared to only outcome supervision. If you can annotate or train a reward model that scores intermediate steps, you will usually gain better guidance during RL.

## 8) Pitfalls and surprising findings

- ArXiv is not a panacea. In DeepSeekMath experiments, training on arXiv-heavy corpora (arXiv-only or arXiv-dominated mixes) produced little or even negative improvement on evaluated math benchmarks. Possible reasons include style mismatch, noisy extraction, or limited coverage of the kinds of elementary-to-competition problems in the benchmarks. Don’t assume domain-adjacent scientific corpora will always help — validate.
- Benchmark contamination is real and must be addressed with conservative exact-matching filtering.
- RL often improves sampling distribution rather than raw capability — measure pass@K and maj@K (or top-K majority) to understand what changed.
- Reward model quality and generalization are a limiting factor. RL can amplify reward model biases or errors.

## 9) Concrete numbers you can reuse (as a starting point)

Use these as reference points — validate them for your domain and compute budget.

- Large domain corpus target: DeepSeekMath harvested ~120B domain tokens from Common Crawl (35.5M domain pages).
- Pre-training tokens on a base 7B model: they did 500B tokens with the mixture noted earlier.
- Instruction tuning: ~776K instruction examples (mix of CoT, PoT, tool-integrated).
- RL sample group size: G = 64 outputs per prompt.
- RL policy learning rate: 1e-6; KL coefficient: 0.04 (their RL improved GSM8K from 82.9% → 88.2% and MATH from 46.8% → 51.7% in-chain-of-thought accuracy for their 7B model).
- Classifier for mining: fastText with dim=256, lr=0.1, n-gram=3, epochs=3.

These are not prescriptive for other domains, but they are useful starting hyperparameters and scales demonstrated to work in this paper.

## 10) Final recommendations

- Invest first in a good domain corpus. High-quality and diverse web-derived domain pages (properly filtered and deduplicated) scale well.
- If your domain benefits from structured derivations or programmatic solutions, prefer an initial checkpoint that has code pre-training.
- Prepare diverse SFT formats (CoT, PoT, tool calls) matching the ways users will ask the model to produce solutions.
- Use RL when you want to push likely-correct outputs higher in the sampled distribution — prefer efficient RL variants (GRPO-like) if memory is a constraint. Use process rewards if you can.
- Validate assumptions: check whether popular sources (e.g., arXiv) truly help your target tasks; don’t assume they do.
- Guard against benchmark leakage with conservative exact-match decontamination.

If you adopt this blueprint, focus first on the data pipeline and an SFT curriculum; RL tends to give further gains, but only after you have a reasonable SFT model and a reliable reward model. DeepSeekMath demonstrates that with careful data engineering and targeted RL, comparatively small models (7B) can approach the performance of much larger closed models on specialized tasks — a promising pattern for teams building domain LLMs.
