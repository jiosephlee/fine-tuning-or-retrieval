Chapter Title: Iterative Domain Expansion and the Assumption of Domain Homogeneity in Math Data Collection

# Overview of the Iterative Data Collection Pipeline

The paper introduces a multi-iteration pipeline to construct a large-scale, high-quality mathematical corpus from Common Crawl. The process begins with a seed corpus composed of curated mathematical web texts and then progressively expands to recall more math-relevant pages. At the core of this pipeline is a fastText-based classifier trained to distinguish mathematical content from general web pages. In the initial stage, the classifier is trained using 500,000 positive examples from OpenWebMath and 500,000 negative examples from Common Crawl, with specific hyperparameters such as a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of $0.1$, an $n$-gram length of $3$, a minimum word frequency of $3$, and $3$ epochs.

After training the classifier, the pipeline applies URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication to the entire Common Crawl, reducing it to 40 billion HTML pages. The classifier then ranks pages by their predicted probability of being mathematical. Only top-scoring pages are retained, and the volume of retained tokens is empirically calibrated through pre-training experiments at scales of 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens. This iterative procedure continuously improves the classifier by augmenting the seed corpus with additional positive examples mined from domains that are likely math-related. Across four iterations, the process yields 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120B tokens, with diminishing returns observed by the fourth iteration, at which point about $98\\%$ of the retrievable relevant content had already been collected in the previous pass.

A crucial stage in this pipeline is the iterative domain-expansion step, where the entire Common Crawl is reorganized into disjoint domains, each defined by a common base URL. For each domain, the pipeline computes the percentage of pages that were collected in prior iterations as math-relevant. Domains with sufficiently high percentages are then flagged as math-related, and their internal URL structures are manually annotated to identify math-specific subpaths (for example, URLs like \\url{mathoverflow.net/questions}). Pages linked to those math-specific subpaths that were not yet collected are added to the seed corpus. This augmentation diversifies the positive examples for retraining the classifier, thereby improving its recall of mathematical pages in subsequent iterations.

# Formalization of the Math-Related Domain Heuristic

At the heart of the domain-expansion step is a simple but powerful heuristic. Let $D$ denote a domain (i.e., all pages sharing a base URL), and let $p(D)$ denote the proportion of pages in $D$ that have been collected as mathematical content in the current iteration. The pipeline marks a domain as math-related when this proportion exceeds a fixed threshold. Formally, one defines a threshold $\\tau$ such that:
$$
\\tau = 0.1,
$$
and declares $D$ math-related if:
$$
p(D) > \\tau.
$$

In practice, the pipeline sets $\\tau$ to $0.1$, that is, a domain is considered math-related if more than $10\\%$ of its pages have been recalled as mathematical in the current iteration. Once such a domain is identified, the pipeline proceeds to manually annotate math-specific URL subpaths within it and harvest all linked pages from those subpaths that were not yet collected. These newly added pages then become part of the seed corpus that trains the next version of the fastText classifier, improving its ability to recall math pages outside the currently known set.

This heuristic operationalizes a pragmatic balance between precision and recall. A domain need not be entirely mathematical to be useful; if a sufficiently large fraction of its content is math-related, the domain can serve as a fertile source of additional positive examples. The threshold $\\tau=0.1$ makes the heuristic permissive enough to capture domains with mixed content while conservative enough to avoid indiscriminately labeling domains with trivial or spurious math-like content.

# The Implicit Assumption of Domain Homogeneity

The iterative domain-expansion method implicitly rests on an assumption of domain homogeneity. While the pipeline does not state this assumption explicitly, it is embedded in the logic of the heuristic. The assumption can be stated informally as follows: if $p(D)$ is large enough (in this case, $p(D) > 0.1$), then the uncollected pages within $D$—especially those reachable via math-specific subpaths—are sufficiently likely to be mathematical that including them as positive examples will improve the classifier.

One can formalize this assumption by positing a latent label distribution over a domain. Let each page $x \\in D$ have a latent binary label $y(x) \\in \\{0,1\\}$ indicating whether the page is mathematical. The heuristic presumes that for domains with $p(D) > 0.1$, the conditional probability $\\mathbb{P}(y(x)=1 \\mid x \\in D, x \\text{ linked via annotated math-specific subpaths})$ is appreciably higher than the unconditional probability across Common Crawl, and is large enough to justify bulk inclusion as positive data. In other words, the heuristic assumes that the domain’s structure, and especially the annotated subpaths, induce a concentration of math-related content, so that pages sampled from those subpaths are likely to belong to the same distribution $\\mathcal{P}_{\\text{math}}$ the classifier is intended to model.

This homogeneity assumption is not absolute. The pipeline mitigates risks associated with heterogeneity by manually annotating math-specific subpaths, such as \\url{mathoverflow.net/questions}, rather than indiscriminately collecting all pages within the domain. Restricting to well-identified math substructures within the domain is a way to refine the assumption: even if the domain contains a mix of topics, the annotated subpaths are treated as relatively homogeneous clusters of math content. This constrained form of domain homogeneity—homogeneity within selected substructures—connects the heuristic threshold $p(D) > 0.1$ with manual curation aimed at preserving quality.

# Intuition Behind the 10% Threshold and Its Role in Expansion

The choice of $\\tau = 0.1$ reflects a practical compromise. If $\\tau$ were too high, many domains with valuable math content but substantial non-math sections would be missed; if $\\tau$ were too low, the pipeline might over-include domains with incidental math-like pages, introducing noise. By setting $\\tau$ at $10\\%$, the pipeline is tuned to admit domains that have a meaningful presence of mathematics without requiring near-complete purity.

The intuition is that domains with at least $10\\%$ of their pages classified as mathematical are likely to possess identifiable math-oriented substructures or sections. When $p(D) > 0.1$, a manual annotation step can usually locate URL paths where math content is concentrated. The pipeline then supplements the seed corpus with pages that were not previously captured by the classifier but are connected to these math-focused URL paths. The net effect is to increase positive example diversity, which reduces classifier bias toward the original seed corpus and improves recall in subsequent iterations.

Moreover, the $10\\%$ criterion provides a scalable and domain-agnostic rule. The pipeline processes an enormous number of domains from Common Crawl. A uniform threshold such as $\\tau=0.1$ simplifies the decision logic and makes the expansion step reproducible, minimizing ad hoc judgments about individual domains while still enabling targeted manual annotation where needed.

# Risks, Trade-offs, and Mitigations in Assuming Homogeneity

The domain homogeneity assumption entails risks. If a domain has a substantial fraction of math pages but also contains highly heterogeneous content, indiscriminately expanding within the domain can introduce non-math pages into the positive set, degrading classifier precision. This risk is further compounded if link structures are broad or if math-like keywords in non-math sections trigger spurious inclusion.

The pipeline addresses these risks in several ways. First, the expansion is not blind: it leverages manual annotation of math-specific URL subpaths within identified domains. This restriction confines expansion to substructures that are strongly associated with math content, thereby operationalizing a localized homogeneity assumption that is more realistic than a domain-wide assumption. Second, the pipeline is iterative. Newly added pages from annotated subpaths are used to retrain the classifier, which then reassesses the entire crawl. Because each iteration is followed by ranking and retention of only top-scoring pages, the classifier can learn to better discriminate between true math content and superficial matches, even within math-related domains.

Finally, the pipeline includes benchmark decontamination. It filters out any web pages that contain exact matches to $n$-gram strings from evaluation benchmarks such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval. Specifically, any text segment containing a 10-gram string that exactly matches a substring from the benchmark is removed, and for benchmark texts shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams, exact matching is used to exclude contaminated pages. This decontamination step ensures that expansions based on the homogeneity assumption do not inadvertently introduce benchmark leakage, preserving the integrity of downstream evaluations.

# Practical Outcomes of the Expansion Policy

The iterative domain-expansion strategy, combined with the $10\\%$ threshold and manual annotation of math-specific subpaths, culminates in a corpus of 120B math tokens after four iterations. Notably, by the fourth iteration, the marginal gain diminishes, with about $98\\%$ of the data already obtained by the previous iteration. This indicates that the combined effect of the heuristic and annotation yields a near-saturation of retrievable math content within the Common Crawl under the constraints and model capacity used.

The downstream results provide empirical validation for the expansion strategy. Models trained on the resulting corpus demonstrate superior performance across multiple mathematical benchmarks relative to those trained on other mathematical corpora. The multilingual composition of the corpus—dominated by English and Chinese, but not restricted to English—supports improvements on Chinese math benchmarks as well. These outcomes suggest that the $10\\%$ domain heuristic, together with the homogeneity assumption restricted to annotated subpaths, effectively balances precision and recall, contributing to a high-quality dataset that sustains strong learning signals over long training horizons.

# Conceptual Understanding: Why Homogeneity Matters

From a learning perspective, homogeneity within the positive dataset is critical because the classifier relies on consistent patterns to distinguish math pages from general content. If positive examples are too heterogeneous, the classifier may overfit to idiosyncratic features of non-math pages or fail to learn stable signals that generalize. The domain homogeneity assumption, applied to annotated subpaths, posits that pages within those substructures share salient mathematical features—notation, problem formats, proofs, terminology, and structured reasoning—that collectively define the target distribution $\\mathcal{P}_{\\text{math}}$.

By enforcing a minimal concentration of math content via $p(D) > 0.1$, and then refining selection through targeted annotation, the pipeline steers the seed corpus toward clusters where homogeneity is high enough to improve classifier training. Each iteration then magnifies these gains, producing a progressively more capable classifier that can identify math pages outside the originally known domains. The process thus operationalizes a simple principle: use global signals (domain-level math proportion) to find promising regions, and use local signals (URL subpath annotation) to ensure that expansions remain within coherent clusters of math-related content.

# Summary

The iterative domain-expansion step in the data collection pipeline marks a domain as math-related when more than $10\\%$ of its pages have been collected as mathematical content. The implicit assumption underlying the use of this heuristic is a form of domain homogeneity: within domains passing this threshold, and especially within manually annotated math-specific subpaths, uncollected pages are sufficiently similar to previously collected math pages to warrant inclusion as positive examples. This assumption is moderated and made practical by manual annotation, iterative retraining, ranking-based retention of top-scoring pages, and strict benchmark decontamination. Empirically, the combination of the $10\\%$ heuristic and constrained homogeneity yields a large, high-quality, multilingual math corpus that sustains superior downstream performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of this principled yet pragmatic approach to web-scale mathematical data collection.
