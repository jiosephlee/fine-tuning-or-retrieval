Chapter Title: Capacity Constraints in One-Stage Code-and-Math Training: Evidence from DeepSeekMath

# Background: Mathematical Reasoning, Code Training, and Evaluation Settings

The DeepSeekMath paper investigates how large language models acquire mathematical reasoning ability through pre-training and post-training interventions, with particular attention to the interplay between mathematical text and code. The authors study both mathematics “without tool use,” where the model solves problems via natural language chain-of-thought reasoning, and “with tool use,” where the model writes and executes Python programs to compute intermediate results. These two evaluation modes probe different facets of reasoning: the former tests internal symbolic manipulation and multi-step logical coherence in natural language, while the latter tests the model’s ability to orchestrate programmatic computation as part of its problem-solving process.

To separate the effects of data composition from model scale, the authors conduct controlled experiments on a model denoted DeepSeek-LLM \(1.3\text{B}\), which follows the architectural framework of DeepSeek LLMs and is small enough to train repeatedly under different curricula. The training corpora include a large math-specific corpus collected from Common Crawl (the DeepSeekMath Corpus), as well as code tokens and general natural language tokens. Across experiments, the authors vary how code tokens and math tokens are combined in training in order to examine their distinct and combined effects on downstream mathematical reasoning, both with and without tool use.

The key configurations compared are two-stage vs. one-stage training. In two-stage training, the model first undergoes a long pre-training phase on a single modality (e.g., code), and then a second phase on math-only data. In one-stage training, math tokens and code tokens are mixed and presented jointly throughout training. Crucially, the evaluation includes benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, where performance is measured in both the chain-of-thought format and the program-of-thought format, allowing the authors to observe how training choices affect each reasoning mode.

# Experimental Setups: Two-Stage and One-Stage Paths

The paper’s analysis of code training and math training proceeds through clearly delineated training regimens on the \(1.3\text{B}\)-parameter model. The authors define two-stage settings where the model is first specialized and then adapted, and one-stage settings where multiple data sources are blended from the start.

In the two-stage regimen, the authors consider:
1. Code \(\rightarrow\) Math: train on \(400\text{B}\) code tokens, then on \(150\text{B}\) math tokens.
2. General \(\rightarrow\) Math: as a control, replace the first stage with \(400\text{B}\) general-language tokens, then train on \(150\text{B}\) math tokens.

In the one-stage regimen, the authors consider:
1. Math-only: train on \(150\text{B}\) math tokens.
2. Mixed Code+Math: train on a mixture of \(400\text{B}\) code tokens and \(150\text{B}\) math tokens in a single stage.

All experiments are conducted under the same training framework, optimizer, and learning rate schedule described in the paper, ensuring that observed differences can be attributed to data composition and curriculum timing rather than to unrelated training variables. Performance is assessed on mathematical benchmarks with and without tool use, which neatly separates improvements in program-aided reasoning from improvements in pure natural-language mathematical reasoning.

# Results: Code Training Helps, But Mixing Can Harm No-Tool Math Reasoning

The authors’ findings can be distilled into three core observations.

First, code training benefits program-aided mathematical reasoning. Under the two-stage setting, the first code stage alone “already significantly enhances the ability to solve GSM8K and MATH problems using Python,” and the subsequent math stage further improves results. Under the one-stage mixed setting, combining code and math tokens “synergizes coding and program-aided mathematical reasoning,” while also mitigating catastrophic forgetting of coding ability that can arise in a strict two-stage curriculum. In other words, as far as tool use is concerned, code exposure functions as a useful catalyst for math problem solving.

Second, code training also helps mathematical reasoning without tool use, but the effect depends on curriculum structure. In the two-stage regimen, the first-stage code training yields “moderate enhancements” on math without tool use, and it “boosts the efficiency of the subsequent math training,” eventually giving the strongest results in this evaluation mode. This indicates that front-loading code may prime the model’s internal representations in a way that makes later math-only training more effective.

Third, and most central to this chapter, mixing code and math tokens in one-stage training compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use for the \(1.3\text{B}\) model. The authors report that while the mixed curriculum alleviates catastrophic forgetting and benefits program-aided reasoning, it “compromises mathematical reasoning without tool use.” They then offer a conjecture about why this harm appears in the one-stage mixed setting: “One conjecture is that DeepSeek-LLM \(1.3\text{B}\), due to its limited scale, lacks the capacity to fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously.” This conjecture links the observed degradation specifically to model capacity, and it is consistent with the contrasting success of the two-stage regimen on no-tool reasoning, where code and math are not simultaneously competing for representational resources.

# Interpreting the Capacity Conjecture

The authors’ conjecture focuses on the parameter budget of DeepSeek-LLM \(1.3\text{B}\) relative to the complexity of jointly learning from code and mathematical natural language when they are mixed in a single stage. Formally, suppose a model with parameters \(\theta\) is trained on two distributions, \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{code}}\) and \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{math}}\), whose token-level objectives are interleaved over the training horizon. In a one-stage regimen, the model must minimize an expected loss of the form
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \alpha \mathcal{D}_{\text{code}} + (1-\alpha)\mathcal{D}_{\text{math}}} \left[\ell(\theta; x)\right],
\]
where \(\alpha \in [0,1]\) encodes the mixture proportion. If the model’s representational capacity is tight relative to the complexity and diversity of both distributions, gradient updates driven by one component can pull parameters in directions that locally worsen performance on the other, especially in subdomains where the two distributions demand distinct internal structures. Under limited capacity, such trade-offs can surface as compromises in the most representation-hungry behaviors, which, according to the paper’s results, appear in the no-tool mathematical reasoning mode.

By contrast, in a two-stage regimen, the model optimizes
\[
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{code}}}\!\left[\ell(\theta; x)\right]
\quad \text{followed by} \quad
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{math}}}\!\left[\ell(\theta; x)\right],
\]
which effectively allows the second stage to re-specialize the model’s internal representations for math without simultaneous pressure to maintain code-oriented generality. The paper observes that this curriculum “boosts the efficiency of the subsequent math training” and produces the strongest results on math without tool use, consistent with the idea that staged specialization fits within the \(1.3\text{B}\) parameter budget more comfortably than simultaneous multi-domain optimization.

Importantly, the same one-stage mixed regimen that harms no-tool math reasoning is beneficial for program-aided reasoning and for preserving coding skill. This asymmetry reinforces the capacity-based intuition: different behaviors draw on overlapping but not identical representational subspaces. The mixed curriculum appears to align well with the needs of tool use, where code tokens and program synthesis are directly instrumental, while it stretches the capacity needed to consolidate purely linguistic mathematical reasoning to its fullest extent.

# Curriculum Design under Capacity Constraints

The empirical patterns in the paper suggest a curriculum-design principle conditioned on parameter count. For the \(1.3\text{B}\) model, a two-stage curriculum—code first, math second—achieves the best math reasoning without tool use. At the same time, a mixed one-stage curriculum confers advantages to program-aided reasoning and guards against coding degradation. This creates a practical trade-off when the model’s capacity is limited: the mixing that preserves and amplifies code-oriented behaviors may dilute the maximal gains in no-tool mathematics.

Let us make the trade-off explicit. Denote by \(S_{\text{no-tool}}(\theta)\) the score on math benchmarks without tool use, and by \(S_{\text{tool}}(\theta)\) the score with tool use. The paper’s results imply that for the \(1.3\text{B}\) model,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Two-stage (Code} \rightarrow \text{Math)} &: \quad S_{\text{no-tool}} \text{ high}, \quad S_{\text{tool}} \text{ high}, \\
\text{One-stage (Mixed Code+Math)} &: \quad S_{\text{no-tool}} \text{ lower}, \quad S_{\text{tool}} \text{ high}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus, if the primary objective is to maximize \(S_{\text{no-tool}}\) under a tight capacity budget, staging appears preferable. If a balanced objective values \(S_{\text{tool}}\) and coding retention more heavily, mixing can be attractive, even at the expense of some no-tool reasoning ability. The authors’ conjecture attributes this divergence in outcomes to the difficulty of “fully assimilating both code and mathematical data simultaneously” within \(1.3\text{B}\) parameters.

# Relation to Broader Findings in the Paper

The capacity conjecture arises specifically from the \(1.3\text{B}\) experiments. Elsewhere in the paper, the authors train a \(7\text{B}\) model, DeepSeekMath-Base, initialized from a code-trained base (DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 \(7\text{B}\)), and then continue training on a mixture where \(56\%\) of tokens come from the DeepSeekMath Corpus, \(4\%\) from AlgebraicStack, \(10\%\) from arXiv, \(20\%\) from code, and \(10\%\) from general natural language. In this larger-capacity regime, the model achieves strong performance across mathematical tasks, and math training even improves general reasoning benchmarks. While the paper does not claim a direct causality between larger capacity and the viability of mixed training for no-tool math, the contrast in outcomes underscores the plausibility of the capacity-based explanation: what appears deleterious for no-tool reasoning at \(1.3\text{B}\) under mixing does not preclude strong overall results at \(7\text{B}\) under a mixed corpus.

Furthermore, the authors report that arXiv-only training did not help mathematical reasoning for either \(1.3\text{B}\) or \(7\text{B}\) in their experiments, and that code pre-training prior to math training yields notable benefits, especially for program-aided reasoning. These surrounding findings refine the interpretation of the capacity conjecture: it is not merely the presence of non-math data that challenges no-tool mathematical reasoning in one-stage training, but specifically the simultaneous co-optimization on code and math in a small model, which appears to stretch representational resources in a way that degrades maximal no-tool performance.

# Conclusion: Why the One-Stage Mixed Regimen Harmed No-Tool Math and the Role of Capacity

The paper’s empirical results reveal a nuanced interaction between training curriculum and model size. On DeepSeek-LLM \(1.3\text{B}\), mixing code and math tokens in a one-stage training regimen helped program-aided mathematical reasoning and preserved coding skill, but it harmed mathematical reasoning without tool use. The authors conjecture that this harm arises because the \(1.3\text{B}\) model “lacks the capacity to fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously.” In contrast, a two-stage curriculum—first code, then math—enabled the small model to achieve its strongest no-tool reasoning performance, likely by allowing the second stage to specialize the model towards mathematical natural-language reasoning without simultaneous representational demands from code.

This capacity-based interpretation is grounded in the paper’s controlled experiments and aligns with the broader pattern that code exposure is beneficial but must be scheduled carefully when parameters are limited. For students of language model training, the lesson is that curriculum design interacts subtly with parameter count: when capacity is tight, staging may be essential to avoid trade-offs that suppress the most demanding behaviors, such as competition-level mathematics solved purely through natural-language chain-of-thought.
