Chapter Title: Decontamination by Exact n-gram Matching in DeepSeekMath: Assumptions, Mechanics, and Implications

# Introduction

When training language models for mathematical reasoning, a central methodological concern is preventing contamination from evaluation benchmarks. Contamination occurs when portions of benchmark content appear in the pre-training data, potentially inflating evaluation results by allowing the model to memorize answers or solution structures. In the DeepSeekMath study, decontamination is explicitly addressed with a concrete matching rule against a set of English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks. This chapter explains, in depth, the decontamination approach used in DeepSeekMath, the implicit assumption encoded by the rule, and the implications of this choice for training and evaluation.

The central fact that guides this discussion is that the decontamination procedure removes any text segment containing an exact n-gram match with evaluation benchmarks, and therefore implicitly assumes that a 10-gram exact match suffices to identify contamination. We will analyze how the method is implemented within the broader data processing pipeline, precisely what the matching rule entails, why such a rule might be chosen, and what trade-offs it induces in practice. By the end of this chapter, a reader should understand the logic of the rule, its scope, its conservative and liberal aspects, and its role in supporting the integrity of the reported evaluation results.

# The Data Collection Pipeline and Where Decontamination Fits

DeepSeekMath constructs a large-scale mathematical corpus from Common Crawl through an iterative selection pipeline. The process begins by training a fastText classifier to recall mathematical web pages, using OpenWebMath as seed positives and diverse web pages as negatives. Through multiple iterations, the classifier is improved by mining additional math-related domains and manually annotating math-specific URLs, eventually collecting 35.5 million web pages totaling 120 billion tokens. The corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese as major components, and is designed to support both mathematical reasoning and general reasoning improvements.

Decontamination is applied during data collection and cleaning to prevent leakage from evaluation benchmarks into the training data. The paper states that, to avoid benchmark contamination, web pages containing questions or answers from English mathematical benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, and Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and AGIEval, are filtered out by exact n-gram matching. This step is crucial for maintaining the validity of the evaluation results that are later reported for the base, instruction-tuned, and RL-improved models. Without such filtering, the model could encounter benchmark content during pre-training, which would undermine the credibility of downstream evaluations on those same benchmarks.

# The Exact n-gram Decontamination Rule

The decontamination rule is defined as follows. Let the set of evaluation benchmark texts be denoted by a collection of token sequences, where each benchmark problem and solution contributes substrings to this set. The rule filters out any text segment in the pre-training corpus that contains an exact n-gram match with any benchmark substring according to specific thresholds.

Concretely, the paper adopts a 10-gram exact matching criterion for general filtering. Formally, let a tokenized pre-training segment be represented as a sequence of tokens, and let a tokenized benchmark text be represented as another sequence. Define an n-gram as any contiguous subsequence of length $n$. The decontamination procedure removes any segment from the pre-training data if there exists an n-gram of length $n=10$ that appears exactly, as a contiguous subsequence, in both the segment and any benchmark text. Symbolically, if $S$ is a pre-training segment and $B$ is a benchmark text, the segment $S$ is removed if there exist indices $i$ and $j$ such that
$$(S_i, S_{i+1}, \dots, S_{i+9}) = (B_j, B_{j+1}, \dots, B_{j+9}).$$
This exact matching criterion is applied across all relevant English and Chinese benchmarks listed in the study.

An additional case is handled for shorter benchmark texts. If a benchmark text has fewer than $10$ tokens but at least $3$ tokens, then exact matching is performed at that shorter length. In other words, for texts with length $m$ where $3 \le m < 10$, an exact match of an $m$-gram leads to removal. Thus, for shorter items, the threshold adapts to the available text length while maintaining exactness.

Two properties of this rule are worth emphasizing. First, the matching is exact and contiguous: only identical token sequences of the specified length cause removal. Second, the rule is asymmetric with respect to segment length: it always considers fixed length matches of $10$ tokens for longer texts, and shorter exact matches only if the benchmark text itself is shorter than $10$ tokens.

# The Implicit 10-gram Assumption

The adoption of a 10-gram exact match threshold encodes an implicit assumption: a contiguous sequence of $10$ tokens is sufficiently specific to identify contamination from benchmark content. This assumption has practical and conceptual components. Practically, it sets a tractable criterion that can be efficiently checked at scale during corpus construction. Conceptually, it posits that if a pre-training segment contains any 10-token substring that exactly matches a benchmark substring, then the segment is likely contaminated and should be excluded to preserve the integrity of evaluations.

We can formalize the assumption as follows. Let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the event that a pre-training segment contains benchmark content, and let $\mathcal{M}_{10}$ denote the event that a 10-gram exact match occurs with some benchmark substring. The rule treats $\mathcal{M}_{10}$ as a sufficient condition for $\mathcal{C}$. In logical terms, the filtering criterion encodes
$$\mathcal{M}_{10} \implies \mathcal{C},$$
and removes any segment satisfying $\mathcal{M}_{10}$. For shorter benchmark texts of length $m$ with $3 \le m < 10$, the analogous condition $\mathcal{M}_m \implies \mathcal{C}$ is used.

The rule does not require that the entire benchmark problem or solution be matched; rather, it treats the existence of any exact 10-gram overlap as grounds for removal. This conservative stance prioritizes the elimination of potential contamination even if the overlapping substring is a small portion of a larger page. It also implies that the procedure may remove more content than is strictly necessary to prevent leakage, since any segment containing a matching 10-gram, regardless of surrounding context, is excluded.

# Precision–Recall Trade-offs in Exact Matching

The choice of a 10-gram exact match involves trade-offs related to the precision and recall of contamination detection. Although the paper does not quantify these trade-offs, they can be discussed conceptually given the rule’s definition.

On the one hand, exact matching at 10 tokens is precise in the sense that spurious matches are less likely than at shorter lengths. The longer the n-gram, the smaller the probability that an arbitrary pre-training segment coincidentally contains the same exact contiguous token sequence as a benchmark substring. This suggests that $\mathcal{M}_{10}$ is a strong signal for contamination, leading to high precision in identifying true benchmark overlaps.

On the other hand, exact matching may reduce recall if benchmark content is paraphrased, reformatted, or otherwise altered in ways that do not preserve contiguous 10-token sequences. For example, if a pre-training segment contains a semantically equivalent but lexically varied formulation of a benchmark problem, or if formatting differences affect tokenization, the 10-gram condition might not be satisfied even though the content is effectively the same. In such cases, $\mathcal{M}_{10}$ would not trigger removal, despite a possibility of semantic overlap. The method therefore prioritizes a clear and operational criterion over exhaustive detection of semantic duplicates.

The rule also includes a specific policy for short benchmark texts with $3 \le m < 10$, where an exact $m$-gram match suffices for removal. This acknowledges that some benchmark items may be short (while still substantive) and ensures that they are not exempt from filtering merely due to length. At the same time, shorter exact matches, such as $m=3$, may be less specific, potentially increasing the chance of incidental overlaps. The rule thus balances the need to protect short items with a simple exact-match mechanism.

# Scope of Benchmarks and Multilingual Considerations

The decontamination targets the benchmarks used in the paper’s evaluations, spanning both English and Chinese tasks. Specifically, the study mentions English benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, and Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and AGIEval. By including both languages in the decontamination procedure, the approach aligns with the multilingual nature of the DeepSeekMath corpus, which contains extensive English and Chinese mathematical content.

The inclusion of Chinese benchmarks in the filtering set is consistent with the paper’s broader emphasis on multilingual performance. The corpus is designed to improve reasoning in both English and Chinese, and the evaluations demonstrate benefits across languages. Ensuring that Chinese benchmark content is excluded from pre-training helps maintain the validity of reported results on Chinese benchmarks, just as it does for English benchmarks.

# Interaction with Training Stages and Reported Results

DeepSeekMath proceeds through multiple stages: large-scale pre-training on a mixture dominated by the DeepSeekMath Corpus; supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and tool-integrated reasoning data; and reinforcement learning (RL) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The decontamination rule pertains to the construction of the pre-training corpus from Common Crawl, where the bulk of tokens are collected and filtered.

The reported results after pre-training show strong performance for the base model on English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks, and subsequent SFT and RL further improve accuracy, with the RL-enhanced model surpassing 50% on the competition-level MATH dataset. The credibility of these reported numbers relies, in part, on the integrity of the pre-training data with respect to the benchmarks. By removing any segments containing exact 10-gram overlaps (or shorter exact matches for short benchmark texts), the procedure aims to prevent leakage that could compromise the fairness of evaluations. While the paper does not isolate the quantitative effect of decontamination on performance, the presence of a clear decontamination policy supports the validity of the benchmark comparisons presented.

# Practical Mechanics and Consequences

The operational mechanics of the rule are straightforward. For each web page segment considered for inclusion in the pre-training corpus, all contiguous 10-grams are checked against the set of all benchmark substrings. If any match is found, the segment is removed. For benchmark items with fewer than $10$ tokens but at least $3$, exact matching is performed at the item’s length. The rule’s consequence is that even partial overlap at the n-gram level leads to exclusion of the entire segment, not just the overlapping portion.

This conservative stance likely reduces the risk that subtle forms of contamination slip through. In a large-scale collection from Common Crawl, mathematical content often includes problem statements and solutions that can circulate across multiple sites. By treating any exact 10-gram overlap as sufficient grounds for removal, the method errs on the side of minimizing contamination risk. At the same time, because the matching is exact and requires $10$ contiguous tokens for general cases, the rule avoids more aggressive strategies that might require semantic similarity measures or edit-distance thresholds, keeping the pipeline tractable and well-defined.

# Conclusion

The DeepSeekMath decontamination procedure adopts a principled and operationally simple rule: remove any text segment containing an exact n-gram that matches an evaluation benchmark, using $n=10$ for general cases and $n=m$ for shorter benchmark texts with $3 \le m < 10$. This choice encodes an implicit assumption that a 10-gram exact match suffices to identify contamination. The rule’s strengths include clarity, scalability, and a bias toward precision in detecting exact overlaps, alongside a conservative policy of removing any segment containing such overlaps. Its limitations, by design, arise from exact matching: paraphrases or tokenization variants may not be detected.

Within the broader DeepSeekMath pipeline, this decontamination strategy supports the integrity of evaluations on both English and Chinese benchmarks. It complements the paper’s overarching goals: constructing a high-quality, multilingual mathematical corpus from Common Crawl; training a strong base model that benefits both mathematical and general reasoning; and improving further through SFT and GRPO-based RL. The explicit decontamination rule provides a clear foundation for trusting that reported benchmark results are not artifacts of pre-training leakage, aligning methodological rigor with the study’s emphasis on robust mathematical reasoning performance.
