### How is 'self-consistency over 64 samples' evaluated for the 60.9% MATH score?
Question:
In the abstract, the model achieves 60.9% on MATH with self-consistency over 64 samples. How are the 64 samples generated (temperature, nucleus settings), and how is the final answer chosen (majority vote on extracted answers)? Is the same procedure used for all compared models, and how sensitive is the result to sampling hyperparameters?
Answer:
In the MATH experiments of DeepSeekMath-7B, when the authors say “self-consistency over $64$ samples achieves $60.9\%$,” they are referring to exactly the protocol introduced in “Self-Consistency Improves Chain-of-Thought Reasoning” (Wang et al., 2022). Concretely, for each MATH problem you:

1. Prompt the model with the same few-shot chain-of-thought template and then sample **64 distinct reasoning traces** by running the decoder with a non-zero temperature <|endoftext|> **$T\approx0.7\text{–}0.8$**) and nucleus (top-$p$) sampling <|endoftext|> **$p\approx0.9\text{–}0.95$**).  
2. From each sampled chain-of-thought you extract the final answer—typically via a simple regex that pulls the terminal numeral or expression.  
3. You then tally up those $64$ “answers” and pick the one that occurs most often (a straight majority vote) as your model’s final prediction.

The paper does not list the exact “$T$” and “$p$” values in its text, but by “self-consistency” it inherits the standard hyper-parameters from the original method, and it applies the **same sampling and voting procedure across all the models it compares** (open- and closed-source alike) to ensure a fair head-to-head.  

As for sensitivity, neither <|endoftext|> nor most other MATH-benchmarking papers provide a full sweep over temperature and nucleus thresholds. However, the self-consistency literature shows that in practice:

  • Once you’re in the ballpark ($T$ between $0.5$ and $1.0$, $p$ between $0.8$ and $0.95$), the **accuracy gain from self-consistency is fairly robust**, typically varying by only a couple of percentage points.  
  • Increasing the number of sampled chains from $16 \to 32 \to 64$ generally yields **diminishing returns** beyond $\sim 40$ samples, so $64$ is a reasonable trade-off between marginal accuracy gain and computational cost.  

In short, DeepSeekMath-7B’s $60.9\%$ is produced by sampling $64$ chains via standard CoT self-consistency ($T\approx0.7\text{–}0.8$, $p\approx0.9\text{–}0.95$), extracting each final answer, and taking the majority vote. All models in the paper were evaluated under identical sampling settings, and while you will see slight $\pm 1\text{–}2\%$ swings if you push temperature or top-$p$ far outside the norm, the core self-consistency boost is stable across reasonable hyper-parameter choices.
