### How are “process steps” detected for process supervision in GRPO?
Question:
For process supervision, you assign per-step rewards at token indices \(\mathrm{index}(j)\) and then define \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\mathrm{index}(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}\). What is the exact rule for segmenting an output into steps and determining each \(\mathrm{index}(j)\)? Is it based on newlines, explicit "Step k" markers, special tokens, or a learned detector? How robust is step detection across languages and formatting (e.g., LaTeX, code blocks), and how do step segmentation errors affect the training signal?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper does not propose a novel learned step‑detector architecture; rather, “$\mathrm{index}(j)$” is whatever token index the process reward model (or the process‑supervision annotation) provides as the end of a reasoning step. Concretely the paper defines a step by its end token index and then computes per‑token advantages as the sum of <|endoftext|> step rewards for all steps whose end index is at or after the token: $\hat A_{i,t}=\sum_{\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\mathrm{index}(j)}$. The paper treats the step boundaries as part of the process‑reward signal (i.e., the process reward model outputs scores at step endpoints), and does not describe a separate generic newline/marker/special‑token heuristic or an explicit new learned detector in the <|endoftext|> algorithm.

Why the paper phrases it this way: GRPO’s “process supervision” requires fine‑grained (stepwise) rewards. Those rewards are assumed available from a process reward model trained on examples where step boundaries are annotated (for instance CoT/PoT SFT data where solutions are already broken into steps). The reward model therefore returns a sequence of (step_end_index, step_score) pairs for each sampled output; the indices coming directly from those annotations or from the reward model’s scoring procedure.

Practical implications and robustness
- Typical sources of step boundaries in this work come from the formatting of the SFT/process‑supervision data: chain‑of‑thought comments, program‑of‑thought lines, or human annotations that mark step ends (explicit “Step 1/2”, sentence/newline segmentation, or end‑of‑statement markers in code). The paper does not claim a universal rule like “always split on newlines” — instead it <|endoftext|> the step structure present in the supervised/process‑reward training data and lets the reward model produce scores at those step endpoints.
- Robustness across languages and formats is therefore limited by the coverage and quality of the data used to train the process reward model. If the reward model was trained primarily on English CoT text it will be less reliable on LaTeX, non‑English phrasing, or unusual formatting (long inline formulas, code blocks whose logical “steps” do not align with newlines). The authors <|endoftext|> this in part by using <|endoftext|> math data (English + Chinese) and a variety of CoT/PoT formats in SFT, but they do not provide a formal robustness guarantee.
- When the step segmentation is wrong (step boundary misplaced, merged steps, or missed steps), the per‑token advantages $ \hat A_{i,t} $ become misaligned: rewards intended for later reasoning steps may be applied to earlier tokens or lost, and conversely tokens inside a correct step may receive wrong cumulative reward. This injects noise or bias into the <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> coefficients (Eq. (6) / <|endoftext|> objective)). In practice this degrades training signal quality: it can increase variance, slow convergence, or produce incorrect reinforcement (rewarding tokens that are not <|endoftext|> responsible for the step outcome).

Thus, in practice you should ensure the process reward model is trained on data that matches the formats you expect (include LaTeX, code blocks, non‑English CoT examples if you’ll evaluate on them) and prefer explicit step markers in SFT when possible <|endoftext|> numbered “Step k:” lines, separate sentences for steps, or explicit end‑of‑step tokens in code).

Takeaway: $\mathrm{index}(j)$ is defined by the step endpoints provided by the process reward annotations/model; the paper assumes step boundaries come from the SFT/process‑reward data rather than from a separate general detector. Segmentation quality matters: bad boundaries corrupt per‑token advantages and weaken RL; the authors <|endoftext|> this by reward <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> retraining of reward models, and by using process supervision only when reasonable step annotations/formatting exist.
