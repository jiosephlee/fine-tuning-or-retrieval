Chapter Title: Multilingual Data and Cross-Lingual Mathematical Reasoning in DeepSeekMath

# Overview

This chapter explores how the multilingual composition of the DeepSeekMath Corpus influences model performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks. We will build an intuitive and rigorous understanding of why the authors’ multilingual data design choices matter, how these choices are validated by experiments, and why it is reasonable—based strictly on the paper’s evidence—to expect that an English-only version of the corpus would have led to worse performance on Chinese benchmarks. The central insight is that the DeepSeekMath Corpus explicitly includes high-quality non-English mathematical content, predominantly English and Chinese, and models trained on this corpus exhibit improved performance in both languages. In contrast, the authors observe that existing math corpora, which are primarily English-centric, provide limited gains for Chinese mathematical reasoning and may even hinder it. From this evidence, it follows that an English-only corpus would most likely degrade Chinese task performance.

# The DeepSeekMath Corpus: Composition and Rationale

The DeepSeekMath Corpus is constructed from Common Crawl through an iterative data selection pipeline designed to extract high-quality mathematical text at scale. The process begins with a seed dataset from OpenWebMath, uses a fastText classifier to recall mathematical web pages, and iteratively enlarges and refines the positive set by domain analysis and manual annotation. After four iterations, the authors obtain 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120B tokens. Crucially, the resulting corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese as the two most represented languages.

The multilingual nature of the corpus is not incidental. The authors emphasize that they intentionally do not limit the corpus to English-only content. Instead, they include high-quality non-English data alongside English, with the explicit observation that this yields improvements in Chinese mathematical reasoning benchmarks. The corpus is therefore both large-scale and multilingual, and the paper argues that these properties jointly contribute to the observed gains.

To provide intuition, consider the model as a function trained to map questions to solutions, denoted abstractly as a conditional distribution $p_{\theta}(o \mid q)$ over outputs $o$ given a prompt $q$. When $q$ is written in Chinese—both linguistically and contextually encoded in Chinese mathematical discourse—good performance requires that $p_{\theta}$ has learned appropriate representations for the input distribution arising from Chinese mathematical text. A monolingual English-only training set skews the learned distribution toward English inputs. By contrast, a multilingual training set better covers the domain of Chinese problems, and this coverage shows up empirically as improved Chinese benchmark scores.

# Evidence from Comparative Pre-Training Experiments

The paper provides direct, controlled evidence for the quality and cross-lingual coverage of the DeepSeekMath Corpus by conducting pre-training experiments with a 1.3B-parameter model on several corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. In these comparisons, the model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperforms alternatives across mathematical benchmarks. Importantly for our focus, the paper explicitly states that the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual and that training on it improves mathematical reasoning performance in both English and Chinese. In contrast, the paper notes that existing corpora, which are primarily English-centric, show limited improvement and may even hinder performance in Chinese mathematical reasoning.

From this, the following pattern emerges. Let $\mathcal{C}_{\text{multi}}$ denote the multilingual DeepSeekMath Corpus, and let $\mathcal{C}_{\text{eng}}$ denote a corpus that is primarily English-centric. The paper’s findings support the relationship
\[
\text{Performance}_{\text{Chinese}}(\text{Model trained on } \mathcal{C}_{\text{multi}}) \;>\; \text{Performance}_{\text{Chinese}}(\text{Model trained on } \mathcal{C}_{\text{eng}}),
\]
where the inequality signifies the observed improvement when using the multilingual corpus. This is not a speculative claim; it is grounded in evaluation results and the authors’ explicit comparison against English-centric baselines.

# Why Multilingual Training Improves Chinese Mathematical Reasoning

The paper motivates a straightforward explanation grounded in data coverage. DeepSeekMath does not follow prior work that restricts math pre-training to English. Instead, it incorporates high-quality non-English content and observes better performance on Chinese benchmarks. This suggests a distributional alignment argument: if training examples reflect both the linguistic form and the problem-solving style of Chinese mathematical texts, then the model acquires representations that are more faithful to the Chinese input distribution seen at test time. Conversely, if the corpus were English-only, the model would have limited exposure to the syntax, terminology, and formatting conventions present in Chinese mathematical discourse.

Although the paper does not formalize this argument in a mathematical theorem, we can express the intuition in terms of distribution shift. Suppose the test distribution for Chinese benchmarks is $P_{\text{test}}^{\text{zh}}(q)$ over prompts $q$ written in Chinese. If training uses an English-only corpus, the induced training input distribution $P_{\text{train}}^{\text{eng}}(q)$ places negligible mass on Chinese prompts. The mismatch between $P_{\text{train}}^{\text{eng}}$ and $P_{\text{test}}^{\text{zh}}$ leads to worse generalization for Chinese tasks. In contrast, a multilingual corpus yields a training distribution $P_{\text{train}}^{\text{multi}}$ that places meaningful mass on Chinese prompts and their associated solution styles, thereby reducing the divergence between training and test distributions for Chinese benchmarks:
\[
\mathbb{D}\bigl(P_{\text{train}}^{\text{multi}} \parallel P_{\text{test}}^{\text{zh}}\bigr) \;<\; \mathbb{D}\bigl(P_{\text{train}}^{\text{eng}} \parallel P_{\text{test}}^{\text{zh}}\bigr),
\]
where $\mathbb{D}(\cdot \parallel \cdot)$ denotes a divergence measure. While the paper does not present this equation explicitly, it supports the underlying idea by reporting improved Chinese performance under multilingual training and diminished or negative effects under English-centric data.

# Observations from Full-Scale Training and Evaluation

Beyond the 1.3B comparison studies, the authors train DeepSeekMath-Base 7B with a mixture prominently featuring the DeepSeekMath Corpus. They report strong results across a wide set of benchmarks, including Chinese ones, and explicitly attribute part of the Chinese gains to the multilingual character of their corpus. The paper highlights that DeepSeekMath-Base is superior on Chinese benchmarks, and it connects this to the deliberate inclusion of high-quality non-English mathematical content. This is repeatedly contrasted with prior works that restrict themselves to English-only math pre-training, which the authors associate with limited or even detrimental effects on Chinese reasoning performance.

To synthesize these points within a simple conceptual model, let $S_{\text{zh}}$ denote a score on a Chinese mathematical benchmark, and let $\theta_{\text{multi}}$ and $\theta_{\text{eng}}$ denote parameters resulting from training on the multilingual and English-only corpora, respectively. The empirical findings suggest
\[
S_{\text{zh}}(\theta_{\text{multi}}) \;>\; S_{\text{zh}}(\theta_{\text{eng}}),
\]
where $S_{\text{zh}}(\cdot)$ is measured across standard Chinese quantitative reasoning datasets used in the paper’s evaluations. This inequality captures the directional effect supported by the paper’s comparisons and the authors’ commentary.

# Contrasting with English-Centric Corpora

The paper explicitly compares the DeepSeekMath Corpus to math corpora that are primarily English-centric. These alternative corpora show limited improvement in Chinese mathematical reasoning and can even hinder it. The authors’ comparative experiments make clear that merely scaling data is not sufficient; the linguistic composition matters when evaluating cross-lingual reasoning. Because DeepSeekMath’s training data explicitly includes Chinese content, the learned representations are better aligned with Chinese problems.

This contrast gives direct support to the stated fact. If one were to modify the DeepSeekMath Corpus to be English-only—thereby aligning it with the English-centric corpora discussed—then, following the paper’s observations, one would most likely expect degraded performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks. The paper’s language is emphatic on this point: multilingual training improves both English and Chinese performance, while English-centric training does not carry over these benefits and may harm Chinese results.

# From Design Choice to Expected Outcome

The authors’ design choice to include high-quality non-English data, particularly Chinese, is validated empirically across multiple benchmarks. The observed benefits in Chinese tasks are not a by-product of other confounders described in the paper; rather, they are directly attributed to the corpus’ multilingual nature. Therefore, altering the corpus to be English-only would remove a key ingredient that the authors identify as necessary for strong Chinese performance.

Summarizing the chain of reasoning grounded in the paper’s evidence:

1. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual, with English and Chinese as the most represented languages.
2. Models trained on this corpus improve mathematical reasoning in both English and Chinese.
3. Existing math corpora that are primarily English-centric show limited improvement and may even hinder Chinese performance.
4. DeepSeekMath-Base and subsequent instruction-tuned and RL-enhanced models exhibit strong performance on Chinese benchmarks, consistent with point 2.
5. Therefore, if the DeepSeekMath Corpus were made English-only, it would remove the multilingual coverage that the paper links to Chinese performance gains, and the most reasonable expectation—based on the paper’s own comparisons—is that Chinese benchmark performance would be worse.

# Conclusion

The paper’s data curation strategy and its evaluations collectively support a clear conclusion: multilingual mathematical pre-training, as executed in DeepSeekMath, benefits Chinese mathematical reasoning. The authors’ explicit comparisons with English-centric corpora, alongside their reported improvements on Chinese benchmarks, make it reasonable to expect that an English-only version of the DeepSeekMath Corpus would lead to worse performance on Chinese tasks. This conclusion follows directly from the paper’s stated observations and experimental results, and it underscores the importance of aligning training data distributions with target evaluation distributions when building domain-specific language models.
