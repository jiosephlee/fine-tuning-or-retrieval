Chapter Title: Data Selection by FastText Ranking in DeepSeekMath: Intuition, Process, and Evidence

# Overview of the DeepSeekMath Corpus Construction

The paper introduces a large-scale mathematical pre-training corpus constructed from Common Crawl, called the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which contains $120\mathrm{B}$ math-related tokens. The central goal of this corpus is to capture high-quality mathematical content at scale, across multiple languages, to strengthen a $7\mathrm{B}$-parameter model’s mathematical reasoning. The process begins with a seed corpus and grows through an iterative mining pipeline, repeatedly improving both coverage and quality. The resulting corpus is used extensively in subsequent pre-training and evaluation, and its quality is empirically validated by downstream performance on diverse mathematical benchmarks.

A key design choice in this pipeline is the use of a fastText-based classifier to identify math-relevant pages and then rank them, preserving only the top-scoring subset. This operationalizes the notion of “quality” indirectly through classifier-assigned scores: the higher the score, the stronger the signal that the page is mathematically relevant and of sufficient usefulness for training a reasoning model. The paper describes this procedure as a means “to filter out low-quality mathematical content,” thereby aligning ranking with content quality.

# The fastText Classifier: Training, Scoring, and Ranking

The classifier is trained to distinguish math-related pages from general web content. In the initial iteration, $500{,}000$ examples from OpenWebMath serve as positive training instances, and another $500{,}000$ pages sampled from Common Crawl serve as negative instances. The fastText model is trained with specific hyperparameters: a vector dimension of $256$, a learning rate of $0.1$, a maximum word n-gram length of $3$, a minimum number of word occurrences of $3$, and $3$ training epochs.

After training, the classifier is applied to deduplicated Common Crawl data. The deduplication procedure uses URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication to reduce the raw web to $40\mathrm{B}$ HTML pages. The trained classifier then assigns a score to each candidate page. Crucially, the pipeline filters by ranking these pages according to the classifier’s predicted score and preserves only the top-ranking pages. The volume of preserved data is governed by pre-training experiments that evaluate downstream performance at various token counts, specifically the top $40\mathrm{B}$, $80\mathrm{B}$, $120\mathrm{B}$, and $160\mathrm{B}$ tokens. In the first iteration, the top $40\mathrm{B}$ tokens are retained; later iterations expand the corpus while continually refining the classifier.

This procedure implicitly ties rank to usefulness for mathematical pre-training. The paper explicitly uses ranking “to filter out low-quality mathematical content,” and then retains only the highest-scoring subset, embedding the idea that higher score implies better training value. In subsequent sections, the paper demonstrates that this selection strategy contributes to superior downstream performance relative to alternative corpora.

# Iterative Enrichment: Improving Coverage and Diversity

An important observation after the first iteration is that many math pages remain uncollected. The likely cause is limited diversity in the positive examples used to train the classifier. To address this, the pipeline organizes Common Crawl into domains, each defined by a shared base URL, and identifies math-related domains where over $10\%$ of pages were collected in the first iteration (e.g., domains like \url{mathoverflow.net}). Within these domains, URLs associated with math content (e.g., \url{mathoverflow.net/questions}) are manually annotated, and uncollected pages linked to these URLs are added to the seed corpus. This expands the positive examples, improving the classifier’s ability to recall math content in subsequent iterations.

After four iterations, the process yields $35.5\mathrm{M}$ math web pages, totaling $120\mathrm{B}$ tokens. The paper notes diminishing returns: in the fourth iteration, nearly $98\%$ of the data had already been collected in the third iteration, motivating the decision to stop further collection. This iterative enrichment not only increases scale but also improves coverage across diverse mathematical sources, strengthening the classifier’s ability to rank pages in a way that aligns with mathematical content quality.

# Decontamination: Preventing Benchmark Leakage

To ensure that evaluations reflect generalization rather than memorization, the pipeline excludes web pages that contain questions or answers from English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks. The criteria are specific: any text segment containing an exact matching $10$-gram with any evaluation benchmark substring is removed. For benchmark texts shorter than $10$ grams but at least $3$ grams, exact matching is used to filter out contaminated pages.

This decontamination supports the validity of the ranking-based selection’s downstream impact. By removing potential benchmark leakage, improvements in accuracy on datasets such as GSM8K and MATH more credibly indicate the corpus’s quality and the effectiveness of preserving top-ranked pages.

# Validating the Corpus: Quality, Multilingual Coverage, and Scale

The paper validates the DeepSeekMath Corpus against other math corpora through controlled pre-training experiments using a $1.3\mathrm{B}$-parameter model trained for $150\mathrm{B}$ tokens under a consistent training setup. The results show a clear performance lead when training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus across $8$ mathematical benchmarks using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. The evidence is presented at multiple scales:

1. Quality: The model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus outperforms Proof-Pile-2 at $50\mathrm{B}$ tokens, indicating higher average quality per token. This directly supports the effectiveness of the ranking-based filtering, since the corpus is formed by keeping only the highest-scoring pages.

2. Multilingual coverage: The corpus is multilingual, predominantly English and Chinese, and training improves performance on both English and Chinese benchmarks. In contrast, English-centric corpora show limited improvement or even degradation on Chinese mathematical reasoning.

3. Scale: The corpus is several times larger than existing mathematical corpora. Training on this larger corpus exhibits a steeper learning curve and more sustained improvements, whereas smaller baselines plateau quickly after several repeats.

Together, these results provide empirical support for the pipeline’s central premise: ranking by classifier score yields a high-quality math corpus that translates into better downstream reasoning performance.

# The Implicit Assumption: Rank as a Proxy for Mathematical Quality

The paper operationalizes “quality” through classifier score ranking and explicitly uses it “to filter out low-quality mathematical content.” Preserving only top-ranking pages assumes that the fastText model’s score correlates with both mathematical relevance and usefulness for training. This assumption is not directly proven within the paper through explicit label-based quality verification across the entire dataset; instead, it is supported indirectly via:

1. Iterative improvement of the classifier using enriched positive examples from math-related domains, implying that higher scores should reflect broader and more accurate coverage of math content.

2. Downstream performance gains, both in-domain and out-of-domain, that surpass comparable models trained on alternative corpora, suggesting that the top-ranked pages collectively form a higher-quality training signal.

3. Comparative experiments showing early superiority at partial training budgets (e.g., outperforming Proof-Pile-2 at $50\mathrm{B}$ tokens), indicating that ranking effectively filters for quality even before fully leveraging the scale.

Thus, while the assumption that “higher rank implies higher math quality” is not explicitly stated, it is embedded in the design and justified by the observed outcomes: the ranked corpus drives strong performance across benchmarks, including multilingual settings.

# Consequences of Ranking-Based Selection: Evidence from Model Performance

The pre-trained base model, DeepSeekMath-Base $7\mathrm{B}$, initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v$1.5$ $7\mathrm{B}$ and trained for $500\mathrm{B}$ tokens on a mixture dominated by the DeepSeekMath Corpus, demonstrates notable improvements:

1. Mathematical problem solving without tool use: Using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting, DeepSeekMath-Base $7\mathrm{B}$ leads among open-source base models across eight English and Chinese benchmarks and surpasses Minerva $540\mathrm{B}$ on the competition-level MATH dataset.

2. Mathematical problem solving with tool use: With program-of-thought prompting that includes Python and libraries such as \textit{math} and \textit{sympy}, DeepSeekMath-Base $7\mathrm{B}$ outperforms prior state-of-the-art models.

3. Formal mathematics: On informal-to-formal theorem proving tasks with Isabelle on miniF2F, DeepSeekMath-Base $7\mathrm{B}$ demonstrates strong autoformalization performance with few-shot prompting.

4. General language understanding, reasoning, and code: Math pre-training benefits MMLU and BBH scores compared to the precursor model while maintaining coding performance on HumanEval and MBPP.

These outcomes collectively reinforce the utility of ranking to select math-relevant, high-quality pages. The corpus produced by preserving only top-ranked pages translates into broad and deep improvements, supporting the implicit link between rank and training value.

# Limitations and Caveats within the Paper’s Scope

The paper is careful to acknowledge areas where further study is needed. It observes that arXiv-focused corpora, even when processed with different pipelines, do not yield notable improvements and may deteriorate performance on several benchmarks for the tested model sizes and training budgets. This suggests that not all ostensibly “math-related” sources contribute positively, and that effective quality filtering—here, realized through fastText ranking—matters.

Additionally, despite strong performance on quantitative reasoning, weaknesses remain in geometry and theorem-proof relative to closed models. The paper hypothesizes potential data selection bias as a contributing factor. If certain subdomains (e.g., triangles and ellipses) are underrepresented among the top-ranked pages, the resulting corpus may lack coverage necessary to generalize well in those areas. This highlights that while ranking is effective, it must be paired with careful coverage analysis to avoid domain-specific gaps.

Finally, the paper notes that DeepSeekMath’s few-shot capability lags behind GPT-4 due to model scale, and that reinforcement learning primarily improves robustness of output distributions rather than fundamental capabilities. These observations suggest that while ranking-based data selection is effective, it is one piece of a larger system that includes model size, training strategies, and reward design.

# Summary: Understanding Rank-Based Filtering as Quality Selection

The DeepSeekMath pipeline uses a fastText classifier to score and rank web pages for mathematical content, preserving only the top-ranked subset as the core of a large multilingual math corpus. This design equates higher classifier scores with higher-quality math pages and uses ranking “to filter out low-quality mathematical content.” The iterative refinement of the classifier with enriched positive examples, the rigorous decontamination procedures, and the extensive downstream evaluations together provide indirect but strong evidence that ranking is an effective proxy for mathematical quality. The resulting corpus fuels significant improvements across mathematical reasoning, tool-augmented problem solving, formal mathematics, and general reasoning benchmarks. While limitations persist in specific subdomains and few-shot scaling, within the paper’s scope, the strategy of preserving top-ranked pages is validated by empirical performance and stands as a central contributor to DeepSeekMath’s success.
