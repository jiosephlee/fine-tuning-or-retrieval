In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', the authors conjecture that mixing code and math tokens in one-stage training harmed math reasoning without tool use for their $1.3\text{B}$ model because of model capacity. This is sensible given their empirical findings: when they trained the $1.3\text{B}$ model on a single joint stream of $400\text{B}$ code tokens and $150\text{B}$ math tokens, performance on math benchmarks requiring purely natural-language chain-of-thought degraded, even though the same mixture improved program-aided math. They explicitly attribute this to the small model’s limited ability to “fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously,” which aligns with well-known multitask interference in small-capacity models. In a mixed objective $\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{code}}(\theta)+(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{math}}(\theta)$, a model with relatively few parameters must allocate representational resources across two distinct distributions and reasoning styles: formal syntax and execution patterns from $p_{\text{code}}$, versus long-form natural-language reasoning from $p_{\text{math}}$. With $|\theta|$ limited, the optimization tends to favor the dominant or easier-to-fit signals, and gradient conflicts (e.g., $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{code}}\cdot\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{math}}<0$) can induce negative transfer on the non-tool math reasoning skill.

Moreover, their controlled comparisons support the capacity explanation through contrasting training curricula. In the two-stage setting (code $\rightarrow$ math), the $1.3\text{B}$ model first acquires strong programming priors, then specializes to math reasoning without tool use, yielding the best text-only math performance. In the one-stage mixture, the model maintains coding ability and strengthens program-aided math (mitigating catastrophic forgetting), but the same shared capacity now has to store both code-specific syntax distributions and the rhetorical, step-structured patterns of chain-of-thought; for a small model, this trade-off appears to suppress the quality of natural-language reasoning. The fact that the mixture helped program-aided math while harming text-only math is consistent with capacity-constrained specialization: code tokens and execution-oriented patterns directly aid tool-use solutions, while pure chain-of-thought requires distinct linguistic structures that compete for parameters when $|\theta|$ is small. The authors’ conjecture is thus a plausible account grounded in observed negative transfer under joint training at $1.3\text{B}$, alongside improved outcomes when specialization is allowed in a staged curriculum.
