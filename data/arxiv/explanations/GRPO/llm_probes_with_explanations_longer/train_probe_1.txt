In the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', preserving only the top-ranking pages predicted by the fastText model as part of the math corpus rests on the unstated assumption that rank indicates math quality. This is sensible because the fastText score is explicitly trained to approximate a probability of “math-ness” using high-quality positives (from OpenWebMath and later human-curated domain URLs) and diverse negatives, so the ranking by score serves as a proxy for the density and relevance of mathematical content. Concretely, if the classifier assigns a score \(s(x) \approx p_\phi(y=\text{math}\mid x)\) to a page \(x\), selecting the top-\(k\) pages by \(s(x)\) maximizes expected mathematical content and minimizes noise, given fixed corpus size. The authors further strengthen this proxy by iteratively improving the classifier with newly mined positives and human annotations at the domain/URL level, then performing URL deduplication and near-deduplication, and finally applying decontamination against benchmarks—each step reducing false positives and sharpening the correlation between rank and true math quality.

Moreover, the downstream evidence supports that higher classifier ranks track higher-quality math data. The authors validate corpus quality by training models on different corpora and token volumes, showing that models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus (constructed by taking top-ranked pages) outperform those trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath alone, and Proof-Pile-2 at comparable token counts. In particular, their 1.3B model trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus achieves better performance than a model trained on Proof-Pile-2 at \(50\)B tokens (one epoch of Proof-Pile-2), implying the average per-token quality in the selected set is higher—consistent with rank reflecting quality. The steeper learning curve and sustained improvements with larger top-\(k\) selections (e.g., \(40\)B \(\rightarrow\) \(120\)B tokens) further indicate that \(s(x)\)-based ranking successfully concentrates mathematically rich content. Finally, the multilingual gains observed—likely due to the classifier recalling non-English math pages and the subsequent human-curated domain expansion—reinforce that the ranking procedure is capturing substantive mathematical material rather than superficial features, making the rank-as-quality assumption pragmatically valid in this pipeline.
