\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### How does math training improve non-math benchmarks like MMLU and BBH?
Question:
The paper observes that math pre-training improves MMLU and BBH. 
- Which MMLU categories benefited most (STEM vs humanities)? 
- Do you have analyses indicating improvements in multi-step reasoning skills (e.g., specific BBH tasks)? 
- Could part of the gain stem from the code component in the training mixture, rather than math text per se?
Answer:
Short answer: most of the cross-benchmark gains trace to STEM / reasoning-heavy subsets, the BBH improvements look like better multi-step answer reliability (higher $\text{Maj@K}$ rather than higher per-sample competence), and a nontrivial fraction of the benefit does come from the code tokens in the training mixture (especially for program-aided problems), although math text itself and GRPO-style RL also contribute.

Explanation anchored to the paper’s findings:

Which MMLU categories benefited most (STEM vs humanities)?
The paper explicitly reports improvements on MMLU-STEM (see the Evaluation summary and the “Natural Language Understanding, Reasoning, and Code” section). The math pre-training and subsequent training stages produced “significant enhancements in performance on MMLU” and the authors call out MMLU-STEM specifically. Intuitively this is expected: math-heavy pretraining teaches symbolic manipulation, formal reasoning patterns and domain knowledge that transfer most directly to STEM topics (physics, math, engineering). The paper does not report a similar or comparable per‑category boost for humanities; therefore the observed uplift is concentrated in STEM / technical MMLU categories rather than humanities.

Do analyses indicate improvements in multi-step reasoning skills (e.g., specific BBH tasks)?
The paper evaluates BBH (which is designed to stress multi-step reasoning) and reports improved BBH scores for DeepSeekMath-Base relative to the precursor. The RL analyses give a useful diagnostic: after GRPO fine-tuning, $\text{Maj@K}$ improves while $\text{Pass@K}$ does not (section “Why RL Works?”). That pattern implies the model becomes more reliable in producing at least one correct multi-step chain among top-K samples (the distribution becomes more concentrated on better solutions), rather than uniformly improving the per-sample reasoning competence. The authors also show process-supervision GRPO (rewarding intermediate steps) outperforms outcome-only GRPO, which suggests that giving per-step feedback helps multi-step reasoning quality. They further report $\text{GRPO+process-supervision} > \text{GRPO+outcome-supervision}$ and that iterative RL boosts performance—again consistent with improvements in multi-step solution generation and in making correct multi-step chains more likely. The paper does not publish a per-BBH-task breakdown in the main text, so you should infer that the improvements are strongest on BBH tasks that (a) reward chain-like, stepwise reasoning and (b) benefit from making a correct chain among sampled outputs (i.e., tasks where $\text{Maj@K}$ is relevant).

Could part of the gain stem from the code component in the training mixture, rather than math text per se?
Yes—this is explicitly studied and discussed. The paper contains an ablation on “Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning.” Two-stage experiments (code training $\to$ math training) and mixed one-stage experiments show that code training improves program-aided mathematical reasoning substantially, and also provides moderate gains for non-tool math. Mixing code and math in a single-stage corpus helps mitigate catastrophic forgetting and can synergize both coding and program-aided math. However, the authors note a caveat: when model capacity is limited, mixing code and math in one stage can hurt non-tool mathematical reasoning (a capacity / interference effect). In the $7\text{B}$ setup used for DeepSeekMath-Base, the final training mixture contained $20\%$ GitHub code and the model both preserved coding performance and gained math capability (see the “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-Base 7B” distribution and the “Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning” subsection). So some of the cross-benchmark gains—especially on tasks that are naturally helped by programmatic solution approaches or require precise symbolic computation—are attributable to the code tokens, but math-specific tokens and the RL/instruction tuning pipeline also produce independent gains (e.g., large improvements on pure math benchmarks like MATH and GSM8K driven by the $120\text{B}$ math tokens and GRPO).

Putting it together, the most defensible interpretation from the paper is: math pre-training mainly helps STEM / technical MMLU categories; BBH gains reflect making correct multi-step solution chains more likely (improved $\text{Maj@K}$, process-supervision helps); and code data is a meaningful contributor (especially for program-aided problems), but it does not fully explain the math-driven improvements—the large math corpus plus the GRPO/RL and instruction tuning are also essential contributors.
