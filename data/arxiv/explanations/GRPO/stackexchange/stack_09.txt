\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### What exactly is the advantage of GRPO over Online RFT in the unified paradigm?
Question:
In the unified view (Equation (7)), you argue GRPO outperforms Online RFT because GRPO uses graded reward magnitudes (positive and negative) in the gradient coefficient, while Online RFT uniformly reinforces all correct responses and does not penalize incorrect ones. Could you provide the explicit gradient coefficient formulas you used for Online RFT vs GRPO in your experiments, and any quantitative ablations showing how penalizing incorrect responses contributed to the final gains?
Answer:
Short answer: Online RFT in our experiments uses a binary, answer-level gradient coefficient that only rewards correct outputs (no negative signal for incorrect ones), while GRPO uses graded (signed, normalized) advantages computed within each sampled group so correct outputs get positive coefficients and incorrect outputs get negative coefficients. Concretely, in the unified-gradient form of Equation (10) in the paper we used the following coefficients.

For Online RFT (offline or online variant), the gradient coefficient we applied is the simple rule-based indicator of correctness:
$$
GC_{\text{Online-RFT}}(q,o,t)=\mathbf{1}\{\text{answer}(o)\ \text{is correct}\}\,,
$$
i.e., every token in a sampled output that yields a correct final answer receives a uniform positive reinforcement (usually scaled by a constant or learning-rate dependent factor), and every token of an incorrect sample receives zero reinforcement (no negative penalty). This matches the intuitive operation of rejection-sampling-style fine-tuning: keep (or upweight) correct sampled outputs, discard or ignore incorrect ones.

For GRPO (as implemented in our experiments) the gradient coefficient is the group-relative (and normalized) advantage computed from reward-model scores. For outcome supervision (reward only at the end of each output) we set
$$
\widetilde r_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^G)}{\operatorname{std}(\{r_j\}_{j=1}^G)}\quad\text{and}\quad
GC_{\text{GRPO, outcome}}(q,o_i,t)=\hat A_{i,t}=\widetilde r_i,
$$
so every token of output $o_i$ gets the same signed coefficient $\widetilde r_i$ (positive for relatively good outputs, negative for relatively bad outputs). For process supervision (fine-grained stepwise rewards) we used the cumulative future normalized step rewards:
$$
\widetilde r_i^{index(j)}=\frac{r_i^{index(j)}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)}\quad\text{and}\quad
GC_{\text{GRPO, process}}(q,o_i,t)=\hat A_{i,t}=\sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{index(j)},
$$
i.e., the advantage at token position $t$ is the sum of normalized process rewards at later step boundaries in the same output. These $\hat A$ values are signed (can be negative), and are plugged into the GRPO surrogate objective (Equation 8) with the clipping and KL regularization described in the paper.

Why this matters (intuitive explanation). Online RFT’s binary coefficient treats all correct answers equally and ignores incorrect ones; it can increase probability mass for correct answers but does not actively push down incorrect modes the policy still assigns probability to. GRPO’s group-relative normalization both centers rewards within the sampled group (subtracting the group mean) and rescales by group standard deviation, so outputs that are worse-than-average get negative coefficients and the learning signal actively reduces their probability. This signed pressure is particularly helpful in multi-modal or noisy output distributions: it both amplifies good modes and suppresses bad modes, which empirically concentrates probability mass onto better solutions (hence the observed improvement in Maj@K rather than Pass@K as discussed in the paper).

Ablation / empirical effect cited in the paper. In our ablations (Section 5 and Figure 6 in the paper) we compared Online RFT vs GRPO (both with outcome supervision) and also compared GRPO with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS) vs GRPO with process supervision (GRPO+PS). The key empirical findings were: (1) GRPO outperforms Online RFT consistently on the math benchmarks we report; (2) GRPO+PS (process supervision) further improves over GRPO+OS (outcome-only), showing the benefit of graded, step-aware negative and positive signals. In the full-system RL run reported in Table 5, GRPO-based RL produced the reported final gains over the instruction-tuned baseline (GSM8K: 82.9% → 88.2%, MATH: 46.8% → 51.7%). The paper’s plots (Figure 6) show that Online RFT gave only modest gains relative to SFT and was noticeably beaten by GRPO; adding process supervision to GRPO gave additional improvements beyond outcome-only GRPO. (The paper’s figures present the per-step learning curves; see Figure 6 for the exact absolute differences across iterations.)

Practical consequences and takeaways. If you want the same algorithmic simplicity as RFT but faster convergence and better final performance, replace the binary upweighting by a signed, normalized group advantage as in GRPO. If you can train a reward model that gives meaningful stepwise feedback, using process supervision with GRPO leverages that extra signal to produce further gains. Finally, centering and scaling rewards within each sampled group (subtract mean, divide std) is important: it keeps the update magnitudes stable and makes the coefficients comparable across groups and steps.
