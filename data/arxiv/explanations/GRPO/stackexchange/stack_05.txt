\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### Why move from a reward-shaped KL penalty (PPO) to a direct KL loss term (GRPO)?
Question:
PPO uses a per-token KL penalty inside the reward (Equation (3)), whereas GRPO places a KL divergence term directly in the objective (Equation (4)). What are the practical benefits of moving the KL out of the reward into the loss? Does this change stability, tuning of \(\beta\), or interactions with clipping in the importance ratio? Empirically, did this change contribute meaningfully to stability/performance, or is it mainly for simplicity?
Answer:
Short answer: moving the KL from inside the per‑token reward (PPO style) to an explicit KL regularizer in the loss (GRPO) is both a practical simplification and a meaningful design choice for stability and correct advantage computation in their RL setup. In DeepSeekMath the change makes the group‑relative advantage easier and cleaner to compute, decouples reward‑scale from the policy regularization, reduces unintended bias in reward normalization, and — together with GRPO’s group baseline — contributes to the reported empirical improvements while also simplifying tuning and lowering implementation complexity.

Why this matters (intuitively and technically)
PPO (as used in many LLM‑RL works) typically implements a per‑token KL penalty by subtracting a term like
$r_t = r_\varphi(q,o_{\le t}) - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t|q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(o_t|q,o_{<t})}$
from the reward (Equation (3) in the paper). That has two intertwined effects: it changes the reward signal itself (so the advantage estimator and value function must absorb that change per token), and it couples the magnitude of the KL penalty to the reward model’s scale and the advantage estimation machinery.

GRPO instead places a KL term directly in the objective as an explicit regularizer:
- it avoids injecting the KL into the reward used to form advantages, so the advantage/normalization step is not contaminated by a policy regularizer,
- it allows the algorithm to compute group‑relative advantages purely from the reward model scores (normalized across the sampled group) without having to correct for per‑token KL noise,
- and it makes the policy regularization independent of the reward normalization and clipping machinery.

Practical benefits explained
1) Cleaner advantage estimation and compatibility with group baselines. GRPO computes advantages by normalizing rewards across a group of sampled outputs for the same prompt (group baseline). If the KL were mixed into those rewards, the relative ordering and normalized magnitudes would reflect both “quality” (reward model) and “distance from ref” (KL), making the group normalization and the intended comparative signal less faithful to the reward model. Moving KL to the loss keeps the reward comparisons pure and the group normalization meaningful.

2) Easier/less brittle tuning of $\beta$. When KL appears inside reward it gets scaled by whatever normalization (and by value estimates in PPO) which complicates choosing $\beta$ because its effective weight depends on the reward scale and on how you normalize advantages. An explicit KL term decouples these effects: $\beta$ directly controls the regularization strength on the policy loss, simplifying interpretation and tuning. (In the paper they set $\beta=0.04$ for GRPO.)

3) Fewer interactions with advantage/value estimation. PPO requires a value function (critic) to compute advantages at the token level; when the reward includes a per‑token KL, the value must model that altered per‑token signal. GRPO avoids training a separate critic (it uses group averages as baseline) and so removing KL from rewards removes a source of mismatch that would otherwise make advantage estimation and critic learning harder.

4) Similar behavior with clipping/importance ratio but simpler bookkeeping. The min/clip term in the PPO surrogate still applies to per‑token importance ratios in GRPO. What changes is that the clipped surrogate is multiplied by advantages that come purely from reward model comparisons, not mixed reward+KL. So clipping interacts the same way with ratios, but the gradient coefficients (the effective learning signals) are clearer to interpret and less entangled.

Empirical contribution (what the paper reports)
The paper attributes GRPO’s effectiveness to two main practical gains: comparable (or better) performance and reduced memory usage (no critic). Empirically, they report substantial gains when applying GRPO on top of their instruction model (GSM8K 82.9% -> 88.2%, MATH 46.8% -> 51.7%). They also explicitly note that moving the KL out of the reward “avoids complicating the calculation of $\hat{A}_{i,t}$” and that GRPO’s group‑relative advantage estimation aligns well with the comparative nature of reward models trained on pairwise comparisons. So the change is not just cosmetic: it materially helped stability of the advantage computation and, combined with the group baseline, contributed to the improved results while saving memory.

Caveats and tradeoffs
This is not a free lunch. An explicit KL in the loss still needs a sensible $\beta$ and can still interact with learning dynamics (e.g., if $\beta$ is too large the policy won’t learn; if too small you may drift from the reference). Also, because GRPO uses group normalization and omits a learned value function, it assumes you can sample multiple outputs per prompt (they sample $G=64$ in their experiments). If you cannot afford that many samples, the benefits of the group baseline may weaken. Finally, moving KL out of reward changes the objective you’re implicitly optimizing compared to classic PPO-with-reward‑KL; that difference is intentional here and aligned with GRPO’s group advantage design.

Bottom line
Putting the KL penalty directly into the loss in GRPO simplifies and stabilizes advantage estimation (especially when using group‑relative normalization), decouples regularization from reward scaling (making $\beta$ easier to reason about), and reduces implementation/memory cost by enabling a critic‑free scheme. According to the paper, this change meaningfully contributed to the empirical gains (together with the group baseline and process supervision), not merely to conceptual simplicity.
