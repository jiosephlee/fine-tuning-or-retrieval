### How exactly is decontamination done for Chinese text using n-gram matching?
Question:
The paper states they filter by exact matching of 10-grams (and shorter texts with at least 3-grams) to avoid contamination from benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and CMATH. For English, word-level n-grams are natural, but for Chinese, what is an "n-gram" here? Are these character n-grams or tokenized word n-grams? If character n-grams, is 10 too short or too long given Chinese morphology? Also, how do you handle punctuation and LaTeX math tokens in this matching? Do you have any estimates of false positives/negatives from this rule-based filter?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper describes decontamination as exact substring (``string'') matching of n-grams taken from the evaluation benchmarks, so for Chinese web text this is best understood as character (raw substring) n-grams rather than word-token n-grams. The paper does not give a separate Chinese tokenization procedure or numeric estimates of filter error rates.

Why I say that: the Methods text says “any text segment containing a $10$-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed.” The phrase “string” and “sub-string” strongly implies raw substring matching on the text as-is (i.e., character sequences), not a word-level n-gram after language-dependent tokenization. Also note they treat “shorter texts” (benchmark items shorter than $10$-grams but at least $3$-grams) with exact matching the same way — again indicating literal substring equality.

Intuition about the $10$-gram choice for Chinese. Chinese written text does not use spaces to separate words, so character n-grams are the natural analogue to English word n-grams when you do raw substring matching. A $10$-character exact substring is fairly conservative: it is long enough that an exact match is uncommon by accident, but short enough to catch many copied problem statements or solutions. In practical terms, a $10$-character match in Chinese often corresponds to multiple content words or a full short sentence fragment, so it is a reasonable tradeoff between catching contamination and avoiding accidental matches.

How punctuation and LaTeX/math tokens are handled. Because the paper specifies exact substring matching, punctuation, whitespace, and LaTeX tokens are part of the string compared. That means an exact match will only be found if the punctuation/LaTeX markup and surrounding spacing match exactly between the benchmark example and the web text. The paper does not report additional normalization steps (e.g., removing punctuation, normalizing whitespace, expanding LaTeX macros), so any such normalization would be an implementation choice beyond what the paper documents.

Likely sources of false positives and false negatives (qualitative, because the paper gives no numeric rates). False positives will be relatively rare given exact $10$-character matches are unlikely to occur randomly, but they can happen when benchmarks contain very common math phrases or widely used formula fragments (e.g., “prove that …”, “let $x$ be …”, or short formula snippets), causing deletion of otherwise unrelated pages. False negatives are more common: paraphrases, reformatting, small edits (a different LaTeX macro, an extra space, reordered terms), tokenization differences, or translations will avoid exact-match detection; likewise, if a benchmark was reproduced but slightly edited (e.g., variable renaming, punctuation change), the filter will miss it. Math material with heavy LaTeX will be especially prone to misses if the webpage has slightly different macro expansions or if the arXiv-style source differs in whitespace or macro names.

Practical implications and mitigations. Because the paper uses exact substring matching on $10$-grams (and $3$–$9$-grams for short items), the filter is simple and fast (scales to Common Crawl) but inherently conservative against paraphrase contamination. Common improvements you might consider if you implement this pipeline are to (a) normalize whitespace and unify common LaTeX macro expansions before matching, (b) add a parallel pass that matches lower n (e.g., $6$–$9$) but only in contexts that contain math delimiters, (c) apply canonicalization for punctuation, and (d) complement exact matching with fuzzy / embedding-based similarity checks for high-risk benchmarks. The paper does not report quantitative FP/FN rates, so if contamination risk is a central concern you should measure contamination on a held-out slice (compute how many benchmark examples are found in the crawl with exact match and with fuzzy matching) and tune $n$ and normalization accordingly.

Summary: the paper’s decontamination step is implemented as exact substring matching (character n-grams) of length $10$ (or $3$–$9$ for short items). This is simple and effective at scale but will miss paraphrases and small edits (false negatives) while occasionally dropping unrelated pages that happen to contain common short formulaic fragments (false positives). The paper does not provide measured false-positive/false-negative rates or extra normalization steps; those are left to implementation choices if you reproduce or adapt their pipeline.
