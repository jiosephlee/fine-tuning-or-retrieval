### SFT seems extremely short (500 steps). How is that sufficient?
Question:
For DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, you train for 500 steps with batch size 256 and 4K context length on 776K examples (CoT, PoT, and tool-integrated). 500 steps sounds tiny relative to dataset size. 
- Are these steps very large (e.g., gradient accumulation, many tokens per step)? 
- Did you observe underfitting or did the model start from a sufficiently capable checkpoint that only minimal SFT was needed? 
- Any ablations on SFT duration?
Answer:
Short answer: $500$ SFT steps is intentionally small because each step processes a very large amount of tokens (long $4\text{K}$ contexts + batch size $256$), and the model already starts from a very capable checkpoint (DeepSeekMath‑Base, which itself was continually trained for $5\times10^{11}$ tokens including extensive math data). The paper does not report a dedicated ablation that varies SFT length, but it shows that a short SFT pass followed by GRPO-style RL yields large gains — implying the SFT stage is mainly an efficient alignment step, not the heavy lifting.

Why $500$ steps can be enough (intuitively and numerically). The paper states they concatenate training examples until reaching a $4\text{K}$ context and use batch size $256$. That means one optimization step processes roughly
$256 \times 4{,}096 \approx 1{,}048{,}576$ tokens (order-of-magnitude). So $500$ steps correspond to on the order of $5\times10^{8}$ tokens of SFT throughput. That is far larger than “500 small mini‑batches” would sound like and is a non‑trivial amount of fine‑tuning data. In addition, the SFT examples are long (CoT/PoT/tool-integrated solutions), so each example contributes many tokens per sequence. The paper does not mention gradient accumulation being used to make steps “larger”; instead the effective per‑step token count comes from long contexts and big batch size.

Why the model didn’t underfit. DeepSeekMath-Instruct is initialized from DeepSeekMath-Base, which itself is DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 continually trained ($1.2\times10^{11}$ math tokens in the DeepSeekMath Corpus plus other code/natural language; the base went through $5\times10^{11}$ tokens total training). That base model already possesses strong math reasoning and code capabilities, so the SFT stage’s job is mainly to align the model to instruction/CoT/PoT/tool formats and to expose it to explicitly annotated reasoning processes. Given such a strong starting point, relatively few (but large) SFT steps are sufficient to move the model into an instruction-following regime without needing many epochs of standard supervised fine‑tuning.

Relation to RL and overall pipeline. The paper shows that reinforcement learning with GRPO (sampling $64$ outputs per question, iterative reward-model updates, outcome/process supervision, etc.) further improves performance substantially. The authors explicitly treat SFT as a compact supervised alignment stage and rely on RL (GRPO) to shape output distributions and boost Maj@K rather than to change core capabilities. They also report that RL improves Maj@K (robustly bringing correct answers into the top $K$) rather than increasing Pass@K, supporting the view that SFT need only be a concise alignment step when preceded by massive pretraining and followed by RL.

Ablations on SFT duration. The paper does not present a detailed ablation that varies the number of SFT steps (e.g., $100$, $500$, $2000$) or the effective epochs over the $776\text{K}$ examples. Instead, the ablations focus on pretraining data, code vs general training, arXiv inclusion, and many RL-related variants (outcome vs process supervision, group size $G$, iterative RL, online vs offline data). So there is no direct plot in the paper showing SFT performance as a function of step count. The closest empirical evidence is: (1) the base model is already strong from extensive pretraining, (2) $500$ SFT steps with the described batching/context achieves the strong Instruct numbers reported, and (3) GRPO after SFT yields further, consistent improvements.

Takeaway and practical implications. When the starting checkpoint has been heavily pre-trained on relevant domain data (here $5\times10^{11}$ tokens including $1.2\times10^{11}$ math tokens) and SFT examples are long and batched into very large token counts per step, a relatively small number of optimization steps can be sufficient to do instruction alignment. If you want to test sensitivity to SFT duration yourself, the paper suggests the logical experiments: vary (a) number of SFT steps, (b) batch/context sizing (since per-step token throughput matters), and (c) whether RL is applied afterward — because RL appears to correct distributional/top‑K behavior that SFT alone may not.
