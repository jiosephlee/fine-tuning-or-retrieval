\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### How sensitive is GRPO to group size G and what are the compute/memory trade-offs?
Question:
You use \(G=64\) samples per question in GRPO. 
- How does performance vary with smaller \(G\) (e.g., 4, 8, 16) versus larger \(G\)? 
- Since GRPO removes the critic, does the saved memory allow you to push \(G\) higher, and is that the main driver of observed gains? 
- Any ablations showing variance reduction or stability improvements as a function of \(G\)?
Answer:
Short answer: GRPO’s use of group baselines (they used $G=64$) is intentionally chosen to reduce advantage variance and match how reward models are trained (comparisons within the same question). Removing a learned critic frees up a lot of memory, which the authors explicitly use to sample many outputs per question, and that larger $G$ is an important contributor to GRPO’s empirical gains. The paper does not report a full sweep of $G$ (e.g., $G$ (e.g., $4/8/16/32/128$) in the main text, so you won’t find an explicit ablation table there — but the paper explains the mechanisms, trade-offs, and why increasing $G$ helps.

Why increasing G helps (intuitively + technically)
GRPO replaces a learned value baseline by the group mean (and uses group-standardization) for advantage estimation. Statistically the variance of the sample mean baseline falls as $1/G$, so larger $G$ gives a more accurate baseline and therefore lower-variance advantages. Lower variance in the advantage estimates yields more stable policy gradient updates and less noisy credit assignment across sampled outputs for the same question, which aligns well with reward models trained on pairwise/comparative labels. Formally, if individual-sample reward variance is $\mathrm{Var}(r)=\sigma^2$, the variance of the group mean baseline is $\sigma^2/G$, so the residual variance in the centered rewards scales like $(1-1/G)\sigma^2$ (ignoring correlations). In short: larger $G$ → better baseline → lower gradient variance → more stable and effective RL.

Memory / compute trade-offs described in the paper
The paper lists two main resource effects. First, a learned critic/value head used by PPO is typically another model of comparable size and must be trained online; that consumes a large fraction of GPU memory and compute. GRPO “foregoes the critic” and regularizes via an explicit KL term, so that saved memory can be reallocated to sampling many outputs per prompt. Second, sampling $G$ outputs per question implies linear increases in forward compute and in reward-model evaluation compute: sampling and scoring scale $O(G)$ per question. Thus the practical trade-off is: you trade critic memory and critic-training compute for increased sample-generation and reward-evaluation compute. Because reward-model scoring and sampling can often be batched and run at lower precision or on separate hardware, in practice removing the critic tends to be a win when you want many samples per prompt (the paper reports using $G=64$).

Is the saved memory the main driver of observed gains?
The paper argues that the gains come from multiple, related factors, but highlights the group-baseline effect and large-$G$ sampling as central. Removing the critic unlocks the ability to use large $G$ without blowing up memory, and large $G$ reduces advantage variance and better leverages comparative reward signals. The authors also emphasize that GRPO’s group-relative advantage (and normalization by group mean/std) matches the comparative nature of the reward model, which is another mechanistic benefit beyond mere memory savings. So the saved memory is a necessary enabler (you can actually run $G=64$ efficiently), but the performance improvement comes from the combination: (1) more accurate, lower-variance advantage estimates from grouping and normalization, (2) better exploitation of comparative reward signals, and (3) having enough sampled outputs (large $G$) so the group statistics are meaningful. They also show other factors (process supervision versus outcome supervision, iterative reward-model updates) further boost performance; the large-$G$ setup interacts with these choices.

Ablations and variance/stability evidence in the paper
The paper does not include a detailed tabular ablation that directly plots performance/variance as a function of $G$ (e.g., $G=4,8,16,32,64$). However, it contains analyses that conceptually justify variance reduction and shows empirical effects consistent with that mechanism: GRPO (with $G=64$) outperforms online RFT and PPO baselines; GRPO+process-supervision (which provides finer-grained step rewards inside the group) beats GRPO+outcome-supervision, supporting the claim that finer-grained, lower-variance advantage signals improve training. The authors also show that iterative RL and group-relative gradients lead to better Maj@K (stabilizing the output distribution) rather than improving Pass@K, again consistent with variance/stability improvements in the learned distribution. In short, while the paper argues and demonstrates that grouping + normalization + larger sampling improves stability and final accuracy, it does not publish a numeric sweep of different $G$ values.

Practical guidance / what I’d run if you want exact numbers
If you want to quantify sensitivity to $G$ (and the compute frontier) reproducibly: (1) keep all other hyperparameters fixed (learning rate, KL coefficient, reward-model), (2) run small-scale sweeps $G \in \{4,8,16,32,64\}$ and monitor (a) task accuracy (Maj@1 or Maj@K), (b) per-update gradient variance (empirical variance of per-example policy gradients or variance of the per-token advantages), (c) wall-time and GPU-memory usage, and (d) reward-model throughput/latency. Expect roughly $1/G$ reduction in baseline variance and near-linear increases in sampling + scoring cost. Look for diminishing returns: improvements usually saturate (and can plateau) beyond some $G$ because additional samples become redundant. Also consider batching reward-model scoring and using mixed precision or separate inference hardware for reward scoring to reduce the effective cost of larger $G$.

Short summary
GRPO’s core benefit is variance reduction via group baselines and alignment with comparative reward models; removing the critic makes it practical to increase $G$, which materially contributes to the gains. The paper demonstrates this mechanism and reports results for $G=64$, but does not publish a direct ablation sweeping smaller/larger $G$. If you need precise sensitivity curves, the sensible experiment is a controlled sweep of $G$ with measurements of accuracy, advantage variance, and compute/memory cost, as outlined above.
