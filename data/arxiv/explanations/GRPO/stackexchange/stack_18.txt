\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### How is fairness ensured in PoT and tool-use evaluations across models?
Question:
For program-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning, the evaluation result depends on the prompt, allowed libraries (e.g., sympy), timeouts, and whether multiple samples or retries are allowed. 
- What exact prompts, temperatures, and timeouts were used? 
- Did you evaluate competitors (e.g., Llemma 34B, ToRA 34B) with identical settings, or rely on reported numbers? 
- If single-shot, did you also report self-consistency variants for tool-use settings?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper reports some evaluation details (few-shot CoT/PoT prompts, allowed libraries, sampling counts used in RL/self-consistency) but does not give a complete, line-by-line specification of prompts, temperature, or timeouts for all evaluations. They evaluate many open-source models under their own prompting pipeline when feasible, but for some larger or closed models they rely on published / reported numbers. The paper also reports a single-model self-consistency result (DeepSeekMath self-consistency, $64$ samples on MATH) but does not report self-consistency variants for tool-use evaluations across competitors.

Below I summarize exactly what the paper states, point out what's missing, and give a short guide for reproducing or making fairer comparisons.

What the paper explicitly states (grounded quotations / paraphrase of the paper)
- Prompt formats: evaluations use few-shot chain-of-thought (CoT) for text solutions and few-shot program-of-thought (PoT) for program-aided/tool-integrated reasoning. Problems are “prompted to solve each problem by writing a Python program where libraries such as math and sympy can be utilized” (Section “Mathematical Problem Solving with Tool Use”).
- Allowed libraries: they explicitly mention allowing Python libraries such as math and sympy for program execution.
- Decoding/sampling used in RL and Self-consistency: during GRPO training and evaluation they sample $G = 64$ outputs per question (Section “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL” and GRPO description). They also say they used “a naive nucleus sampling” for exploration during RL (Section 4.2.3 / “How to Achieve More Effective RL?”).
- Self-consistency: they report DeepSeekMath-7B self-consistency on MATH — “Self-consistency over $64$ samples from DeepSeekMath-7B achieves $60.9\%$ on MATH.” This is an explicit self-consistency result for their model.
- Evaluation re-run vs reported numbers: the paper compares DeepSeekMath models to many open-source and closed-source models. For open-source baseline families (e.g., Llama/Mistral, Llemma, ToRA) they present comparative tables and claim to have evaluated models on the same benchmarks with their prompting pipeline. For closed-source models (GPT-4, Gemini-Ultra, etc.) they report published numbers/metrics for comparison. The paper does not include an exhaustive per-model experimental config table saying “we re-run X with exactly these seeds / temps / timeouts” for every competitor.

What the paper does NOT specify (gaps you should be aware of)
- Temperature / sampling parameters for the evaluation runs (CoT, PoT, and tool-run inference): the paper does not give a single explicit temperature value or top-p setting used across evaluations (except the RL sampling note above). It also does not provide per-evaluation decoding hyperparameters like top-k/top-p/temperature in the main text.
- Timeouts and execution resource limits for program execution: no explicit timeouts for Python-run execution (e.g., per-test-case wall time) or memory limits are documented.
- Full prompt text: the few-shot prompts are described at a high level (few-shot CoT, PoT with allowed libraries) but the paper does not print the exact prompt templates and few-shot exemplars in the main body. (They may be in an appendix or released repo; not in the text excerpt.)
- Whether every competitor was re-run under identical inference settings: the paper implies they re-ran many open-source baselines, but it is not unambiguously stated that every competitor in the tables was evaluated under the exact same prompt, decoding, library allowance, and timeout settings. For closed-source systems the paper necessarily uses reported numbers.
- Self-consistency for tool-use: aside from the single-model self-consistency result for DeepSeekMath-7B on MATH ($64$ samples), the paper does not report self-consistency / majority-vote variants for tool-use evaluations across models (e.g., self-consistency over multiple program samples for tool runs).

How this affects fairness and how to reproduce/compare fairly
Because tool-use and PoT results are sensitive to prompt wording, decoding settings (temperature/top-p), number of samples, execution timeouts/failures, and allowed libraries, the lack of a fully specified, shared inference protocol can produce differences that are due to evaluation configuration rather than model capability. The paper mitigates this somewhat by: (1) stating the allowed libraries (math, sympy); (2) using the same few-shot formats across evaluations; and (3) sampling $64$ outputs when doing RL and reporting self-consistency for their model. But to be fully reproducible and maximally fair you (or the authors) should publish the exact prompt templates, the decoding hyperparameters used for each evaluation, the program-execution timeout/memory settings, and the code for running the evaluation harness.

Practical checklist to make comparisons fair (recommended)
- Publish the exact few-shot templates (exemplar problems and solutions) used for CoT and PoT evaluations; include any special instruction phrasing.
- Fix and report decoding hyperparameters used for each reported score: temperature, top-k/top-p, nucleus/top-p details, and random seeds when sampling is used.
- For program execution, state timeouts and memory limits used to run generated programs, and the execution environment (Python version, Sympy version, etc.).
- When reporting self-consistency / majority-vote results, do so both for plain text CoT and for PoT/tool runs (i.e., sample many programs, execute each, then majority-vote their outputs) and include the same across baselines if you re-run them.
- Distinguish in tables between numbers you re-ran under your harness and numbers you took from prior papers or model cards (and provide citation / source).
- If feasible, open-source the evaluation harness and prompts so others can re-run baselines under identical conditions.

Conclusion / short summary
The paper provides important bits: few-shot CoT/PoT formats, allowed libraries (math, sympy), nucleus sampling and $64$-sample counts used in RL/self-consistency, and comparative evaluations where many open-source baselines were re-run while closed-source numbers are reported. However, it omits explicit decoding temperatures, timeouts, exact prompt exemplars, and full clarity on which competitors were re-run under identical settings vs taken from prior reports. For strict reproducibility and the fairest possible model comparisons you should ask the authors for the exact prompt files, decoding hyperparameters, program execution limits, and the evaluation code; if not available, re-running baselines under a standardized harness (and reporting both re-run and literature numbers separately) is the cleanest approach.
