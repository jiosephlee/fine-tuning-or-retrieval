\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### Does GRPO’s per-token advantage being constant across a sequence cause length bias?
Question:
For outcome supervision, \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i\) is the same for all tokens in the output \(o_i\), but you also divide the per-sequence contribution by \(|o_i|\). Does this exactly remove incentives to be verbose (since the per-sequence gradient mass is roughly proportional to \(\widetilde{r}_i\))? Are there residual effects from the clipping term or the KL penalty that could still favor longer or shorter outputs?
Answer:
Short answer: Mostly yes — the $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ averaging makes the total (unnormalized) per-sequence policy gradient proportional to the sequence-level normalized reward $\widetilde{r}_i$ in the linear part of the objective, so a trivial length incentive is removed — but not exactly. Nonlinear terms (the PPO-style clipping and the per-token KL penalty), plus how the reward model and the group normalization behave in practice, introduce residual length biases that can favor longer or shorter outputs.

Why the averaging cancels the simple length incentive. For outcome supervision GRPO sets the same per-token advantage across the sequence: $\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i$ for every token $t$ of output $o_i$. The GRPO objective (Eq. (6) in the paper) includes a factor $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ in front of the per-token sum, so if you ignore the clip operator and the KL regularizer for a moment, the contribution of sequence $i$ to the expected policy gradient is proportional to
$\left(\frac{1}{|o_i|}\right) \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \widetilde{r}_i \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})
= \widetilde{r}_i \cdot \left(\frac{1}{|o_i|}\right) \cdot |o_i| \cdot (\text{average token gradients})
= \widetilde{r}_i \cdot (\text{average token gradients}).$
In other words, the scalar multiplier in front of the token log-prob gradients reduces to $\widetilde{r}_i$, so the total gradient mass assigned to the whole sequence is roughly proportional to the sequence-level normalized reward, independent of length. Intuitively, the $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ factor distributes the sequence reward evenly across tokens, removing a simple “reward-per-token” drive to add tokens.

Where exact cancellation breaks down. Several elements in the GRPO formulation and in practice reintroduce length-dependent effects:

1) Clipping (the PPO $\min[\text{ratio},\text{clip}(\text{ratio})]$ term) is nonlinear in the per-token probability ratio. The clipping acts per-token, so whether a token’s ratio is inside or outside the clipping window depends on token-level probabilities and can interact with sequence length. Two practical consequences follow. First, longer outputs have more tokens and thus more opportunities for some token ratios to hit the clipping boundary; because the clip replaces the raw ratio by a bounded constant, the effective per-token gradient coefficient does not scale linearly with $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ across different length outputs. Second, for positive advantages the clipped term can reduce the effective reinforcement more for long sequences if many tokens are clipped, or conversely cause more aggregate clipping attenuation for long outputs; therefore clipping can produce a length-dependent attenuation of effective gradient mass.

2) The KL regularization term is applied per token inside the same $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ average, but it is not linear in the same sense: the estimator used is a nonlinear function of $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and is computed token-wise. Averaging makes the KL penalty roughly length-independent in expectation (because of the $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$), but in practice shorter sequences can have different average per-token KL (e.g., if short outputs concentrate probability mass differently or terminate earlier), so the net regularization experienced by sequences of different lengths can differ subtly. Also, the KL estimator has higher variance on short sequences (fewer terms to average), which again can produce biases.

3) Reward normalization across the group (subtracting mean and dividing by std) can create length coupling. The normalized reward $\widetilde{r}_i$ is computed across sampled outputs in the group, and if output length correlates with raw $r_i$ (for example, longer outputs are more likely to include correct or partially-correct steps and so get higher raw scores), normalization will implicitly favor those lengths. Even if the objective is unbiased, the comparative nature of the baseline depends on the sampled length distribution; if the group contains many long outputs, shorter outputs’ normalized advantages will shift accordingly.

4) The reward model itself can be length-biased. If the reward model prefers fuller explanations or penalizes terseness (or vice versa), then the normalized reward $\widetilde{r}_i$ will encode that bias and GRPO will reinforce it. The $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ averaging does not remove incentives coming from the reward function’s correlation with length.

5) Tokenization and stop-token effects. The decision to stop (produce an end-of-sequence / stop token) is a token-level event. Because the same advantage is applied to all tokens, the marginal gradient that changes the stop-vs-continue probability is not identical across lengths in practical sampling regimes, especially because decoding dynamics and early termination interact with next-token probabilities. This can slightly bias length decisions.

Net effect and practical significance. The $\frac{1}{|o_i|}$ trick successfully eliminates the simple per-token accumulation incentive (i.e., blindly adding tokens to increase total reward mass). But because GRPO retains per-token clipping and per-token KL regularization and relies on a reward model and group normalization, it cannot guarantee exact length neutrality. In practice these residual effects can be small or noticeable depending on (a) how strongly the reward model correlates with length, (b) the variance of token-level ratios (which affects clipping frequency), and (c) the composition of the sampled group (length distribution).

Practical checks and mitigations. To detect or reduce unwanted length bias you can (1) monitor output length distributions before/after RL and correlate length with reward; (2) include explicit length-aware terms if desired — e.g., subtract a small explicit length penalty or use a target length prior; (3) train or calibrate the reward model to be length-neutral (e.g., condition on length, or include length-augmented negatives in reward-model training); (4) use process supervision (per-step rewards) when appropriate — finer-grained rewards reduce reliance on a single end-of-sequence signal that can correlate with verbosity; and (5) adjust clipping / KL hyperparameters and group sampling so that token-level clipping and KL variance are less likely to induce length-dependent effects.

Conclusion. The design choice in GRPO (setting per-token advantage equal to the normalized sequence reward and dividing by $|o_i|$) largely removes the trivial incentive to be verbose, but it does not mathematically guarantee zero length bias once clipping, KL regularization, group normalization, and reward-model correlations are taken into account. Those residual mechanisms are the likely source of any empirical preference toward longer or shorter outputs, and they can be monitored and mitigated as described above.
