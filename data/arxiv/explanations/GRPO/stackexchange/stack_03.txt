### Does GRPO’s group-normalized reward introduce bias or just reduce variance?
Question:
In GRPO, for outcome supervision, you set the per-token advantage to the group-normalized reward: \(\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i = (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))/\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\), computed over \(G\) samples for the same question. Conceptually, this looks like REINFORCE with a baseline (mean) and a variance scaling (std). Is this estimator unbiased with respect to the original (unnormalized) objective, or does dividing by \(\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})\) change the target and introduce bias? How should we think about the trade-offs (variance reduction vs bias) as \(G\) varies?
Answer:
Short answer: subtracting the group mean is just a baseline and does not change the optimization target (it removes bias from the gradient estimator), but dividing by the group standard deviation is a random, group-dependent rescaling that does change the expected gradient and therefore introduces bias relative to the original un-normalized objective. In practice advantage-normalization (mean+std) is a common trick because it often reduces variance and stabilizes training; the bias introduced by dividing by the sample std gets smaller as the group size $G$ grows and the sample std converges, but for small $G$ the bias can be non-negligible and can also amplify instability if the std is small.

Why (intuitively and technically)
Consider the REINFORCE-style gradient for a single sampled output $o$ (dropping per-token sums for clarity):
$$E[r\cdot\nabla\log\pi(o\mid q)].$$
If you subtract a baseline $b(q)$ that depends only on the question $q$ (not on the sampled action distribution in a way that depends on $\nabla\log\pi$), replacing $r$ with $r-b(q)$ yields
$$E[(r-b(q))\cdot\nabla\log\pi] = E[r\cdot\nabla\log\pi] - b(q)\,E[\nabla\log\pi].$$
Because $E[\nabla\log\pi]=0$ under the sampling distribution (the score-function identity), the second term vanishes and the estimator remains unbiased. This is the standard baseline argument: subtracting any function of the state $q$ (including the group mean if it is viewed as a deterministic function of $q$) does not change the expected gradient.

GRPO’s group-mean subtraction plays the same role conceptually: it centers rewards within the group for the same question, so that high-scoring outputs are reinforced and low-scoring ones are suppressed without altering the expected gradient direction that maximizes the original reward—at least under the baseline theory. That explains why subtracting the group mean is treated as a no-bias variance reduction baseline in the paper.

Now consider dividing by the group standard deviation. GRPO uses
$$\tilde{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r)}{\mathrm{std}(r)}$$
as the per-output advantage. This introduces a multiplicative factor $1/\mathrm{std}(r)$ that depends on the whole sampled group and is random. The expected gradient becomes
$$E\left[\frac{(r-\mathrm{mean})}{\mathrm{std}}\cdot\nabla\log\pi\right].$$
Because $1/\mathrm{std}$ is a random scalar that correlates with $r$ and with $\nabla\log\pi$ (it is computed from the same sampled group), you cannot pull it out of the expectation and the identity $E[\nabla\log\pi]=0$ no longer cancels terms in the same way. In short, advantage division by the sample std is not a linear, state-only baseline and therefore changes the expectation of the gradient — i.e., it biases the estimator relative to the original (unnormalized) objective. Intuitively dividing by std reweights how much positive and negative deviations contribute, and because that reweighting depends on the sample, you are not optimizing the same objective any more.

Trade-offs as $G$ varies
The bias vs variance trade-off depends strongly on $G$, the number of group samples per question:

- Small $G$ (e.g., $2$–$8$): the sample mean and sample std are noisy. Subtracting the mean still behaves like a baseline and helps variance, but dividing by the noisy sample std can both introduce noticeable bias and amplify variance when std is small or poorly estimated. The estimator can also become unstable if std occasionally is very small. So with small $G$ the std-normalization risk (bias + instability) is relatively large.

- Moderate/large $G$ (e.g., $32$, $64$ as used in the paper): the sample mean and std are better estimates of the population moments. The group std becomes closer to a deterministic scaling factor (approximately constant for that $q$ under the current policy), so the random rescaling introduces much less bias in expectation. Practically, large $G$ reduces the estimation noise of std, so advantage-normalization behaves similarly to subtracting a baseline and then applying an (almost) deterministic scaling, which mainly affects learning rate but not the objective direction. This is why the paper samples $G=64$ and reports good results: large groups make normalization more stable and reduce the bias introduced by std-division.

Practical mitigations and common practice
Normalizing advantages (subtract mean, divide by std) is a very common empirical trick in policy-gradient practice and in many RL-for-LLM pipelines because it tends to produce stable gradients and faster learning. But you should keep in mind:

- The mean subtraction is safe (baseline) and recommended.
- The std division is a pragmatic variance-scaling trick that technically alters the expected gradient; treat it as introducing a controlled bias that can help optimization.
- To reduce the harmful side-effects of std-division use larger $G$ (so sample std is close to the true std), clip or floor std (avoid division by tiny numbers), and/or use moving-average normalization (so the scale is less correlated with the current minibatch).
- If you need strict unbiasedness with respect to the original reward objective, avoid dividing by the sample std or replace it with a deterministic scaling factor (fixed rescale) that does not depend on the sampled group.

Connection to the paper
The DeepSeekMath paper explicitly uses group-based normalization with $G=64$ in GRPO. That choice matches the practical point above: large group size makes the group std a stable scaling and thus empirically reduces variance while keeping bias modest. The authors also emphasize that GRPO leverages comparative (within-question) rewards; the mean subtraction aligns with that comparative signal and is unbiased, while the std division is a pragmatic stabilizer whose bias becomes small with large $G$.
