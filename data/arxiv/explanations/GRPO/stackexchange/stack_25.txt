### Online vs offline sampling: how do you prevent distribution collapse in online RL?
Question:
You show Online RFT outperforms RFT and motivate GRPO as an online method. However, online sampling can suffer from mode collapse or reduced diversity if the policy becomes overconfident. 
- What safeguards did you use (KL to reference, temperature floors, exploration schedules)? 
- Did you observe decreased diversity or failure modes during training, and how did you correct them?
Answer:
Short answer: we protected against online collapse primarily by (1) strong reference-model regularization (a $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ term between the current policy and a reference/SFT model), (2) using large-group online sampling plus group-normalized rewards so updates are relative (not absolute), (3) limiting how aggressively the policy is updated (one policy update per exploration batch, small learning rate), and (4) iterative reward-model training with a replay buffer so the reward signal does not drift. With those elements GRPO did not show catastrophic mode collapse in our runs; instead the learned policy became “more robust” ($\text{Maj@K}$ improved) rather than collapsing to a narrow incorrect mode.

What we actually used in the paper (implementation details tied to GRPO)
- KL regularization to a reference policy: GRPO explicitly adds a $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ penalty between the trained policy and the reference model in the objective (equation (6) / Equation (GRPO-obj)). In practice we set the $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ coefficient to $0.04$ for the reported RL runs. We also estimate the KL with the unbiased estimator discussed in the paper rather than folding KL into the numeric reward (so the regularizer is directly in the loss).
- Group sampling + relative normalization: for each question we sample a group of outputs ($G = 64$ in reported runs). Rewards are normalized within the group (subtract mean, divide by std) and advantages are computed relative to the group. That forces updates to be comparative among contemporaneous samples rather than pushing absolute probabilities up for any sample that happens to score higher — this reduces the chance of runaway concentration on a single mode.
- Process supervision (when used): we can score intermediate reasoning steps and turn those into token-level advantages (summing later step normalized rewards). This provides gradient signal spread across the chain-of-thought rather than concentrating all training on final tokens, which helps avoid collapsed short/high-probability answers that game only the final reward.
- Conservative policy updates and exploration pattern: we use a small policy learning rate ($1\times 10^{-6}$ reported), a clipping parameter (PPO-style clip $\varepsilon$), and only perform one policy update after each exploration stage (the algorithm in the paper). Sampling is done with a decoding strategy (naive nucleus sampling in our RL experiments), and we sample many outputs per prompt to retain diversity.
- Iterative reward training with replay: the reward model is periodically retrained using newly collected comparisons plus $10\%$ historical data (replay). This reduces reward-model drift that could push the policy to exploit spurious shortcuts found by a single snapshot of the reward model.
- Sampling scale and max length: we sampled $64$ outputs per question with max length $1024$ for the reported GRPO experiments, which increases the chance of encountering diverse outputs to learn from.

Did we observe decreased diversity or failure modes?
We did not observe catastrophic mode collapse in the reported GRPO runs. Empirically the RL improvement manifested as higher $\text{Maj@K}$ (the model makes the correct answer more likely to appear in the Top-$K$) rather than higher $\text{Pass@K}$ (overall capability). That suggests GRPO concentrated probability mass more on correct modes but did not collapse to a single degenerate output. In comparisons, GRPO outperformed Online RFT: Online RFT has the simpler gradient coefficient (it treats all correct answers equally and does not downweight incorrect ones), whereas GRPO uses graded reward magnitudes which lets it penalize bad modes and reward better ones — this graded signal helped shape the distribution without collapsing it.

When training instability or weaker signals appeared, the practical fixes we applied (and which we describe in the paper) were: keep the $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ term active, normalize rewards within each sampled group, use process supervision where possible (token/step-level feedback), limit policy update aggressiveness (small LR and few updates per exploration), and retrain reward models iteratively with replay.

Practical recommendations (consistent with our paper and useful if you try this):
- Maintain a reference (SFT) policy and a KL regularizer; tune its coefficient to trade improvement vs. drift.
- Sample many outputs per prompt (large $G$) and use group-relative normalization so updates are comparative, not absolute.
- If you have access to step-level signal, use process supervision; it spreads credit across tokens and reduces shortcutting.
- Retrain reward models iteratively and include historical examples in a replay buffer to avoid reward drift and the resulting policy exploits.
- Monitor distributional stats (policy entropy, token-level KL to reference, Top-$K$ diversity) and $\text{Maj@K}$ vs $\text{Pass@K}$: a rise in $\text{Maj@K}$ with stable entropy is what we observed and is a healthy sign; sharp entropy drops suggest aggressive KL/learning-rate clipping adjustments or adding entropy regularization.

Summary: GRPO’s combination of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ regularization to a reference, large-group comparative sampling with normalization, conservative updates, and iterative reward-model retraining with replay are the core safeguards used in the paper to avoid distribution collapse during online RL. We saw improved robustness (correct modes promoted) rather than harmful collapse; if collapse appears, the levers above are the remedies validated in our experiments.
