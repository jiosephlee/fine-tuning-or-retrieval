\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### How were RL hyperparameters (\(\varepsilon, \beta, \mu\), LR) chosen and how sensitive are results?
Question:
You set the policy LR to \(1\times 10^{-6}\), KL coefficient \(\beta=0.04\), and say “The policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage,” which suggests \(\mu=1\). 
- How were these values selected (grid search, prior work, rough tuning)? 
- Are the results sensitive to \(\beta\) or \(\varepsilon\) (the clipping parameter)? 
- Does increasing \(\mu\) (multiple PPO-style policy updates per batch) help or hurt performance/stability for GRPO?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper did not perform an exhaustive grid search; the RL hyperparameters (policy LR = $1\times10^{-6}$, KL coefficient $\beta = 0.04$, and single policy update per exploration stage $\mu = 1$) were chosen by rough tuning guided by prior PPO practice and stability considerations. The authors report that GRPO is stable and effective with those settings; they do not publish a large sweep over $\beta$ or $\epsilon$, and they used a single policy update ($\mu = 1$) because more updates tended to increase distribution shift and instability unless compensated by other conservative choices (smaller LR, stronger KL). Below I unpack these points with intuition, what the paper actually says, and practical implications if you want to tune further.

How the values were selected
The paper states the concrete training choices for GRPO (Section "Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL"): policy learning rate $1\times10^{-6}$, KL coefficient $\beta = 0.04$, $G = 64$ samples per question, max length $1024$, batch size $1024$, and “The policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage,” i.e., $\mu = 1$. It does not claim a full grid search. From the text and the way these numbers are presented, they were chosen by rough tuning informed by standard PPO practice and stability considerations for LLM fine-tuning:

- The policy LR is very small ($1\times10^{-6}$) consistent with common practice when fine-tuning large language models with RL to avoid sudden large updates.
- The KL coefficient $\beta = 0.04$ plays the role of a trust-region regularizer (paper moves the KL penalty into the loss rather than the reward). A modest nonzero $\beta$ is chosen so the policy does not drift far from the SFT reference.
- $\mu = 1$ (single policy update per exploration stage) matches the paper’s iterative GRPO algorithm and is chosen to limit the mismatch between the data-generating (old) policy and the updated policy. This is analogous to PPO guidelines that favor small updates and limited epochs on the same batch when sampling is online.

So, these are rough/empirical choices built on prior PPO-style recipes, not an exhaustive hyperparameter search.

Sensitivity to $\beta$ and $\epsilon$ (clipping)
The paper does not publish a systematic sensitivity table for $\beta$ or $\epsilon$. However, the manuscript highlights what these hyperparameters control and the consequences qualitatively:

- $\beta$ (KL coefficient): This controls how strongly the policy is regularized toward the reference policy. Intuitively, too small $\beta$ lets the policy drift quickly (risking collapse or exploitation of reward-model errors), while too large $\beta$ prevents meaningful improvement (policy remains close to reference). The authors pick $\beta = 0.04$ as a compromise; the discussion in Section 4 (Insights of Reinforcement Learning) emphasizes that regularization against the reference is crucial to avoid over-optimizing noisy reward signals. So results are moderately sensitive to $\beta$: it must be large enough to stabilize training, but small enough to allow learning. They do not report precise sensitivity curves, but the qualitative message is that $\beta$ matters and should be tuned if you see either collapse (reduce $\beta$?) or no improvement (increase $\beta$? actually if no improvement while KL remains tiny you may need smaller $\beta$ to allow changes).
- $\epsilon$ (PPO clipping): The paper describes the GRPO objective with the PPO-style clipping parameter $\epsilon$ but does not give a numeric value for $\epsilon$ in the RL training paragraph. In PPO practice, $\epsilon$ values around $0.1$–$0.2$ are common; clipping is used to limit per-token importance-ratio updates. While the authors do not report sweeps over $\epsilon$, the clipping mechanism is part of the stability toolkit: looser clipping (larger $\epsilon$) allows larger per-step updates (potentially unstable), tighter clipping (smaller $\epsilon$) is more conservative. Expect moderate sensitivity: if you observe large variance or stale-policy mismatch, tighten clipping; if learning is too slow, allow a slightly larger $\epsilon$ but compensate (smaller LR or larger $\beta$).

Effect of increasing $\mu$ (multiple policy updates per batch)
The paper explicitly states they used a single policy update per exploration stage ($\mu = 1$). The rationale in the text and in the GRPO algorithm is that GRPO uses outputs sampled from the old policy as the on-policy data, and performing many gradient updates on that same batch increases the distribution mismatch between the data collector (old policy) and the current policy. This mismatch tends to destabilize RL fine-tuning in practice unless you compensate with:

- much smaller learning rates,
- stronger KL regularization (larger $\beta$),
- fewer update epochs on the same samples, or
- use of importance weighting / off-policy corrections.

Therefore, increasing $\mu$ (doing many PPO-style epochs per sampled batch) is likely to hurt stability for GRPO in the default configuration used in the paper. It can sometimes improve sample efficiency if you take compensatory measures (reduce LR, raise $\beta$, tighten clipping), but the paper’s stable recipe was $\mu = 1$. The authors’ choice and wording imply they observed that a single update after exploration was the pragmatic, stable option.

Practical advice if you want to tune further
If you want to explore hyperparameter sensitivity yourself, the sensible, minimal experiments that align with the paper are: start from LR = $1\times10^{-6}$, $\beta = 0.04$, $\mu = 1$, $G = 64$, and try the following one-variable ablations:

- $\beta$ sweep (e.g., $0.01$, $0.02$, $0.04$, $0.08$): watch KL, reward improvement, and stability. Lower $\beta$ permits faster change but watch for exploitation of reward model noise.
- $\epsilon$ sweep if not fixed (e.g., $0.05$, $0.1$, $0.2$): tighter clipping improves stability; looser speeds learning but risks instability.
- $\mu$ ablation: try $\mu = 1$ (baseline), $\mu = 2$ with half LR, $\mu = 4$ with quarter LR and stronger $\beta$. If multiple epochs on the same data help, you should see consistent gains without KL exploding; otherwise revert to $\mu = 1$.
- Monitor Maj@K vs Pass@K (as the paper did): RL improvements often show up as increased Maj@K (robustness of top outputs) rather than improvements in Pass@K.

Summary
The paper’s hyperparameters were chosen by rough tuning informed by PPO conventions, with explicit choices reported (LR = $1\times10^{-6}$, $\beta = 0.04$, $\mu = 1$). The authors do not present large hyperparameter sweeps for $\beta$ or $\epsilon$, but they emphasize that KL regularization and conservative updates are important to prevent instability. Increasing $\mu$ without compensatory conservative settings is likely to hurt stability; multiple updates per batch can be used but require lowering LR and/or increasing KL regularization and tightening clipping.
