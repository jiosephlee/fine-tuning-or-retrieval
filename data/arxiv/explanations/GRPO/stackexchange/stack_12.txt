\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: GRPO}

### What is the exact training signal for the reward model(s) in outcome vs process supervision?
Question:
You state that reward model training follows prior work, and then you do iterative GRPO with a replay buffer containing 10% historical data. 
- For outcome supervision, is the reward model trained with pairwise comparisons, scalar labels, or a mix? 
- For process supervision, how are step-level labels collected (human annotations, automatic heuristics, or distillation)? 
- In iterative GRPO, how exactly are new datasets constructed from policy outputs, and how do you ensure label quality (especially if the initial reward model is weak)?
Answer:
Short answer: the paper follows the standard distinction used in recent RL-for-reasoning work. The outcome-reward model provides a single scalar per full solution (normalized across the sampled group) and is trained from the SFT / sampled outputs dataset constructed as in Wang et al. (2023). The process-reward model scores individual reasoning steps (gives step-level scalar labels) and is trained from a dataset constructed in the same way (the paper explicitly says “we construct the training set of reward models following [wang2023math]”). In iterative GRPO new data are produced by sampling many outputs from the current policy, labeling them with the current reward model, adding those labeled tuples into the reward-model training set (with a replay buffer that keeps $10\%$ historic data), and then re-training the reward model before the next GRPO iteration. Several mechanisms in the pipeline are used to limit poor labels from an initially weak reward model: large-group normalization ($G$ up to $64$), per-group standardization of rewards, replaying historic examples ($10\%$ buffer) to stabilize learning, and regularization of the policy (KL term to the reference policy) during policy updates.

Explanation and details grounded in the paper

1) Outcome supervision — what the reward model predicts and how it is trained
The paper treats outcome supervision in the usual comparative/reward-model way: for each question $q$ a group of outputs $o_1,\dots,o_G$ is sampled from the old policy, the reward model $r_\phi$ assigns a scalar $r_i$ to each full output $o_i$, and those scalars are normalized within the group (subtract group mean and divide by group standard deviation). In GRPO the normalized scalar for the final token of $o_i$ becomes the advantage used for every token in that output: $\hat A_{i,t} = \tilde r_i$. The paper also states that the reward-model training follows prior work and that reward models are "typically trained on datasets of comparisons between outputs on the same question", i.e., the training data used to supervise $r_\phi$ is constructed from model outputs on the same prompts so the model learns relative preferences across solutions on the same question (this is the same training style used in the cited prior work).

So in short: outcome supervision yields a scalar reward per complete solution; the reward model is trained on data composed of multiple outputs per question so it can learn comparative scoring (the paper explicitly ties this to prior work).

2) Process supervision — how step-level labels are obtained and used
Process supervision in the paper provides scalar rewards at the end of each reasoning step (the notation in the paper: $r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ is the reward for the $j$-th step of output $i$). The process reward model is trained to score steps, and GRPO computes per-token advantages by summing normalized future step rewards: $\hat A_{i,t} = \sum_{\text{index}(j) \ge t} \tilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$. The paper says they “follow Wang et al. (2023)” to construct the reward-model training set, which implies the process labels are produced using the same training-set-construction strategy as that prior work (i.e., using sampled outputs and labeling steps according to heuristics / comparisons used in that earlier paper). The paper does not claim large-scale crowdsourced human step-by-step labeling was used for all data; rather it points to the established recipe from Wang et al. for building process-labeled datasets. Thus the reader should understand that step-level labels come from the paper’s reward-dataset construction pipeline (which in practice in Wang et al. used model-sampled outputs, heuristics and/or comparison-based labels rather than exclusively human annotation).

3) Iterative GRPO — how new datasets are constructed and how label quality is controlled
Algorithmically, iterative GRPO proceeds as in Algorithm 1 of the paper. Starting from an initial SFT policy and an initial reward model $r_\phi$ (trained as described), the loop does:
- Sample questions $q$ from the RL question set.
- For each $q$, sample a group of $G$ outputs from the old policy (the paper uses $G=64$ in experiments).
- Score each sampled output (and for process supervision each step) with the current reward model $r_\phi$ to produce labels $r_i$ (and $r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$).
- Use those labels (normalized per group) as training targets to compute advantages and run GRPO updates on the policy.
- After an exploration/training stage, update the reward model $r_\phi$ using the newly labeled samples, but train the reward model with a replay mechanism that retains $10\%$ historical data (i.e., each reward-model training batch is a mixture of new samples plus a fixed $10\%$ fraction of previous examples).

The paper explicitly states that the replay buffer contains $10\%$ historical data to stabilize the reward-model training. Additional safety/stabilization mechanisms quoted in the paper are the within-group normalization of rewards (subtract mean / divide by std), the use of large groups ($G=64$) which reduces label noise by giving the reward model more comparative context, and a KL regularizer on the policy objective (the GRPO loss includes a KL divergence to the reference policy) that prevents the policy from moving too far when the reward signal is imperfect.

Intuition about robustness to a weak initial reward model
If the initial reward model is weak, the iterative pipeline mitigates harm in several ways that the paper emphasizes: (1) group-based normalization centers scores so optimization uses relative rather than absolute values; (2) large $G$ gives good statistical context for comparisons so advantages are more stable; (3) the reward model itself is continuously retrained with a replay buffer containing historical examples ($10\%$ old) so it does not overfit to transient or adversarial outputs; and (4) the policy update is regularized with a KL term to a reference policy so extreme updates from noisy signals are damped. The combination of these mechanisms is the paper’s practical approach to maintaining label quality during iterative RL.

What the paper does not claim
The paper does not claim that step-level labels were mostly human-annotated at scale, nor does it detail any new human-label collection procedure beyond “constructing the training set of reward models following [wang2023math].” If you need to reproduce their reward-model dataset exactly, the paper points you to Wang et al. (2023) for the reward-data construction recipe and states their initial reward model was trained starting from DeepSeekMath-Base with learning rate $2\times 10^{-5}$.
