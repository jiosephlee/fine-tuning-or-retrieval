# Prompting for reasoning: chain-of-thought (CoT) and program-of-thought (PoT)
Two prompting paradigms are central to eliciting multi-step mathematical reasoning from large language models in this work: chain-of-thought (CoT) and program-of-thought (PoT). Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to produce a step-by-step natural-language reasoning trace that leads to a final answer. In practice the evaluation in the paper uses few-shot CoT prompting: a small set of exemplar problems with their worked-out stepwise solutions is prepended to each test question, and the model is expected to continue in that style. The intuition is that the model can be guided to reveal intermediate reasoning steps rather than only a final numeric answer, and those intermediate steps often make it easier to arrive at correct solutions for multi-step problems.

Program-of-thought (PoT) is a closely related but distinct style that asks the model to express its reasoning by writing a short program (rather than purely natural-language steps) that computes the answer. In PoT prompts the model is required to produce code—commonly Python—whose execution yields the numeric or symbolic solution. The paper treats PoT as an interface between natural reasoning and precise computation: when a model writes correct code, execution removes ambiguity in e or algebraic manipulation and produces an authoritative result. The PoT paradigm is therefore particularly effective for problems where exact numeric computation, algebraic simplification, or symbolic manipulation matters.

Both CoT and PoT are used in few-shot settings in the experiments described: CoT to evaluate the model’s naked reasoning ability (step-by-step natural language), and PoT to evaluate program-aided reasoning where the model’s output can be executed for a ground-truth check.

# Tool-integrated reasoning and Python execution (e.g., sympy, math)
Tool-integrated reasoning extends PoT by allowing models to call and rely on external libraries and execution environments during solution generation. In the experiments reported, program-aided reasoning prompts ask the model to write Python code that may import and use standard libraries such as math and domain-specific symbolic libraries such as sympy. The model’s generated program is executed, and the execution result (for example, a numeric value or a symbolic expression simplified to an answer) is taken as the model’s final answer.

This setup has two practical consequences. First, it separates reasoning | from mechanical calculation: a correct program that implements the right solution logic will produce a correct numeric answer even when the textual reasoning might be terse. Second, it creates an evaluation protocol that can be objectively checked (the program output) rather than relying only on text matching. The paper uses this approach to report program-of-thought accuracy on t such as GSM8K and MATH by executing the generated programs and judging their outputs.

Because execution is authoritative, program-aided evaluation requires care in designing prompts (to constrain use of external or unsafe operations) and in permitting a fixed set of libraries (the paper explicitly allows libraries such as math and sympy). When comparing models under PoT/ tool-integrated evaluation, the reported metric reflects whether the executed program yields the correct answer.

# Formal mathematics and autoformalization (Isabelle, Sledgehammer, miniF2F)
Formal mathematics evaluates a model’s ability to translate informal mathematical text into a formalized proof that a proof assistant can check. The paper studies informal-to-formal autoformalization using Isabelle as the target proof assistant and miniF2F as the benchmark. In this task, each problem is presented with an informal statement, its formal counterpart, and an informal proof sketch; the model must produce a formal Isabelle proof script that can be verified.

A common practical pipeline used in the experiments is to prompt the e to produce a formal proof sketch or partial formalization and then rely on an automated prover to fill in routine details. Specifically, the paper follows the approach of generating proof sketches and invoking an off-the-shelf automated prover—Sledgehammer—to discharge subgoals and complete the formal proof. Success on miniF2F is measured by whether the generated Isabelle proof is accepted by the proof assistant (i.e., type-checks and closes the theorem).

Autoformalization is a demanding task because it requires (1) mapping informal mathematical language to the formal definitions and lemmas of the proof assistant, (2) constructing a chain of logically valid steps in the assistant’s language, and (3) coordinating with automated tools (like Sledgehammer) to fill low-level details. The paper reports that its math-d base model demonstrates strong few-shot autoformalization performance in this setting, showing the benefits of domain-specific pre-training for formal tasks.

# Evaluation practices: few-shot CoT, PoT execution accuracy, self-consistency, Pass@K vs e@K
Evaluation in the paper follows several established practices tailored to the type of output and the intended use of tools.

Few-shot chain-of-thought evaluation: For CoT-style assessment the model is prompted with a handful of solution exemplars in the required step-by-step format and then asked to solve held-out problems in the same format. Scoring accepts the final numeric answer produced by the CoT chain as the model’s response. This evaluates the model’s ability to produce coherent stepwise reasoning.

Program-of-thought execution accuracy: For PoT and tool-integrated prompts, evaluation runs the model-generated program in a controlled execution environment with a specified set of libraries (e.g., math, sympy). The program’s returned value is compared against the ground-truth answer; t is therefore decided by program execution. This allows exact, objective judgment for problems that are amenable to computation.

Self-consistency and sampling: The paper reports self-consistency sampling results, where multiple reasoning chains or programs are e for a single question and then aggregated (for example, by majority voting). Self-consistency increases robustness: the paper reports that sampling 64 outputs from t-7B and applying self-consistency raised MATH performance from 51.7\% (single-sample RL result) to 60.9\%. Self-consistency e the idea that correct reasoning paths may recur among samples and that voting or aggregation can amplify the correct answer.

Pass@K vs d@K: These two metrics capture different ways of aggregating multiple f outputs. Pass@K measures whether at least one of K x outputs is correct (an “existential” criterion), while x@K measures whether the majority of K outputs are correct (a “consensus” criterion). The paper studies both and finds that t learning tends to improve n@K significantly but not Pass@K. In empirical terms this indicates RL consolidates the model’s output distribution to increase the probability mass on the correct answer (so the correct answer appears more often among samples), rather than merely adding a few isolated correct samples that would raise Pass@K. This distinction helps interpret what RL is doing: it makes the model more likely to generate the correct solution consistently rather than only occasionally.

When reporting program-based evaluations, the paper treats execution-based n (the executed program’s result) as the authoritative signal, and when reporting self-consistency or t aggregation metrics it clearly notes the number of samples used (for example, 64 samples).

# Data contamination risks and o principles
Pre-training data contamination—when evaluation benchmark examples appear in pre-training corpora—can artificially inflate measured performance. The paper describes explicit d measures taken when assembling the x Corpus from Common Crawl to avoid contaminating English and Chinese mathematical > (e.g., GSM8K, MATH, t, and AGIEval).

The practical d rule used is a string-match filter: any text segment in the candidate training corpus that contains a f-| exact substring matching any substring from the evaluation o is removed. For | whose examples are shorter than x-grams but at least 3-grams, exact matching on those shorter n-grams is used. This conservative filtering ensures that long exact overlaps—which are most likely to indicate copied benchmark content—are removed, reducing the risk that the model memorizes specific evaluation problems during pre-training.

Additional data hygiene steps taken in the pipeline include URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication of Common Crawl pages (the paper reduced to about 40B HTML pages before classification and filtering), iterative <-based retrieval of math pages, and manual annotation to enrich the seed corpus. These steps both increase the quality of math-relevant pages and help control for accidental inclusion of benchmark content.

Finally, when evaluating models it is important to be explicit about which pre-training and fine-tuning corpora were used, whether any benchmark examples might have leaked into training, and which x methods were applied. The paper documents these choices and applies the e-f rule to ensure the reported benchmark results reflect learned reasoning ability rather than memorization of test questions.
