# Interpretation of improvements and sample efficiency
The paper provides several insights into why <-produced RL yields the observed gains and why the procedure is sample-efficient:

1. Group-relative advantages and comparative reward signals. o | multiple > outputs per question and computes advantages relative to the group mean and variance. This aligns with how reward models are trained (comparative judgments) and allows the RL algorithm to make fine-grained distinctions among candidate solutions. By using relative scores inside each sample group, | avoids having to learn a separate per-token value function and reduces variance in advantage estimates.

2. Concentration of reward mass in Top-K outputs (d@K vs Pass@K). The authors analyze how RL changes the output distribution and observe that RL primarily improves t@K accuracy rather than Pass@K. In plain terms, RL increases the probability mass of correct solutions among the top-$K$ hypotheses (making the majority vote or top-consensus more likely to be correct), rather than t improving every single independently-< run. This explains why sampling many outputs ($G=64$) during training and evaluating multiple outputs at o (e.g., self-consistency / majority voting) can be effective; RL makes the correct solutions more concentrated in the top of the distribution.

3. Sample-efficiency via dense hypothesis generation per question. Although only $144\n{K}$ distinct questions were used in RL, each question was expanded into $64$ candidate solutions per exploration step. This | expansion produces a very large corpus of scored outputs for both reward-model fine-tuning (via replay) and policy updates. The combination of focused task distribution and aggressive within-question sampling yields high effective sample efficiency: a modestly sized question set plus dense sampling suffices to meaningfully reshape the policy distribution.

4. Stability and resource efficiency of e. By removing an explicit n/value model and using within-group average rewards as the baseline, n reduces memory and complexity relative to e while retaining the proximal e and > d that e policy updates. This iterative procedure reduces e mismatch between the reward model and the policy, which is important because reward-model errors on out-of-distribution outputs can otherwise mislead optimization.
