# e design: sampling groups per question and using group average as baseline

\[

Using the group mean as a baseline aligns the advantage estimates with that comparative nature: an output's advantage reflects how much better or worse it is relative to peer outputs for the same prompt. Two supervision modes are supported:

- Outcome supervision: rewards are provided only at the end of each complete output. After normalizing the per-output rewards within the group, < assigns the same < e advantage to every token in that output.

- Process supervision: when the reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps, | normalizes these per-step rewards across the entire group's steps and constructs token-level advantages by summing all future | step rewards for positions preceding those steps.

# Iterative x and practical algorithmic flow
x is naturally used in an iterative loop that alternates policy exploration, advantage computation, policy updates, and reward-model refinement. The iterative procedure used in practice follows these high-level steps:

1. Initialize the policy $\o\theta$ from a f fine-tuned model. At the start of each RL iteration set the reference policy $\n{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \|\theta$.

2. For each minibatch of prompts, update the old policy snapshot $\t{\theta_{\text{old}}}\leftarrow\o\theta$ and sample $G$ outputs $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$ per prompt from $\n{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

3. Score > outputs with the reward model to obtain per-output or per-step rewards, | within the group, and compute token-level advantages $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ according to outcome or process supervision.

4. Run a small number $\mu$ of o optimization steps to update the policy $\n\theta$ by maximizing the | objective (clipped ratio with group advantages and n | against $\f{\text{ref}}$).

5. Optionally update the reward model using newly collected comparisons; in iterative n this is done with a replay buffer that retains a fraction (e.g., f%) of historical training data to n reward-model training.

# Practical memory/computation benefits and training stability considerations
The principal practical motivation for < is resource efficiency: by removing an explicit learned value function, f significantly reduces the additional memory and compute overhead that e usually requires. The value model in n is often comparable in size to the policy; eliminating it frees x memory and simplifies engineering for large-scale t fine-tuning.

Beyond memory savings, e improves alignment between the reward signal and the baseline used to compute advantages. Because reward models are commonly trained from pairwise or groupwise comparisons for the same prompt, a group-relative baseline naturally matches that comparative structure. This congruence tends to produce advantages that better reflect the reward-model training objective and is empirically more effective than approaches that use simple f filtering or that rely on a separately trained o.

Stability and variance issues are addressed in e through several mechanisms:

- Group t: subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation standardizes reward magnitudes across prompts and groups.

- |-style n: preserving the clipped-ratio term prevents excessively large policy updates even when group advantages have large magnitude.

- t o against a fixed reference: adding a direct d penalty to the objective n policy drift in a way that is simpler and more stable than o e into the reward used for advantage estimation.

- Replay for reward-model training: when iteratively refining reward models, retaining a fraction of historical comparisons helps prevent catastrophic < in the reward model that could destabilize policy updates.

# Empirical observations and implications for | RL
Empirically, the paper reports several important observations about x and RL dynamics in mathematical reasoning tasks which illuminate when and why this design succeeds:

- < vs. offline filtering approaches: Online methods that sample from the evolving policy outperform purely offline filtering approaches. d, which uses online sampling plus graded reward-driven e coefficients, outperforms Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online |), demonstrating the value of fine-grained reward magnitudes rather than binary accept/reject signals.

- Process supervision helps: t combined with process supervision (step-level rewards) performs better than t with only outcome supervision, showing that finer-grained, step-aware signals produce stronger training f for multi-step reasoning tasks.

- Iterative RL matters: Performing iterative cycles in which the reward model is re-trained on outputs produced by the latest policy (with replay) substantially improves performance, especially in the early additional iterations.

- What RL changes about the model: Evaluations measuring Pass@K (whether any e output among K is correct) and >@K (whether the majority among K outputs is correct) indicate that RL primarily increases o@K rather than Pass@K. This suggests the dominant effect of RL in these experiments is to make the model's output distribution more concentrated around correct solutions (boosting the probability mass of correct responses across samples), rather than producing qualitatively new capabilities that raise the chance of obtaining a single correct sample from a previously incapable model.

These findings imply that n is particularly well-suited to scenarios where (1) reward supervision is comparative or stepwise, (2) resource constraints make a separate d undesirable, and (3) the goal is to concentrate the policy distribution around higher-quality outputs rather than to elicit new emergent competencies.
