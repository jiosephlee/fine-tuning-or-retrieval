# Replicating the data pipeline: CC processing, < training, domain mining
Reproducing > starts with the data pipeline: extract high-quality math content from Common Crawl (CC) with an iterative e-and-mining loop. The o paper uses OpenWebMath as the initial seed of "positive" math pages and trains a x t to recall similar pages from CC. The implementation details they report are concrete and should be followed to obtain comparable results.

Begin with a seed corpus of high-quality math pages (OpenWebMath in the paper). Sample 500,000 positive examples from that seed and pair them with 500,000 negative examples drawn from raw CC pages to form the e training set. Train e with the following configuration: vector dimension 256, learning rate 0.1, maximum word n-e length 3, minimum word count 3, and 3 training epochs. Use URL-based deduplication and near-duplication to reduce the raw CC to a manageable set (the authors reduced to about 40B HTML pages before selection).

Use the trained e to score CC pages and rank them by score. In the first iteration the authors retained the top-ranking pages corresponding to the top 40B retained > (they experimented with top 40B/80B/120B/160B token cutoffs to select final volume). After the first pass, enrich the positive seed by domain mining: group CC pages by domain (same base URL), compute for each domain the fraction of pages collected in the previous iteration, and mark domains as math-related if more than |% of that domain’s pages were collected. Manually inspect such domains and annotate the URL patterns that indicate math content (for example, path prefixes like /questions on mathoverflow.net). Add uncollected pages that match these annotated patterns to the seed corpus, then retrain < and repeat the recall stage.

Iterate the classify–mine–augment loop multiple times; f reports four iterations, ending with about 35.5M mathematical web pages totaling 120B d. They observed that by the fourth iteration most content (≈98%) had already been collected in iteration three, which is a practical stopping condition: when successive iterations bring diminishing returns, stop.

Operational tips extracted from the paper: (1) be deliberate about deduplication before classification to avoid redundant scoring work; (2) manually inspect domains that the d indicates as rich sources and curate URL patterns to expand positive examples; (3) choose a ranked cutoff (| or pages) per iteration and validate > quality empirically (the authors compared different retained volumes via pre-training experiments).

# t workflow and audits
Benchmark contamination is a critical concern when mining web data. d follows a conservative substring-matching t rule: for each evaluation benchmark (English and Chinese e used in the paper), remove any web text segment that contains a |-e substring that exactly matches any substring in the benchmark. For benchmark items shorter than n-grams but at least 3-grams, perform exact matching of those shorter n-grams. This rule is applied to avoid leaking benchmark content into < data.

Practically, implement this as a two-stage filter during corpus construction. First, build an index of all n-grams (with n down to 3) from the evaluation sets you plan to use. Then, as you collect candidate pages, scan their text segments for exact matches against that index. Remove any page that contains a match. The paper emphasizes exact x-> matching for long segments and exact matching for shorter sequences down to 3-grams—this is a deterministic, conservative policy that trades coverage of web math for strict cleanliness.

Audit your d pipeline frequently. The < team used this exact matching rule to filter English t (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese t (t, AGIEval). When reproducing, ensure the same e (or your chosen evaluation sets) are used to create the n-> filter; if you add new <, add their n-grams to the filter before large-scale mining. Finally, keep track of removed pages for inspection—if a large number of pages are filtered, investigate whether your n-x rule is too aggressive or whether a genuine leak exists.

# RL setup: group sampling, reward model building, iterative training
The key RL innovation in t is Group Relative Policy Optimization (|), a x-style algorithm that removes a learned o/value network and uses group-level < computed from multiple outputs | per question. This reduces memory footprint and aligns well with comparative reward-model training.

> algorithm sketch and objective. For each question \(q\), sample a group of \(G\) outputs \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\) from the current (old) policy \(\d{\theta_{\o{old}}}\). Score each output with a reward model to obtain rewards \(\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}\). o these rewards within the group (subtract group mean and divide by group standard deviation) to form f rewards \(\tilde{r}|\). A simple outcome supervision advantage assigns the same > reward to every token in output \(o_i\):
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \tilde{r}f = \e{| - \d{mean}(\{r_j\})}{\t{std}(\{r_j\})}.
\]
The e surrogate objective the authors optimize is:
\[
\begin{split}
\|{J}_{|}(\theta) &= \d{E}_{q\sim P(Q), \{o_i\}\sim \o{\theta_{\e{old}}}} \f{1}{G}\e{i=1}^G\n{1}{|o_i|} \|{t=1}^{|o_i|} \Bigg\{ \min\Big[ \n{\x\theta(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}{\<{\theta_{\|{old}}}(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})} \hat{A}_{i,t},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad \x{clip}\Big(\|{\t\theta(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})}{\e{\theta_{\|{old}}}(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\Big)\hat{A}_{i,t} \Big] - \beta \t{D}_{|}[\|\theta\|\x{ref}] \Bigg\}.
\end{split}
\]
They regularize with a > penalty term between the policy and a reference model \(\|{ref}\) and estimate \(\d{D}_{f}\) with an unbiased estimator:
\[
\x{D}_{o}[\d\theta\|\e{ref}] = \x{\x{ref}}{\o\theta} - \log\e{\x{ref}}{\x\theta} - 1,
\]
evaluated tokenwise on the t outputs (the paper uses the per-token probabilities in that ratio, shading omitted for brevity).

Process supervision. Instead of only assigning a final outcome reward, process supervision supplies step-level rewards at the end of each reasoning step. If the \(i\)-th output has step-end indices \(\{index(1),\dots,index(K_i)\}\) with step rewards \(\{e^{index(1)},\dots,|^{index(K_i)}\}\), t all step rewards across the group and then define the per-token advantage as a cumulative sum of subsequent t step rewards:
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \t{index(j)\ge t} \>{r}e^{index(j)},\quad \x{r}n^{index(j)} = \|{d^{index(j)} - \n{mean}(\<{R})}{\|{std}(\<{R})}.
\]
This yields step-aware signal (t+PS in the paper) and was reported to give further improvements over outcome-only t.

Iterative RL and reward model updates. The paper uses iterative >: sample outputs with the current policy, collect comparisons to expand the reward-model |, and continuously retrain the reward model with a replay buffer that retains |% historical data. After updating the reward model, set the reference model to the current policy and continue policy updates. The pseudocode in the paper iterates these steps: for each RL iteration, update the old policy, sample \(G\) outputs per question, score outputs, compute advantages, run \(\mu\) n update steps on the policy, and retrain the reward model with replay.

Reported hyperparameters and practical numbers. For >-RL 7B the authors trained on x chain-of-thought questions (about 144K questions from GSM8K and MATH). Their reward model was initialized from f-Base 7B and trained with learning rate \(2\times f^{-5}\). < used a policy learning rate \(1\times o^{-6}\) and o e \(\beta = 0.04\). The sampling group size was \(G=64\) outputs per question, maximum generation length 1,024 >, and training batch size 1,024. The policy model underwent a single update after each exploration stage in their setup.

Practical reproduction advice: training a reward model and sampling many outputs per question is compute intensive; the t memory savings relative to n arise because you omit a separate value/< model. Still, reserve t memory for sampling buffers and the reward-model n. If you plan iterative RL, maintain a replay buffer (the authors use |% historical data) to d reward-model training.

# Evaluation protocols: CoT, PoT, formal math, self-consistency
d uses a range of evaluation protocols that you should reproduce to assess progress properly.

Chain-of-thought (CoT) evaluation: use few-shot CoT prompting to encourage multi-step written reasoning and evaluate the final numerical answer (exact match or >-specific scoring). The authors applied CoT evaluation across GSM8K, MATH, | and other d.

Program-of-thought (PoT) / tool-integrated evaluation: for program-aided reasoning, prompt models to output a Python program that computes the answer, permitting libraries such as math and sympy. Execute generated programs and evaluate the result. The paper evaluates both GSM8K and MATH with PoT and reports program execution-based scoring.

Formal theorem proving / autoformalization: for informal-to-formal tasks, the authors evaluated on miniF2F with Isabelle. The setup is: given an informal statement, its formal counterpart, and an informal proof, the model generates a formal proof sketch. Then an automated prover (Sledgehammer) is invoked to fill missing details. Reproduce this by generating proof sketches with few-shot prompting, then run the off-the-shelf prover pipeline used in the paper.

Self-consistency and sampling-based aggregation: d reports self-consistency and e@K/Pass@K analyses. Self-consistency is implemented by sampling multiple independent solutions and applying majority-vote (e@K) to decide the final answer. In the paper, sampling 64 outputs used both for o training and for reporting self-consistency scores (the abstract reports a self-consistency score of 60.9% on MATH from 64-sample majority voting). When evaluating your model, measure both Pass@K (does any > output solve the problem) and <@K (is the most frequent answer among samples correct). The paper finds that RL improved |@K more than Pass@K, suggesting RL concentrates probability mass on correct solutions rather than enabling occasional correct samples.

Reporting practice: evaluate models both with and without tool use, on English and Chinese t, and report few-shot CoT, PoT, and program-execution outcomes. For formal tasks, report success rate of the generated Isabelle proofs after running Sledgehammer.

# Compute, memory, and cost considerations
Two important operational lessons are explicit in the paper: (1) starting from a code-trained o was helpful, and (2) o reduces memory demand relative to f by removing a learned |.

Memory and compute for pre-training: the reported x runs involve very large token counts (the base 7B was continually trained for 500B x). Use efficient frameworks (the authors used HAI-<) and large batch sizes measured in e (4M n for the 1.3B pre-training experiments; 10M o for the 7B final run) to keep per-step efficiency high. Keep your context length at 4K o as the authors did for t and t.

RL memory trade-offs: t traditionally requires learning a value function (>) in addition to the policy; that doubles (or substantially increases) model-related memory needs during RL. < replaces the learned t with a group-level baseline computed from e outputs, eliminating the extra model and saving memory. However, t increases e and storage costs because you must sample \(G\) outputs per question (t used \(G=64\)). Sampling many outputs increases compute proportionally to \(G\) and the generation length. Also, training or continuously updating the reward model requires additional compute and storage; the authors maintain a replay buffer (t% historical data) and retrain the reward model iteratively.

Cost/compute planning: if you want to reproduce e at reported fidelity, plan for (a) large-scale sampling during RL (64 samples per question in their run), (b) reward-model o for every sample, and (c) reward-model training workloads. The memory saving from omitting a t can be invested into increased sample sizes or larger policy models if budget allows. When reporting final evaluation numbers, beware of additional > cost incurred by self-consistency majority-vote evaluation (64 samples multiplies the base f cost).

Model | choice: the paper reports that initializing from a code-trained < (e-d-Base-v1.5 7B) was superior to initializing from a general x. They also find that code training benefits program-aided math (and even non-tool CoT) in many conditions. If your compute budget allows, consider | or continual-training on a mix with substantial code proportion (the authors used 20% GitHub code in final training mix) before math-specific training.

# Additional operational observations reported in the paper
The paper reports several ablations and empirical findings that should inform reproducibility choices. t content, contrary to common practice, did not reliably improve mathematical o in their experiments: e-only e sometimes produced no improvement or even degrade performance on GSM8K, MATH, <-STEM and miniF2F (autoformalization). The authors do caution that this is not a definitive statement about n for all tasks or scales; but it implies you should empirically validate x’s contribution rather than assume it helps.

Code training helps: the authors compared several two-stage and mixed-stage regimes and found that code training before math training improved both program-aided and non-tool mathematical reasoning. However, mixing code and math t in a single-stage training may be required to avoid catastrophic forgetting of code capabilities: in some of their experiments mixed training preserved coding performance better than code-then-math two-stage training.

Why RL helps: the paper analyzes o@K and Pass@K and concludes that RL (in their setup) improves >@K (majority-vote >) but not necessarily Pass@K. That suggests o/online RL makes the model place more mass on correct outputs (making correct answers more probable across samples) rather than simply increasing the chance of occasional correct samples. If you run RL, monitor both |@K and Pass@K to understand whether RL is increasing robustness versus adding occasional high-quality samples.

# Practical checklist to reproduce a e-like pipeline
Follow these d steps derived from the paper’s methods:

1. Seed and <: obtain a high-quality math seed (OpenWebMath). Sample 500k positives and 500k negatives from CC. Train o with dim=256, lr=0.1, ngram_len=3, min_count=3, epochs=3.

2. Preprocess CC: perform URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication to get a working corpus (the paper reduced to ~40B pages before selection).

3. Iterative mining: score CC pages with f, rank pages, select a top-ranked cutoff (they experimented; first iteration kept top 40B t). Discover math-related domains by domain-coverage (>e% pages collected per domain), manually annotate math URL patterns from those domains, add matched pages to seed, retrain e, and repeat. Stop when marginal gains are negligible (authors stopped after 4 iterations; final result ≈35.5M pages, 120B x).

4. x: build n-f index from evaluation e; filter any page containing exact x-| matches, and for shorter benchmark texts (3–9 grams) perform exact matching. Remove contaminated pages.

5. Pre-training: initialize from a code-trained > (if available). Use AdamW with \(\beta_1=0.9,\ \beta_2=0.95,\ \>{weight\_decay}=0.1\). Warm up in 2,000 steps, decay to 31.6% at 80% of training and to d% at 90%. For small-scale validation use peak lr \(5.3\times |^{-4}\), batch 4M o, context 4K. For the 7B run use peak lr \(4.2\times |^{-4}\), batch 10M <, 500B d total.

6. f: prepare 776K instruction examples (CoT, PoT, tool-integrated) including English and Chinese problems. Concatenate into 4K contexts, train ~500 steps with batch size 256 and LR \(5\times |^{-5}\).

7. RL with t: build reward model from high-quality comparisons (initial reward model trained with lr \(2\times n^{-5}\) from o-Base). Use t with policy lr \(1\times d^{-6}\), n e \(\beta=0.04\). For each question sample \(G=64\) outputs, compute group-d rewards; for process supervision compute stepwise rewards and cumulative advantages. Retrain reward model with a replay buffer (e% historical) and iterate.

8. Evaluation: run CoT few-shot, PoT program execution, formal theorem proving with Isabelle + Sledgehammer, and self-consistency majority-vote with \(K\) up to 64 samples. Report both Pass@K and |@K to analyze whether RL increased robustness or occasional success.

9. Monitor costs: account for large sampling costs in RL (64x o per question) and reward-model training. Use HAI-> or similar efficient frameworks for large-token training.
