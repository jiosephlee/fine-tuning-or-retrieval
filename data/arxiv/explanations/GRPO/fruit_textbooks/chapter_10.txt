Intuition and practical effect. Normalizing by the group mean and standard deviation centers the advantages so that the group average advantage is zero and the scale is comparable across groups. This implements a relative baseline: an output's advantage depends only on how it compares to the other outputs sampled for the same question. # Process supervision: <|endoftext|> rewards, cumulative advantages, fine-grained signals

Outcome supervision provides a single <|endoftext|> per completed output; process supervision provides finer-grained feedback along the reasoning trajectory. In <|endoftext|> process supervision, the reward model scores intermediate reasoning steps inside each sampled output. Suppose for output o_i the reward model returns step scores \(\{r_i^{index(1)},\dots,r_i^{index(K_i)}\}\), where each score is attached to a step-ending token index index(j). All step scores across the group are collected into

\[
\mathbf{R}=\{\{r_1^{index(1)},\dots\},\dots,\{r_G^{index(1)},\dots\}\},
\]

and <|endoftext|> by the global mean and standard deviation across the entire collection of step rewards:

\[
\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} = \frac{r_i^{index(j)} - <|endoftext|>
\]

Process supervision converts these <|endoftext|> per-step rewards into <|endoftext|> advantages by attributing future step rewards to earlier tokens. Specifically, the advantage for a token at position t in output i is computed as the sum of all <|endoftext|> step rewards that occur at or after t:

\[
<|endoftext|> = \sum_{index(j)\ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.
\]

Thus tokens that precede many positively-scored steps receive larger cumulative advantages, and tokens after a final good step receive only the residual advantage of subsequent steps (if any). These <|endoftext|> advantages are then plugged into the <|endoftext|> surrogate objective in place of the outcome-supervision constant advantages.

Why this matters. Process supervision produces fine-grained, temporally-aware learning signals that can better guide multi-step chain-of-thought style reasoning. The paper reports that <|endoftext|> combined with process supervision (GRPO+PS) outperforms <|endoftext|> with outcome supervision (GRPO+OS), showing the value of step-aware rewards for complex mathematical reasoning. From an algorithmic viewpoint, process supervision gives different <|endoftext|> coefficients for different tokens depending on the part of the reasoning they support, which can more precisely raise the probability of good intermediate steps and lower it for harmful ones.

# Group size and sampling strategy <|endoftext|> G=64) and its effects

Group size G is central to GRPO’s design because the group average and group standard deviation are the <|endoftext|> used to normalize rewards. The paper’s experiments used G=64 (they sampled 64 outputs per question) and batch size 1024, with max sequence length 1024 during RL. The choice of a large group serves several purposes:

- Baseline stability. A larger G produces more stable estimates of mean and standard deviation across outputs for the same prompt, which reduces variance in the <|endoftext|> advantages <|endoftext|> Stable <|endoftext|> helps the policy receive meaningful comparative signals instead of noisy signs from small groups.

- Comparative alignment. Reward models are often trained on pairwise preferences; normalizing within a reasonably large sample respects the comparative structure of those reward signals and yields advantages consistent with relative quality.

- Critic-free variance reduction. Using group statistics substitutes for a learned value function by providing a sample-based baseline across alternatives. When G is large enough the sample baseline performs the <|endoftext|> role with much lower memory cost.

Trade-offs and practical implications. Larger G increases sampling and <|endoftext|> cost for each prompt because the policy must generate and the reward model must score many outputs. This raises compute and storage demands for exploration. Smaller G reduces cost but yields noisier <|endoftext|> and possibly inferior training stability. The authors chose G=64 as a practical working point that yielded strong performance; practitioners should balance compute budget and baseline variance when selecting G.

Sampling strategy. The paper samples outputs from the current old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) (online sampling) rather than relying only on outputs sampled from the fixed SFT model (offline). The experiments show online sampling leads to better performance than offline-style methods because the sampled distribution better reflects the policy that is being updated. For each exploration stage the <|endoftext|> pipeline samples G outputs per question (the group), computes rewards for them, computes group-normalized advantages, then applies the surrogate updates.

# Iterative RL loop: replay buffer, reference model update, reward model continual training

<|endoftext|> is implemented in an iterative RL loop that jointly evolves the policy (actor) and the reward model. The <|endoftext|> structure used in the paper is given in Algorithm 1 and can be summarized in these repeating phases:

1. Reference and old policy setup. At the start of an outer iteration the procedure sets the reference policy \(\pi_{ref}\leftarrow\pi_\theta\). During the inner steps the current policy is used for exploration and then temporarily regarded as \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) while the policy receives <|endoftext|> updates.

2. Exploration (online sampling). For each sampled batch of prompts, the algorithm draws G outputs per prompt from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and computes rewards for these outputs using the current reward model r_\varphi.

3. Advantage computation. Depending on the supervision mode (outcome or process), compute <|endoftext|> rewards and <|endoftext|> advantages <|endoftext|> as described earlier.

4. Policy update. Use the <|endoftext|> surrogate objective with <|endoftext|> and the <|endoftext|> regularizer towards \(\pi_{ref}\) to update the policy. The algorithm allows running \(\mu\) <|endoftext|> optimization steps per exploration batch; in the reported experiments the policy received a single update following each exploration stage (i.e., \(\mu=1\)).

5. Reward-model training with replay. After a series of inner steps, the reward model is updated using newly collected comparison data. Critically, the reward-model training uses a replay mechanism that includes a fraction of historical data — the paper reports keeping 10% historical examples in the replay buffer. This stabilizes reward-model updates and avoids catastrophic skewing from the most recent policy outputs.

6. Iterate. Repeat exploration with the updated reward model and policy, resetting \(\pi_{ref}\) to the current policy at the start of each outer iteration.

Why iterative RL and replay matter. As the policy improves, the distribution of candidate outputs <|endoftext|> a reward model trained only on old data may become misaligned. Iterating with periodic retraining of the reward model on fresh outputs (and a historical fraction) keeps the reward signal relevant and mitigates drift. The 10% historical-replay strategy trades off freshness and stability. The authors also observed that performing two iterations of this iterative RL yields noticeable gains, particularly in the first iteration.

# Engineering tips: <|endoftext|> context length, update frequency

The paper reports concrete engineering choices that worked in their <|endoftext|> experiments and are useful starting points:

- Group size and sampling: G = 64 outputs sampled per question was used in their main runs. Expect the per-prompt compute cost to scale linearly with G.

- Max sequence length: RL runs used max length 1024 tokens for sampled outputs and reward computation. Make sure your <|endoftext|> and reward scoring pipelines handle this context length efficiently.

- Batch size and parallelism: Training batch size for policy updates was 1024 (number of prompts per batch). Combined with G=64, this implies heavy parallel <|endoftext|> and reward scoring, so use distributed or batched <|endoftext|> for both policy and reward models.

- Learning rates: The reported learning rates were small and conservative: policy learning rate \(1\times 10^{-6}\) and reward-model learning rate <|endoftext|> 10^{-5}\). Small policy LR helps prevent destructive updates when each batch’s <|endoftext|> advantages can be large.

- <|endoftext|> coefficient and <|endoftext|> The paper used a <|endoftext|> coefficient \(\beta\) of 0.04 and <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> with parameter \(\varepsilon\). Tune these so that the policy improves while remaining close to \(\pi_{ref}\). If the policy diverges rapidly, raise \(\beta\) or lower LR; if improvement stalls, consider lowering \(\beta\) or increasing \(\varepsilon\) slightly.

- Policy update frequency (\(\mu\)): The algorithm supports multiple inner <|endoftext|> steps per exploration batch. The authors used a single policy update per exploration stage (i.e., \(\mu=1\)), which avoids repeatedly optimizing against the same sampled set of outputs and reduces <|endoftext|> to a particular batch. If you increase \(\mu\), consider refreshing sampled outputs more often.

- Reward-model replay buffer: Maintain a replay buffer and include 10% historical data when retraining reward models. This prevents the reward model from fitting only on the most recent (possibly narrow) policy outputs.

- Compute architecture: Because exploration requires generating many outputs and scoring them, organize <|endoftext|> to produce candidate outputs in large parallel batches and score them with the reward model in a vectorized manner. This both reduces wall-clock time and stabilizes memory usage.

- Single update design: The reported pipeline sampled G outputs and then performed a single policy update. This design, together with direct group-baseline advantages, makes <|endoftext|> far less memory-intensive than PPO (which needs a learned <|endoftext|> while retaining effective variance reduction.

# Putting it together — practical workflow summary

A practical <|endoftext|> workflow, reflecting the paper’s choices, is:

1. Initialize policy from SFT model and initialize reward model (trained offline from comparisons derived from SFT outputs).

2. For outer iterations:
   a. Set \(\pi_{ref}\leftarrow\pi_\theta\).
   b. For mini-batches of prompts (batch size 1024):
      i. Sample G = 64 outputs per prompt from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
      ii. Score each output (and/or each reasoning step) with reward model r_\varphi.
      iii. Normalize rewards across the group (outcome: across outputs; process: across all step rewards in group).
      iv. Compute <|endoftext|> advantages <|endoftext|> (outcome: broadcast <|endoftext|> output reward; process: cumulative future-step sum).
      v. Run \(\mu\) <|endoftext|> updates (typical \(\mu=1\)) on the policy using the clipped surrogate and direct <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> with \(\beta\).
   c. Retrain reward model with new comparison data, using replay that retains 10% historical data.

3. Repeat for a small number of iterations (the paper reports iterative RL yields strong gains in the first iteration).
