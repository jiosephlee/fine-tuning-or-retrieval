In the paper “DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models”, in everyday terms, GRPO’s group-relative baseline most resembles the classroom grading scheme called grading on a curve. GRPO replaces PPO’s learned value function baseline with a cohort-based baseline: for each question, it samples a group of outputs, scores them, and then centers and scales those scores relative to the group before turning them into advantages. Concretely, for a group of rewards $\mathbf{r}=\{r_1,\ldots,r_G\}$, GRPO computes normalized, group-relative rewards
$\widetilde{r}_i = \dfrac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$ and sets token-level advantages to $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$ (outcome supervision) or to sums of step-level normalized rewards (process supervision). This is exactly the logic of “grading on a curve”: one’s score is interpreted relative to the cohort’s mean and variability, so being above the mean yields positive reinforcement (like a higher letter grade) and being below the mean yields negative reinforcement (like a lower letter grade), regardless of the absolute raw score.

Moreover, GRPO explicitly uses the group average as the baseline in its advantage computation, mirroring how curved grading treats the class average as the reference point. In PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is computed via a learned value function $V_\psi$ (an individualized baseline), whereas in GRPO the baseline is the empirical group mean for the same prompt, and the training signal is scaled by the group standard deviation to modulate how strongly outliers are rewarded or penalized. Because the objective
$\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}$ optimizes per-token updates with $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ that are positive if $r_i > \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})$ and negative if $r_i < \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})$, it embodies a relative-performance scheme: the model is pushed to produce outputs that are better than its own current cohort on the same question, just as curved grading pushes students’ evaluations to reflect where they stand relative to their peers.
