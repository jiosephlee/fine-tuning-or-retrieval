According to the paper 'DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models', under outcome supervision the normalized reward is defined as $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})}$; if all group rewards are identical, the normalization leads to division by zero. This follows directly from their GRPO outcome supervision scheme: for a given question $q$, they sample a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G$, score them with a reward model to obtain $\mathbf{r}=\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}$, and then apply z-score normalization within the group. By definition, $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{r}))^2}$. If all $r_i$ are identical, then each term inside the sum is zero, so $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r}) = 0$, making $\widetilde{r}_i$ undefined due to division by zero. This is the standard degeneracy of z-score normalization when the sample variance vanishes.

Moreover, the reason for using group-relative normalization is to produce advantages that reflect comparative quality among the sampled outputs, aligning with reward models trained on pairwise or groupwise preferences. When the rewards have no variation, there is no comparative signal: every output is scored equally, so a relative advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ based on $\widetilde{r}_i$ becomes ill-posed. In GRPO, these normalized rewards are propagated as per-token advantages ($\hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i$ for outcome supervision), so a zero standard deviation implies the policy receives no informative gradient direction from the reward model. While in practice one can mitigate this by ensuring $G$ is large (they use $G=64$) and by using sufficiently sensitive reward models so that $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})>0$ almost always, the mathematical edge case of identical rewards necessarily yields $\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{r})=0$ and hence a division by zero under the stated normalization.
