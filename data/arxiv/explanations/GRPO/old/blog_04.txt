# Small Models, Big Math: How a 7B LLM Reached Competition-Level Accuracy

Mathematical reasoning has long been one of the most demanding frontiers for large language models (LLMs). Cutting-edge systems like GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra can solve tricky competition problems, but they remain behind closed doors. What if an open-source model with just 7 billion parameters could get nearly as good? Enter DeepSeekMath 7B—a compact LLM that harnesses smart data curation, specialized instruction tuning, and a lean reinforcement-learning trick to score over 50 percent on the notoriously hard MATH benchmark, all without leaning on external toolkits or expensive tricks.

## Pre-Training on High-Quality Web Data

The first secret to DeepSeekMath’s success is gathering the right pre-training material. Rather than simply downloading every piece of text on the web, the authors built an iterative pipeline to mine 120 billion truly math-rich tokens from Common Crawl. Here’s how it works, at a high level:

1. **Seed with trusted math pages**: They started with OpenWebMath, a small but top-notch collection of math web pages.  
2. **FastText classifier**: Using fastText embeddings, they trained a lightweight model to spot “mathy” pages in the 40 billion-page Common Crawl.  
3. **Iterative expansion**: After one round of mining, they examined which domains contributed most of the math data, manually labeled new examples from those domains, and retrained the classifier. Four iterations later, they harvested 35.5 million math pages covering English, Chinese, and more.  
4. **Decontamination**: Any pages matching 10-gram snippets from evaluation benchmarks (like MATH or GSM8K) were removed to ensure a fair test.

By focusing on breadth (120 B tokens versus 13 B in OpenWebMath or 8.9 B in MathPile) and multilingual content, the resulting DeepSeekMath Corpus turned out to be both large and high-quality. A smaller 1.3 B model pre-trained on this data outperformed counterparts trained on competing corpora.

## Instruction Tuning with Chain-of-Thought and Program-of-Thought

Once the model had absorbed all that math text, it needed guidance on how to explain its reasoning step by step. The team assembled 776 000 examples of math problems in three flavors:

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)**: A free-form explanation, walking through algebra, number theory, or geometry in prose.  
- **Program-of-Thought (PoT)**: A Python snippet that computes the answer, making heavy use of libraries like `math` or `sympy`.  
- **Tool-Integrated Reasoning**: A hybrid approach where natural-language reasoning is interleaved with program calls.

Fine-tuning DeepSeekMath 7B on this mix of English and Chinese problems helped it internalize both the art of explaining steps and the muscle of writing code to compute answers.

## Lightweight Reinforcement Learning: Group Relative Policy Optimization

Supervised fine-tuning can only go so far. To push accuracy even higher, many teams have turned to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), but PPO requires a separate “critic” network to estimate values—doubling memory and compute. DeepSeekMath introduces a twist called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, which eliminates the critic altogether.

Here’s GRPO in a nutshell:

- For each question, sample a group of $G$ answers from the current policy.  
- Score each answer with a learned reward model.  
- Compute each answer’s advantage by comparing it to the group mean (and normalizing by the group standard deviation).  
- Update the policy using a clipped objective, just like PPO, but regularize directly with a KL term instead of embedding it in the reward.

Because GRPO uses only policy networks and a batch of samples to estimate baselines, it slashes memory costs. Moreover, it supports both **outcome supervision** (a single score per answer) and **process supervision** (stepwise rewards for each reasoning chunk), allowing fine-grained control over how the model learns to justify each step.

## Impressive Results: Near-GPT-4 Performance

After pre-training, instruction tuning, and GRPO, DeepSeekMath 7B hits:

- 51.7 percent on MATH with single-sample chain-of-thought prompting (up from 46.8 percent before RL).  
- 60.9 percent on MATH using self-consistency over 64 samples.  
- 88.2 percent on GSM8K with chain-of-thought reasoning.  

These scores rival those of proprietary giants like Gemini Ultra and even approach GPT-4’s benchmarks—remarkable for a model that fits on a handful of GPUs and never drops out to external calculators or vote-ensembles.

## Democratizing Advanced Reasoning

What does this mean for the broader community? First, you don’t need a 100 billion-parameter behemoth behind closed doors to tackle tough math. A smartly trained 7 B model can get you most of the way there. That opens up possibilities for researchers, educators, and hobbyists to run competitive mathematical reasoning locally or in the cloud at a fraction of the cost.

## Lessons Learned and Future Directions

DeepSeekMath’s journey also highlights a handful of practical takeaways:

- **Code training helps math reasoning**: Models warmed up on GitHub code solve math with and without tools more effectively.  
- **ArXiv papers alone aren’t magic**: Surprisingly, feeding millions of arXiv tokens by themselves didn’t boost reasoning, possibly due to noise in raw LaTeX dumps.  
- **A unified view of RL methods**: SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO can all be cast as variants of policy gradient methods differing in data source, reward signal, and gradient coefficient.  
- **Room for a stronger reward model**: Building more robust, uncertainty-aware reward models could make RL even more reliable.  
- **Advanced sampling**: Pairing GRPO with tree-search or speculative decoding might further amplify gains.

Looking ahead, refining the reward functions, exploring out-of-distribution problem sets, and scaling the approach to richer geometry or proof assistants could close the remaining gap to GPT-4.

## Conclusion

DeepSeekMath 7B is a compelling proof of concept: with targeted data selection, careful instruction tuning, and a lean reinfor­cement-learning algorithm, small open-source models can punch well above their weight in competition-level math. By making advanced reasoning more accessible, this work paves the way for a new generation of democratized AI tutors, research partners, and problem solvers—no 100 billion-parameter budget required.
