# Sampling for Stronger Reasoning: Self-Consistency, Maj@K, and Decoding Tricks

When it comes to solving challenging math problems with language models, asking the model just once often isn’t enough. You might get a plausible chain of thought that ends up with a slip in arithmetic or a flawed logical step. A surprisingly simple trick fixes this: ask the model to “think” several times, then aggregate its answers. In this post, we’ll unpack why sampling multiple chains of reasoning can dramatically boost correctness on benchmarks like MATH, introduce the notions of Pass@K and Maj@K, and offer practical decoding‐strategy tips to help you balance accuracy, cost, and latency.

## Why Multiple Samples Matter

Imagine you ask a language model a complex algebra problem. With a single sample (the default), you get one chain of thought and one final answer. If that sample missteps, you lose. But language models are inherently stochastic: subtle randomness can produce different chains on each run. Some of these will arrive at the right answer. By sampling $N$ chains-of-thought and then combining their outcomes, you:

  • Increase your chances that **at least one** chain is correct.  
  • Gain a sense of which answer the model “trusts” more (when many samples agree).  

This simple strategy—often called **self‐consistency**—turns out to be a powerful lever on math benchmarks.

## Self-Consistency in Action

Self‐consistency works like this:

1. **Sample** $N$ chains of thought from the same prompt (by using a nonzero temperature or top-$p$ decoding).  
2. **Extract** the final answer from each chain.  
3. **Vote** or otherwise aggregate those answers to decide on a single output.  

In the DeepSeekMath paper, running self‐consistency with $N=64$ samples bumped the model’s score on the competition‐level MATH benchmark from about 51.7% to 60.9%—a nearly 9‐point gain without any extra model training or tool use. That jump shows how much untapped potential lives in sampling diversity.

## Pass@K vs. Maj@K: Two Ways to Measure Success

When sampling multiple outputs, you need a metric that reflects how you aggregate votes. Two popular measures are **Pass@K** and **Maj@K**:

  • **Pass@K** asks: “Is **any** one of the $K$ sampled answers correct?” Formally,  
    $$\text{Pass@}K = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{K} \bigl(1 - p_i\bigr)$$  
    where $p_i$ is the probability that the $i$-th sample is correct.  
  • **Maj@K** asks: “Does a **majority** of the $K$ answers agree on the correct solution?” In practice, you take the mode of the $K$ outputs and check if it matches the true answer slot.  

Pass@K is great for applications where you just need one correct attempt—say, in an interactive tutoring system where you can retry. Maj@K (essentially self‐consistency) is a stricter bar: it only succeeds when multiple chains converge on the same correct solution, giving you higher confidence but requiring more agreement.

In the DeepSeekMath study, reinforcement learning (RL) fine‐tuning improved Maj@K substantially without a clear lift in Pass@K. That suggests RL makes the model's top answers more **robust** (less jittery) rather than simply boosting the raw chance of a single correct sample.

## Decoding Tricks and Trade-Offs

Sampling more chains isn’t free: each extra sample adds compute cost and latency. So how do you tune your decoding strategy?

Temperature and Top-$p$  
  • **Temperature** ($T$) controls randomness. A small $T$ (e.g. 0.3) keeps samples focused; a larger $T$ (e.g. 0.8) increases diversity. For math, a moderate value like $T=0.7$ often hits a good balance.  
  • **Top-$p$** sampling restricts each token choice to the top cumulative probability mass. In practice, $p=0.9$ or $0.95$ helps avoid long‐tail nonsense while retaining enough variety to find the correct chain.

Number of Samples ($N$)  
  • Gains from self‐consistency exhibit **diminishing returns**. You might see a 7–8% jump going from $N=1$ to $N=16$, then a smaller bump from $N=16$ to $N=64$.  
  • If you’re latency-sensitive, start with $N=8$ or $16$ and measure. You’ll often capture most of the accuracy gain long before $N=64$.

Parallel vs. Sequential  
  • **Parallel sampling** on GPU/TPU lets you generate all $N$ chains at once, capping wall-clock time.  
  • **Sequential sampling** (on CPU or with limited batch size) means latency grows linearly with $N$. In those settings, you really want to keep $N$ small.

Beam Search + Sampling Hybrid  
  • You can first run a small beam of size $B$ (e.g. $B=3$) to get diverse hypotheses, then apply stochastic sampling on each beam to refine reasoning paths.  
  • This hybrid can outperform pure sampling when you want to “seed” exploration with a few strong partial chains.

## Practical Tips

1. **Measure Both Metrics**: Track Pass@K for your retry budget and Maj@K for your one‐shot confidence.  
2. **Tune Temperature and Top-$p$ Together**: A higher $T$ but lower $p$ can yield cleaner diversity than tweaking one alone.  
3. **Profile Latency**: If you max out your inference budget at 200 ms per prompt, find the largest $N$ that keeps you under that cap.  
4. **Use RL-Fine‐Tuned Models**: Models like DeepSeekMath-RL 7B are already biased towards agreement on correct solutions, so they can deliver stronger Maj@K gains at lower $N$.  
5. **Monitor Diminishing Returns**: Plot accuracy vs. $N$ and look for the “elbow.” That knee is your sweet spot.

## Conclusion

Sampling multiple chains of thought is one of the most cost-effective levers to improve a model’s mathematical accuracy. By embracing self‐consistency and understanding Pass@K versus Maj@K, you can tune your decoding strategy to hit target accuracy with minimum cost and latency. Whether you’re building an AI tutor, a homework-help chatbot, or a research‐grade problem solver, these tricks will help you squeeze every drop of reasoning power out of your model.
