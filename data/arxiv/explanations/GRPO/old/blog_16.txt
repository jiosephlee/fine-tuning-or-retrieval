# What’s Next for RL in Reasoning LLMs: Process Rewards, Iteration, and Weak-to-Strong

Large language models have made remarkable strides in mathematical reasoning by combining supervised fine-tuning with reinforcement learning (RL). Yet as we’ve seen in the DeepSeekMath project, there is still considerable room to refine how RL shapes a model’s internal reasoning and output behavior. In this post, we explore three promising directions for the next wave of research: process versus outcome rewards, iterative RL with replay, and embracing weak-to-strong alignment to cope with noisy supervision.

## Process Rewards vs. Outcome Rewards

Traditional RL in reasoning tasks often focuses on an outcome-only feedback signal: you solve the problem, you get a reward. However, mathematical problem-solving naturally decomposes into intermediate steps—line by line derivations, sketch proofs, or code snippets. Process rewards aim to leverage that structure by providing feedback at each reasoning step rather than only at the end. 

Outcome supervision gives every token in a solution the same normalized score based on whether the final answer is correct. By contrast, process supervision scores each reasoning step—say, each lemma in a proof or each iteration in a multi-step calculation—and then propagates those intermediate rewards back through the token sequence. Empirically, DeepSeekMath’s experiments showed that process-based RL (GRPO+PS) outperforms outcome-only RL (GRPO+OS), likely because it encourages the model to focus on sound reasoning patterns early in its chain of thought. Future research could explore richer process signals—automatically detecting subgoals, verifying partial proofs with external assistants, or even assigning negative feedback to known dead ends.

## Iterative RL with Replay

Another clear lesson from DeepSeekMath is that the quality of the reward model can lag behind the improving policy. If you keep punishing and rewarding the model with a stale critic, you might reinforce spurious artifacts or miss new failure modes. Iterative RL with replay closes that loop. In each round, you:

1. Sample new outputs from the current policy.
2. Fine-tune the reward model on both fresh comparisons and a fraction of past data.
3. Use the updated critic to RL-fine-tune the policy again.

This iterative procedure raised the benchmark scores significantly, especially in the first couple of rounds. It suggests that continual co-training of the policy and reward model can prevent the critic from becoming obsolete, and can surface new areas for the policy to improve. Going forward, we expect more efficient replay strategies (e.g., reservoir sampling, prioritized experience replay) and mechanisms to track the reward model’s confidence over time.

## Reward Model Challenges: Generalization and Uncertainty

Any RL pipeline is only as good as its reward signal. Yet reward models trained on a finite set of comparison data can overfit to familiar problem-styles and collapse on out-of-distribution queries or novel decoding patterns. Two urgent challenges emerge:

1. **Generalization to Unseen Prompts and Decodings.** We need reward models that hold up when the policy generates reasoning behaviors that were never seen during training. This might involve mixing in synthetic data, unsupervised contrastive objectives, or adversarial perturbations at decoding time.

2. **Quantifying Uncertainty.** A well-trained reward model should know when it doesn’t know. By modeling its own predictive uncertainty—whether via Bayesian neural networks, ensembles, or other techniques—we can temper RL updates when the critic is unsure, avoiding over-confident pushes in the wrong direction.

Addressing these challenges can help RL become a tool for genuine capability improvement rather than just distributional fine-tuning.

## Towards Weak-to-Strong Alignment

Finally, we must face the reality that reward models—even when carefully annotated—contain noise. In DeepSeekMath, annotators still disagreed or made errors on roughly 20% of comparison judgments. If an RL algorithm blindly trusts every reward, it risks amplifying annotation artifacts. Weak-to-strong alignment offers a path forward: begin with coarse, noisy signals and gradually refine both the critic and the policy as higher-quality feedback becomes available. Concretely, this could look like:

- **Stage 1 (Weak Signals):** Use heuristic judgments or low-cost crowdsourcing to bootstrap the policy.
- **Stage 2 (Moderate Signals):** Introduce a small set of expert-verified comparisons or programmatic verifiers for key subcases.
- **Stage 3 (Strong Signals):** Expand the high-quality validation set, using formal proof assistants or domain experts to certify model outputs.

By progressively strengthening the feedback loop, we can smooth the transition from “RL as distribution stabilizer” to “RL as genuine problem-solver.”

## Conclusion

Reinforcement learning has already shown its power to push reasoning LLMs to new heights. Yet the next breakthroughs will come from richer feedback signals—rewarding intermediate logic steps, continually refreshing our reward models, and carefully managing noise in supervision. By combining process rewards, iterative replay training, and weak-to-strong alignment, researchers can build the next generation of LLM reasoners that not only squirt out correct answers, but truly internalize robust mathematical reasoning skills. The journey continues, and these emerging directions offer a roadmap for what’s next.
