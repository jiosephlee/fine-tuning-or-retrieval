# Formal Math Meets LLMs: Auto-Formalization with Isabelle and miniF2F

Mathematics has always had two faces. On one side, there’s the familiar informal style you learn in school—the hand-wavy arguments, diagrams, and narrative that guide you through a solution. On the other side, there’s formal mathematics: the austere, precise world of symbols and logic that proof assistants like Isabelle demand. Bridging those two worlds—turning an “explain like I’m five” proof into something a computer can verify—has been a long-standing dream. Thanks to recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and automated proof tools, that dream is becoming reality. In this post, we’ll walk through how modern LLMs can help you go from an informal proof sketch to a fully formalized Isabelle proof, using the miniF2F benchmark and the Sledgehammer automation tool.

## Why “Formal” Math?

When we write a proof on paper, we rely on shared intuition. We gloss over trivial steps, assume readers know how to expand an algebraic expression, or cite well-known theorems without detail. A proof assistant, by contrast, requires every logical inference to be spelled out in a rigid, machine-checkable language. That level of rigor brings important benefits:

- **Unambiguous correctness**. If Isabelle accepts your proof, you know every step follows logically, leaving no room for hidden gaps or human oversight.  
- **Reusability**. Formal proofs can be composed, refactored, and even mined to build larger libraries of verified results.  
- **Automation**. Once your theorem is in a proof assistant, automated tactics can often discharge routine cases at lightning speed.

But the downside is clear: writing a formal proof by hand can be extremely tedious and requires a deep understanding not only of the mathematics but also of the proof assistant’s language.

## The Informal-to-Formal Translation Challenge

Informal proofs read like great storytelling. A typical geometry solution might say:

> “Draw the altitude from the apex to the base. Notice that the two right triangles share an acute angle, so they are similar by AA. Therefore the ratios of corresponding sides are equal, hence …”

A proof assistant demands something like:

```isabelle
theorem triangle_similarity:
  assumes "angle A = angle C" and "angle B = angle D"
  shows "similar ΔABC ΔCDA"
proof -
  have "AA_similarity A B C C D A" using assms by ...
  thus ?thesis .
qed
```

Every inference—Angle‐Angle similarity, construction of an altitude, ratio arguments—must be encoded in Isabelle’s libraries and tactics. That translation is the heart of auto-formalization.

## The LLM + Proof Assistant Workflow

Recent work has shown that LLMs, trained extensively on both natural language and formal proof corpora, can generate good first drafts of formal proofs. Here’s a typical pipeline:

1. **Few-shot prompting**  
   You feed the LLM a handful of examples, each containing:
   - An informal statement (“Prove that in any triangle, the altitudes from two vertices are concurrent.”)  
   - Its formal Isabelle statement and proof sketch.  

2. **Proof sketch generation**  
   Given a new informal problem from a benchmark like miniF2F, the LLM produces an Isabelle proof “sketch.” This sketch typically outlines tactic calls without filling in every low-level detail.

3. **Automated filling with Sledgehammer**  
   Isabelle’s Sledgehammer tool takes the proof sketch and tries to fill in the missing inferences by calling external automated theorem provers under the hood. If it succeeds, you end up with a fully verified proof.

4. **Checking and iteration**  
   If Sledgehammer can’t close a goal, you might refine the sketch, add relevant lemmas, or adjust tactics. But often the LLM plus Sledgehammer combo nails the bulk of routine reasoning.

## miniF2F and Isabelle: The Proving Ground

miniF2F is a benchmark of Olympiad-style problems rendered in informal English. It selects challenging exercises that require genuine ingenuity. On the formal side, Isabelle/HOL serves as our proof assistant of choice—one of the most mature systems with a rich library of mathematics.

In recent experiments, a 7-billion parameter LLM (DeepSeekMath-Base 7B) was prompted in this workflow. For each miniF2F problem, the model generated a formal statement in Isabelle, followed by a sketch that invoked tactics like `induction`, `simp`, or `auto`.

After sketch generation, Sledgehammer was unleashed to fill in the logical gaps. Impressively, this combination achieved strong few-shot autoformalization performance on miniF2F. That means many of these Olympiad problems, once considered out of reach for automation, can now be turned into fully machine-verified proofs with minimal human intervention.

## A Closer Look at Proof Sketches

Proof sketches are the bridge between human reasoning and machine rigor. They usually follow this pattern:

- **State the theorem** in Isabelle syntax, using `theorem` or `lemma`.  
- **Begin a `proof` block**, often hinting at which method to use (`induction` for natural numbers, `cases` for datatypes, etc.).  
- **Invoke high-level tactics** like `simp` (simplification), `auto` (automation), or domain-specific tactics for linear arithmetic.  
- **Mark unfinished subgoals** with placeholders or `sorry` if necessary.  

For example:

```isabelle
lemma sum_square:
  shows "(\<Sum>i < n. i^2) = n*(n - 1)*(2*n - 1) div 6"
proof (induct n)
  case 0 show ?case by simp
next
  case (Suc k)
  have "(\<Sum>i < Suc k. i^2) = (\<Sum>i < k. i^2) + k^2" by simp
  also have "... = k*(k - 1)*(2*k - 1) div 6 + k^2"
    using Suc.IH by simp
  finally show ?case by simp
qed
```

Here, the LLM outlines an induction proof and leaves the heavy lifting (`auto`/`simp`) to the assistant.

## What the Results Tell Us

The fact that a relatively compact 7B-parameter model can auto-formalize a broad selection of Olympiad problems is a major milestone. It suggests:

- LLMs have internalized not just natural language but substantial formal proof patterns.  
- High-level proof structuring (“by induction”, “apply simp”) can be generated reliably with few examples.  
- Integrating LLMs with existing proof automation (Sledgehammer) leverages the best of both worlds: creative strategy from the model plus brute-force search from provers.

## Looking Ahead

Auto-formalization is still in its early days. Current systems may struggle with highly specialized domains or very deep proofs, and human oversight remains essential. But as LLMs become more capable and proof libraries grow, we can imagine a future where:

- **Mathematics papers ship with formal artifacts** that readers can check and build upon automatically.  
- **Interactive theorem proving** becomes more accessible to students and researchers who lack expert knowledge of proof assistant syntax.  
- **Large-scale formal libraries** accelerate new discoveries by ensuring definitions and proofs remain consistent across fields.

By bringing informal mathematical reasoning and formal verification closer together, LLMs and tools like Isabelle/Sledgehammer are reshaping how we do mathematics. Whether you’re a curious student or a seasoned researcher, now is an exciting time to explore the frontier of auto-formalization.
