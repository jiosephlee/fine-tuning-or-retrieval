### How does GRPO compare to DPO and RFT under the unified gradient paradigm?
Question:
Eq. (4) frames SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO via a common gradient form with a 'Gradient Coefficient' $GC_{\mathcal A}$. Could someone explicitly write out $GC$ for DPO and GRPO side-by-side and explain how GRPO's group-relative normalization and online sampling change the effective learning signal compared to DPO's pairwise preference loss? In what regimes should one expect DPO to outperform GRPO, or vice versa?
Answer:
In the “unified gradient” view of eq. (4) in the paper, every fine-tuning or policy-gradient method can be written as  
$$
\nabla_{\theta}J_{\mathcal A}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal D}\Biggl[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
  GC_{\mathcal A}(q,o,t)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\Biggr],
$$  
where all of the “magic” of RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO, etc., is hidden in the choice of the Gradient Coefficient $GC_{\mathcal A}$, the data source $\mathcal D$, and—where appropriate—the reference or reward policy $\pi_{\mathrm{rf}}$.  Below we write out $GC$ for DPO and for GRPO side by side, then discuss how GRPO’s group-relative normalization and online sampling change the effective learning signal.

1) Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  
DPO assumes you have a static preference dataset of triples $(q,o^+,o^-)$ collected (usually) by sampling both $o^+$ and $o^-$ from the frozen SFT model $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and asking a human or reward model which one is better.  One then minimizes the logistic pairwise loss  
$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{(q,o^+,o^-)}\Bigl[-\log\sigma\bigl(\beta\,(s_\theta(o^+;q)-s_\theta(o^-;q))\bigr)\Bigr]
$$
where 
$$
s_\theta(o;q)=\log\pi_\theta(o\mid q)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(o\mid q)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.
$$ 
Differentiating under the policy-gradient identity  
$$
\nabla_\theta s_\theta(o;q)
=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})
$$ 
gives a token-wise coefficient
$$
\begin{aligned}
GC_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q,o^+,t)
&= +\,\beta\,\sigma\!\bigl(-\beta\,(s_\theta(o^+;q)-s_\theta(o^-;q))\bigr),\\
GC_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q,o^-,t)
&= -\,\beta\,\sigma\!\bigl(-\beta\,(s_\theta(o^+;q)-s_\theta(o^-;q))\bigr),
\end{aligned}
$$
and zero elsewhere.  Note that:

  – The same constant $\beta\,\sigma(\cdot)$ is applied to every token of the winning sequence $o^+$, and its negation to every token of the losing one $o^-$.  
  – There is no *online* resampling of $o^\pm$; DPO uses an *offline* pairwise dataset drawn from the fixed $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

2) Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)  
GRPO, by contrast, is an *online* RL method with no separate critic.  At each update you sample a *group* of $G$ completions $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid q)$, score each with a reward model $r_\varphi(q,o_i)$, and form group-relative normalized advantages.  Under *outcome supervision* (single reward at the end) one computes
$$
\mu \;=\;\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G r_i,\qquad
\sigma \;=\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G(r_i-\mu)^2},\qquad
\widetilde r_i=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma}\,,
$$
and then—for every token $t$ in completion $o_i$—sets
$$
GC_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(q,o_i,t)
=\widetilde r_i
=\frac{r_i-\mu}{\sigma}.
$$
Under *process supervision* (rewards at multiple reasoning steps) one simply accumulates the *future* normalized step-rewards in each partial prefix; formally
$$
GC_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(q,o_i,t)
=\sum_{\substack{\text{step }j\\\mathrm{index}(j)\ge t}}
\frac{r_i^{\mathrm{step}(j)}-\mu}{\sigma},
$$
where $r_i^{\mathrm{step}(j)}$ is the $j$th step’s raw score and $\mu,\sigma$ are computed over *all* steps in the group.  Finally, GRPO wraps this in the usual PPO-style clipped surrogate and adds a direct $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ regularizer.

  • Because $\sum_i\widetilde r_i=0$, the group-relative normalization *centers* your gradient so that above–average answers are reinforced and below–average ones are suppressed, automatically rescaled to unit variance.  
  • Sampling answers *online* from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ rather than replaying a static offline set ensures you track the policy’s evolving distribution.  
  • No separate value-network is needed: the group mean plays the role of a baseline.

Regime-wise trade-offs  
– If you have a *static*, high-quality preference dataset (e.g.\ human-labeled pairwise judgments) and want a very stable, low-variance update without running a large reward model at each step, then DPO’s simple pairwise logistic loss can be preferable.  It uses no online sampling and no group-normalization, so it behaves more like supervised fine-tuning on preferences.  

– If you have an *auto-trained* scalar reward model, you can afford to sample *many* candidates per query and you want your updates to adapt as the policy drifts, then GRPO’s online, group-relative advantage will typically give a richer, more finely graded learning signal—pushing up the best-of-group answers and pulling down the worst.  It also sidesteps the memory/computation cost of training a separate critic.

In practice one often finds that DPO is most robust when reward signals are sparse but very reliable (e.g.\ trusted human preferences), whereas GRPO shines when your reward model produces continuous scores and you want to carve out *relative* gains among many sampled candidates.
