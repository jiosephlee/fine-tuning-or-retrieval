### How do you ensure fairness when comparing to Minerva 540B given different data and training budgets?
Question:
DeepSeekMath-Base 7B reportedly outperforms Minerva 540B on MATH. Since training corpora, data cleaning, tokenizers, and training budgets differ, how do the authors ensure the comparison isolates the effect of data curation and methods rather than confounds like dataset size, pretraining objective details, or evaluation protocols? Are any robustness checks performed (e.g., same few-shot prompts, same answer extraction rules)?
Answer:
In the paper, the authors are quite careful to make the comparison with Minerva 540 B as “clean” as possible, even though the two models differ greatly in scale, architecture and overall budget.  Broadly speaking they isolate the effect of their data pipeline and training tricks in two ways:

1)  A fixed-budget *corpus ablation* on a small “probe” model.  
   Before ever touching their $7$ B-parameter model, they take a $1.3$ B-parameter seed model (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3 B) and train it for *the exact same* $150$ B tokens on each of the four public math corpora (MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2, and DeepSeekMath).  Everything else — model code, hyperparameters, optimizer schedule — is held *identical*.  The only difference is the training data, so the large performance gap on MATH (and on Chinese benchmarks) can be unambiguously traced to corpus quality rather than to more compute, a bigger model, or a different training recipe (see § 2.2.2 and Figure 2).

2)  A *unified evaluation protocol* across all baselines on the final 7 B model.  
   When they report that DeepSeekMath-Base 7 B (with $500$ B pre-training tokens) surpasses Minerva 540 B on MATH, they do so *strictly* under the standard MATH benchmark settings: the same few-shot chain-of-thought template (8 exemplars, taken from the original MATH codebase), the same decoding hyperparameters (temperature, max length), and the official answer-extraction script that matches only the final numeric answer.  They explicitly *decontaminate* their pre-training corpus by removing any 10-gram overlap with the MATH test problems (§ 2.1), so there is no leakage.  They do not ensemble, vote or use external toolkits in that head-to-head comparison.  In the program-of-thought and formal-proof evaluations, they likewise use the exact same prompts, the same Python/Sledgehammer calls, and the same verify-and-score logic as prior work.

Taken together, these two design choices let them convincingly attribute the gains on MATH to (a) a carefully curated *$120$ B-token* DeepSeekMath corpus versus Minerva’s $\approx 16$ B math tokens, and (b) the downstream finetuning/GRPO recipe — rather than to accidental differences in model size, compute budget, tokenization, or evaluation code.
