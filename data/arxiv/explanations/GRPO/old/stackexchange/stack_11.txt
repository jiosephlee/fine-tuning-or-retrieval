### How was the fastText classifier evaluated and calibrated across iterations?
Question:
The data selection pipeline trains a fastText classifier using OpenWebMath positives and diverse negatives, then iteratively augments the positives with mined pages and human annotations. What metrics (e.g., ROC-AUC, precision-recall) guided the threshold selection? How was domain shift handled across Common Crawl snapshots? Were calibration techniques (e.g., Platt scaling) used to set score cutoffs for the top-40B/80B/120B/160B token experiments?
Answer:
In the DeepSeekMath paper the authors do not in fact report any fancy probability‐calibration or “$\text{ROC-AUC}=0.98$”–style analysis of the fastText classifier—instead, they treated it fundamentally as a high-recall ranking model whose numerical scores were only ever used to sort pages and slice off the top $N$ tokens they could afford. Here is what we can piece together from Section 2.1 (“Data Collection and Decontamination”):

1) No held-out AUC/PR curves. The fastText model is trained on 500 K seed positives (OpenWebMath) and 500 K negatives (random CC pages). They do not leave out a standard $10\%$ “validation” split to report ROC-AUC or average precision. Instead what they do is:

   • Score *all* deduplicated Common Crawl pages with the fastText model.  
   • Rank them by the sigmoid-like score (fastText’s built-in `predict_proba`).  
   • Pick the highest-scoring pages until they hit their token-budget target $(40B,80B,120B,160B,\ldots)$.

Because they’re not trying to hit a particular absolute precision target, they never explicitly translate that score into a calibrated probability cutoff. They simply say “give me the top 40 B tokens,” then “the top 80 B,” and so on, re-running their downstream pre-training probe to see where it starts to plateau.

2) Iterative seed augmentation to handle domain shift. After the first pass they observe that certain domains (e.g. mathoverflow.net) are overrepresented among the pages they recovered, so they manually inspect those domains’ URL structure, add uncollected URLs from those domains into the *positive* seed set, retrain the fastText model, and repeat. This has two effects:

   – It broadens the *distribution* of positive examples so that as the CC snapshot drifts over time you still recapture fresh math pages.  
   – It acts as a kind of *domain adaptation*—by exposing fastText to more diverse math sites, the newly trained classifier is less prone to “forget” under-represented patterns in later CC dumps.

3) No explicit Platt scaling or logistic calibration. They rely on the raw fastText scores purely as a ranking mechanism. The only real “calibration” is the manual, qualitative sanity check they perform at each slice: they look at a handful of sampled pages right below and right above the token-cutoff point to verify that the pages still “look mathematical.” If they see garbage, they will either tweak their manual URL seeds or terminate the iteration (in their case they stopped after iteration 4 when $98\%$ of the pages were already collected).

So in short, the pipeline never pivots on an ROC-AUC threshold or a Platt-scaled probability. It simply (a) ranks by raw fastText score, (b) slices off the top pages to fill their chosen token budgets, and (c) iteratively improves coverage of different sub-domains via human annotation of URLs. That implicit “precision check” is the only feedback loop on threshold quality, rather than a formal calibration step.
