### Why does DeepSeekMath underperform on geometry and theorem-proof tasks relative to closed models?
Question:
The limitations mention weaker performance on geometry and theorem proving (e.g., triangles, ellipses). Is this primarily a data coverage issue (fewer diagram-heavy or formal proof steps), modeling challenges (no spatial/diagram understanding), or reward model limitations? Did adding more geometry-focused web data or formal proofs from Isabelle/Lean training improve these specific domains?
Answer:
In “DeepSeekMath” we saw that, despite state-of-the-art performance on algebraic and numeric benchmarks, the model still lags behind closed-source giants in geometry and theorem-proof tasks—especially those involving Euclidean diagrams (triangles, ellipses) and formal proof assistants.  Reading between the lines of the paper, this gap is less about the GRPO algorithm or a broken reward model and more about two intertwined issues: the kind of data the model saw during training, and the fact that a purely text‐based transformer lacks any built-in spatial or diagrammatic reasoning machinery.

First, on data coverage.  DeepSeekMath’s 120 B math tokens were mined from Common Crawl via a fastText classifier seeded on OpenWebMath and similar Web pages.  This pipeline excels at picking up algebraic write-ups, code snippets, and prose proofs, but it systematically underrepresents content that depends on images, SVG diagrams, or LaTeX-encoded figures—precisely the medium of most geometry textbooks and many formalized proofs.  In fact, the authors report (in § “Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training”) that even adding arXiv papers—which one might hope would include plenty of geometry theorems—did not measurably improve geometry performance.  In short, the pre-training and instruction-tuning corpora simply didn’t contain enough diagram-heavy geometry or high-quality Isabelle/Lean proof scripts for the model to develop those capabilities.

Second, on model and reward limitations.  DeepSeekMath is a text-only transformer.  It has no vision component or graph-based spatial encoder, so it can’t “see” a triangle or internalize angle–side relations the way a human interpreting a diagram can.  Its reward models and RL fine-tuning (§ “Reinforcement Learning”) were calibrated primarily on chain-of-thought traces from GSM8K and MATH, where geometric reasoning is a small fraction of the data.  As a result, the RL stage never really sharpened geometry-specific skills, and even the process-supervision reward models (§ “Process Supervision RL with GRPO”) were blind to visual constructs.

To date, the paper does not report any successful experiments in which the authors augmented their pre-training or instruction-tuning set with a dedicated corpus of geometry-focused web pages, diagram-OCR transcripts, or large formal-proof libraries extracted from Isabelle/Lean.  Their few‐shot formal-proof evaluation on miniF2F shows promising autoformalization in Isabelle, but that remains an evaluation rather than a fine-tuning source.  Consequently, geometry and formal proof remain under-represented niches in both the data and reward pipelines.

If one wanted to close this gap, the most direct remedy suggested by the paper’s own ablations would be to enrich the DeepSeekMath Corpus with (1) HTML/Markdown pages that embed SVG or TikZ diagrams of Euclidean constructions, (2) scraped Lean4 or Isabelle/HOL theory files (with careful deduplication against test benchmarks), and then (3) to create corresponding chain-of-thought or process-supervision proofs so that the RL reward models actually see geometry reasoning steps.  Absent that, a text‐only LLM simply cannot match the closed models—some of which have proprietary diagram parsers or have been fine-tuned on massive formal libraries—for those geometry-heavy tasks.
