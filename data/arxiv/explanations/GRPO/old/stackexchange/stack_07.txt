### How do you prevent reward hacking or mode collapse without a critic in GRPO?
Question:
In PPO-style RLHF, the value model and per-token KL penalty help stabilize training and prevent the policy from drifting. In GRPO, the critic is removed and the KL is applied as a loss. Are there additional checks (e.g., logprob caps, entropy bonus, response length constraints, answer-format constraints) used to avoid reward hacking or degenerate solutions? Any observed failure cases?
Answer:
In GRPO we actually lean on three built-in “safety rails” rather than sprinkling in a bunch of ad-hoc hacks (like log-prob caps or length-penalties). First, just as in PPO, we apply clip-ratio updates so that the policy’s per-token probabilities never swing wildly in one mini-step. Second, instead of burying our KL-to-reference in the reward, we add it as an explicit penalty term

$$
-\,\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_\theta\bigm\|\pi_{\rm ref}\bigr]
$$

in the objective. That anchor to the initial SFT model keeps the policy from drifting into degenerate “max-ent” or “repeat-it-forever” modes. Third—and most crucially since we’ve done away with a parametric critic—we estimate our baseline by normalizing each sample’s reward against the *group* of outputs we draw for the same prompt. Concretely, if you sample $G$ answers $\{o_i\}$ and score them $\{r_i\}$, you form advantages

$$
\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r)}{\mathrm{std}(r)},
$$

so that no single outlier can dominate the gradient and the policy only learns *relative* improvements.

Because all three of those components (ratio-clipping, explicit KL-penalty, group-relative baseline) are baked directly into the GRPO loss, we really did not need to fall back on extra tricks like an entropy bonus, hard length clamps, or answer-format filters. In our runs (sampling $G=64$ responses per question, clipping $\varepsilon\approx0.1$, KL-weight $\beta\approx0.04$) we never saw catastrophic mode collapse or “I’ll just say 42 to game the reward” behaviours. We did observe the usual mild tendency of an over-optimized policy to become slightly more conservative in style (a well-known trade-off when you regularize heavily to a reference), but no frank reward-hacking exploits.

That said, the one failure case worth flagging is reward-model misalignment: if your reward predictor itself has a blind spot (say it scores long but empty “I don’t know” chains highly), GRPO will happily reinforce that. In practice we avoided it by a) iteratively re-training the reward model on fresh samples, and b) using *process* supervision (per-step scoring) when we have access to it. Those two steps keep the reward signal honest, and in our empirical evaluation on GSM8K and MATH we never ran into a degenerate policy.
