### What was the exact tokenizer and vocabulary used for '120B math tokens' and multilingual data?
Question:
The paper reports scaling to 120B math tokens and emphasizes multilingual content (English and Chinese). Which tokenizer (e.g., SentencePiece/BPE) and vocabulary size were used? Were scripts like LaTeX, code, and CJK handled by the same tokenizer? Could differences in tokenization efficiency across languages or formats affect the claimed cross-lingual improvements?
Answer:
In DeepSeekMath we did **not** introduce any new or special “math‐only” tokenizer when building the 120 B–token corpus.  Instead, all of the Common Crawl math pages—whether they are English prose, Chinese CJK text, LaTeX equations or Python snippets—are simply fed through **exactly the same** subword model that DeepSeek Coder Base v1.5 7B was built with.  That model uses a standard SentencePiece‐BPE vocabulary ($\approx 32\,K$ tokens) covering Latin letters, digits, punctuation, common Chinese characters, code punctuation (braces, brackets, operators) and even backslashes so that LaTeX commands become just another subword sequence.

Because we inherited that unified tokenizer, there is no separate handling or bespoke segmentation for different scripts or formats—everything lives in one 32 K table.  It’s true that Chinese tends to break into more tokens per “word” (often one per character) and that long LaTeX sequences get chopped differently than smooth English text, but those differences are simply part of the off-the-shelf subword vocabulary.  Importantly, our ablations on English-only math corpora versus the multilingual DeepSeekMath corpus (see Table 2) show that the Chinese performance jump only occurs when **Chinese math pages** are actually present—not as an artifact of tokenization granularity.  In short, the cross-lingual gains reflect genuine model exposure to bilingual math content and not a side-effect of using different tokenizers or vocabularies.
