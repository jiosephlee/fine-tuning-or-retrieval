### Why is there no discount factor $\gamma$ in the process-supervised advantage definition?
Question:
For process supervision, the paper defines $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \tilde r_i^{index(j)}$, i.e., a sum of all future normalized step rewards with no discounting. In sequential RL we typically see a discount factor $\gamma \in (0,1]$. Why is $\gamma$ omitted here? Would adding a discount help with credit assignment for long chains of reasoning, or would it degrade performance?
Answer:
In the GRPO paper you’re referring to, “process‐supervised” RL means that the reward model scores *each* reasoning step (rather than just the final answer), and the advantage at token $t$ is defined as the sum of *all* normalized future step‐rewards:  
$$
\hat A_{i,t} = \sum_{j:\,\text{index}(j)\ge t} \tilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)},
$$  
with no extra discount factor $\gamma<1$.  At first glance this looks odd if you’re used to classic RL, so let’s unpack why they set $\gamma=1$ here and what would happen if you tried to introduce a discount.

---

1) Finite, Short “Episodes” with Dense Feedback  
In standard RL, you use a discount $\gamma<1$ to handle *infinite*‐horizon tasks or to reflect a time‐preference (we care more about immediate rewards and ensure the infinite sum converges).  In DeepSeekMath’s chain‐of‐thought setting, each “episode” is just one proof trace or one reasoning chain of bounded length (e.g.\ at most 1 K tokens), and the process reward model already gives you a **dense** signal at every step.  Since the horizon is finite, the undiscounted return 
$$
\sum_{t' \ge t} r_{t'}
$$  
is well‐defined and won’t blow up.  

2) Relative, Group‐Normalized Advantages  
GRPO further normalizes rewards *across* a group of sampled outputs for the same question, subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation.  This “group‐relative” advantage is already correcting for scale and variance—effectively serving the same role that a value‐function baseline and discounting might in traditional actor‐critic.  Introducing $\gamma<1$ here would just re‐shape those normalized returns in an ad hoc way, requiring you to re‐tune $\gamma$ every time your step–reward distribution shifts.

3) Credit Assignment in Long Reasoning Chains  
You might think, “If a proof has 20 steps, shouldn’t I de‐emphasize very‐early steps so the model focuses on nearer‐term reasoning?”  But in mathematical proofs, an error in step 1 almost certainly invalidates everything that follows.  By taking the *full* sum of future step–rewards you give each token credit (or blame) for *all* downstream correctness signals.  If you were to set $\gamma<1$, you would underweight distant steps and might lose the ability to propagate feedback from a late‐step failure all the way back to the earliest missteps.  

4) Empirical Stability and Simplicity  
As the authors note, GRPO aims to be both *efficient* and *simple*.  Every extra hyperparameter—especially one as sensitive as a discount factor—adds complexity.  In practice they found that undiscounted, group‐normalized process supervision was stable, sample‐efficient, and improved long‐chain reasoning without needing to tweak $\gamma$.  

---

In short, **there is no $\gamma$** in their process‐supervised advantage because:

• The reasoning episodes are finite and densely rewarded so an undiscounted return is safe.  
• Group normalization already plays the role of variance reduction and “baseline.”  
• Summing *all* future step–rewards enforces clean credit assignment across the entire proof.  
• Adding a discount would introduce an extra hyperparameter that, in their experiments, didn’t improve—and could even *hurt*—the model’s ability to learn from late‐step feedback.

So if you tried to insert a $\gamma<1$, you would simply re‐weight the same normalized step‐rewards (emphasizing near‐term tokens at the expense of far‐term ones) without gaining any of the usual benefits (convergence guarantees, infinite‐horizon bias) you need *only* in classic, sparse‐reward, infinite‐horizon RL.  For chain‐of‐thought math, it turns out the simplest choice, $\gamma=1$, works best.
