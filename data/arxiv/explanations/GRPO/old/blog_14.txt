# Data Decontamination in the Age of Benchmarks: How to Stay Honest and Still Scale

In the fast-moving world of large language models, achieving ever-higher benchmark scores is a badge of honor. But what happens if your model has effectively “seen” the test questions during training? That’s a classic case of data contamination, or test leakage, and it can give an illusion of progress where none exists. In this post, we’ll walk through the concrete techniques the DeepSeekMath team used to mine mathematical text from Common Crawl at massive scale—while rigorously preventing any overlap with standard benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH. You’ll learn how to deduplicate web data, apply n-gram filters, and set up audit checks so your evaluation results remain trustworthy.

## Why Test Leakage Matters

Imagine you’re baking a cake, but you already know exactly how it’ll taste before you even start. That’s what test leakage does for a language model: it “pre-bakes” solutions by training on the very questions you plan to evaluate. This inflates your scores but delivers no real generalization. In research and production alike, we need to be confident that high benchmark numbers reflect genuine capability, not a data shortcut.  

## Building a High-Quality Corpus from the Web

The DeepSeekMath team started with Common Crawl—a  petabyte-scale web archive packed with everything from blog posts to forum threads. Their goal was to extract the richest possible mathematical content without sneaking in any benchmark questions. Here’s a bird’s-eye view of their iterative pipeline:

 1. **Seed Corpus Initialization**  
    • Begin with a trusted small math collection (e.g., OpenWebMath).  
    • Train a lightweight classifier (fastText) to recognize “math-y” pages.  

 2. **Web-Scale Recall and Ranking**  
    • Apply the classifier to 40 billion deduplicated HTML pages.  
    • Rank pages by math-likeness and select the top slice (e.g., 40 billion tokens).  

 3. **Domain-Level Enrichment**  
    • Group pages by domain (all pages under the same base URL).  
    • Identify domains where ≥ 10 % of pages were initially selected.  
    • Manually annotate their “math” subpaths to seed the next iteration.  

 4. **Repeat Until Convergence**  
    • After four cycles, the team converged on 120 billion math tokens from 35.5 million web pages.  

Throughout these iterations, the classifier keeps improving, yielding ever more focused math content without human designers having to hand-pick every site.

## URL-Based Deduplication and Near-Dedup

Large web archives contain massive redundancy—mirror sites, reposted articles, boilerplate footers. Training on duplicates wastes compute and can reintroduce leaked content. The DeepSeekMath pipeline used:

 • **URL-Based Deduplication**: Strip query strings, sort URL parameters, and collapse multiple access points to the same resource.  
 • **Near-Deduplication**: Compute similarity hashes (e.g., MinHash or SimHash) on the page text to filter out pages that are nearly identical.  

By pruning both exact and near-copies, you reduce both training cost and the risk of accidentally retaining a contaminated page.

## N-Gram Filtering to Prevent Leakage

Even after carefully selecting pages, snippets of benchmark problems can slip through. To stop that, the team applied an n-gram filter directly against benchmark corpora:

 • For every page in the math corpus, scan its text for any 10-gram (a sequence of 10 consecutive tokens) that matches exactly a 10-gram from any evaluation benchmark (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.).  
 • If you find a match, drop that segment (or discard the entire page).  
 • For shorter benchmark items (3 ≤ n < 10 tokens), require exact matches on those shorter spans.  

This simple yet powerful rule—“no 10-gram in common with any test set”—protects against even partial leaks of test questions or answers.

## Auditing Your Pipeline for Trustworthiness

Data pipelines can be complex, and bugs happen. Building trust in your model’s evaluation requires independent auditing steps:

 1. **Random Spot Checks**  
    • Sample pages before and after filtering.  
    • Manually verify no benchmark content remains.  

 2. **Automated Coverage Reports**  
    • Track how many pages or tokens are removed by URL dedup, near-dedup, and n-gram filters.  
    • Alert if the filter rates drop unusually low or high.  

 3. **Benchmark Divergence Analysis**  
    • After training, compare performance on seen vs. unseen question distributions.  
    • If “seen” style questions outperform drastically, investigate potential leakage.  

 4. **Versioned Corpora and Logs**  
    • Keep a versioned record of the corpus at each step.  
    • Log classifier decisions, filtering thresholds, and audit outcomes.  

By baking in these checks, you not only protect against subtle contamination but also build confidence—within your team and in the broader community—that your benchmark scores are real.

## Key Takeaways and Best Practices

1. **Iterate Your Data Selection**: Start with a small, high-quality seed and expand via classifier-based recall, enriched by domain analysis.  
2. **Prune Redundancies Rigorously**: Combine URL-based deduplication with near-duplication to collapse mirrors and reposts.  
3. **Enforce N-Gram Guards**: No exact 10-gram matches with any benchmark text—period.  
4. **Audit Continuously**: Use both manual spot checks and automated metrics to catch unexpected leaks.  
5. **Version Everything**: Corpus versions, classifier snapshots, and filter logs let you roll back or scrutinize decisions later.  

In an era where benchmark performance can be the currency of progress, data decontamination is not an afterthought—it’s a foundational requirement. By applying these concrete techniques, you can mine the vast web for training data at scale, all while ensuring your evaluation remains honest and your model’s abilities are genuinely learned.
