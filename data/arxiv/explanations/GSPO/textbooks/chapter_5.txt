\title{A Textbook about the Paper: GSPO}

Chapter 5: GSPO: Group Sequence Policy Optimization

# Sequence-level importance ratio s_i as geometric mean of likelihood ratios

In GSPO the central quantity that replaces token-level importance ratios is a sequence-level importance ratio, denoted $s_{i}(\theta)$ for a sampled response $y_i$ to query $x$. Intuitively, $s_{i}(\theta)$ measures how much the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ would change the likelihood of emitting the entire response $y_i$ compared to the behavior (old) policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Formally GSPO defines this ratio as the geometric mean of the per-token likelihood ratios:
\[
s_{i}(\theta)
=
\left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

There are two important perspectives that help build intuition for this definition. First, as an importance weight it directly compares entire-sequence probabilities under the target and behavior policies; this respects the fact that rewards are computed at the sequence (response) level. Second, expressing $s_{i}(\theta)$ as the geometric mean of per-token likelihood ratios gives a compact, numerically stable summary: it compresses the product of token-wise ratios (which would be the full sequence ratio) into a per-token scale by raising to the $1/|y_i|$ power. That is, while the full sequence ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}$ multiplies many terms and can become numerically extreme for long $|y_i|$, $s_i(\theta)$ lives on a normalized per-token scale and is therefore better behaved for optimization and clipping.

This geometric-mean construction also connects cleanly to importance sampling: it is an importance weight that corrects expectations over responses (sequences), not over individual next-token samples. Since the verifier reward $r(x,y)$ is defined for entire responses, forming the importance correction at the same granularity avoids an internal mismatch between where the reward is observed and where the off-policy correction is applied.

# Sequence-level clipping: accepting or rejecting entire responses for gradient use

GSPO uses clipping at the sequence level. Concretely the GSPO policy objective applies a clipped surrogate to each sampled response $y_i$:
\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot \mid x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min \big( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \big( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \big) \widehat{A}_{i} \big) 
\right].
\]

Here the clipping operation bounds $s_i(\theta)$ to lie in $[1-\varepsilon,\, 1+\varepsilon]$ before it multiplies the estimated advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for that response. Conceptually this has the effect of either accepting a sampled response with its importance weight (when the weight is within the trusted range) or replacing its weight by the clipped value (when it is too far off-policy). In practice this means that whole responses are either trusted or curtailed as the atomic unit for computing gradients, rather than selectively trusting or curtailing individual tokens inside a response.

Treating entire responses as the unit for clipping has two practical consequences. First, it ensures that the optimization step treats the reward signal coherently: the reward was assigned to the whole sequence, and the importance correction and clipping should therefore also act on the whole sequence. Second, by operating on a length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ (see the next section) the clipping threshold $\varepsilon$ can be set in a way that is stable across different response lengths. Because clipping is applied before forming the gradient contribution for a response, responses whose sequence-level ratios fall far outside the clipping window are effectively prevented from exerting outsized influence on parameter updates, which reduces high-variance, off-policy-induced gradient noise.

# Group-based advantage: within-query standardization for responses

GSPO avoids a learned value function by using a group-based advantage estimator. For each query $x$ we sample a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ from the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Rather than estimating a scalar value function, GSPO computes a relative advantage for each response by standardizing the verifier scores within the group:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{ r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}\big(\{ r(x, y_j) \}_{j=1}^G\big) }{ \mathrm{std}\big(\{ r(x, y_j) \}_{j=1}^G\big) }.
\]

This simple procedure produces zero-mean, unit-variance advantages across the sampled responses to the same query, and is sufficient to provide a learning signal without training a separate value model. There are several practical and conceptual reasons this works well. Practically, it avoids the considerable memory and compute cost of maintaining a value model of comparable scale to the policy model. Conceptually, the group-based standardization centers the reward signal so that only relative differences between candidate responses to the same query drive updates; this is aligned with many RL-for-RLHF setups where the verifier's absolute scale may be arbitrary or non-stationary, but relative quality within a query matters most.

Because the standardization uses the sampled group, it also adapts naturally to the difficulty and variance present in each query: queries that are inherently harder and produce more spread in scores lead to different advantage magnitudes than queries that produce tightly clustered scores. This property reduces the need to tune global reward scaling and can improve numerical stability in optimization.

# Length normalization of the ratio to control variance and unify clipping scales

A key design choice in GSPO is to normalize the full-sequence likelihood ratio by the sequence length. The raw sequence ratio is
\[
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }.
\]
For long responses this product can vary over many orders of magnitude, making both numerical computation and clipping difficult. GSPO takes the $|y_i|$-th root (or, equivalently, averages log-ratios before exponentiating) to obtain
\[
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i \mid x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
\]

Length normalization yields two practical benefits. First, it dramatically reduces the variance of importance weights across responses of different lengths. Because $s_i(\theta)$ is a per-token scale, the same clipping window $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ can be used across responses with diverse lengths without needing length-dependent tuning. Second, length normalization avoids the situation where a small change in a few token probabilities causes an enormous change in the full-sequence ratio, which would otherwise both destabilize learning and force very tiny clipping ranges for longer sequences. By compressing the sequence-level change into a per-token effective scale, GSPO keeps ratios in a numerically manageable range and allows stable clipping and optimization.

# Why GSPO’s clipping ranges differ in magnitude from token-based methods

Because GSPO defines importance ratios differently than token-based methods (e.g., PPO or GRPO), the numerical range and typical scale of those ratios differ, and so the sensible clipping ranges differ in order of magnitude. Token-based methods form per-token importance ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t}) },
\]
and clip each token's ratio directly. Those per-token ratios are usually close to 1, and practical implementations choose clipping thresholds such as $1 \pm 0.2$ (or similar numbers) so that a token's contribution can be moderately up- or down-weighted.

GSPO's length-normalized sequence ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is a different statistic with a different distribution. Numerically, because it compresses multiplicative changes across many tokens into a per-token scale, the meaningful perturbations of $s_i(\theta)$ that correspond to acceptable policy drift can be much smaller in absolute terms. The paper reports that GSPO’s clipping ranges are typically orders of magnitude smaller (e.g., left/right clipping values like $3\times10^{-4}$ and $4\times10^{-4}$ in the experiments) than those used for token-level ratios in GRPO. This does not mean GSPO is stricter in a semantic sense; rather, the same degree of permissible change in the underlying sequence likelihood manifests at different numeric scales for $s_i(\theta)$ than for $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

Furthermore, because GSPO clips entire responses, an individual response that is slightly out-of-distribution under the new policy can be fully curtailed by a small adjustment to its $s_i(\theta)$ without selectively clipping particular tokens. In practice, experiments show that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (when measured token-wise) than token-level methods while nevertheless achieving superior sample efficiency. This apparent paradox arises because GSPO’s clipping operates coherently at sequence granularity and reduces high-variance token-level noise that token-wise clipping does not control effectively.

# Alignment principle: same granularity for reward, ratio, and optimization

The central conceptual principle behind GSPO is that the granularity at which you define the reward, the importance ratio, and the optimization surrogate should be the same. In many RL-for-language settings the verifier reward $r(x,y)$ is a scalar assigned to the entire response $y$. If we then apply importance sampling corrections and clipping at the token level, we create a mismatch: the reward is at the sequence level, but the off-policy correction and gradient weighting are applied to individual tokens, each based on a single token sample from the behavior policy. This mismatch undermines the basic statistical justification for importance sampling.

Recall the importance sampling identity: to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, we re-weight sample function values by the ratio $\pi_\text{tar}/\pi_\text{beh}$. Crucially, this correction is a per-sample factor and is precise only when we are computing expectations over the same sample space as the importance weight. In the language-generation setting, if the quantity of interest (the reward) is defined on full responses, the appropriate importance weight is the full-response likelihood ratio. Token-level ratios attempt to correct for distribution mismatch at a different granularity, but each token ratio is computed from just one next-token sample under the behavior policy; there is no averaging over the next-token distribution to justify each per-token ratio as an importance estimator. As argued in the paper, this misuse introduces high-variance noise that accumulates across tokens and across long responses, which empirically leads to catastrophic instability in large-scale RL training.

GSPO resolves this mismatch by aligning all three elements: the reward (sequence-level), the importance ratio (sequence-level length-normalized), and the optimization surrogate (clipping and gradient contributions applied per response). The gradient of the unclipped GSPO objective (omitting clipping for clarity) is
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x, \{y_i\} \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} }
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} 
s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
Compare this to GRPO's gradient:
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x, \{y_i\} \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} }
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} \mid x,y_{i,<t})} 
\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]
The difference is that GSPO multiplies the entire per-response token-gradient sum by a single sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$, whereas GRPO multiplies each token gradient by its own per-token weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. In practice the per-token weights can vary across tokens in the same response and—because they are computed from single next-token samples—are noisy; these unequal per-token scalings can accumulate across long sequences and across layers, producing unstable and high-variance updates. GSPO's per-response weighting ensures that all tokens in a response are treated coherently, matching the level at which rewards are observed and thereby stabilizing the optimization dynamics.

Finally, GSPO-token is presented in the paper as a variant that preserves the sequence-level ratio for clipping and overall scaling but allows per-token advantages when desired (for example in multi-turn settings where token-specific credit assignment is meaningful). When token-level advantages are set equal across the response GSPO-token becomes numerically identical to GSPO, illustrating that the central novelty is sequence-level importance and clipping rather than token-level weighting.

In summary, aligning the reward unit, importance ratio, and optimization unit to the sequence level yields theoretical consistency with importance sampling and practical robustness in large-scale RL training for language models. The GSPO design implements this alignment through length-normalized sequence ratios, sequence-level clipping, and group-based advantage estimation, and the paper demonstrates that this yields more stable and efficient RL fine-tuning in practice.
