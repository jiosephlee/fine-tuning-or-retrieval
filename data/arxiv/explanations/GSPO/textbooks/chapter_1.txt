\title{A Textbook about the Paper: GSPO}

Chapter 1: Why Reinforcement Learning for LLMs Needs Stability at Scale

# How large-scale RL enables deeper, longer reasoning in LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central technique for improving the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). When we apply RL at scale to language models, we are not just nudging probabilities of next tokens; we are shaping model behavior across entire sequences in pursuit of high-level goals expressed by reward functions. The paper frames this as enabling models to undertake "deeper and longer reasoning processes" — capabilities that manifest in solving more complex problems such as competition-level mathematics and advanced programming tasks. The key idea is that by repeatedly sampling responses, scoring them with verifiers, and updating the model to prefer higher-scoring sequences, RL can push a model’s reasoning behavior beyond what is achievable with supervised pretraining or simple fine-tuning alone.

However, realizing these gains requires pushing RL training to very large scale: larger models (more parameters), longer responses (more tokens), sparser architectures (e.g., Mixture-of-Experts), and much greater compute. Scaling in all these dimensions is what unlocks the next step in capability: longer chains of thought, more correct multi-step solutions, and better use of domain-specific heuristics. The paper positions large-scale RL as a necessary component for continued capability improvements in the latest generation of LLMs.

# Training at scale: large batches, minibatching, and off-policy effects

Scaling RL for LLMs imposes practical demands on rollout and optimization. To fully utilize hardware and throughput of large models, practitioners collect very large batches of rollout data (queries and sampled responses). For optimization efficiency and memory reasons, these large rollout batches are typically partitioned into several smaller mini-batches for gradient updates. This standard engineering practice, however, introduces an important algorithmic consequence: the learning updates are performed on samples that were generated by an older policy parameterization, while the optimizer is updating the current policy. In other words, optimization becomes an off-policy problem.

Off-policy updates require careful correction to avoid biased gradient estimates. The classical tool for correcting expectations is importance sampling. If we want an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ but only have samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, the identity is

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z) \right].
\]

This correction hinges on using importance weights computed across many samples from the behavior distribution so that the re-weighted estimator reduces the bias introduced by the mismatch. In the language-modeling RL setting, the target is the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ while the samples come from the rollout (or old) policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

Algorithms designed for actor-critic or policy-gradient style training of LLMs (for example, PPO-based variants) therefore incorporate importance ratios and clipping mechanisms intended to limit the influence of excessively off-policy samples. The clipping idea is to prevent very large importance weights from dominating the update; in PPO this is typically enforced at the token level when optimizing autoregressive models. But as scale increases — longer responses, larger models, minibatching across many updates — the off-policy correction mechanism and where it is applied become critical to training stability.

# Catastrophic collapse with state-of-the-art methods (symptoms and why it matters)

When off-policy correction is applied incorrectly for LLM RL, training can become unstable in dramatic ways. The paper identifies a concrete failure mode observed with a recent algorithmic baseline, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO eliminates the need for a value model by estimating relative advantages among a group of $G$ responses to the same query, and then applies token-level importance ratios and clipping. Its objective (omitting KL terms) is written as

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\Big)\right],
\]

with token-level importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
and group-based advantages $\widehat A_i$ shared across tokens in a response.

The paper argues that this token-level application of importance sampling is fundamentally misaligned with the unit of reward (the whole sequence) and with the statistical role of importance weights. Each token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is constructed from a single sampled token $y_{i,t}$ drawn from the old next-token distribution. That single-sample ratio cannot serve the role of re-weighting an expectation over the next-token distribution: importance sampling is meaningful when you re-weight many samples from the behavior distribution to approximate the target expectation. Using a single-sample ratio at each time step therefore injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimator.

As response length grows, these per-token noise terms accumulate across tokens. The clipping mechanism intended to stabilize updates can paradoxically amplify this noise because clipping interacts with these noisy, position-dependent weights in complex ways. Empirically, the paper reports catastrophic and often irreversible model collapse under GRPO when scaling to large models, long responses, or large compute budgets. Collapse here means that the model’s behavior degrades substantially and cannot be rescued by reverting to checkpoints or retuning clipping ranges. This is a severe practical problem: it prevents continued RL scaling and makes large-scale RL runs risky and costly.

An additional, architecture-specific failure mode arises in sparse, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, which route different tokens through different expert sub-networks, the set of activated experts for the same response can change between the old policy and the new policy after a few updates. The paper quantifies this expert-activation volatility (e.g., ~10% of experts differing per update in a 48-layer MoE model). That volatility causes token-level likelihoods under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to be computed via different activated sub-networks, making token-level importance ratios wildly unstable. This further invalidates the token-level correction and destabilizes training. To work around this for GRPO, the authors previously used a "Routing Replay" mechanism that replays the old routing masks when recomputing likelihoods under the new model — a costly and restrictive fix.

Why does this matter? Instability and collapse are not minor inconveniences: they invalidate long training runs, waste compute, and prevent researchers from scaling RL to the regime where the desired capabilities emerge. The combination of increased model size, response length, and sparsity makes stability an essential, not optional, property of any RL algorithm intended for large-scale LLM training.

# Design desiderata for scalable RL with LLMs: stability, efficiency, simplicity

Given the observed failure modes, the paper articulates practical design desiderata for RL algorithms applied to large LLMs. These desiderata are guided by the problems above and by what is required to scale training reliably.

Stability is the foremost requirement. An algorithm must produce reliable, low-variance gradient estimates that do not accumulate pathological noise over long sequences or across many updates. This means the off-policy correction mechanism should be theoretically sound in the context of sequence rewards and should avoid per-token noise amplification. Stability also encompasses robustness to architecture-specific behaviors such as expert-activation volatility in MoE models. An algorithm that tolerates natural routing changes without relying on special replay tricks is greatly preferred.

Efficiency is the next key desideratum. Large-scale runs are costly, so the algorithm should make effective use of sampled responses and should not need excessive recomputation or complex value models that duplicate policy size. Avoiding a large value model reduces both memory footprint and compute. Moreover, the algorithm should exploit samples in a way that yields consistent improvement as more compute and longer training are applied.

Simplicity is also valuable in practice. Complex engineering countermeasures (e.g., caching and replaying routing modes, tightly coupling inference and training engines) add overhead, increase failure modes, and limit deployability. Prefer algorithms that align with natural modeling units (sequences for sequence rewards) and that tolerate small practical discrepancies between inference and training engines. Simpler designs also make it easier to incorporate RL into large-scale production pipelines and to distribute computation across heterogeneous systems.

How can an algorithm meet these desiderata? The paper proposes three central principles:

1. Match the unit of importance sampling to the unit of reward. If rewards are assigned to entire responses, importance ratios and clipping should be applied at the sequence level rather than token by token. This restores the meaningfulness of the importance weights as measures of how "off-policy" a sampled response is under the current model.

2. Normalize sequence importance by length to keep importance ratios numerically well-behaved across responses of differing lengths. Concretely, defining a sequence-level, length-normalized importance ratio avoids explosive ratios for long sequences and makes clipping ranges interpretable regardless of response length.

3. Preserve flexibility where needed. While sequence-level correction is core to stability, some settings (e.g., multi-turn RL) may still require token-level advantage granularity. A token-level variant that uses a sequence-level, detached scaling factor retains the stability of sequence correction while allowing per-token advantage shaping.

The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm embodies these principles. GSPO’s objective replaces token-wise weights with a sequence-level importance ratio and performs clipping at the sequence level:

\[
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\Big(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\Big)\right],
\]
where the advantage is the group-normalized reward
\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})},
\]
and the length-normalized sequence importance ratio is
\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

Two practical advantages follow from this design. First, the gradient analysis shows that GSPO weights the token log-likelihood gradients uniformly within each response, avoiding the unequal, potentially large per-token weights used by token-level methods. Writing the (unclipped) gradient compactly:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta \log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
\]

Because tokens in a sequence are uniformly scaled by one sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$, instability arising from per-token importance weight variance is eliminated. Second, since GSPO operates on sequence likelihoods, it is robust to modest per-token fluctuations caused by MoE routing changes: while token-level likelihoods can change abruptly if different experts are used, the sequence likelihood under the model's language modeling capability tends not to fluctuate wildly, so sequence-level ratios are more stable.

The paper also introduces GSPO-token, a variant that retains the sequence-level scaling but allows token-level advantages. GSPO-token constructs token-wise objectives where each token’s numeric scaling equals the sequence-level factor (detached to prevent backpropagating through the per-token factor), thereby enabling per-token advantage shaping without reintroducing token-level importance-weight variance in the gradient.

Empirical results reported in the paper support these design goals. GSPO exhibits stable training dynamics where GRPO could collapse. It improves sample efficiency and benchmark performance under the same compute. For MoE models, GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay: it can be used without replaying old routing masks and without incurring the associated memory and communication overhead, thereby simplifying training infrastructure and allowing full model capacity to be used.

A final practical benefit noted is that sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of minor numerical differences between training engines and inference engines. That tolerance opens the possibility of directly using likelihoods returned by inference engines (e.g., vLLM) without recomputing them under the training engine, simplifying RL pipelines that separate rollout and training stacks.

# Concluding perspective

The paper argues that if RL is to be a reliable lever for unlocking deeper reasoning in LLMs at large scale, the community must adopt algorithms whose off-policy corrections and clipping operate at the same granularity as the reward. Token-level importance weighting and clipping — while natural from a per-token autoregressive view — can be ill-suited to sequence-level rewards and large-scale settings, inducing high-variance gradients that accumulate with length and amplify instabilities introduced by model architecture (e.g., MoE routing). Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses these issues by defining length-normalized sequence-level importance ratios, applying clipping at the response level, and using group-normalized sequence advantages. This design yields the stability, efficiency, and simplicity required to scale RL for modern LLMs, according to the empirical and analytical evidence provided.
