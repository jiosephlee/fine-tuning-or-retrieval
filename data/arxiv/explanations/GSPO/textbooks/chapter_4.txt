\title{A Textbook about the Paper: GSPO}

Chapter 4: Diagnosing Instability: The Misapplication of Token-Level Importance Weights

# Importance sampling requires averaging over many samples per distribution

Importance sampling is the fundamental tool that GRPO and related off-policy methods rely on to correct for the fact that training uses samples drawn from an old (behavior) policy while optimizing a new (target) policy. The basic identity is simple and central:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}} [f(z)]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} \, f(z) \right].
\]

Two aspects of this identity are critical when it is used in practice. First, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is an importance weight: it re-weights samples from the behavior distribution so that the re-weighted average over behavior samples estimates an expectation under the target distribution. Second, and often less emphasized, is that the correction becomes effective only when the expectation on the right-hand side is estimated by averaging over many independent samples from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. In other words, the law of large numbers is doing the heavy lifting: the importance weights correct distributional mismatch by averaging their effect across many samples, so that the estimator’s bias and variance behave in predictable, controllable ways.

The paper emphasizes this point that averaging is not a mere implementation detail but a theoretical requirement. Importance sampling weights are intended to operate on functions of whole samples $z$ drawn repeatedly from the same distribution. If we attempt to apply an importance weight that is meaningful only as an expectation-correcting multiplier but do so where each weight is computed from a single sample and applied in isolation, we lose the statistical mechanism by which importance sampling stabilizes off-policy estimation. That loss is the root of the instability analyzed next.

# GRPO’s token-level ratios use a single token sample per step ⇒ high-variance weights

GRPO constructs importance ratios at the token level. For a generated response $y_i = (y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,|y_i|})$, the token-level importance weight used by GRPO at position $t$ is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Crucially, $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is computed using a single observed token $y_{i,t}$ sampled from the next-token distribution under the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. That means for each conditional distribution (each time step), GRPO forms an importance weight based on one draw from that distribution.

Why is this problematic? The importance-sampling identity described earlier assumes that the importance weight is associated with a whole-sample random variable and is averaged across many independent whole-sample draws. When the weight is computed from a single draw of a conditional distribution and applied locally at that token, it no longer performs the role of correcting a distributional expectation through averaging. Instead, it behaves like a multiplicative noise term with large variability: the ratio of the densities at a single sampled outcome can be highly variable, particularly when the two distributions differ even modestly. Therefore, token-level weights act as high-variance multipliers in the gradient estimator rather than as stable correction factors.

The paper makes this point concretely: token-level importance weights are “based on a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from each next-token distribution,” so they “fail to perform the intended distribution-correction role” and instead “introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients.” This is not just a modest increase in variance — it fundamentally changes the character of the gradient estimates and makes them unreliable for stable learning.

# Variance accumulation over long sequences; clipping amplification effects

Language-model responses often span many tokens. Even if each token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ has a moderate variance, when these noisy multipliers appear repeatedly across the tokens of a long response their effects accumulate. To see this intuitively, consider that the sequence-level likelihood ratio decomposes into the product of token ratios:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
= \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Working with token-level weights is effectively working with the individual multiplicative factors of this product. Random fluctuations in each factor compound multiplicatively along the sequence in the gradient, so the variance of the whole update can grow quickly with $|y_i|$. The GSPO paper reports exactly this behavior: “the high-variance training noise... progressively accumulates with increased response length,” producing unstable updates.

Compounding the problem is the clipping mechanism used by PPO-style algorithms. Clipping is intended to exclude excessively off-policy samples by capping importance weights into a bounded interval, preventing extreme multipliers from dominating the update. But when clipping is applied at the token level to many noisy ratios, it has two deleterious effects. First, per-token clipping introduces systematic bias because many token contributions get truncated in inconsistent ways across the sequence; these local biases can add up to a large net bias across a long response. Second, clipping can amplify instability: when a significant fraction of tokens is clipped, the effective gradient is determined by the interplay of clipped and unclipped tokens in unpredictable ways. The paper notes that “this weight... introduces high-variance training noise, which accumulates over long sequences and is further amplified by the clipping mechanism,” and that such amplification can precipitate catastrophic model collapse.

The empirical observation in the paper supports this chain of reasoning: GRPO exhibits severe stability issues that become worse as response length and model scale increase. By contrast, sequence-level importance weights (used in GSPO) condense the multiplicative structure into a single, averaged quantity per response, substantially reducing the variance accumulation as sequence length grows.

# Unit-of-reward vs unit-of-optimization mismatch: sequence-level reward vs token-level weights

A central conceptual diagnosis offered by the paper is that GRPO mismatches the granularity of the reward and the granularity of the off-policy correction and optimization unit. In their setup, the verifier provides a scalar reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$ for an entire response $y$. That reward is inherently a sequence-level signal: it evaluates the whole generated sequence, not individual tokens.

GRPO, however, applies importance sampling and clipping at the token level while assigning the same group-based advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ to every token in response $y_i$. That produces an awkward hybrid: a sequence-level reward is distributed uniformly across tokens, yet the off-policy correction is performed locally per token using token-level importance ratios. The paper characterizes this as an “ill-posed” objective: the unit on which we compute importance weights should align with the unit that receives the reward and whose expected value we intend to approximate.

This mismatch has concrete consequences. Token-level weights do not serve as meaningful importance corrections for a sequence-level reward — they were never designed to correct the expectation of a function of the whole sequence using one sample per conditional distribution. As a result, GRPO’s objective oscillates between two incompatible granularities: tokens are treated as independent importance-sampling problems, but the optimization target is an aggregate sequence metric. Such an objective lacks the statistical justification that importance sampling provides when the same unit is used for weights and rewards, and that mismatch manifests as instability in training.

GSPO resolves this by defining a sequence-level importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)
= \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
\]

and performs clipping and optimization at the response level. By matching the optimization unit to the unit of reward, GSPO restores the statistical consistency of the importance-weighted objective and avoids the pathological behavior introduced by token-level weighting.

# Irreversibility of collapse and why tuning clipping often fails

The paper reports a striking empirical phenomenon: when GRPO undergoes the instability described above, the model can experience catastrophic and often irreversible collapse. Several attempts to recover — reverting to a previous checkpoint, finely tuning clipping ranges, increasing generation length, or changing the RL queries — can fail to restore normal training. Understanding why this collapse is hard to reverse requires tying together the previous diagnostics.

Because token-level importance ratios inject high-variance, multiplicative noise at each token and because the clipping mechanism can bias and amplify this noise, an unstable sequence of updates can push model parameters into regions of parameter space where the conditional likelihoods (and thus downstream importance ratios) are very different. In MoE models, this can also change which experts are activated for particular tokens, introducing additional discontinuities in the parameter-to-likelihood mapping. When parameters shift sufficiently, the behavior policy embodied by those parameters can generate outputs that are qualitatively different; continuation of training from such a point can further entrench these differences. Thus a sequence of biased, high-variance updates can create a self-reinforcing divergence from the previously learned behavior.

Because the root cause is statistical — an invalid application of importance sampling at the token level — simple surface remedies like changing the clipping threshold often do not succeed. Reducing clipping can increase variance further; tightening clipping can introduce stronger bias. In either case, these adjustments do not address the fundamental misuse of single-sample token-level weights to approximate sequence-level expectations. The paper documents that even careful hyperparameter tuning after collapse is frequently ineffective: “Once the collapse occurs, resuming training is unavailing, even when reverting to a previous checkpoint and meticulously tuning hyperparameters (e.g., the clipping ranges), extending generation length, or switching the RL queries.”

In MoE models, an additional mechanism makes reversibility even more difficult. The paper describes expert-activation volatility: after updates, the set of experts used to process a particular response can change significantly (e.g., roughly 10% different experts per update in one example). Such volatility alters the functional form of conditional likelihoods for the same input sequence and magnifies the variance and instability induced by token-level ratios. The earlier mitigation used by the authors, Routing Replay, attempts to freeze routing to make token-level ratios meaningful again, but that is an engineering workaround rather than a principled solution. GSPO, by focusing on sequence-level likelihoods, sidesteps this entire class of problems and thereby avoids irreversible collapse originating from token-level misapplication of importance sampling.

In summary, the collapse is irreversible in practice because the misapplied token-level importance weights create biased, high-variance updates that push the model into qualitatively different behaviors — and because correcting such behavior by tuning clipping or rolling back checkpoints does not solve the underlying statistical mismatch between the unit of reward and the unit of off-policy correction.
