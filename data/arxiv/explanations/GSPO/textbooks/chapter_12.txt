\title{A Textbook about the Paper: GSPO}

Chapter 12: Simplifying RL Infrastructure with Sequence-Level Likelihoods

# Precision mismatches between training and inference engines

Large-scale RL pipelines for language models commonly separate components that generate samples (inference) from components that optimize model parameters (training). In practice, these components are implemented in different engines that trade off performance, memory layout, or numerical precision. The paper uses the terms training engine (examples: Megatron) and inference engines (examples: SGLang, vLLM) to denote these two classes of systems. Training engines are typically optimized for distributed, high-throughput gradient computation and often use particular numerical formats, kernel implementations, or fused operations. Inference engines are optimized for low-latency sampling and may use different kernels, batching strategies, or lower-precision representations to maximize throughput.

Because the two classes of engines compute likelihoods with different numerical behavior, a rollout produced by an inference engine will typically come with likelihoods that differ slightly from the likelihoods recomputed by the training engine on the same sequence. For token-level likelihoods these discrepancies can be amplified: a small floating-point difference in a single token probability appears directly in the token-level importance ratio
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\]
so even tiny numerical differences can change the per-token ratio and therefore the gradient weights used by token-level objectives. The paper emphasizes that algorithms which rely on token-level importance ratios (e.g., GRPO) therefore tend to be precision-sensitive in practice and require recomputing token likelihoods under the training engine to obtain stable and correct importance weights.

# Using inference-engine likelihoods directly for GSPO

GSPO changes the unit of importance correction from tokens to entire sequences. Its sequence-level importance ratio for a response $y_i$ is defined (with length normalization) as
\[
s_i(\theta)
\;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\;=\;
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Two properties of this sequence-level form make it inherently more tolerant to the precision mismatches between engines.

First, the sequence likelihood aggregates information across all tokens of the response. Numerical perturbations in individual token probabilities enter the log-sum and are averaged across the length $|y_i|$. Small, random per-token differences tend to partially cancel out in the average, reducing their influence on the final $s_i(\theta)$ value compared to their effect on individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ values.

Second, working in log-space and averaging stabilizes the dynamic range. The exponential of the averaged log-ratio is less sensitive to a small additive error in a single log-probability than a direct multiplicative perturbation at the token level. Concretely, an inference engine likelihood for the whole sequence, or the sum of per-token log-likelihoods it returns, will often be sufficiently accurate for $s_i(\theta)$ even if individual token probabilities differ slightly from the training engine's values.

Because GSPO's optimization and clipping use these sequence-level ratios, the algorithm can in many cases accept the sequence likelihoods produced by the inference engine directly, avoiding the usual practice of recomputing token-level likelihoods under the training engine for every sampled response. The paper explicitly notes this practical implication: GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods is "much more tolerant of precision discrepancies" and therefore can enable directly using inference-engine likelihoods for optimization.

# Benefits in partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated train-infer systems

The infrastructure and operational burdens of recomputing likelihoods are especially acute in several practical RL settings. GSPO reduces these burdens for each of the scenarios the paper highlights.

Partial rollout. In some pipelines, an inference engine may generate only part of a long trajectory (a partial rollout). To compute correct token-level importance ratios for token-based algorithms, the training engine often needs to recompute likelihoods for every token in the partial rollout while reproducing the exact numerical routing, attention masks, and precision characteristics. These recomputations are expensive and fragile. GSPO requires only a sequence-level likelihood for the sampled response. If the inference engine provides the sequence log-likelihood (or normalized likelihood) for the partial rollout, GSPO can use it directly. This avoids repeating the expensive forward pass in the training engine for every partial rollout sample.

Multi-turn RL. In multi-turn settings the response for one user query becomes part of the context for subsequent turns, and rollouts can be long and interdependent. Token-level recomputation becomes combinatorially more expensive when turns must be re-scored many times or when the training engine must reproduce the exact tokenization and routing across many partial histories. Because GSPO's objective and clipping are sequence-oriented, a single sequence likelihood for each completed response suffices to compute its importance ratio, simplifying bookkeeping across turns and reducing the number of expensive recomputations required to evaluate or re-evaluate rollouts.

Disaggregated train-infer systems. Modern production systems often separate inference and training into distinct services or clusters (a disaggregated framework) for scaling and cost-effectiveness. Shipping samples and metadata (including token-level likelihoods) from inference to training can be expensive in bandwidth and storage, and differences between engines can force recomputation anyway. GSPO's tolerance for inference-engine sequence likelihoods enables a cleaner disaggregation: inference can produce responses together with sequence log-likelihoods (or normalized sequence likelihoods), and the training service can consume those directly to compute $s_i(\theta)$ and the GSPO objective. This reduces coupling between the two services and simplifies the end-to-end pipeline.

# Operational simplifications: fewer recomputations, reduced latency and cost

By design, GSPO reduces the occasions where the training engine must re-run forward passes purely for likelihood recomputation. The paper identifies several operational gains that follow from this reduction.

First, fewer recomputations mean lower computational cost. Forward passes over large models, especially when done in high precision or across distributed training hardware, are a significant portion of rollout-processing cost. Eliminating or reducing recomputation directly reduces GPU/TPU time consumed by the pipeline.

Second, latency is improved. When the training engine no longer needs to wait for costly likelihood recomputation, the loop from sample collection to gradient update tightens. In practice this can lead to faster iteration and higher effective throughput of consumed queries per unit time.

Third, engineering complexity decreases. Maintaining correct, bit-for-bit reproduction of inference behavior in the training engine (including routing replay or other tricks required by token-level methods in sparse models) is a brittle and costly engineering burden. The paper notes that GSPO obviates techniques such as Routing Replay for MoE models because sequence likelihoods are not sensitive to individual-token routing volatility. Removing these special-case engineering paths simplifies codepaths and lowers the risk of subtle bugs.

Finally, the cost and complexity reductions compound in production-scale settings: fewer data transfers, less storage of per-token metadata, simplified service contracts between inference and training, and reduced need for synchronized versions of inference and training stacks all save operational effort and budget.

Taken together, these operational simplifications are not merely conveniences: they enable more flexible architectures (e.g., disaggregated training/inference, mixed-precision inference engines) and make it easier to scale RL on large language models. The paper frames these infrastructure gains as one of GSPO's practical advantages in addition to its theoretical and empirical improvements in stability and performance.
