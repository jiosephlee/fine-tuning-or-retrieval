\title{A Textbook about the Paper: GSPO}

Chapter 9: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): Stability Challenges and GSPO’s Advantage

# MoE routing: expert activation patterns and volatility across updates

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models route each token (or token position) through a small subset of expert subnetworks selected by a learned routing mechanism. For a given input context and a particular token position, the router picks one or more experts whose parameters will be used to compute that token’s conditional distribution. The pattern of which experts are activated for which tokens—the expert activation pattern—thus depends both on the input and on the current parameters of the model (including the router).

An important empirical property highlighted in the paper is that these activation patterns are not perfectly stable across gradient updates. As training updates the router and the experts, the set of experts chosen for the same rollout sample can change. In the experiments reported, for a deep MoE model (the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), after each RL gradient update roughly 10% of the experts activated for the same rollout sample under the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ were different from those activated under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. This expert-activation volatility becomes more pronounced for deeper and larger MoE models.

Why does this matter? Because every token’s conditional probability $\pi_{\theta}(y_{t} \mid x, y_{<t})$ is computed by the specific subnetwork(s) selected for that token. If the network selected for that position shifts between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$, then the two probabilities in any ratio compare outputs of different computational graphs (different experts), not merely different parameter settings of the same graph. This mismatch is the root of the instability issues we describe next.

# Why token-level ratios become erratic under routing changes

Off-policy correction in policy optimization relies on importance sampling: to evaluate or optimize with samples drawn from a behavior policy, one multiplies by a likelihood ratio that corrects for the difference between the target and behavior distributions. In token-level objectives such as GRPO (and token-level PPO variants), that importance correction is applied per token. The token-level importance ratio used in GRPO is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }.
\]

This ratio is meaningful when both the numerator and denominator are probabilities produced by comparable computations. However, when MoE routing changes between the old and new policies, the denominator and numerator can come from different expert subsets. Two problems follow.

First, the ratio can become numerically unstable. If the activated expert under $\pi_{\theta}$ has very different output scale or inductive behavior compared to the expert under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the ratio can take extreme values. Second, the ratio is no longer a faithful correction of the sampling distribution: it does not represent reweighting from one fixed conditional distribution to another because the underlying computational support (which expert produces the probability) has changed. In practice, these effects introduce high-variance noise into gradient estimates that accumulates across token positions and long responses. The paper reports that this accumulation can lead to catastrophic, often irreversible model collapse during RL training when algorithms use token-level importance weights naively.

The gradient-level distinction is instructive. GRPO’s gradient effectively weights each token gradient by $w_{i,t}(\theta)$:

\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) \;=\; 
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta)\, \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right].
\]

If $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fluctuates drastically or becomes unreliable because of routing changes, the effective gradient signal becomes noisy and biased in ways that are hard to control with clipping alone. Clipping compresses extreme ratios but cannot fully correct for the fact that the importance weight no longer represents a principled reweighting of comparable probabilities.

# Routing Replay: how it stabilizes GRPO and at what system cost

Routing Replay is a practical workaround designed to address expert-activation volatility when using token-level importance ratios. The idea is simple: when computing token-level likelihoods under the new policy for importance weighting, force the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ to use the same routing choices that were used when the sample was generated under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Concretely, for each rollout sample one caches the routing decisions (which experts were selected at each token) produced by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, and when evaluating $\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})$ for importance ratios, one “replays” those cached routings so that both numerator and denominator share the same activated experts.

Routing Replay restores the comparability that token-level ratios require: the ratio then compares outputs of the same computational path under different parameter settings, which is what importance sampling presumes. Empirically, the paper finds that Routing Replay is essential for GRPO training of MoE models to converge properly; without it, the routing volatility prevents normal convergence.

However, Routing Replay incurs nontrivial system and modeling costs. Caching routing decisions for every rollout sample increases memory usage. Replaying cached routings inside the forward pass of the new model typically requires extra engineering to force a particular routing path and may increase communication or synchronization overhead in distributed training (since routing metadata must be preserved and shared). Moreover, replaying old routings artificially constrains the MoE model: it prevents the new policy from freely choosing different experts when evaluating importance ratios, thereby reducing effective utilization of the model’s full expert capacity during optimization. In short, Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO at the cost of increased system complexity, overhead, and a limitation on true model capacity exploitation.

# GSPO’s insensitivity to token-level fluctuations via sequence-likelihood focus

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) takes a different design choice: instead of applying importance correction at the token level, GSPO formulates importance ratios and clipping at the sequence level. The sequence-level importance ratio in GSPO is defined as

\[
s_i(\theta) \;=\; \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\;=\;
\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

This ratio aggregates token-level log-likelihood differences across the whole response and applies length normalization via the $1/|y_i|$ exponent. Two properties make this sequence-level ratio robust to MoE routing volatility.

First, it aggregates information across all token positions. Local token-level fluctuations due to a small subset of tokens being routed differently are averaged into the whole-response likelihood. Small or local routing changes therefore have less influence on $s_i(\theta)$ than on individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ ratios.

Second, the length normalization reduces variance arising from response length differences: without the $1/|y_i|$ exponent, sequence likelihood changes for longer responses could be extremely large simply because they multiply many per-token ratios. With normalization, the ratio is scaled to a per-token geometric mean, which keeps importance ratios in a more uniform numerical range and reduces sensitivity to occasional tokenwise spikes.

At the gradient level, GSPO weights all token gradients within a response equally, modulated only by the scalar sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ and the group advantage $\widehat{A}_i$:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) \;=\;
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\right]
\]

(omitting clipping for clarity). Because $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar per sequence, the per-token gradient contributions are not reweighted unevenly by possibly erratic token-level ratios; instead, the model receives a coherent sequence-level update that matches the sequence-level reward signal. This alignment between the unit of reward (the sequence) and the unit of importance correction (also the sequence) is central to GSPO’s improved stability.

Empirically, GSPO is shown to converge normally for MoE models without any Routing Replay. The paper emphasizes that GSPO focuses only on sequence likelihoods—which remain relatively stable because the model maintains language modeling competence—even when token-level routings change. Hence GSPO is not sensitive to expert-activation volatility in the same destructive way GRPO is.

# Practical gains: no routing cache, lower overhead, full-capacity utilization

Because GSPO does not require masking or replaying previous routing decisions, it eliminates the routing cache and replay mechanism. This has several practical implications:

- Lower memory and communication overhead: There is no need to store and transmit routing metadata alongside each rollout sample, reducing extra memory consumption and network traffic in distributed settings.

- Simpler implementation and systems pipeline: Without Routing Replay, the RL training pipeline does not have to enforce specific routing choices during probability recomputation, simplifying engineering complexity.

- Full-capacity utilization of the MoE: Routing Replay, by forcing the new policy to use old routing patterns during ratio computation, artificially ties the evaluation of $\pi_{\theta}$ to $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$’s expert choices and thus limits the model’s ability to exploit different experts. GSPO avoids this restriction, allowing the router and experts to evolve naturally and the model to use its full capacity during optimization.

Beyond the MoE-specific benefits, GSPO also reduces certain infrastructure constraints. The paper notes that GSPO’s reliance on sequence-level likelihoods is more tolerant to precision discrepancies between training engines (e.g., Megatron) and inference engines (e.g., SGLang or vLLM). In practice, teams often recompute likelihoods of sampled responses under the old policy using the training engine to ensure numerical consistency. GSPO’s robustness suggests that it may be possible to use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine directly, avoiding recomputation, which simplifies workflows in partial-rollout or multi-turn RL settings and in training-inference disaggregated frameworks.

Finally, an interesting experimental observation in the paper complements these practical gains: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (when clipping is applied at sequence level) compared to GRPO’s token-level clipping, yet still achieves better training efficiency and stability. This counter-intuitive result supports the interpretation that GRPO’s token-level signal is intrinsically noisy and inefficient to exploit, whereas GSPO’s sequence-level approach yields a more reliable learning signal even when it discards a larger proportion of tokens via clipping.

# Summary

MoE routing volatility creates a fundamental challenge for token-level importance-weighted RL methods because changes in which experts are activated mean token-level likelihoods under the old and new policies are computed by different subnetworks. Token-level ratios can therefore be erratic and high variance, destabilizing training. Routing Replay can stabilize token-level algorithms like GRPO by forcing consistent computation paths, but at substantial memory, communication, and capacity-utilization costs. GSPO sidesteps the problem by defining importance ratios at the sequence level, using a length-normalized sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta)$ that aggregates token effects and is intrinsically less sensitive to local routing changes. This design removes the need for Routing Replay, simplifies infrastructure, reduces overhead, and enables stable, efficient RL training of large MoE language models—contributions that the paper reports as critical to the success of recent large-model RL efforts.
