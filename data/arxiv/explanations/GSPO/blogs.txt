# From Tokens to Tales: Why Sequence-Level RL Beats Token-Level

## The mismatch problem in RL for language models

Modern RL fine-tuning for large language models often follows the reinforcement-learning-as-language-modelling pattern: treat the autoregressive model as a policy, sample responses, score entire responses with a verifier (reward in $[0,1]$), and update the policy to prefer better responses. Yet many practical algorithms—like GRPO—apply importance sampling and clipping at the token level, i.e., compute importance ratios per next-token distribution and weight each token's gradient individually.

That design hides a fundamental mismatch. The reward signal is given to an entire response $y$ for a query $x$, but GRPO reweights and clips at each token position $t$ using
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$.
Importance sampling theory requires reweighting whole-sample probabilities to correct distribution mismatch. Applying importance weights that are computed from a single next-token draw (one sample per next-token distribution) does not perform that corrective role; instead it injects high-variance noise into gradient estimates. The problem compounds over long responses: sequence length multiplies token-level noise, and the clipping mechanism—designed to mitigate off-policy contributions—can further amplify instability. Empirically, this can produce catastrophic, often irreversible, model collapse in large-scale RL runs.

In short: optimizing at the token granularity when rewards are sequence-level is ill-posed and unstable for long responses and very large models.

## The core idea of GSPO: align unit of optimization with unit of reward

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses the mismatch by moving importance weighting, clipping, and optimization to the sequence level. Instead of token-level ratios, GSPO uses a length-normalized sequence-level importance ratio

s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).

GSPO then forms group-based advantages (relative scores within G responses to the same query) and applies clipping and optimization at the response level:

\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right].

Two design choices matter here:

- Sequence-level weighting aligns with the unit that receives the reward, making importance sampling conceptually correct.
- Length normalization in $s_i(\theta)$ keeps the scale of importance ratios comparable across responses of different lengths, reducing variance and allowing a unified clipping range.

## How GSPO stabilizes gradients (intuition from gradient forms)

Compare the (unclipped) gradients to see what changes. GSPO's gradient (per response) looks like

s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).

All tokens in a response are weighted uniformly by the same scalar $s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i$. By contrast, GRPO weights each token's log-probability gradient with its own token-level importance ratio:

\widehat{A}_i\cdot\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot).

Those per-token multiplicative factors vary across positions and can range across the clipping interval, producing heterogeneous and often large perturbations to token gradients. Because these perturbations are sampled from single-token next-token draws, they have high variance and accumulate over sequence length. GSPO avoids that instability by collapsing the per-token fluctuation into a single, theoretically grounded sequence weight.

## Practical wins demonstrated in the paper

The paper reports several empirical advantages of GSPO over GRPO, all grounded in the above theoretical fix:

- Stability: GSPO training proceeds stably over long runs, avoiding the catastrophic collapses observed with GRPO in some large-model settings.
- Efficiency and performance: Under comparable compute and query budgets, GSPO achieves higher training rewards and better benchmark performance (AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces metrics reported in the study).
- Robust scaling: GSPO continues to improve when training compute, query updates, or generation length are increased.

One particularly counter-intuitive empirical observation is about clipping fractions. GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (because it clips entire responses based on sequence ratios) than GRPO does at the token level, yet it still extracts more training signal and produces better learning. This supports the diagnosis that GRPO's token-level gradients are noisy and inefficient: keeping many token gradients (by clipping fewer tokens) does not help if those gradients are corrupted by token-level importance noise.

## Why GSPO matters for MoE models

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models introduce a new source of instability: expert-activation volatility. After even a single gradient update, the set of experts activated for the same response can change (the paper cites ~10% different experts per update in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base example). That volatility makes token-level likelihoods—and thus token-level importance ratios—fluctuate drastically, further invalidating GRPO's tokenwise reweighting.

The previous workaround, Routing Replay, cached the old policy's routing decisions and replayed them during gradient computation so token likelihoods under $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ shared the same activated network. Routing Replay restores GRPO convergence but adds memory, communication, and capacity constraints.

GSPO obviates Routing Replay. Because GSPO relies on sequence likelihoods, which are more stable even when token-level activation patterns change, it tolerates expert-activation volatility without caching routing modes. That both simplifies infrastructure and lets the model leverage its full MoE capacity.

## Secondary infrastructure benefits

GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it more tolerant to numerical differences between training and inference engines. In practice, teams often recompute token-level likelihoods in the training engine to avoid precision mismatches with the inference engine. GSPO is more forgiving: using sequence-level likelihoods directly from the inference engine is often sufficient, which can reduce recomputation overhead in deployment settings like partial rollout, multi-turn RL, or disaggregated training/inference pipelines.

## When might you still want token-level granularity?

The paper offers a token-level variant, GSPO-token, which is basically the same numerically when all token advantages are equal but allows per-token advantages in settings like multi-turn RL where different tokens or turns should receive different credit. GSPO-token uses the detached sequence ratio as a global scalar and permits tokenwise advantage customization without reintroducing the instability of token-level importance sampling.

## Takeaway

If your reward is a whole-response score, measuring and correcting distribution mismatch at the whole-response level is both principled and practical. Token-level importance ratios estimated from single next-token samples do not realize the corrective power of importance sampling, and they inject variance that scales with sequence length and model size—especially painful for sparse MoE architectures. GSPO restores alignment between the unit of reward and the unit of optimization by using length-normalized sequence likelihood ratios and response-level clipping, yielding markedly better stability, efficiency, and scaling behavior in large-scale RL for language models.



# Inside GSPO: A Plain-English Guide to Group Sequence Policy Optimization

Reinforcement learning has become a key recipe for improving large language models, but scaling RL to very large models and long responses exposes fragile training behavior. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is an algorithm designed to make RL for language models stable, efficient, and practical at scale. This post walks through GSPO’s three core ideas—sequence-level importance ratios (with length normalization), group-based relative advantages, and sequence-level clipping—and explains why their combination reduces variance, avoids pathological token-level corrections, and stabilizes training (especially for Mixture-of-Experts models).

## The problem GSPO tries to solve (in plain terms)

When we run RL on language models, we typically sample whole responses (sequences) from an older policy, score them with some reward function, and then update the model. Because we optimize the new policy using samples drawn from the old policy, we need to correct for that mismatch. Classic PPO-style approaches do importance sampling at the token level: for each next-token probability they compute a ratio between the new and old policy and use that to weigh the learning signal.

Two practical issues arise with token-level importance weighting when responses are long, models are large, or the model is sparse (Mixture-of-Experts):

- High variance and accumulated noise: a token-level ratio is computed from a single sampled token from the next-token distribution. That single-sample ratio is a noisy, unstable estimator for how the whole distribution changed, and noise accumulates across tokens in a long sequence.
- In MoE models, which can route different tokens through different experts, the activated subnetwork for the same token can change between updates. That makes token-level ratios fluctuate and sometimes invalidates the correction entirely.

GSPO addresses these issues by changing the unit of correction and clipping from tokens to sequences, aligning the correction with the unit that actually receives the reward.

## Core idea 1 — sequence-level importance ratios (with length normalization)

Instead of computing importance weights per token, GSPO computes an importance ratio for the whole sampled response y given query x:

s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.

A few important points about this choice:

- The numerator and denominator are sequence likelihoods: \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
- GSPO applies a length-normalizing exponent 1/|y_i|. This keeps s_i(\theta) on a comparable scale across responses of different lengths and avoids exponential explosion/vanishing that would result from multiplying many token ratios.
- Intuitively, s_i(\theta) reflects how much the model's average per-token log-probability of the whole response changed between \theta_{\text{old}} and \theta. Because the reward is assigned to the whole sequence, this sequence-level correction is a better match to the learning target.

Why this reduces variance: the sequence-level ratio pools information across all tokens of the response (via the product / sum of log-probabilities), so it is a more stable estimator of distributional mismatch than single-token ratios based on a single next-token sample.

## Core idea 2 — group-based relative advantages

GSPO uses a group-based relative advantage instead of relying on a learned value model. For each query x, we sample a small group of G responses \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G from the old policy and compute normalized advantages within that group:

\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}.

This does two useful things:

- It removes the need for a separate value network (which is costly in memory and can be hard to train well for long sequences).
- Normalizing within the group produces relative rewards that are on a stable scale, which plays nicely with the clipping mechanism described next.

The idea is simple: compare responses to each other for the same query, and use those relative scores as advantages for policy improvement.

## Core idea 3 — sequence-level clipping

PPO-style clipping prevents overly “off-policy” samples from dominating the gradient. GSPO applies the clipping to whole sequences using the sequence-level ratio s_i(\theta):

min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right).

That is, if a sequence’s importance weight is too far from 1 (beyond the clipping window), GSPO clips the effect of that sample on the objective. Two practical differences from token-level clipping are worth emphasizing:

- Unit match: clipping acts on the same unit that receives the reward and provides the learning signal (the whole response). This prevents mismatches that occur when you clip at token-level but reward at sequence-level.
- Stability in MoE: because s_i(\theta) is based on the overall sequence likelihood, it is less sensitive to token-level routing volatility in sparse models. That means GSPO often does not require complicated engineering fixes (like replaying cached routing decisions) needed by token-level algorithms.

## How these pieces work together

The GSPO objective (ignoring KL regularization) is:

\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\left(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right)\right].

Mechanically, the algorithm does the usual RL loop: sample, score, compute advantages across each sample group, compute s_i(\theta) for each sampled sequence (length-normalized), clip as needed, and update parameters to maximize \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}.

Why it reduces catastrophic behaviors seen with token-level methods:

- Gradient weighting is uniform across tokens of a given sequence (tokens are combined through the log-likelihood sum and length normalization). This avoids per-token weight noise that can add up over long sequences.
- Group-relative advantages replace the need for a separate value model and keep advantage estimates stable within each query’s batch of samples.
- Sequence-level clipping excludes extreme off-policy sequences in a way consistent with the reward structure.

## GSPO-token: a flexible variant for token-level control

If you want finer-grained per-token advantages (for example in multi-turn RL where intermediate tokens have different roles), GSPO provides a variant called GSPO-token. It preserves the same sequence-level importance signal s_i(\theta), but allows per-token advantages \widehat{A}_{i,t}. GSPO-token uses a detached scaling trick so that, numerically, each token sees the same sequence-level scale but can be assigned individual advantages. When all token advantages are identical, GSPO and GSPO-token are mathematically the same. GSPO-token therefore gives flexibility without reintroducing the high-variance token-level importance weights that caused problems.

## Practical benefits highlighted by the paper

The paper reports several practical advantages of GSPO:

- More stable and efficient training than GRPO, achieving better reward and benchmark performance under comparable training compute.
- Stability when training large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. GSPO avoids the need for the Routing Replay trick that was previously used to stabilize token-level importance ratios in MoE training. Because sequence likelihoods are less sensitive to token-level routing changes, GSPO converges without replaying cached routing modes.
- Simpler infrastructure: because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it is more tolerant of precision differences between inference and training engines. That opens the door to using inference-engine likelihoods directly (avoiding recomputation in the training engine) in some setups.

The experiments in the paper show GSPO enabling continuous improvement while increasing training compute, regularizing queries, and extending generation length—things that token-level schemes struggled with at scale.

## Takeaways

- Align the unit of importance-weight correction with the unit of reward. If the reward is per-sequence, correct and clip at sequence level.
- Length-normalize sequence-level ratios (\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\right)^{1/|y|}) to keep scales consistent across response lengths and reduce variance.
- Use group-based relative advantages to avoid a separate value model and to stabilize advantage estimates.
- Sequence-level clipping and weighting produce more robust gradients and remove several failure modes that arise with token-level correction—especially for long responses and sparse models like MoE.

GSPO is a conceptually simple change—move importance sampling and clipping to sequence-level and normalize by length—but that change fixes several practical instability sources and makes RL for large language models far more robust in practice.



# Stability at Scale: How GSPO Prevents Model Collapse

## Overview

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a core tool for improving large language models, but it comes with a practical hazard: training instability. In particular, algorithms that apply importance sampling at the token level (e.g., GRPO-style approaches) can accumulate high-variance noise over long sequences and large models, sometimes producing catastrophic, irreversible model collapse. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses this failure mode by moving the importance-weighting and clipping units from tokens to whole responses. The result is a more stable, efficient, and scalable RL update that plays well with long generations, large rollout batches, and sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures.

This post unpacks the core ideas behind GSPO, why token-level importance ratios are problematic, and how sequence-level weighting plus equal token treatment stabilizes gradients and training dynamics.

## The core problem: token-level importance ratios are high-variance and ill-matched to rewards

In policy-gradient-style RL we often collect responses y from an old policy π_{θ_old} and want to update to a new policy π_θ. Importance sampling tells us how to reweight off-policy samples to form unbiased estimates for expectations under the target policy. Formally,

E_{z ∼ π_tar}[f(z)] = E_{z ∼ π_beh}[(π_tar(z)/π_beh(z)) f(z)].

The key is that the importance weight should correct the distribution at the same granularity as the expectation we care about, and it needs enough samples to make the correction meaningful.

GRPO (and PPO adapted to language models) applies importance ratios at each token position:

w_{t}(θ) = π_θ(y_t | x, y_{<t}) / π_{θ_old}(y_t | x, y_{<t}),

and uses those token-level weights (with clipping) to scale token log-likelihood gradients. But the reward signal in most RL for language is assigned to entire sequences or responses r(x,y), not individual next-token distributions. This mismatch creates two linked issues:

- Each token-level importance ratio is computed from a single sampled token. That single-sample ratio is a noisy estimate of how the whole next-token distribution has shifted, so it injects high variance into the gradient.
- The per-token clipping and unequal token weights can accumulate unpredictably across long sequences, producing noisy, biased updates that destabilize training—this is especially harmful when sequence lengths grow or when model changes (e.g., MoE routing) alter token likelihoods.

Empirically, this can lead to irreversible collapse: once the model drifts into a degenerate regime under these noisy updates, merely reverting hyperparameters or checkpoints often cannot recover stable training.

## GSPO’s fix: sequence-level importance ratio and equal token weighting

GSPO replaces token-level importance weights with a sequence-level importance ratio and performs clipping at the response level. Concretely, for a sampled response y with length |y|, GSPO defines the (length-normalized) sequence importance ratio

s(θ) = (π_θ(y | x) / π_{θ_old}(y | x))^{1/|y|}
     = exp( (1/|y|) ∑_{t=1}^{|y|} log (π_θ(y_t | x, y_{<t}) / π_{θ_old}(y_t | x, y_{<t})) ).

The algorithm then computes group-based advantages among G responses to the same query and optimizes a clipped objective that uses s(θ) to weight each full-response advantage. Two design choices are key:

- Length normalization of the sequence ratio stabilizes numerical scale across different response lengths.
- Tokens inside the same response are weighted equally in the gradient. The gradient contribution for a response becomes s(θ) times the average of the token log-likelihood gradients, rather than a sum of differently weighted token gradients.

Why this helps:

- The unit of correction now matches the unit of reward: we correct at the response/sequence level because the reward is given to the whole response. That aligns importance sampling with the quantity we actually evaluate.
- Averaging log-likelihood changes across tokens reduces variance versus relying on single-token ratios. The length-normalized sequence ratio is a more robust indicator of how "off-policy" the whole response is.
- Equal token weights prevent a few token-level ratio fluctuations from disproportionately shifting updates, so instability from accumulation across positions is greatly reduced.

## Practical consequences: stability, efficiency, and scaling

From the paper’s experiments and analysis (summarized here without introducing new claims), GSPO brings several concrete benefits:

- Training stability: GSPO produces smooth, continued improvements in reward and downstream benchmark performance while GRPO can suffer catastrophic collapses, especially as response length and model scale increase.
- Better sample efficiency: Even though GSPO clips at the response level (and therefore may clip a larger fraction of tokens compared to token-level clipping), it yields more effective gradient signals and faster performance gains per training compute.
- Better behavior with longer generations: Because variance accumulates more slowly under sequence-level weighting, GSPO tolerates lengthening generations and regular updates to the query distribution.

A somewhat counter-intuitive empirical observation reported in the paper: GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO (when comparing their respective clipping rules), yet still achieves higher training efficiency. This indicates that the token-level gradients GRPO retains are noisy and less useful, whereas GSPO’s retained samples produce cleaner, more effective updates.

## Why GSPO matters for MoE models

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts models bring additional instability because expert activation (routing) can change between policy updates. Under GRPO, token-level importance ratios can swing wildly when the underlying computational path (which experts are activated) changes, further invalidating token-level corrections.

Previously, the authors used a Routing Replay trick: cache the exact expert routing from the old policy and replay it when evaluating token likelihoods under the new policy, so both numerator and denominator share the same activated network. That stabilizes GRPO but adds memory, communication, and capacity limitations.

GSPO avoids this entirely by relying on sequence-level likelihoods, which are far less sensitive to per-token routing differences. As the paper argues, the model’s overall language-modeling capability ensures sequence likelihoods do not fluctuate as drastically as individual token likelihoods when routing changes. Therefore, GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay, simplifying infrastructure and removing that extra overhead while restoring stable convergence for MoE RL.

## Additional infrastructure benefit

Another practical advantage is that GSPO tolerates precision discrepancies between training and inference engines better. Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it can use the likelihoods returned by inference engines directly instead of recomputing token-level likelihoods in the training engine. This reduces recomputation cost and simplifies workflows in disaggregated or partial-rollout setups.

## Takeaway

GSPO’s central idea is simple and principled: match the granularity of importance-correction and clipping to the granularity of the reward (the whole response). By switching from noisy, single-sample token-level ratios to length-normalized sequence-level ratios and by treating tokens within a response equally, GSPO substantially reduces variance in policy gradients, prevents the accumulation of destabilizing noise across long sequences, and removes brittle workarounds required by sparse MoE architectures.

For practitioners scaling RL with large LLMs—especially with long generations or MoE architectures—GSPO provides a stable, efficient, and infrastructure-friendly alternative to token-weighted approaches that have proven fragile at scale.



# MoE Models, Meet Your Match: GSPO Without Routing Replay

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models give us a powerful way to scale language models: at each token, only a subset of experts are routed and activated, which keeps computation manageable while increasing capacity. But that very sparsity introduces a thorny instability during reinforcement learning (RL): expert-activation volatility. When experts activated for the same response change across gradient updates, token-level importance ratios become meaningless and training can fail. In practice, people introduced Routing Replay—a hack that replays cached routing decisions from the old policy to stabilize token-level importance weights. Routing Replay works, but at a cost: extra memory/communication overhead and limited effective MoE capacity.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a much cleaner solution. By moving importance weighting and clipping to the sequence level, GSPO sidesteps expert-activation volatility entirely and thus removes the need for Routing Replay. The result is simpler, faster, and more reliable RL for MoE models.

## The core problem: expert-activation volatility breaks token-level importance weights

Standard off-policy RL in autoregressive models relies on importance sampling. GRPO (and PPO variants) compute per-token importance ratios
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
and then clip these ratios when optimizing with group-relative advantages. The idea assumes that these token-level ratios meaningfully correct the distribution mismatch between the old and current policy.

In MoE models this assumption fails in practice. After just one or a few gradient steps, the experts activated for the same token/response can change significantly. The paper reports roughly a 10% difference in activated experts per rollout sample for a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model after an update. When the underlying computational graph that produced the token likelihood changes, the ratio w_{i,t} becomes noisy or invalid. This introduces high-variance gradients that:

- Accumulate across long sequences,
- Interact badly with clipping, and
- Can drive catastrophic, often irreversible model collapse.

Routing Replay was introduced to address this by caching and replaying the old routing decisions so that both numerator and denominator in w_{i,t} use the same activated network. That stabilizes GRPO for MoE, but it is an infrastructure-intensive workaround.

## GSPO’s idea: importance at the sequence level, not per token

GSPO takes a simple, theoretically principled turn: define the importance ratio using the sequence likelihood instead of per-token likelihoods. For a sampled response $y_i$ to query $x$, GSPO uses a length-normalized sequence importance ratio

s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\right).

GSPO then clips and weights whole responses by $s_i(\theta)$ and uses group-based advantages computed across multiple responses to the same query:
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}.

The objective is
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G \min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\big)\right].

Two practical details matter: (1) GSPO length-normalizes the sequence ratio (the 1/|y_i| exponent) to keep the ratios numerically stable across response lengths, and (2) clipping is applied to entire sequences, so off-policy samples are excluded at the response granularity.

## Why sequence-level ratios avoid routing volatility

There are two complementary reasons GSPO is robust to MoE routing volatility:

1. Sequence likelihood aggregates token likelihoods and reflects the model's overall preference for the response. Even if individual token likelihoods fluctuate due to different expert activations, the aggregated sequence likelihood is much less sensitive to per-token routing changes, because the model’s overall language modeling competence remains consistent.

2. GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally in the gradient. The gradient of the unclipped GSPO objective (omitting averages for clarity) contains the term
s_i(\theta) \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
so the sequence importance factor multiplies an averaged token-gradient. This avoids the unequal, per-token multiplicative noise introduced by token-wise importance ratios in GRPO, which can vary drastically when routing changes.

Because GSPO's importance ratio is defined at the sequence level, it does not require that numerator and denominator share identical activated experts for each token. Therefore, there is no need to cache and replay routing modes; GSPO can compute $s_i(\theta)$ conventionally and proceed without Routing Replay.

## Practical benefits: simpler, faster, and full-capacity MoE training

Removing Routing Replay has concrete advantages that the paper documents:

- Simpler infrastructure: no need to store and replay routing modes or to coordinate extra communication between rollout and training systems.
- Lower overhead: Routing Replay consumes memory and communication bandwidth; GSPO avoids these costs.
- Full model capacity: Routing Replay can constrain the effective capacity of an MoE by forcing reuse of past routing patterns. GSPO lets the model use its routing flexibly at each update.
- Stability and efficiency: Empirically, GSPO converged stably on MoE models where GRPO required Routing Replay, and achieved better training efficiency and downstream performance under matched compute/query budgets.

The paper also notes an extra practical upside: because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods (rather than per-token likelihoods), it is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between rollout/inference engines and training engines, enabling simpler rollout-to-train pipelines in disaggregated frameworks.

## A short caution: sequence-level vs. token-level trade-offs

GSPO is motivated by aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward: rewards are sequence-level, so importance sampling and clipping should be sequence-level too. If finer-grained token-level advantages are required (for multi-turn scenarios or per-token shaping), the authors also propose a GSPO-token variant that retains sequence-level importance scaling while allowing token-wise advantage customization. Numerically, GSPO-token is constructed so that its sequence importance factor is detached from token-level gradients to preserve the same stability properties.

## Takeaway

For MoE models, the practical instability comes from routing-induced fluctuations at the token level. GSPO sidesteps this by elevating importance weighting to the response level: length-normalized sequence likelihoods, sequence-level clipping, and group-based advantages. That single change removes the need for Routing Replay, simplifying infrastructure, lowering overhead, and enabling stable, efficient RL training that leverages the full capacity of MoE architectures. According to the paper, these properties were instrumental in scaling RL for the Qwen3 models.



# Clipping More, Learning Faster? The Counterintuitive Edge of GSPO

## TL;DR

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) intentionally clips whole responses (sequences) rather than individual tokens. Empirically it ends up clipping a much larger fraction of tokens than a token-level method like GRPO — yet GSPO trains faster, is more stable, and produces better downstream performance. The reason is simple: GSPO trades many noisy token-level gradient contributions for fewer, but much cleaner, sequence-level updates. Those cleaner signals give better sample efficiency and avoid the instability that causes catastrophic collapse in large or sparse models.

This post explains why that counterintuitive tradeoff works and why it matters in practice for large language model RL, especially for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models and realistic training-inference pipelines.

## The core mismatch in GRPO: importance sampling at the wrong granularity

Both GRPO and GSPO operate in an off-policy RL setup: you sample responses under an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and use them to update a new policy $\pi_\theta$. Importance sampling corrects the mismatch between sampling and target distributions. The textbook identity is:

E_{z ~ \pi_\text{tar}}[f(z)] = E_{z ~ \pi_\text{beh}}\left[\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z)\right],

which is meaningful when the importance weight $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ corrects for a distributional shift over many samples.

GRPO applies importance weights at each token: for token $y_{i,t}$ it uses
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
and then clips these per-token weights before multiplying by a group-based advantage. But the reward and advantage are defined at the sequence (response) level, not at each next-token distribution. Using a single sampled token to estimate the corrective role of an importance weight is high-variance and often invalid: we are trying to correct the whole next-token distribution with one sample. That mismatch introduces noisy gradient contributions that accumulate over long sequences and over many tokens. In practice that noise is amplified by the token-level clipping, and can lead to catastrophic, often irreversible training collapse as model size, depth, response length, or sparsity increase.

## GSPO’s fix: importance sampling and clipping at the sequence level

GSPO flips the granularity to match the unit of reward and importance sampling. It defines a sequence-level importance ratio (with length normalization):

s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).

GSPO then clips and weights whole responses by $s_i(\theta)$ and uses group-based advantages computed across multiple responses to the same query. Conceptually this does three important things:

- It aligns the unit of importance correction with the unit of reward. The weight $s_i(\theta)$ meaningfully reflects how "off-policy" the entire response is.
- By length-normalizing the sequence ratio, it controls the numerical scale and reduces variance across different lengths.
- By clipping entire sequences, GSPO excludes overly off-policy responses rather than muting many individual token gradients inside otherwise reasonable responses.

The gradient of the unclipped GSPO objective is proportional to
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
so tokens in the same response contribute equally (modulo the shared scalar $s_i$), rather than receiving unequal and noisy per-token weights.

## Why "clipping more tokens" can be better

One of the striking empirical findings reported with GSPO is that, with their tuned clipping ranges, GSPO clips a much higher fraction of tokens than GRPO — by about two orders of magnitude — yet still learns faster and achieves higher final performance. How can clipping more (i.e., using fewer token-gradient contributions) improve learning?

The intuition:

- GRPO keeps many token-level gradients but each token gradient is multiplied by a noisy importance weight derived from a single sample of the next-token distribution. These per-token weights have high variance and can systematically bias or destabilize updates as noise accumulates across token positions and across updates. Token-level clipping cannot remove the fundamental mismatch: it only truncates individual weights but still lets through many noisy contributions.
- GSPO discards entire responses that are too off-policy and keeps intact the gradients from responses that are within the clipping band. The kept gradients are sequence-consistent and low-variance relative to the token-weighted gradients in GRPO. So even though more tokens are clipped overall, the remaining gradients are "cleaner" and more informative for improving the sequence-level objective (the actual reward).
- Cleaner sequence-level signals mean better sample efficiency: each preserved response provides a stable, coherent update aligned with the reward, rather than a mixture of many noisy token-level pushes and pulls.

In short: fewer but higher-quality gradient signals beat many noisy ones.

## Practical payoffs

The paper highlights several practical benefits from this change in granularity.

Stability and performance. GSPO shows stable training curves where GRPO can suffer catastrophic collapse, especially on large models and long-response tasks. With GSPO, Qwen3 models could be scaled with continued RL training without instability-related setbacks.

MoE models: no more Routing Replay. MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) models exhibit "expert-activation volatility": after a gradient update the set of active experts for the same response can shift, which makes per-token importance weights wildly unstable. Previously, the authors mitigated this with a Routing Replay trick that forces $\pi_\theta$ to use the same routed experts as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ when computing token likelihoods — an extra complexity that increases memory/communication overhead and constrains true model capacity. GSPO is robust to expert-activation volatility because it relies on sequence likelihoods rather than per-token likelihoods. Empirically GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay, simplifying training and allowing full model capacity to be used.

Training-inference practicalities. Many production RL pipelines generate samples with inference-serving engines and then recompute token likelihoods using a training engine to avoid precision discrepancies (which matter for token-level weights). Since GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods, it is experimentally more tolerant to such precision gaps. That opens the door to avoiding costly recomputation and simplifies disaggregated training-inference infrastructures.

## When might you still want token-level control?

GSPO has a token-level variant, GSPO-token, which effectively uses the same sequence-level scalar $s_i(\theta)$ but allows per-token advantages (e.g., in multi-turn setups where different tokens have different local credit). GSPO-token recovers flexibility while preserving the stable, sequence-based importance correction. So if you need fine-grained reward shaping, you can still get it without reverting to noisy per-token importance weights as in GRPO.

## Bottom line

GSPO demonstrates a clear lesson: match the granularity of your importance correction and clipping to the granularity of your reward and optimization objective. Token-level importance weights look appealing, but when the reward is sequence-level they can produce large variance and instability, particularly for long outputs and sparse/specialized architectures like MoE. Clipping more at the token level (by virtue of clipping whole sequences) can paradoxically yield better learning because you end up optimizing only on reliable, coherent sequence-level signals.

For RL over large language models, especially in the regimes of long responses, large batches, and sparse architectures, GSPO’s cleaner sequence-level updates give better sample efficiency, stronger stability, and simpler infrastructure — even though it clips many more tokens.



# Beyond Value Models: Simpler RL Infrastructure with GSPO

## TL;DR

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a practical PPO-style algorithmic redesign that (1) removes the need for a heavy value model by using group-based sequence advantages, (2) defines importance weights at the sequence level rather than per-token, and (3) is tolerant to likelihoods produced by inference engines. These three properties together let you simplify RL pipelines: fewer large auxiliary models to host, less recomputation of token log-probs, easier partial rollouts and multi-turn setups, and simpler training–inference disaggregation — all while improving stability and efficiency, especially for large and sparse models like MoEs.

## Why the infrastructure pain exists today

Most modern RL setups for language models trace back to PPO. Two practical burdens are especially painful at scale:

- Value model cost and fragility. PPO requires a value function (often a model comparable in size to the policy) to estimate token-level advantages. Keeping, training, and serving that extra model increases memory, compute, and engineering complexity, and the algorithm’s effectiveness depends heavily on the value estimator’s reliability — which gets harder as response length grows.

- Token-level likelihood dependence. Tokenwise importance ratios w_t = π_θ(y_t | context) / π_{θ_old}(y_t | context) are used to correct for off-policy samples. In practice, we often generate rollouts using an inference stack (vLLM, SGLang, etc.) but recompute token log-probs on the training stack (Megatron) to get consistent, high-precision likelihoods. That recomputation is costly, introduces coupling between inference and training engines, and complicates partial-rollout or disaggregated pipelines.

These two issues are amplified when scaling: longer responses (more accumulated variance), large models (MoE routing volatility), and the need to maximize hardware utilization with big rollout batches and mini-batching.

## GSPO: two small changes with big infra benefits

GSPO makes two principled changes compared to token-level PPO/GRPO that directly reduce infrastructure needs:

1. Group-based sequence advantages (no value model).  
   Instead of estimating token-level advantages with a value model, GSPO computes relative advantages across a group of responses to the same query:
   \widehat{A}_i = ( r(x,y_i) - mean(\{r\}) ) / std(\{r\}).
   All tokens in response y_i share the same advantage \widehat{A}_i. This removes the value model entirely and still provides an effective learning signal.

2. Sequence-level importance weights and clipping.  
   GSPO uses a sequence likelihood importance ratio (length-normalized) per response:
   s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
   and does clipping on that sequence-level scalar rather than per-token clipping. This aligns the unit of optimization (sequence) with the unit of reward and gives a numerically stable, lower-variance correction compared to per-token weights.

These changes also have a token-level variant (GSPO-token) that keeps the same numerical treatment while allowing per-token advantages when needed (e.g., certain multi-turn setups), but the core infra benefits derive from using sequence-level likelihoods.

## How that simplifies RL infrastructure

Here are the concrete infrastructure simplifications GSPO enables, grounded in the paper’s findings:

- Eliminate a large value model. With group-relative advantages, the extra model and all associated memory, GPU hosting, and synchronization logic are unnecessary. That reduces operational cost and removes a frequent source of instability.

- Avoid recomputing token log-probs on the training engine. Because GSPO only needs sequence-level likelihoods (the product of token probabilities, length-normalized), it is empirically much more tolerant to precision differences between inference and training engines. In practice this means you can use the likelihoods returned by your inference stack directly for optimization instead of recomputing them on the training stack. That eliminates costly recompute cycles and the tight coupling between rollout/inference and training engines.

- Easier partial rollouts and multi-turn workflows. In partial rollouts or multi-turn scenarios you may only materialize parts of sequences in an inference engine. GSPO’s reliance on sequence likelihoods (and optional GSPO-token for per-token advantages while still anchoring gradients with a detached sequence scalar) fits these workflows better: you can collect and score responses on the inference side and pass compact sequence-level statistics to the trainer, reducing data movement.

- Training–inference disaggregation becomes practical. By removing the need for exact tokenwise consistency and a second model, you can fully decouple the inference service (used for data collection and scoring) from the training system. This separation streamlines deployment (different hardware/serving requirements), scaling (independent autoscaling), and engineering ownership.

- No Routing Replay for MoE stability. GRPO required a Routing Replay trick for MoE models: cache the activated experts under π_{θ_old} and replay those routings when computing token likelihoods under π_θ, to avoid expert-activation volatility breaking token-level importance ratios. GSPO sidesteps this complexity because it uses sequence-level likelihoods that remain stable even when per-token activated experts differ across updates. That removes the memory/communication overhead and capacity constraints imposed by replaying routing modes.

## Practical implications and caveats

- Clipping behavior changes. Because s_i(\theta) is length-normalized, clipping ranges are numerically different (often orders of magnitude different) from token-level clipping; you need to tune them accordingly. The paper reports stable training with small absolute clipping ranges on s_i.

- More clipping (but better signal). Empirically GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens (because clipping is applied per-sequence), yet it still achieves higher training efficiency than token-level methods. This counterintuitive outcome suggests token-level gradient estimates can be noisy and detrimental; sequence-level clipping removes many such spurious contributions.

- GSPO-token exists when per-token granularity is required. If you need to apply tokenwise rewards or per-step advantages (e.g., detailed multi-turn credit assignment), GSPO-token provides a way to keep per-token advantage flexibility while anchoring correction with a detached sequence-level scalar.

- Precision tolerance is empirical. The paper shows GSPO is “much more tolerant” of inference/training precision discrepancies for sequence likelihoods, enabling use of inference-side likelihoods without recompute. You should still validate precision and stability in your stack (different inference engines and quantization choices can still affect likelihoods), but the tolerance margin is substantially larger than with per-token importance ratios.

## Takeaways

If your RL pipeline is suffering from operational complexity — heavy value models, frequent recomputation of token log-probs, brittle MoE training, or a desire to separate inference and training systems — GSPO offers a principled and practical path to simplify the stack. By moving from token-level to sequence-level importance ratios and adopting group-relative advantages, you:

- Remove the value model and the costs it brings,
- Reduce the need to recompute token log-probs on the training engine (use inference engine outputs instead),
- Make partial rollouts and multi-turn workflows much easier,
- Eliminate Routing Replay and stabilize MoE RL, and
- Enable cleaner training–inference disaggregation.

These infrastructure benefits come together with improved training stability and efficiency as reported in the paper, which helped power successful large-scale RL in the Qwen3 models. If you’re building or scaling RL pipelines for language models, GSPO is worth evaluating as both an algorithmic and an engineering simplification.



# GSPO-token: When You Still Need Token-Level Nuance

## TL;DR
GSPO-token gives you the best of both worlds: it preserves GSPO’s sequence-level, low-variance importance weighting and clipping while allowing per-token advantages so you can give finer, position-specific feedback (useful in multi-turn, safety-sensitive, or instruction-following scenarios). Numerically and in gradient form it is consistent with GSPO, but it exposes a practical API to assign token-wise rewards without falling back to the unstable token-level importance weighting used in GRPO.

## Why GSPO-token?
GRPO and other token-level importance-weighted approaches apply importance ratios per token:
w_{i,t}(θ) = π_θ(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / π_{θ_old}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}).

As the paper shows, using these token-level ratios as gradient weights introduces high variance and instability—this problem is amplified for long responses, large models, and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. GSPO addressed this by moving to sequence-level importance ratios:
s_i(θ) = (π_θ(y_i | x) / π_{θ_old}(y_i | x))^{1/|y_i|},
and performing sequence-level clipping and optimization. That stabilizes training because importance weighting aligns with the unit of reward (the whole response), and the length-normalization controls variance.

However, there are practical settings where you want finer-grained control than a single scalar per response:
- Multi-turn or hierarchical RL where different tokens correspond to different turns or objectives.
- Safety-sensitive tasks where you want to penalize or reward specific tokens/phrases.
- Instruction-following where certain tokens (e.g., mandated statements) should be emphasized more than others.

GSPO-token is designed for these situations: it preserves GSPO’s stable sequence-level correction and clipping, but lets you plug in token-level advantages.

## What GSPO-token does (formally)
GSPO-token uses the GSPO-style sequence importance ratio as the controlling factor, but allows token-wise advantages inside the loss. The objective is:

J_{GSPO-token}(θ) =
E_{x, {y_i}~π_{θ_old}}
[ (1/G) ∑_{i=1}^G (1/|y_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
  min( s_{i,t}(θ) Â_{i,t}, clip(s_{i,t}(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) Â_{i,t} )
].

Crucially, s_{i,t}(θ) is defined so that it is numerically equal to the sequence-level score s_i(θ) but avoids backpropagating token-level likelihood ratios:

s_{i,t}(θ) = sg[s_i(θ)] · ( π_θ(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / sg[π_θ(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})] ).

Here sg[·] denotes stop-gradient (a numerical detach). Because the ratio π_θ(y_{i,t}|...) / sg[π_θ(y_{i,t}|...)] evaluates to 1 numerically, s_{i,t}(θ) is numerically s_i(θ). The detach prevents token-level importance weights from contributing gradients; only the sequence-level term s_i(θ) (detached numerically into token positions) influences the gradient magnitude.

When you set Â_{i,t} = Â_i for all tokens, GSPO-token reduces exactly to GSPO in objective and gradient. But if you provide distinct Â_{i,t}, you get token-wise shaping of the gradient while keeping the stable sequence-level correction.

## Gradients — why stability is retained
Omitting clipping for clarity, the gradient of GSPO-token can be written as:

∇_θ J_{GSPO-token}(θ) =
E_{x,{y_i}~π_{θ_old}}
[ (1/G) ∑_{i=1}^G s_i(θ) · (1/|y_i|) ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
  Â_{i,t} ∇_θ log π_θ(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ].

Compare this to GRPO’s gradient:

∇_θ J_{GRPO}(θ) =
E[ (1/G) ∑_i Â_i · (1/|y_i|) ∑_t (π_θ(y_{i,t}|...) / π_{θ_old}(y_{i,t}|...)) ∇_θ log π_θ(y_{i,t}|...) ].

The problematic term in GRPO is the per-token multiplicative factor π_θ(y_{i,t}|...)/π_{θ_old}(y_{i,t}|...), which varies across tokens and can be far from 1 (and fluctuates with expert activation in MoE). GSPO-token avoids injecting these token-level importance ratios into the gradient; instead it multiplies the entire token-sum by the sequence-level s_i(θ). That keeps the per-token signals (the ∇_θ log π terms) evenly weighted within a response and controlled by a single low-variance scalar s_i(θ).

Because s_i(θ) is computed from the full sequence likelihood (length-normalized), it has a clear importance-sampling interpretation and does not depend on single-token realizations. That is the root reason GSPO-token inherits GSPO’s stability.

## Practical implications and recommended uses
- Use GSPO-token when you need token-level feedback: multi-turn RL, selective safety penalties, or when different tokens map to different reward sources.
- Keep advantages Â_{i,t} standardized (e.g., z-score within the group) as in GSPO to avoid scale issues.
- Retain sequence-level clipping: clip(s_{i,t}(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) — even though s_{i,t} is numerically s_i(θ), clipping at the sequence granularity is essential to control off-policy samples.
- For MoE models, GSPO-token retains the advantage of avoiding Routing Replay and the instability caused by expert-activation volatility, since the optimization does not require per-token importance ratios that would otherwise be sensitive to routing changes.
- Implementation note: s_{i,t} uses stop-gradient on both s_i(θ) and token likelihood denominators, so ensure your framework detaches as specified (e.g., .detach() in PyTorch).

## Summary
If you simply want stable, robust RL for language models, GSPO is the right baseline: sequence-level importance ratios, length-normalized weighting, and sequence-wise clipping. When you also need per-token control, GSPO-token provides that flexibility without reverting to GRPO’s unstable token-level importance weighting. It is numerically consistent with GSPO, preserves the low-variance, theoretically grounded sequence correction, and gives you a practical lever for token-wise advantage shaping.

If you are designing multi-turn or safety-sensitive RL objectives, try GSPO-token to get fine-grained credit assignment while keeping training stable.



# Scaling Qwen3 with GSPO: Practical Tips and Takeaways

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) is a simple change with outsized practical impact: instead of applying importance weights and clipping at the token level, GSPO computes importance ratios at the sequence (response) level and clips entire responses. In Qwen3 training this design choice produced more stable RL, better sample efficiency, and made RL work reliably for large MoE models without special routing hacks. Below I summarize the practitioner-facing lessons from that work and the concrete decisions that mattered in production runs.

## High-level intuition (short)
- Importance sampling should correct the distribution over whole sequences, because the reward is granted at the sequence level. Token-level importance ratios (as used by GRPO/PPO variants) are computed from single-token draws and act more like high-variance noise when you use them to reweight gradients across long sequences.
- GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio and then length-normalizes it so ratios for different-length responses are comparable. Clipping and advantage estimation are done per response, not per token. This both stabilizes gradients and matches the unit of reward to the unit of correction.

## Picking group size G
GSPO follows the group-relative advantage idea (as in GRPO) for value-free advantage estimation: for each query x you sample a group of G responses {y_i} and compute normalized advantages across that group. Practical takeaways from the paper:

- Use a non-trivial group size (G > 1). The group is essential because the advantage for each response is computed as the standardized score within the group:
  \[
  \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j)}.
  \]
  A group of size 1 removes this standardization and loses the relative ranking signal.
- The paper does not prescribe a single best G; choose G large enough to give reliable mean/std estimates but small enough to fit memory and throughput constraints. In production Qwen3 experiments a group-based scheme was used (the paper refers to typical group setups), and the mini-batching strategy (splitting rollout batches into multiple mini-batches) requires G > 1 to benefit from relative advantage normalization.

If in doubt: start with the same grouping scheme you used for GRPO experiments; GSPO benefits from the same group-based sampling but is significantly more robust to the downstream optimization because it clips at sequence level.

## Clipping ranges: much smaller than token-level methods
One of the most surprising but important practical facts from the experiments is that GSPO uses clipping ranges that are orders of magnitude smaller than GRPO/PPO token-level clipping. In the reported Qwen3 fine-tuning runs:

- GSPO left/right clipping ranges were around 3e-4 / 4e-4 (i.e., extremely tight when applied to the length-normalized sequence ratio).
- GRPO’s token-level clipping ranges used for fair comparison were 0.2 / 0.27.

Why this is reasonable:
- GSPO's importance ratio is
  \[
  s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
  = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right),
  \]
  i.e., a per-token normalized geometric mean of likelihood ratios. After this normalization, typical deviations are numerically small and should be clipped much more tightly than token-level ratios.
- Empirically, GSPO clipped a far larger fraction of tokens/responses (two orders of magnitude difference in clipped-token fraction), yet still produced more efficient learning. This demonstrates that the token-level gradient estimates in GRPO are noisier even when many tokens go unclipped.

Practical recipe:
- Expect clipping thresholds to be tiny when using length-normalized sequence ratios. Tune in the small range (1e-5 .. 1e-3) rather than typical PPO-style 0.1–0.3 ranges. The paper’s working numbers (3e-4 / 4e-4) are a good starting point for large models and long responses.

## Why length-normalization matters
Length normalization is a small but crucial design choice for the sequence ratio:

- Without dividing by sequence length $|y|$, sequence likelihood ratios are multiplicative over tokens and explode (or vanish) with longer sequences. That makes clipping ranges length-dependent and unstable across responses of different lengths.
- GSPO uses the per-token geometric mean (i.e., raising the sequence ratio to the power $1/|y|$). This keeps $s_i(\theta)$ numerically stable across varying $|y|$ and allows a single clipping policy to apply across lengths:
  \[
  s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}.
  \]
- The length-normalized ratio reduces variance and prevents a few token-probability changes from producing huge swings in sequence-level importance.

In practice: always apply length normalization for sequence-level importance ratios, especially for variable and long-generation tasks.

## Scheduling compute, query refresh, and generation length
The Qwen3 experiments reported continuous gains when the team increased training compute, refreshed queries, and extended generation length. The paper highlights these practices as complementary rather than contradicting each other.

Practical guidance:

- Schedule compute increases gradually: GSPO is stable enough to continue improving when you add more RL compute. If you hit plateaus, consider (a) increasing rollout volume, (b) increasing update steps, or (c) increasing group size—each affects how much signal you feed into the objective.
- Regularly refresh the query set: the paper reports that updating the RL query set during training contributes to continuous performance improvement. In other words, avoid training for many steps on a stale query distribution; periodically add new or harder queries to keep the model improving on current needs.
- Increase generation length when appropriate: GSPO remains stable as you extend response length, because the sequence-level ratio is length-normalized. If your task benefits from longer generations (deeper reasoning or multi-step answers), extend generation length to let RL practice those behaviors, and GSPO should continue to learn rather than destabilize.

A practical schedule used in the experiments: scale training compute, update the query set at intervals, and extend generation length as model capability and task requirements grow. The exact cadences depend on infrastructure and labeling costs, but the core point is GSPO scales with these knobs without the catastrophic instability observed under GRPO.

## MoE-specific benefits: no Routing Replay required
Large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models introduce a new failure mode for token-level importance ratios: expert-activation volatility. After a parameter update the set of experts activated for the same response can change (the paper observed roughly 10% difference in activated experts per update for a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), which makes token-level likelihoods under the new policy incomparable to the old policy unless you pin the routing decisions.

Previously the team used a Routing Replay trick: cache the activated experts under the behavior policy and replay those routing masks when scoring under the current policy. This stabilizes token-level ratios but adds memory, communication, and capacity constraints.

Why GSPO helps:
- GSPO uses sequence-level likelihoods and is robust to modest per-token routing changes because the overall sequence likelihood remains a sensible measure of how probable the whole response is under each policy.
- In practice GSPO removed the need for Routing Replay: it converged and optimized MoE models stably with conventional likelihood computation, simplifying infrastructure and allowing full model capacity during RL.

If you are training MoE models, try GSPO first. It can eliminate the extra engineering complexity and overhead of routing-replay schemes.

## Infrastructure and likelihood recomputation
GSPO reduces sensitivity to precision/engine mismatch:

- In many deployments, rollout generation is done with an inference engine (vLLM / SGLang) while training uses a separate engine (Megatron). Token-level recomputation of likelihoods in the training engine can be required because of minor numeric differences between engines.
- Sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of those precision differences. GSPO therefore opens the door to using the inference-engine-provided sequence likelihoods directly for optimization without re-running every response in the training engine, which can reduce I/O, latency, and compute overheads in large-scale pipelines. This is particularly helpful in partial-rollout, multi-turn RL, and disaggregated train/infer setups.

## GSPO-token: when you want token-level customization
If you need token-level advantage shaping (for example, to weight earlier vs. later tokens differently in a multi-turn or partially supervised setting), GSPO offers a variant called GSPO-token. The key points:

- GSPO-token keeps the same sequence-level importance scalar detached from gradients, then applies token-level advantages per token but multiplies them by the same detached sequence ratio.
- When all token advantages in a response are equal, GSPO-token is numerically and theoretically identical to GSPO. Use GSPO-token only if you need per-token advantage modulation; otherwise prefer the simpler GSPO.

## Summary: practical checklist to get started
- Use GSPO (sequence-level importance ratio, length-normalized) instead of token-level GRPO/PPO variants when training RL on language models.
- Keep group sampling (G > 1); compute advantages by standardizing rewards within each group.
- Expect and tune very small clipping ranges for the length-normalized sequence ratio (start near the reported 3e-4 / 4e-4 range).
- Always length-normalize the sequence likelihood ratio: raise the sequence ratio to the power $1/|y|$ or equivalently average log-ratios over tokens.
- Schedule more compute, refresh queries periodically, and extend generation length as capability improves — GSPO is stable under all three.
- For MoE models, GSPO removes the need for Routing Replay and simplifies infrastructure.
- Consider GSPO-token only if you must customize token-level advantages.

These choices were central to the Qwen3 RL improvements described in the paper: more stable training, better sample efficiency, and reliable scaling to large MoE models. If you are scaling RL on language models, GSPO is a low-friction change that deserves to be part of your baseline.
