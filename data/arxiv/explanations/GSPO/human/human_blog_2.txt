# GRPO vs GSPO
## Introduction
Language models have become powerful tools for reasoning, coding and other complex tasks. After pre‑training on large text corpora, these models are often aligned with human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL). In RL fine‑tuning a policy (the language model) is updated to maximise a reward signal that reflects quality or user preference. Most RL algorithms used for language models, like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), rely on a separate value network to estimate future rewards. This value network consumes memory and compute, so researchers have been looking for alternatives that scale better with model size.
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) appeared as one such alternative. It removes the need for a separate value function and instead compares the quality of responses within a group of samples. More recently, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) has emerged as an improvement on GRPO. While GRPO estimates importance ratios at the token level, GSPO shifts the focus to entire sequences. This blog explains how GRPO works, why GSPO was proposed, and what you need to know when choosing between them. Citations come from publicly available sources such as the Qwen team’s blog and technical documentation.
## Why reinforcement learning matters for language models
In supervised fine‑tuning, a model learns from examples of correct answers. However, many tasks require open‑ended responses and subtle judgements that are hard to capture in a single correct label. RL fits naturally in these situations: the model generates responses, a reward model scores them, and the policy updates itself to produce better responses in the future. A family of policy optimisation algorithms, including PPO, DPO, ORPO and GRPO, has been developed to make this process stable.
Policy optimisation algorithms maintain two policies: the old policy that generated the current batch and the new policy that will be updated. They also use an **importance ratio** , the ratio of the new policy’s probability of producing a response to the old policy’s probability. This ratio is multiplied by an advantage value (how much a response is better or worse than a baseline) and then clipped to prevent large updates. The challenge is to compute the importance ratio and advantage in a way that leads to stable and efficient learning.
## What is GRPO?
## Core idea
Group Relative Policy Optimization is designed to train large language models without an additional value network. Instead of relying on a separate network to estimate future rewards, GRPO generates several candidate responses for each prompt and scores them with a reward model. The average score across the group is used as a baseline, and each response’s _advantage_ is the difference between its reward and this group average. The policy then updates itself to favour responses with higher advantages while staying close to the previous policy. Because the group average plays the role of a baseline, the algorithm reduces variance and saves memory.
## Training steps
In practice, GRPO proceeds through the following steps:
* **Sample multiple responses.** For each prompt, the current policy produces several possible answers.
* **Score responses.** A reward model assigns a numeric score to each answer (for example, awarding higher scores for correct mathematical derivations).
* **Compute the average reward.** The average score across all answers for a prompt becomes the baseline for that prompt.
* **Calculate advantages.** The advantage of each response is its score minus the average. Responses above the average have positive advantages, and those below have negative advantages.
* **Update the policy.** The policy multiplies the importance ratio at each token with the advantage and then applies a clipping procedure similar to PPO. A regularisation term, often a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty, prevents the new policy from drifting too far from the old one.

This process eliminates the value network but still provides a direction for improvement. The memory savings make GRPO attractive for large models and tasks requiring many samples.
## Strengths
* **Memory efficiency.** By removing the separate value network, GRPO reduces the memory footprint of RL fine‑tuning.
* **Group baseline reduces variance.** Using the group’s average reward as a baseline helps stabilise updates and improves performance, as seen in models like DeepSeek R1 for math and coding tasks.
* **Simple implementation.** GRPO’s steps mirror PPO but omit the value network, making it easier to implement on existing training pipelines.

## Limitations
Despite its advantages, GRPO has practical issues:
* **Token‑level importance ratios.** The importance ratio is computed at each token. This means that the ratio is a product of per‑token probabilities over the entire sequence. Token‑level sampling happens only once per token and can introduce high‑variance noise. The resulting noise destabilises gradient estimates and may cause training collapse over long runs.
* **Instability in Mixture‑of‑Experts models.** When GRPO is used on Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures, expert activation patterns can fluctuate. Training with GRPO often fails to converge without extra strategies like **routing replay** , which reuses past routing decisions to compute importance ratios. Routing replay adds memory and communication overhead and limits the model’s capacity.
* **Sensitivity to precision.** Because token‑level ratios amplify small differences across long sequences, GRPO is sensitive to numerical precision. This sensitivity can complicate infrastructure choices and may require recomputations on different hardware.

These limitations motivated the development of GSPO, which addresses GRPO’s weaknesses by changing the unit of optimisation from tokens to sequences.
## How GSPO differs from GRPO
## Sequence‑level importance ratios
In GRPO, the importance ratio is computed at the token level. However, the Qwen team observed that this approach produces high‑variance noise, making it ineffective for proper distribution corrections. Tokens are sampled only once, so the importance ratio can fluctuate widely across a sequence. Moreover, rewards are typically assigned at the sequence level (for the entire response), so aligning the unit of optimisation with the unit of reward makes more sense.
GSPO shifts the importance ratio computation from tokens to entire sequences. Instead of multiplying per‑token probabilities, it computes the ratio based on the likelihood of the whole generated sequence. It then applies clipping, reward weighting, and optimisation at the sequence level. This design results in several benefits:
* **Higher training efficiency.** GSPO achieves higher training efficiency than GRPO. Experiments on a cold‑start model showed that GSPO attained better performance under the same training cost. Increasing compute, refreshing the query set, and extending sequence length yielded continuous performance improvements.
* **Stable training.** Sequence‑level ratios smooth out noise and remove the volatility inherent in token‑level products. GSPO resolves the instability issues seen in GRPO and prevents model collapse during long training sessions.
* **Infrastructure friendliness.** Because GSPO uses the likelihood of entire sequences, it is less sensitive to minor precision discrepancies. It can directly use likelihoods computed by inference engines for optimisation without recomputing them on training hardware.
* **No routing replay.** GRPO requires routing replay for MoE models. GSPO’s sequence‑level design makes routing replay unnecessary, simplifying the training pipeline and freeing memory for larger model capacity.

## Clipped token fractions
One interesting observation in the Qwen experiments is that GSPO clips a higher fraction of tokens than GRPO, two orders of magnitude more. Despite this aggressive clipping, GSPO still delivers higher training efficiency, highlighting the inefficiency of token‑level updates. The sequence‑level approach provides a stronger learning signal even when more clipping occurs.
## Performance and empirical results
The Qwen team evaluated GSPO and GRPO on multiple benchmarks, including AIME’24 (a math competition), LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces. Using a cold‑start model fine‑tuned from Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base, they compared reward curves and performance metrics. Key findings include:
* **GSPO outperforms GRPO under equal compute.** For the same training budget, GSPO achieved higher rewards and better benchmark scores.
* **Scalability with more compute.** When training compute was increased and the query set refreshed, GSPO continued to improve, showing no signs of plateauing. GRPO, on the other hand, often plateaued or even collapsed at longer training horizons.
* **Reduced need for routing replay.** In MoE models, GRPO required routing replay to achieve convergence. GSPO removed this dependency, making training more straightforward and less resource‑intensive.

These empirical results suggest that GSPO is a more reliable choice for large models and long training runs, particularly when working with MoE architectures or limited infrastructure.
## Practical considerations
## When to use GRPO
* **Resource‑constrained environments.** If memory is extremely limited and training runs are short, GRPO still offers an advantage over PPO because it does not require a value network. For small models or quick experiments, token‑level variance may not severely affect stability.
* **Familiarity and existing tooling.** GRPO has been implemented in several open‑source frameworks and integrated into training tools. If your current pipeline already uses GRPO and you are training on relatively simple tasks, it may remain sufficient.

## When to choose GSPO
* **Long training runs.** Sequence‑level optimisation avoids the cumulative noise that arises from token‑level ratios. For projects requiring many RL steps or large query sets, GSPO is more stable and produces consistent improvements.
* **Mixture‑of‑Experts models.** MoE architectures are sensitive to routing patterns. GSPO’s design eliminates the need for routing replay and thus removes a significant source of overhead.
* **Infrastructure simplicity.** Because GSPO can use sequence likelihoods directly from inference engines, it reduces the need to recompute log probabilities on training devices. This can simplify the training‑inference pipeline and make RL fine‑tuning easier to scale across heterogeneous hardware.

## Broader context and future directions
The development of GRPO and GSPO reflects a wider trend in RL for language models: aligning the optimisation objective with the nature of the reward. Since reward models typically assess complete responses, it makes sense to operate at the sequence level. Token‑level methods may still have niche applications, but the trajectory of research points toward sequence‑level or even sub‑sequence‑level approaches. Some researchers have proposed subsequence‑level clipping methods to balance the granularity between tokens and full sequences, suggesting that GSPO is not the end of the road but a step along the path.
As models grow and tasks become more complex, the demands on RL algorithms will increase. Future work may explore adaptive clipping schemes, better variance reduction techniques, and algorithms that combine the strengths of GRPO and GSPO. For now, GSPO stands out as a practical and robust choice for training large language models with reinforcement learning.
## Key points :
* **GRPO** uses group averages to estimate advantages and computes importance ratios at the token level. It is memory‑efficient but suffers from high variance and instability, especially on long sequences and MoE models.
* **GSPO** introduces sequence‑level importance ratios and clipping. This change yields higher training efficiency, greater stability, and better scalability.
* **Empirical results** show that GSPO outperforms GRPO under equal compute and continues to improve with more compute, while GRPO can collapse during long training runs.
* **Infrastructure benefits** include the removal of routing replay for MoE models and better tolerance to numerical precision, making GSPO more infrastructure‑friendly.

Understanding the trade‑offs between GRPO and GSPO helps practitioners make informed choices when fine‑tuning language models with reinforcement learning. For most large‑scale applications, GSPO is likely to be the better default due to its stability and efficiency, while GRPO may still be useful in constrained settings where memory savings outweigh the risks of instability.
