\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the relationship between DPO and the “Best of N” baseline?
Question:
Both DPO and the Best‑of‑N (BoN) approach use the same underlying preference model, but BoN maximises the reward using one‑step sampling. Can we view BoN as a special case of DPO with a particular choice of \(\pi_{ref}\) or \(\beta\)? Why does DPO outperform BoN even though BoN can directly select the highest‑reward sample at inference time?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Best‑of‑\(N\) (BoN) can be seen as a *sampling‑based* way of estimating the optimal DPO policy, but it is not a special case of DPO.  DPO already optimizes the constrained‑reward objective **exactly** in parameter space; BoN only approximate that optimum by drawing random samples under the reference policy and picking the one with highest reward.  Because BoN relies on a finite budget of samples, it typically produces lower‑quality \(N\)-best responses than the policy that DPO learns to output directly, and it suffers from the same issues that make RL‑HF hard: bias toward the reference distribution and high variance when rewards are noisy.  That is why DPO can outperform BoN even though BoN has a “cheat” – picking the best sample – because DPO learns a *distribution* that already concentrates most of its probability mass on the high‑reward region, whereas BoN still samples from the reference distribution and only selects among the few draws it makes.

---

## 1.  BoN as an approximate DPO estimator

In BoN the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (usually the SFT model) is used to produce a set of \(N\) completions \(\{y^{(1)},\dots ,y^{(N)}\}\).  
Given a reward function \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) fitted from the same preference dataset, one selects

\[
\hat{y}_{\text{BoN}} = \arg\max_{i}\; r_{\phi}\!\bigl(x,y^{(i)}\bigr).
\]

If we look at it through the lens of the DPO objective (Equation \(\eqref{eq:optimum_model}\)), BoN is performing a *hard‑max* over a biased sample from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
In the limit \(N\!\to\!\infty\), the sampled set would contain an almost exact empirical realization of the *optimal policy* \(\pi^{*}\) that DPO computes.  Therefore BoN can be thought of as a Monte‑Carlo estimator of the DPO solution, but it is constrained to the reference distribution and a finite \(N\).

A key difference is that DPO directly *updates* the policy parameters \(\theta\) so that

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \;\propto\;
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\;\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

which is the analytical optimum of the KL‑constrained reward objective.  BoN has no such update; it merely chooses among the draws it has already made.

---

## 2.  Why BoN can’t beat DPO

| Aspect | BoN | DPO |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **Objective** | Maximize reward over a *finite* sample set | Maximize expected reward subject to KL‑divergence in *parameter space* |
| **Sampling bias** | Still samples from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (often SFT) | The learned policy is *the* probability distribution that satisfies the KL constraint |
| **Variance** | High, because it relies on a small \(N\) to approximate the optimum | Low: gradient is a smooth (cross‑entropy) objective |
| **Mode collapse** | Can occur if the reward function strongly prefers very few completions and these happen to be sampled rarely | The KL term regularizes against collapsing to a single answer |
| **Computational cost at inference** | Must generate \(N\) samples and evaluate each with a reward model | Generates a single token at a time (greedy or sampled) |

Because BoN evaluates only \(N\) draws, the probability that a *globally* optimal response is among those draws decays exponentially with the complexity of the task.  DPO, on the other hand, reshapes the whole distribution so that high‑reward outputs are *more probable*.  Even if the reward model is imperfect, the learned policy is still robust to noise thanks to the KL penalty that keeps it near the reference.  In practice, the Paper’s experiments show that for the same \(\beta\) hyperparameter, DPO yields higher win‑rates and higher reward‑KL frontiers than BoN, even when BoN uses the *same* reward model.

---

## 3.  What would be a “special case” of DPO?

Mathematically, one could artificially set the temperature \(N\) to infinity and replace the *sampling* step in BoN with a deterministic evaluation of all possible completions (i.e., evaluate \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)’s softmax distribution).  If you then replaced the reward model by the implicit reward \( \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \) and set \(\theta\) to a policy that *exactly matches* the Boltzmann reweighting (Equation \(\eqref{eq:op_policy}\)), you would recover the DPO optimum.  However, such a procedure is computationally infeasible because it would require enumerating all completions.  Hence BoN is *convergent* to DPO only in the asymptotic limit of infinite sampling, which is not realistic for language generation.

---

## 4.  Intuition from the reward‑policy correspondence

The DPO derivation shows that the reward model used in RLHF can be re‑parameterized purely in terms of the *ratio* of the target policy to the reference policy.  This means that the policy and the reward become two sides of the same coin.  BoN, by contrast, keeps the reward and the policy separate: the policy stays the reference distribution, while the reward is only used at inference to cherry‑pick a sample.  DPO turns the problem inside‑out: it first fixes the reward implicitly via the policy, then directly trains that policy.  Because the reward is no longer an external stand‑alone model, DPO eliminates the stochasticity of sampling during training, and the KL constraint prevents the policy from drifting too far—issues that plague pure sampling approaches like BoN.

---

## 5.  Take‑away

* **BoN is an *inference‑time* approximation of the solution DPO finds analytically.**  
* **DPO learns a policy that already embodies the optimal trade‑off between reward and reference probability, so it does not need multiple samples at inference.**  
* **Consequently, DPO exhibits lower variance, better generalization, and higher empirical performance on the tasks reported in the paper, even when BoN gets to use the same learned reward model.**  

So while BoN can be viewed as a heuristic that treats the optimal DPO policy as “sample‑and‑pick”, it is not a special case of DPO, and it is precisely the fact that BoN must rely on finite sampling that makes it inferior in practice.
