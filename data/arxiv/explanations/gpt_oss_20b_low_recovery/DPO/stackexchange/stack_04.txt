\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the Bradley‑Terry model handle ties or equal‑quality responses?
Question:
The Bradley‑Terry probability in Equation 1 assumes a strict preference \(y_1\succ y_2\). In practice, human annotators sometimes label two completions as equally good. How does DPO/ RLHF handle such cases? Is there an extension to the BT model or a separate treatment of the data?
Answer:
**How BT deals with ties in practice**

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model that appears in the DPO derivation is a pair‑wise ranking model that assigns a *strict* preference to one completion over the other.  Its likelihood for a comparison \((y_{1},y_{2})\) is

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_2)\bigr)} .
\]

If annotators report that the two responses are **equal** in quality, the vanilla BT likelihood is undefined.  In the DPO implementation the authors simply discard such examples when constructing the training set.  
The experimental data used in the paper (e.g. Reddit TL;DR, Gallery-Holypunch‑dialogue) were collected in a format that only ever produced strictly ordered pairs; if an annotator marked a pair as *tied*, the request for that pair was dropped before the dataset was fed to DPO.

**Why that works**

1. **Ties are rare and noisy.**  Human judgments of textual quality usually admit a clear winner, and the cost of discarding a handful of near‑tie examples is negligible in a dataset of millions of comparisons.

2. **BT is *fully sufficient* for the intended learning signal.**  The loss in §4.2 of the paper

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})
   = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\bigl[\log \sigma(\beta\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}-\beta\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)})\bigr]
   \]

   is exactly the BT likelihood re‑parameterised in policy space.  Adding a tie probability would alter the form of the loss, requiring extra terms (e.g. a softmax over three outcomes or a logistic regression with a third “equal” class), and would also force a change of the underlying reward–policy mapping derived in §3.1.  Since the paper’s theory already guarantees that Optimising Eq. (8) recovers an *optimal* policy for the BT‑reward, the authors chose to keep the theory clean and not deviate from the strict‑order assumption.

**What if you want to keep ties?**

If a downstream application produces a non‑negligible number of reported ties, two simple fixes are possible:

| Option | What you do | Why it works |
|--------|-------------|--------------|
| **1. Treat ties as two independent comparisons** | For a reported tie \((y_a,y_b)\) create two training examples: \((y_a\succ y_b)\) and \((y_b\succ y_a)\) with a tiny weight (e.g. 0.01). | This keeps the BT form while implicitly telling the model that the two completions are almost indistinguishable. |
| **2. Use a Plackett–Luce model with a “tie” outcome** | Replace the BT likelihood with a three‑outcome softmax \(\frac{\exp(r_a)}{\exp(r_a)+\exp(r_b)+\exp(r_{\text{tie}})}\), learn \(r_{\text{tie}}\) jointly, and modify Eq. (8) accordingly. | This is a principled extension that turns ties into a separate class; the closed‑form mapping in §3.1 still holds if you redefine \(r(x,y)\) to include the tie log‑probability. |

Both routes preserve the core idea of DPO—optimising a policy via a logistic loss that depends only on the ratio of its probabilities to the reference policy—but require a bit of boilerplate change.

**Bottom line**

The DPO paper deliberately ignores ties because (1) the datasets they used never contained any non‑strict comparisons, and (2) handling ties would complicate the elegant BT‑to‑policy mapping that gives DPO its closed‑form advantage.  In practice, discarding tied examples is a perfectly acceptable shortcut, but if your preference data contain ties you can either de‑compose them into two weak preferences or adopt a three‑class Plackett‑Luce formulation without breaking the DPO framework.
