\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is the hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) chosen and what does it control?
Question:
Throughout the paper \(\beta\) appears as the coefficient of the KL‑entropic term as well as in the DPO loss. In practice, the authors report sweeping over {0.05,0.1,1,5}. What is the theoretical interpretation of these values? How does a large vs. small \(\beta\) affect the trade‑off between staying close to the reference policy and following human preferences?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) the scalar $ \beta $ plays the same role as the *trust‑region* coefficient that appears in every RLHF objective of the form  

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\bigl[\,r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta\; \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl[\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\,|x)\bigr].
$$

Mathematically $ \beta $ is the Lagrange multiplier that balances two competing goals:

1. **Reward maximisation** – how much the policy should favour tokens that the (implicit) reward system thinks are good, which is captured by the log‑probability ratio term inside the DPO loss,
2. **KL‑regularisation** – how tightly the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference policy $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $ (usually the SFT or a carefully initialised LM).

When we re‑parameterise the reward as  

$$
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
$$

the coefficient $ \beta $ appears in two places:

* In the **interaction term** of the DPO loss:  
  $$
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\bigl(
  \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
  -\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\bigr)\Bigr] .
  $$
  The factor $ \beta $ rescales the *log‑ratio* that quantifies how wrong the current policy is at ordering the two responses. A large $ \beta $ makes the loss put more weight on examples where the policy mis‑orders a pair, thereby giving the optimiser a stronger incentive to pull the preferred completion up and push the dispreferred one down.  

* In the **KL‑term** that appears implicitly in the optimal policy: $ \pi^\star(y|x)\propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\hat r_\theta(x,y)/\beta) $.  
  When $ \beta $ is small the exponential weight changes the distribution only slightly, forcing the new policy to stay very close to $ \pi_{\text{ref}} $. When $ \beta $ is large the model can hedge more aggressively on the reward signal and may drift further from the reference policy.

---

### Intuitive picture

Think of the policy as a *soft‑max* over all tokens where the logits are the sum of two vectors:

* a **reference logit** $ \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) $, fixed for a given prompt, and  
* a **reward‑induced logit** $ \hat r_\theta(x,y)/\beta $.

Adjusting $ \beta $ changes how much weight the reward logit receives relative to the reference.  

* **Small $ \beta $** (e.g. $0.05$, $0.1$):  
  The reward part is heavily dampened. The updated policy remains close to the reference model. The algorithm behaves conservatively: it only follows human preferences when the reference already thinks the answer is good.  
* **Large $ \beta $** (e.g. $1$, $5$):  
  The reward part dominates. The model rapidly re‑weights its probabilities in favour of the preference‑favoured token, potentially at the cost of generating responses that differ more from the reference distribution. This can lead to higher reward but also higher KL divergence.  

In practice, the paper reports a sweep over $ \{0.05,\,0.1,\,1,\,5\} $.  
The results in the sentiment‑generation experiment (Figure 5 of the paper) show that a slightly larger $ \beta $ (around $1$) gives the best trade‑off: high reward with a modest KL penalty.  Very small $ \beta $ produced policies that almost never deviated from the SFT policy, yielding little gain in win‑rate. Very large $ \beta $ sometimes caused mode‑collapse or produced unrealistic outputs because the policy moved too far from the distribution where the reward model was trained.

---

### Practical implications

| Effect | Small $ \beta $ | Large $ \beta $ |
|--------|------------------|--------------------|
| **KL divergence to reference** | Low | High |
| **Bias toward reference policy** | Strong | Weak |
| **Follow‑through of human preferences** | Mild | Aggressive |
| **Risk of reward over‑optimisation / degeneration** | Low | Higher |
| **Sensitivity to noisy or miss‑specified reward signal** | Lower | Higher |

Because the reward that DPO implicitly optimises grows linearly with $ \beta $, the *scale* of the log‑ratio term also changes.  Consequently, the *loss surface* becomes steeper for larger $ \beta $, which means more aggressive gradient steps but also a higher chance of stepping into optima that satisfy the hard $ \text{KL}$ bound only weakly. In bounded‑accuracy experiments the authors found that $ \beta = 1 $ worked well across their three tasks (sentiment, summarisation, dialogue), and that the performance deteriorated only slightly for $ \beta = 0.05 $ or $ \beta = 5 $.

---

### Bottom line

- $ \beta $ is the regularisation coefficient that sets how far the fine‑tuned policy may drift from the reference model while still following human preferences.  
- A **small** value keeps the model conservative and close to the pre‑training distribution, which is safer when the reward estimate is noisy.  
- A **large** value allows the model to chase the reward signal more strongly, potentially improving alignment but at the cost of more deviation and a higher risk of mode collapse.  

The choice of $ \beta $ thus depends on the quality of the preference data, the risk tolerance for departing from the reference policy, and the particular downstream task, but in most practical settings the sweep over $ \{0.05,\,0.1,\,1,\,5\} $ and picking the value that gives the best trade‑off seen on a validation set (often $ \beta \approx 1 $) is an effective strategy.
