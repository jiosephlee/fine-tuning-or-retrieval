\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Is it possible to apply DPO to multi‑turn dialogue or other reinforcement learning tasks?
Question:
The paper demonstrated DPO on single‑turn dialogue, summarisation and sentiment control. Could DPO be extended to multi‑turn dialogue or tasks that require a long‑horizon reward signal? How would the KL‑constraint and the re‑parameterisation change in that setting?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – DPO can be applied to multi‑turn dialogue and to tasks that require a long‑horizon reward. The algorithm itself is agnostic to the length of the trajectory; it only needs a set of replacement‑pairs \((y_{\text{win}},y_{\text{lose}})\) that are sampled from the same policy that will be fine‑tuned. In a multi‑turn setting you generate two *full* dialogue histories up to the current turn, ask a human (or a heuristic) which one is preferable, feed the pair into the same DPO loss, and update the policy. The KL‑constraint and the re‑parameterised reward work exactly the same way, but you must compute the KL over the *entire* trajectory, not just one token. In practice you treat the whole dialogue as a single token sequence \(y\) and use the same \(\alpha\)-soft‑max form \(\pi_\theta(y|x)\) (where \(x\) now contains the previous context). This yields a *sequence‑level* DPO objective, which is simply

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}
   \Big[ \log \sigma\!\big(
        \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
      -\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
     \big)\Big].
\]

The only change is that \(\pi_\theta(y|x)\) is now the *joint probability* of the whole multi‑turn sequence and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the reference model for the same sequence.

Below is a more detailed discussion of why this works, what changes are required, and how the KL–constraint is handled in a long‑horizon setting.

---

### 1.  DPO in the original paper  

DPO’s core idea is that the optimal KL‑constrained policy for a reward \(r\) is

\[
\pi_r(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
\]

so the reward can be expressed in terms of the policy itself:

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x).
\]

Plugging this into the Bradley–Terry preference model cancels the partition function \(Z(x)\), giving a *policy‑only* preference likelihood. Optimising the negative log‑likelihood of this likelihood with respect to the policy yields the DPO loss in the paper. Crucially the loss depends **only** on the ratio of the policy to the reference policy and on the *log‑probability* of the whole sequence, which is a standard feature of transformer LM training.

### 2.  Why multi‑turn dialogue fits the same framework  

A multi‑turn dialogue can be seen as an *ordered* set of tokens. If you stop at turn \(t\), the model’s output is a whole sequence \(y = y_1,\dots,y_T\). In the DPO derivation nothing asserts that \(y\) must be a single token or a short response; the equations hold for any length. The only difference is the larger value of \(\log \pi_\theta(y|x)\), which is the sum of log‑probs of all tokens in the sequence. As long as you have a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that can produce the same sequence (e.g., the original SFT model), the ratio \(\pi_\theta(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\) is still well defined. Therefore, the same DPO loss can be applied verbatim to multi‑turn dialogue examples.

### 3.  Building a preference dataset for multi‑turn dialogue  

In single‑turn experiments the data set \(\mathcal D\) consists of pairs \((x,y_w,y_l)\), where \(y_w\) is the human‑preferred answer to prompt \(x\) and \(y_l\) is a competing answer. For multi‑turn dialogue you generate two complete *conversation histories* (from the same current state) and ask a human to pick the preferable one. The resulting pair can be fed into the same loss. The paper’s claim that DPO “has not yet been shown” on multi‑turn dialogue is simply a reflection of the lack of experiments, not a theoretical obstacle.

### 4.  Long‑horizon reward signal

When a task requires a *long‑horizon* reward (e.g., a goal‑driven dialogue where the reward is only awarded at the end), the reward function \(r(x,y)\) may be zero at every intermediate turn and non‑zero only at the final token. In the DPO re‑parameterisation this is perfectly fine: the log‑probability of the whole sequence automatically incorporates the entire horizon, and the KL‑constraint penalises only the *difference* between the new policy and the reference policy across all tokens. The re‑parameterised reward

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
\]

remains a *sequence‑level* quantity. In practice you do **not** need to modify the loss; the KL constraint continues to keep the policy close to the reference across the whole trajectory. If you want a “per‑turn” KL you can decompose the sequence log‑probability into a sum of token‑wise KL terms, but the DPO objective is already correct at the sequence level.

### 5.  KL‑constraint in a multi‑turn setting  

The paper’s KL term in the RL objective is \(\mathbb D_{\rm KL}[\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)]\). For a sequence, this is the *joint* KL over all tokens. Because language models factorise across tokens, you can compute it as the sum of per‑token KLs:

\[
\mathbb D_{\rm KL}[\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)]
   =\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb D_{\rm KL}\!\big[\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})\big].
\]

This decomposition is convenient if you want to clip or normalise the KL across turns. In the DPO loss you never compute this KL explicitly; it is implicitly enforced by the \(\beta\) multiplier in the log‑ratio. If you do want to monitor the KL during training, you can compute it token‑wise as shown above.

### 6.  Changing the hyperparameter \(\beta\)

In single‑turn experiments the authors sweep \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\). For long‑horizon tasks \(\beta\) plays the same role: it scales how strongly the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference. A larger \(\beta\) lets the policy explore risky trajectories (useful for goal completion); a smaller \(\beta\) enforces safety. The usual trick—starting with a conservative \(\beta\) and increasing it as training progresses—works unchanged because the KL constraint is still sequence‑level.

### 7.  Practical implementation notes

| Item | Single‑turn | Multi‑turn / long‑horizon |
|------|-------------|---------------------------|
| **Sampling** | Generate two completions, label them | Generate two *full* dialogue histories, or two *goal‑achieving* sequences |
| **Data format** | \((x, y_w, y_l)\) | Same format, but \(y\) can contain many turns |
| **Loss** | Eq. (7) of the paper | Same loss, no change |
| **KL computation** | E.g. global KL at request time | Token‑wise KL if you wish to analyze training dynamics |
| **Gradient scaling** | \(\beta\) keeps gradient magnitudes stable | Same *relative* scaling; you might need to clip overall norm because sequences are longer |
| **Reference policy** | Usually the SFT model | Same, or use an SFT fine‑tuned on comparable long dialogues |

Because the derivation is **sequential‑agnostic**, the loss does not care how many tokens are in \(y\). The only computational cost is that computing \(\pi_\theta(y|x)\) now means evaluating the LM on a longer sequence, but that is exactly what you already do during inference.

### 8.  Summary

* The DPO objective is mathematically defined over *any* pair of sequences, irrespective of length.  
* A multi‑turn or long‑horizon preference dataset can be collected exactly like a single‑turn one: two sequences, one labeled preferable.  
* The KL‑constraint remains a KL over the joint distribution of the whole trajectory.
