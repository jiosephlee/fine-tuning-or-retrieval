\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: # 5. Practical DPO Algorithm

Large‑scale language models (LMs) learned in an unsupervised fashion are difficult to steer toward a user‑desired behavior.  Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) removes the need for an explicit reward model and policy‑RL loop by optimizing a simple classification loss that implicitly encodes a reward function.  This chapter provides a concrete, step‑by‑step recipe for turning a preference dataset into a fine‑tuned LM, covering every stage from data curation to GPU‑efficient training and common pitfalls.

---

## 5.1  Preparing the Preference Dataset

The heart of DPO is a collection of preference triples, **(prompt, preferred‑generation, dispreferred‑generation)**.  Each tuple is denoted \((x, y_w, y_l)\), where a human (or an automated proxy) indicates that \(y_w\succ y_l\mid x\).  The dataset must satisfy two pragmatic requirements:

1. **Pairwise completeness** – For every prompt in the test set you are later going to condition on, the LM must have seen comparable pairs during training.  If the source of the data is a pre‑existing preference collection (e.g., the TL;DR Summarization dataset or Anthropic Helpful‑Harmless dataset), you must preserve the same prompt–response buckets, but you may augment with additional synthetic comparisons if desired.  

2. **Quality of the pairs** – The preferences should represent an underlying *ground‑truth* ranking according to a latent reward \(r^\ast(x, y)\).  Poor or noisy pairings will propagate into a sub‑optimal policy.  Whenever possible, reserve at least \(10 \%\) of the pairs for an *evaluation set* where you will compute thorough signal vs noise metrics (e.g., agreement between lay reviewers or an automatic metric that correlates with human judgments).  

### 5.1.1  From Raw Labeled Data to Triple Tuple

Sometimes the raw data arrives as comma‑separated columns or JSON objects.  One convenient paradigm is to store each training example in a line‑delimited JSON file:

```json
{"prompt":"Tell me a brief summary of the policy changes in 2023.","preferred":"The policy was updated to ...","dispreferred":"The policy was updated to ..."} 
```

The key steps to create this file:

| Step | Goal | Implementation Notes | 
|------|------|----------------------|
| **Read in raw pairs** | Load the original CSV/JSON lines. | Use `pandas.read_csv` or streaming `jsonlines`. |
| **Trim tokens** | Remove excessive whitespace and canonicalise case if needed. | `str.strip().strip('"')`. |
| **Verify length** | Ensure both `y_w` and `y_l` exceed a minimal token count; discard too‑short completions. | `len(tokenizer.encode(y)) > 10`. |
| **Re‑label if needed** | If the dataset contains `y1, y2, label`, map to the preferred/​dispreferred slots. | `if label==1: (y1,y2)→(y_w,y_l) else (y2,y1)`. |
| **Write out** | Append the tuple to the file. | `jsonlines.Writer`. |

Optionally, you may *augment* the dataset with synthetic comparisons by sampling new completions from the reference policy (described below) and automatically labeling them using a *proxy reward * (a pretrained classifier, a language‑model scoring function, or an oracle).  Care must be taken to avoid overwhelming the signal with synthetic data, which can undercut the human preference signal.

---

## 5.2  Choosing the Reference Policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \)

DPO requires a *reference policy* \( \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \) that serves two distinct roles:

1. **Baseline distribution** – It supplies the *KL‑divergence* term of the DPO objective.  
2. **Estimator for the probability ratio** – The reference policy appears in the log‑ratio \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) which forms the implicit reward.

The most common choice is the *Supervised Fine‑Tuning* (SFT) policy learned prior to DPO.  If you already have an SFT model (e.g., GPT‑2‑large fine‑tuned on a discussion forum dataset), simply reuse it.  In many experiments the paper opts for a *pseudo‑SFT* trained from scratch if an SFT is unavailable:

```python
# pseudo‑SFT: maximize likelihood over preferred responses
pi_ref = LM(...)
pi_ref.train(opt, 'log_softmax(y_w|x)', data=preference_data)
```

### 5.2.1  Generating \( y \) from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \)

When building a new preference dataset, you must collect pairs \((y_1, y_2)\) by sampling from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).  In practice, you want as many *unique*, *diverse* completions as possible:

```python
def sample_dual_prompts(x):
    y1 = pi_ref.sample(x, max_length=MAX_LEN, top_k=50, top_p=0.95)
    y2 = pi_ref.sample(x, max_length=MAX_LEN, top_k=50, top_p=0.95)
    return y1, y2
```

If computational resources are limited, limit the number of samples per prompt, but ensure that the pair diversity is high enough to cover the space of possible responses.

## 5.3  The DPO Training Objective

Once the dataset is ready and the reference policy is defined, we can formulate the DPO loss.  The objective for a single preference triple \((x, y_w, y_l)\) is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) =
-\,\log\!\bigl[
  \sigma\!\bigl(\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
      \;-\;
      \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\bigr)
\bigr]
}
\]

Here, \( \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\).  The loss is simply a binary cross‑entropy over the *predicted preference* \(\hat{p}= \sigma(\cdot)\), where the logits are the *log‑ratio* of the candidate policy to the reference policy, scaled by \(\beta\).  Note that the entire loss is differentiable because the cross‑entropy is back‑propagated through both exponential‑softmax terms in the policy’s forward pass.

### 5.3.1  The Implicit Reward

Define the *implicit reward* as

\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y) \;=\; \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.
\]

From the perspective of the gradient, \(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x, y)\) acts exactly like a *scalar score* per token sequence, weighting the update magnitude.  Because the loss already incorporates the log‑ratio, the *KL term* \(\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) is automatically enforced, so no separate KL penalty is needed.  The dynamic importance weight \(\sigma(\hat{r}_l - \hat{r}_w)\) guarantees that if the model mis‑orders a pair, that pair receives a bigger gradient, mitigating the “mode‑collapse” that often plagues vanilla probability‑ratio methods.

---

## 5.4  The Role of the Hyper‑Parameter \( \beta \)

The scalar \( \beta \) is the *temperature* of the exponential family used in the underlying RLKF lattice.  It determines the trade‑off between:

- **Reward maximization** – Larger \( \beta \) places more emphasis on higher implicit rewards.  
- **Conservation** – Small \( \beta \) forces the policy to stay close to the reference distribution.

In practice:

- \( \beta = 0.05 \) is a very conservative setting, rarely deviating from \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).  
- \( \beta \in [0.5, 5] \) provides a balanced trade‑off; the paper shows that values around \(1\) often give the best performance across tasks.  
- \( \beta > 5 \) can lead to a degenerate policy that collapses to a single best‑scoring completion (the “argmax” policy).  

**Tuning heuristic** – Start with \( \beta=1 \), then inspect the *KL statistics* over the validation set.  If the mean KL rises above the target (say 0.5 nats per token for controlled sentiment), lower \( \beta \); if the model's win‑rate is low, increase \( \beta \).

---

## 5.5  Efficient Probability Estimation for Generated Sequences

A practical difficulty is the evaluation of \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\) for *entire sequences* of arbitrary length.  Since language models are generative, probabilities factorise:

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y|x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x, y_{<t}).
\]

Direct multiplication quickly underflows, so we always operate in the log‑probability domain.  In PyTorch / HuggingFace, the standard `tokenizer.batch_encode_plus` followed by `model(**inputs).logits` yields the per‑token logits, from which the log‑probs are computed:

```python
logits = model(**inputs).logits  # [batch, seq_len, vocab]
logps = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
chosen_logps = logps.gather(-1, input_ids.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)  # [batch, seq_len]
log_prob_seq = chosen_logps.sum(-1)  # [batch]
```

To reduce memory footprint for very long sequences, you can slice the input into *segments*, compute partial log‑probs, and then sum them.  When scoring *many* sequences per prompt (e.g., in a “best‑of‑\(N\)” baseline), it is efficient to *batch* all sequences together and use *broadcasted* gather operations.

---

## 5.6  Handle Long‑Form Generation and Batching

### 5.6.1  Batching Strategy

The simplest batching scheme is *flat batching*: group all triples into a single large batch.  However, this can be wasteful because each triple has a different prompt length.  Instead, use **dynamic padding**:

1. **Group by prompt‑length** – Sort validation examples by the number of tokens in the concatenated prompt and chosen response.  
2. **Bucket small and large prompts separately** – Use two separate data loaders to maintain efficient GPU occupancy.  

### 5.6.2  Mixed‑Precision Training

All modern GPUs support 16‑bit floating‑point arithmetic.  Adopt the following procedure:

- Enable the `accelerate` library or `torch.cuda.amp.autocast`.  
- Wrap the optimizer in `GradScaler`.  
- Use `-bf16` or `-fp16` flags when launching training to get the most memory savings without sacrificing numeric stability.

### 5.6.3  Sequence‑Length Limit

If you want to fine‑tune a model on generation up to \(K\) tokens, then **truncate** all sampled completions to \(\min(K, \text{len}(y))\).  This keeps the log‑probability evaluation fast and prevents overflow.

## 5.7  Step‑by‑Step Training Loop

Below is a minimal PyTorch‑style training loop that captures all the earlier components.  The code is intentionally concise; you should adapt it to your specific framework (e.g., HuggingFace Trainer, DeepSpeed, fairscale).

```python
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer
from torch.cuda.amp import autocast, GradScaler
import torch.nn.functional as F

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load reference policy
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
ref_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")
ref_tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Load target model (will be fine‑tuned)
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")  # start from SFT
model.resize_token_embeddings(ref_tokenizer.vocab_size)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Create dataset
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
class PreferenceDataset(torch.utils.data.Dataset):
    def __init__(self, pref_file, tokenizer):
        self.samples = []
        with jsonlines.open(pref_file) as reader:
            for item in reader:
                self.samples.append(item)
        self.tokenizer = tokenizer

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self.samples)
    def __getitem__(self, idx):
        data = self.samples[idx]
        prompt = data['prompt']               # string
        pref  = data['preferred']             # string
        dis   = data['dispreferred']          # string
        return prompt, pref, dis

pref_dataset = PreferenceDataset("prefs/majilt.jsonl", ref_tokenizer)
train_loader = DataLoader(pref_dataset,
                           batch_size=4,
                           shuffle=True,
                           collate_fn=collate_fn)          # custom collate

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Training hyper‑parameters
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
beta = 1.0
learning_rate = 5e-5
epochs = 5
scaler = GradScaler()

optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=learning_rate)
model = model.to('cuda')
optimizer.zero_grad()

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Forward‑Backward Loop
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
for epoch in range(epochs):
    for batch in train_loader:
        prompt, pref, dis = batch                      # each string batch

        ## Tokenise + forward from reference policy
        ref_inputs = ref_tokenizer([prompt[i]+pref[i] for i in range(len(prompt))],
                                   truncation=True,
                                   max_length=MAX_LEN,
                                   return_tensors='pt')
        ref_logits = ref_model(**ref_inputs).logits
        ref_logprobs = F.log_softmax(ref_logits, dim=-1)
        ref_selected = ref_logprobs.gather(-1, ref_inputs['input_ids'].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
        ref_seq_logprob = ref_selected.sum(-1)          # shape: (batch,)

        ## Same for disfav seq
        ref_inputs_dis = ref_tokenizer([prompt[i]+dis[i] for i in range(len(prompt))],
                                       truncation=True, max_length=MAX_LEN,
                                       return_tensors='pt')
        ref_logits_dis = ref_model(**ref_inputs_dis).logits
        ref_logprobs_dis = F.log_softmax(ref_logits_dis, dim=-1)
        ref_selected_dis = ref_logprobs_dis.gather(-1,
                                               ref_inputs_dis['input_ids'].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
        ref_seq_logprob_dis = ref_selected_dis.sum(-1)

        ## Target policy forward (gradient w.r.t. targets!)
        inputs = tokenizer([prompt[i]+pref[i] for i in range(len(prompt))],
                           truncation=True, max_length=MAX_LEN,
                           return_tensors='pt').to('cuda')
        logits = model(**inputs).logits
        logprobs = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        selected = logprobs.gather(-1, inputs['input_ids'].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
        seq_logprob = selected.sum(-1)                    # shape: (batch,)

        ## same for disfav seq
        inputs_dis = tokenizer([prompt[i]+dis[i] for i in range(len(prompt))],
                               truncation=True, max_length=MAX_LEN,
                               return_tensors='pt').to('cuda')
        logits_dis = model(**inputs_dis).logits
        logprobs_dis = F.log_softmax(logits_dis, dim=-1)
        selected_dis = logprobs_dis.gather(-1,
                                           inputs_dis['input_ids'].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
        seq_logprob_dis = selected_dis.sum(-1)

        ## Compute implicit rewards
        r_pref = beta * (seq_logprob - ref_seq_logprob)     # shape: (batch,)
        r_dis  = beta * (seq_logprob_dis - ref_seq_logprob_dis)

        ## Crunch into cross‑entropy
        probs = torch.sigmoid(r_pref - r_dis)              # 0..1
        loss = - torch.mean(torch.log(probs + 1e-12))       # log‑cross‑entropy

        ## Back‑prop (gradient‑scaling for AMP)
        scaler.scale(loss).backward()
        scaler.step(optimizer)
        scaler.update()
        optimizer.zero_grad()

    print(f"Epoch {epoch+1}: loss={loss.item():.4f}")
```

### 5.7.1  Collate Function

The `collate_fn` should concatenate prompt and response, pad to the *longest* sequence in the mini‑batch, and return the concatenated token IDs and segment masks.  Do *not* split the prompt and generated tokens; the log‑ratio will automatically consider the entire concatenated sequence.

```python
def collate_fn(batch):
    prompts, prefs, disps = zip(*batch)
    # Items are strings
    inputs = [p + ' ' + pr for p, pr in zip(prompts, prefs)]
    inputs_dis = [p + ' ' + di for p, di in zip(prompts, disps)]
    return prompts, prefs, disps
```

## 5.8  Computational Considerations

| Issue | Recommendation |
|-------|----------------|
| **Memory** | Use *checkpointing* on the LM if you exceed GPU RAM; only the final forward pass needs gradients. |
| **Speed** | Inference of the reference policy can be *shared* across all prompts in a batch: compute `ref_seq_logprob` once for each unique prompt. |
| **Distributed** | With multiple GPUs, use `torch.distributed` or `accelerate` for parallel data loading; the same loss applies locally so you can simply *average* gradient across devices. |
| **Long‑form** | For documents > 300 tokens, use *past‑key‑value caching* so that the reference policy only computes logits for the *generated* portion, re‑using the prompt logits. |
| **Mixed‑Precision** | Enable BF16 if your GPU supports it; often yields better performance than FP16. |

## 5.9  Worked Mini‑Example

Suppose you train a 6‑B parameter LM on *sentiment control* data:

1. **Reference policy** – SFT model fine‑tuned on movie reviews with “positive” labeling.  
2. **Dataset** – 10 k human‑labeled pairs for the “positive” class; each pair contains a prompt opening and two possible completions.  
3. **Hyper‑parameters** – \( \beta = 1.2\), learning rate \(2 \times 10^{-5}\), batch size 16, 4 epochs.  
4. **Training** – The loss per batch is ~0.9 and stabilises after 30 k steps.  
5. **Evaluation** – Sample at temperature 0.0; the new policy wins 73 % of the time vs. the reference SFT policy (established by GPT‑4).  

The entire training took ~1 hour on 4 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs (≈ 60 GB total VRAM).  The resulting policy could be distilled or deployed as the `best-of-1` alternative to PPO‑HF.

## 5.10  Troubleshooting & Common Pitfalls

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| **Degenerate output** – Model always returns the same prompt | \( \beta \) too large, or reference policy produces very low log‑probability for all completions | Reduce \( \beta \) to < 1, or increase reference diversity |
| **Drifting away from reference** – KL is high even after many epochs | Too small \( \beta \) or insufficient data | Monitor KL with a target; increase \( \beta \) or lower KL target |
| **Training stalls** – Loss tends to zero or becomes NaN | Numerical instability in log‑ratio or sigmoid overflow | Add epsilon in log‑operations (`+1e-12`), clip gradients |
| **Memory errors** – GPU OOM during back‑prop | Using too large batch or long prompts | Decrease batch size, use gradient accumulation |
| **Slight degradation post‑training** – Beyond 1 M steps the policy degrades | Over‑fitting to the finite preference set | Employ early‑stopping or regularisation (L2, dropout) |

A good sanity check after training is to *visually inspect* a handful of front‑ended prompts and the produced completions.  If the completions still appear plausible but are not respecting the human‑labelled nuance you intended—say, the model is anti‑sentiment—it indicates a failure in the probability ratio computation or in the selection of \( \pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Replace the reference or augment the dataset and re‑train.

---

## 5.11  Summary

DPO turns the traditionally two‑stage RLHF pipeline into a single, end‑to‑end supervised-style training loop by re‑parameterizing the reward function as a log‑ratio between the candidate policy and a reference policy.  The loss is a binary cross‑entropy with a natural importance weight that protects against degeneracy.  With careful data preparation and an appropriately chosen \( \pi_{\text{ref}}\), DPO achieves performance comparable to PPO‑HF while being simpler, faster, and more stable.  By following the procedure laid out in this chapter, you can adapt DPO to any human‑preference–driven language‑model task, enabling rapid experimentation and production‑grade fine‑tuning.
