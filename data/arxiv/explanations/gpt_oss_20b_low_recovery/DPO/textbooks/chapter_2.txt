\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: # 2. From Human Feedback to Reinforcement Learning  
*Learning from human preferences to steer language models*  

In this chapter we trace the evolution of methods that turn *human judgments* about machine‑generated text into *behavioral constraints* for large‑scale language models (LMs).  We begin by describing how pairwise preference data are gathered, move through the statistical models that translate such data into a numerical reward, and end with the reinforcement‑learning (RL) objective that drives policy updates.  Along the way we explain the theoretical foundation of each step and the practical implications for building aligned, controllable LMs.

---

## Human preference data collection (paired completions)

The core ingredient of any preference‑based training pipeline is a *dataset of human judgments* about the relative quality of two candidate responses to the same prompt.  In practice we usually proceed as follows:

1. **Prompt sampling** – a large set of prompts \(x\) (questions, incomplete passages, user instructions) is selected from the target domain.  
2. **Completion generation** – for each \(x\), two completions \((y_1, y_2)\) are produced by a *reference policy* \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). This reference policy is often the base language model that has been fine‑tuned on high‑quality data (supervised instruction‑tuning).  
3. **Human labeling** – crowd‑source workers or domain experts are presented with \((x, y_1, y_2)\) and asked which completion they prefer. The output is a binary preference \(y_w \succ y_l \mid x\) where \(y_w\) is the *winning* (preferred) and \(y_l\) the *losing* (dispreferred) response.  
4. **Dataset construction** – the collection of tuples \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)}, y_{w}^{(i)}, y_{l}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N\) constitutes the *offline preference dataset* that will be used downstream.

Because the reference policy is usually a strong language model, the pairs \((y_1,y_2)\) are diverse enough to expose a wide variety of potential behaviors, yet remain within the distribution where the model has been trained.  The human reviewers are asked only a *binary* question; this keeps labeling costs low while still capturing rich information about relative quality.  The resulting dataset is the bridge between raw human judgments and a formal mathematical model of preference.

---

## Bradley‑Terry and Plackett‑Luce models for preferences

Once we have pairwise preferences, we need a *statistical framework* that expresses the probability that a given answer wins against another.  Two closely related models are used in the literature:

| Model | Probability of preference | Key property |
|-------|---------------------------|--------------|
| **Bradley‑Terry (BT)** | \(\displaystyle p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{e^{r^{*}(x,y_1)}}{e^{r^{*}(x,y_1)}+e^{r^{*}(x,y_2)}}\) | Uses only the difference of rewards. |
| **Plackett‑Luce (PL)** | \(\displaystyle p^{*}(y_1\succ\cdots\succ y_{k}\mid x)=\frac{e^{r^{*}(x,y_1)}}{Z}\cdot\frac{e^{r^{*}(x,y_2)}}{Z-e^{r^{*}(x,y_1)}}\cdots\) | Handles rankings of more than two items. |

Both are *choice models*: they infer a latent *reward function* \(r^{*}(x,y)\) that assigns a scalar score to every completion.  In the BT model, the probability that \(y_1\) beats \(y_2\) depends only on the exponentiated difference \(r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)\).  This is precisely the logistic (sigmoid) function applied to that difference, and it is the simplest model that is rich enough to capture human preferences.  

The PL model generalizes BT by allowing the *ranking* of multiple items.  In practice, most preference datasets use BT because pairs are the simplest annotation form.  However, the DPO derivation works for any Plackett‑Luce–type model—see the appendix for the generalization.

## Maximum‑likelihood reward learning (binary cross‑entropy)

Given the choice model, we now turn to *learning* the reward function that best explains the human labels.  Let us parameterize \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) as a neural network (often a small head on top of the LM).  We can write the likelihood of a single comparison \((x,y_w,y_l)\) as

\[
p_{\phi}(y_w\succ y_l \mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr),
\]

where \(\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}\) is the logistic function.  For a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), the *negative log‑likelihood* (NLL) becomes

\[
\boxed{%
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}
\left[ \log \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\right]}
\]

This is exactly a *binary cross‑entropy* loss where the target label is \(1\) for the winner and \(0\) for the loser.  The loss encourages the reward head to assign higher scores to choices that humans prefer, while respecting the BT model’s softmax over reward differences.

In practice we often *normalize* the reward output so that its mean is zero across the dataset, which stabilises training and matches the *zero‑mean* assumption implicit in the BT model.  Once \(r_{\phi}\) is trained, we regard it as a *proxy* for the unknown true reward function \(r^{*}\).

## KL‑constrained RL objective for policy optimisation

With a reward estimate in hand, we turn to *learning a new language‑model policy* \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) that will generate answers more aligned with human preferences.  Because we do not want the policy to deviate too far from the reference model \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) (where the reward is well‑calibrated), a *Kullback–Leibler* (KL) divergence penalty is inserted:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}
\Bigl[ r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta \,
\underbrace{\!D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)}_{\text{penalty}}
\Bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(\beta>0\) controls how tightly the new policy is constrained to stay close to the reference.  The objective balances **exploitation**—maximizing reward—and **exploration-centric safety**—preventing mode collapse or catastrophic shift in distribution.  Importantly, the objective is *non‑differentiable* because it requires sampling from \(\pi_{\theta}\), so the standard practice is to use policy‑gradient RL algorithms.

---

## Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) in RLHF

A common choice for optimizing (1) is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, a first‑order actor‑critic method that stabilises policy gradients by clipping surrogate objective ratios.  In the RLHF setting, the PPO algorithm proceeds as follows:

1. **Sample a mini‑batch** \(\{(x_i,y_i)\}\) from \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
2. **Compute advantages**:  
   \[
   A_i = r_{\phi}(x_i,y_i) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x_i)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i\mid x_i)} - V_{\psi}(x_i),
   \]
   where \(V_{\psi}\) is a value‑network that estimates the expected advantage.  
3. **Update the actor** by maximizing the clipped surrogate:
   \[
   \mathbb{E}\!\left[
   \min\!\Bigl(
   r^{\text{PPO}}\!\bigl(\theta\bigr)\,A,
   \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(r^{\text{PPO}}\!(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\bigr)\,A
   \Bigr)\right],
   \]
   where \(r^{\text{PPO}}\!(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\).  
4. **Update the critic** \(V_{\psi}\) by MSE to the empirical return.

The KL penalty appears implicitly in the advantage term, ensuring that the policy does not diverge arbitrarily.  In practice, hyperparameters such as \(\beta\), the clipping parameter \(\epsilon\), batch size, and learning rate must be tuned carefully.  PPO requires numerous sampling‑and‑evaluation steps, making fine‑tuning large language models expensive.

Nonetheless, PPO is attractive because it *directly optimises* the RL objective (1) and is robust to many stability issues that plague other RL algorithms.  However, the *necessity of an explicit reward model* and the *iterative sampling loop* are major bottlenecks—a motivation for the Simple Preference Optimization methods that follow in the next chapters.
