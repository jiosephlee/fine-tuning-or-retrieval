\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: # Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): Core Idea  

Large language models (LMs) are typically honed on vast corpora of unlabelled text, after which their behavior is steered toward human‑desirable outputs by training on curated supervision or, more recently, on human preferences.  The most successful methods in the second step resort to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): a reward model is first fitted to a dataset of pairwise preferences and then a policy is updated to maximise this reward under a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty that keeps the policy close to the seed model.  Although RLHF produces impressive, task‑specific behaviour, the approach is two‑stage, sample‑intensive, and requires a stable RL algorithm that can handle the combinatorial and discrete nature of language generation.  

The pivotal insight introduced in *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) is that, for the specific class of reward‑maximisation objectives used in RLHF, the optimal policy can be written in a closed‑form expression that depends only on the reward.  Consequently, the reward may be eliminated entirely, and the policy may be trained directly from human preference data via a simple maximum‑likelihood (cross‑entropy) loss.  The remainder of this chapter explains how this change of variables is derived, why the partition function cancels under the Bradley–Terry preference model, and why the resulting optimisation is equivalent to the vanilla RLHF objective, but without any reinforcement‑learning machinery.

## 1. Change‑of‑variables from a reward to a policy

In RLHF the objective a language model is asked to optimise is a KL‑constrained reward maximisation

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}
\Bigl[r(x,y)-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\Bigr],
\tag{1}
\]
where $r$ is a scalar reward function that measures how human‑preferred a completion $y$ is for a prefix $x$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a reference policy (typically the self‑supervised or instruction‑tuned model that the reward function was trained on), and $\beta>0$ trades off reward gain against deviation from the reference policy.  

Because the objective is convex in $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is fixed, the optimal policy can be derived analytically by a standard Lagrangian-Tian gradient argument.  Long story short, the policy that maximises (1) is the Gibbs (or Boltzmann) distribution

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]
with $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\bigr)$ the partition function.  Equation (2) is a celebrated result from variational RL ([Peters et al., 2007]; [Schulman et al., 2018]) and is effectively the *optimal* policy corresponding to the reward $r$.  

Equation (2) shows that once a reward $r$ is specified, finding the optimal policy is trivial: we merely reweight the reference distribution with an exponential of the reward.  Conversely, if we could express the reward itself in terms of a *policy*, we would bypass reward fitting and sampling entirely, since the policy would already embed the reward information.

Solving (2) for the reward gives

\[
r(x,y)
= \beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} 
   \;+\;\beta\,\log Z(x),
\tag{3}
\]
which is simply an algebraic rearrangement of (2).  Every optimal policy $\pi_{r}$ is thus paired with a reward that differs from the *log‑likelihood ratio* by a state‑dependent constant $\beta\,\log Z(x)$ that normalises the reward across all completions.  Importantly, this constant does **not** affect relative preferences over pairs of texts: for two completions $y_1,y_2$,
\[
r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)
=\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
-\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}.
\]
Deriving the reward in this way is the key *change‑of‑variables* that DPO exploits.

## 2. Re‑parameterised reward: $r=\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z(x)$

In practice we do not know the *true* optimal policy $\pi_{r}$, only an *offline* collection of pairwise human preferences,
\[
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)},\, y_{w}^{(i)},\, y_{l}^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where the subscript $w$ stands for “winner” (preferred) and $l$ for “loser” (dispreferred).  
These data are supposed to come from a latent reward function $r^{*}(x,y)$ that is continuous and differentiable in $y$. The classical RLHF pipeline estimates a reward model $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ by fitting a *Bradley–Terry* (BT) likelihood,
\[
\log p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
= r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l}) - \log\bigl(e^{r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})}+e^{r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})}\bigr),
\tag{4}
\]
leading to the binary cross‑entropy loss in Eq. (2.1) of the paper:
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D})
=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)})-r_{\phi}(x^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr).
\]

Now we substitute the re‑parameterised reward (3) into the BT model. Because the BT likelihood depends only on the *difference* $r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})$, the partition‑function term $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels.  Specifically,
\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)
= \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})\bigr)
= \sigma\!\bigl(\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)} 
          -\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr).
\tag{5}
\]
Equation (5) expresses the human preference distribution **solely** in terms of the policy $\pi$ that we wish to learn and the known reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, with no reference to the latent reward $r^{*}$.  The crucial cancellation of $\log Z(x)$ means that the *normalisation* of $r$—which is otherwise hard to estimate—does not appear in the likelihood, bypassing the need to compute partition sums or to normalise estimated rewards.

Thus, the likelihood of the preference data factorises through a *policy* parameterisation rather than a reward function, giving rise to a direct optimisation objective over $\pi$.

## 3. Cancellation of the partition function in BT likelihood

To see the partition function’s demise more concretely, write the BT probability for the winner and loser pair as
\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)
=\frac{\exp(r(x,y_{w}))}{\exp(r(x,y_{w}))+\exp(r(x,y_{l}))}.
\]
Inserting $r(x,y)$ from (3) turns each exponential into
\[
\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)\bigr)=
\exp\!\bigl(\beta\,\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\bigr)
= \pi(y\mid x)\,\underbrace{\frac{Z(x)^{\beta}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}}_{=Z(x)^{\beta}}.
\]
Since $Z(x)^{\beta}$ is a common factor in numerator and denominator, it cancels out.  Leftover terms are only ratios of $\pi$ to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and the entire BT likelihood becomes a logistic function over these log‑ratios.  
This algebraic simplification is the core formal anchor that permits DPO to avoid both the construction of a reward model and the sampling‑heavy RL loop.

## 4. Derivation of the DPO loss function

With the preference likelihood (5) in hand, we can immediately write the negative log‑likelihood of the entire dataset:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi;\pi_{\text{ref}})
= -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log\sigma\!\bigl(
\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi(y_{w}^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}
-\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi(y_{l}^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})}
\bigr).
\tag{6}
\]
Equation (6) is precisely the DPO loss.  It is a *binary cross‑entropy* that can be optimised by **stochastic gradient descent** (SGD) directly on the language‑model parameters $\theta$, using the probability that the model assigns to each completion.  Importantly, the only computationally non‑trivial operation is the evaluation of the policy’s conditional log‑probabilities for the two completions in each preference pair—a trivial requirement for any LM.

The hyperparameter $\beta$ that appears in the loss modulates the *effective temperature* at which the policy’s logits are compared; at $\beta\to\infty$ the loss reduces to the unlikelihood loss that merely increases the probability of winners and decreases that of losers; at finite $\beta$ the loss re‑introduces an element analogous to the KL penalty that regularises the policy to stay close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

## 5. Relationship to the ordinary least‑squares policy and KL‑regularised objective

The DPO objective can be seen as a closed‑form surrogate for the standard RLHF objective (1).  Substituting the re‑parameterised reward (3) into the reward‑maximisation objective and simplifying yields
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}
\Bigl[
\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\;-\;
\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\Bigr]+\text{const}
= \text{const},
\]
so that *whenever* the policy equals the optimal policy $\pi^*$, the reward term cancels the KL penalty exactly.  Any deviation from $\pi^*$ leads to a non‑zero gradient that pulls the policy back toward the optimum.  This is why the DPO loss, when minimised, directly yields the optimal policy that would have been found by a KL‑constrained policy gradient algorithm such as PPO.

In computational experiments, DPO achieves a *reward–KL frontier* that dominates that of PPO (Fig. 2 of the paper).  This means that for any given KL divergence from the reference, DPO attains higher expected reward, or equivalently, for any desired reward level it keeps the policy closer to the reference distribution.  The derivation above clarifies that this superior efficiency comes from avoiding the stochastic gradients and higher‑variance policy estimators used by RLHF; DPO’s gradient is a *flat* cross‑entropy that directly encodes the preferences.

---

### Summary

- A KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem admits an analytic solution: the optimal policy is a Gibbs reweighting of the reference policy.
- By rearranging that solution, the reward can be written as a log‑likelihood ratio between the policy and the reference, up to a state‑dependent constant.
- Because the Bradley–Terry model for human preferences depends only on rank differences, the normalising constant cancels from the likelihood of preference pairs.
- The resulting *Direct Preference Optimization* loss is a binary cross‑entropy applied to log‑probability ratios, requiring only forward policy probability evaluations and no reward model or RL sampling.
- Minimising this loss directly yields the same policy that RLHF would converge to, but with a far simpler training pipeline.
