\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 9: # 9. Theoretical Guarantees and Properties of DPO

Large language models trained with RL‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) traditionally explore a complex two‑stage recipe: first a neural reward function is fit to a dataset of pairwise human preferences; then a policy is updated, usually by an RL algorithm such as PPO, to maximize the fitted reward while staying close to a reference policy.  The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework abandons this explicit two‑stage pipeline, replacing it with a single maximum‑likelihood objective that directly learns a policy from preference data.  At first glance this appears to be an unconventional “policy‑only” formulation; after all, the RL objective contains an unknown reward function that must, in principle, drive the policy.  The key insight of DPO is that the optimal policy for a KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem admits an explicit closed‑form expression in terms of the reward (Section 4 of the paper).  By rewriting the reward in terms of its optimal policy, the preference‑likelihood objective can be expressed purely as a function of the policy.  The remainder of this chapter formalises that observation, and establishes the theoretical properties that make DPO sound, such as invariance to reward‑shifting, consistency with Bradley–Terry preferences, and recoverability of the reward class that underlies the optimal policy.

Throughout we adopt a predictable notation:  
- \(x\in\mathcal X\) denotes a context or prompt;  
- \(y\in\mathcal Y\) denotes a full completion;  
- \(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\) denotes the language‑model policy parametrised by \(\theta\);  
- \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\) denotes the reference policy (typically the SFT model);  
- \(r(x,y)\) is a real‑valued reward function;  
- \(p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)\) is the human preference distribution over two completions.

The following sections walk through the three main theoretical ingredients introduced in the paper: equivalence classes of reward functions, two supporting lemmas, and the central theorem that guarantees that every reward class admissible under Bradley–Terry (BT) preferences can be realized by a DPO policy.  We then discuss the implications for identifiability and normalisation that arise naturally from this construction.

---

## 9.1 Equivalence Relation on Reward Functions  

The reward function \(r(x,y)\) that would be maximised under an RLHF objective is not uniquely identified by the human preference data.  The BT model defines the probability of a preference event only through a *difference* of two rewards:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)
\tag{9.1}
\]

where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function.  Consequently, adding a function of the context to *every* reward preserves the preference distribution.  This motivates defining an equivalence relation.

> **Definition 1 (Reward Equivalence).**  
> Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are equivalent—denoted \(r\sim r'\)—if there exists a function \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\) such that  
> \[
> r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x),\qquad\forall x,y\,.
> \tag{9.2}
> \]

The relation \(\sim\) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so it partitions the set of reward functions into equivalence classes, each class containing all rewards that differ only by a context‑dependent constant.  Every BT likelihood computation depends only on the difference \(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\), so rewards from the same class produce identical likelihoods.

---

## 9.2 Lemma 1: Equivalent Rewards ⇒ Same Preference Distribution  

> **Lemma 1.**  
> If \(r\sim r'\), then for all prompts \(x\) and all pairs of completions \((y_{1},y_{2})\),  
> \[
> \sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr)=\sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})\bigr).
> \tag{9.3}
> \]

*Proof.*  
By definition \(r(x,y_{k})=r'(x,y_{k})+f(x)\) for \(k=1,2\).  Substituting into the left‑hand side of (9.3) gives

\[
r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})
= \bigl(r'(x,y_{1})+f(x)\bigr)-\bigl(r'(x,y_{2})+f(x)\bigr)
= r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2}),
\]

so the logistic functions are identical. ∎

This lemma tells us that from the perspective of the BT preference model, the reward function is only identifiable up to a constant shift that may depend on the prompt.  Consequently, the optimisation of a reward model under BT likelihood is inherently *under‑specified*.

---

## 9.3 Lemma 2: Equivalent Rewards ⇒ Same Optimal Policy  

Optimal policies in RLHF are derived via a KL‑constrained maximisation problem

\[
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigl[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\,r(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\tag{9.4}
\]

whose solution can be shown (see Appendix A.1) to be

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr),
\tag{9.5}
\]

with partition function \(Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\).

> **Lemma 2.**  
> Let \(r\sim r'\).  Then the policies \(\pi_{r}\) and \(\pi_{r'}\) obtained by solving (9.4) with rewards \(r\) and \(r'\) are identical.

*Proof.*  
Because \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\), we can write

\[
\exp\!\bigl(r'(x,y)/\beta\bigr)
=\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)\exp\!\bigl(-f(x)/\beta\bigr).
\tag{9.6}
\]

Inserting (9.6) into the numerator of (9.5) for \(r'\) yields

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r'(x,y)/\beta\bigr)
=\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)\exp\!\bigl(-f(x)/\beta\bigr).
\]

The multiplicative constant \(\exp(-f(x)/\beta)\) does not depend on \(y\) and therefore cancels with the analogous term in the partition function \(Z_{r'}(x)\).  Consequently, the ratio in (9.5) is the same for both rewards; thus \(\pi_{r}=\pi_{r'}\). ∎

This lemma formalises that the *policy* that maximises the constrained RL objective is blind to the particular representative of a reward equivalence class.  Hence, any reward model within an equivalence class yields the same trained policy.

## 9.4 Theorem 1: DPO Re‑Parameterisation Captures All Reward Classes  

The DPO objective replaces the reward model by its optimal policy and rewrites the BT likelihood purely in terms of the policy.  The main theoretical promise is that this re‑parameterisation is *complete*: any reward function consistent with the BT model has a representative that can be expressed in the DPO form.  

> **Theorem 1 (Completeness of the DPO Parameterisation).**  
> Consider any reward function \(r\) consistent with the BT preference model.  
> Let \(\pi_{r}\) be its optimal policy obtained by solving (9.4).  
> Then there exists a function \(\hat r\) of the form  
> \[
> \hat r(x,y)=\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
> \tag{9.7}
> \]
> such that \(\hat r\) lies in the same equivalence class as \(r\).  
> Consequently, the DPO objective (which optimises a policy under the BT likelihood expressed via (9.7)) is able to recover *any* reward class appropriate for the data.

*Sketch of Proof.*  
The proof proceeds in two stages.

1. **Expressing the reward in terms of its optimal policy.**  
   Starting from definition (9.5), re‑arrange to solve for \(r(x,y)\):

   \[
   \frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
   =\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y)/\beta\bigr)
   \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
   r(x,y)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z_{r}(x).
   \tag{9.8}
   \]

   The second term \(\beta\log Z_{r}(x)\) depends only on \(x\), hence it is a member of the equivalence class of \(r\).  
2. **Normalisation to obtain a reward in canonical form.**  
   Define the *projected* reward

   \[
   \hat r(x,y)=r(x,y)-\beta\log Z_{r}(x)=\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
   \tag{9.9}
   \]

   By construction, \(\hat r\sim r\).  Moreover, inserting \(\hat r\) back into (9.5) shows that \(\pi_{r}\) is still the optimal policy for \(\hat r\): the extra constant has cancelled out in the partition function.  

   Thus, for any reward consistent with BT preferences, the reward *class* contains a specific representative of the form (9.7).  This representative is precisely the reward that DPO implicitly optimises. ∎

**Implications.**  
- *Expressiveness.*  The DPO parameterisation is **as expressive** as the full reward‑model approach: no admissible reward class is excluded.  
- *Identifiability.*  Since the reward is only identifiable up to a context‑dependent constant, DPO naturally absorbs that ambiguity into the form of the reward.  In practice this means that the algorithm does not require any external normalisation of rewards; the KL‑constraint and the exponential transform automatically normalise the implicit reward.  
- *Recoverability.*  Conversely, if one were to fit a conventional reward model \(r_{\phi}\) to the same preference data and then construct the optimal policy by (9.5), the policy recovered would be *exactly* the DPO policy trained directly on the same data (up to optimisation tolerance).

## 9.5 Discussion: Identifiability, Normalisation, and Practical Consequences  

1. **Identifiability in Bradley–Terry.**  
   The BT family is notoriously under‑specified; any reward shift \(f(x)\) leaves the likelihood unchanged.  Prior works therefore impose ad‑hoc constraints, such as zero‑mean rewards or normalisation to a specific distribution, to obtain a unique estimator.  DPO sidesteps this issue by insisting that the reward is *implicitly* derived from a probability ratio.  The KL‑constraint ensures that the ratio remains well‑scaled, and the implicit normalisation term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) in (9.8) is automatically absorbed during training.  Thus, DPO does not suffer from reward drift or arbitrary scaling problems that can plague explicit reward‑model training.

2. **Normalisation and the Partition Function.**  
   The DPO objective (9.10 below) contains the logarithm of the ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\):

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}) 
   = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\!\Bigl[
      \log\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \beta\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
        - \beta\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
      \Bigr)\Bigr].
   \tag{9.10}
   \]

   Because the arguments of the logistic are *difference* terms, the explicit partition functions of \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) cancel out.  The residual bias effect that would normally appear as a state‑dependent normalisation in the RL objective is thus automatically handled, removing the need for baseline estimators or explicit value‑function training.

3. **Practical Training Stability.**  
   By eliminating the extraneous baseline term, DPO avoids a principal source of high variance in policy gradients commonly associated with actor‑critic RLHF approaches that must estimate value functions for the reference policy.  In the experiments (Section 6.1), the authors found a naïve version of the same update lacking the weighting factor (see equation (9.13) in the paper) to cause severe degeneration of the language model.  The weighting term—essentially an implicit importance weight derived from the current policy’s reward estimate—stabilises training by scaling the policy gradient to reflect how poorly the current policy orders the two completions.

4. **Hyperparameter Simplicity.**  
   Whereas PPO‑based RLHF requires careful tuning of learning rates, clipping ratios, KL‑penalty coefficients, and reward normalisation hyper‑parameters, the DPO objective depends on a single scalar \(\beta\) that directly controls the trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to the reference policy.  In the experiments, the authors set \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\) and found robust performance across the board without additional fine‑tuning.

---

## 9.6 Summary  

The DPO framework rests on a mathematically clean transformation that maps any reward function consistent with Bradley–Terry preferences to a *policy‑directed* reward of the form \(\beta\log\,\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The equivalence relations and supporting lemmas establish that this mapping is loss‑free: it preserves both the human‑preference distribution and the optimal policy under the KL‑constrained RL objective.  Consequently, DPO achieves the same expressiveness as the traditional two‑stage RLHF pipeline while providing a single, simple cross‑entropy loss and producing a stabilised, reward‑free training regimen.  From a theoretical standpoint, DPO is thus both sound and complete with respect to the class of Bradley–Terry preferences—offering an elegant and efficient alternative to policy‑gradient RL in the realm of large language model alignment.
