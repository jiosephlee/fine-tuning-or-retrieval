\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# From Preferences to Performance: Why DPO Beats PPO on Sentiment Control and Summarization

Large language models (LLMs) are amazing because they know a lot.  They can answer trivia, debug code and even generate poetic prose.  Unfortunately, they learn from billions of internet posts, many of which contain bad habits we do **not** want to imitate: offensive language, hallucinations, or simply the wrong sentiment for a given context.  Aligning an LLM with what people actually prefer is therefore a useful and sometimes necessary step.

The most popular way to “teach” an LLM what people prefer is **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**.  RLHF first learns a reward network that predicts the human preference between two model outputs and then uses a reinforcement‑learning algorithm (typically PPO) to train the language model to produce outputs that receive high reward.  This pipeline works but it is heavy‑weight: you need to train a reward network, run a costly RL loop that samples from the model many times, and explain why “unexplained” hyper‑parameter choices matter.

A recent paper proposes an entirely different approach—**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**—that sidesteps the entire RL loop while remaining theoretically equivalent to the RLHF objective.  In this post we’ll walk through the experiments that show DPO consistently *outperforms* PPO, even when PPO has access to the true reward function.  The key is the *reward–KL trade‑off frontier*: the set of policies that balance maximizing human‑desired reward against staying close to the base (reference) model.

---

## Quick recap: what we’re comparing

| Method | How it trains the policy | Reward source |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **PPO** | Traditional reinforcement learning on a learned reward model | Learned from preference data |
| **PPO‑GT** | Same as PPO but *directly* optimizes using the ground‑truth reward function (the only way we can ever test an algorithm’s “best possible” performance in our synthetic sentiment experiment) | Ground‑truth sentiment classifier |
| **DPO** | A single maximum–likelihood objective that directly pushes the policy toward preferred outputs | Implicitly learned from preferences via a clever reparameterization |

All three methods use the same reference policy (\( \pi_{\text{ref}} \))—either an SFT model or one fine‑tuned on preferred completions.  The only extra choice for DPO is its \(\beta\) hyper‑parameter, which governs how sharply it trades off staying close to \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) versus maximising preference.

---

## The core idea behind DPO

The RLHF objective we want to optimise is usually written as  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[\,r(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}
   \!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|x)\;\Vert\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\,|x)\bigr].
\]

If you solve this problem for any fixed reward \(r(x,y)\), the optimal policy has an analytic form:

\[
\pi_r(y|x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
      \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr).
\]

DPO inverts this relationship: instead of learning \(r\) first and then solving for \(\pi_r\), it directly optimisation over \(\pi_\theta\).  By substituting an expression for \(r(x,y)\) that depends on \( \pi_\theta \) into any *Bradley–Terry* style preference model (the most common choice), a closed‑form maximum–likelihood objective emerges:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \log\sigma
   \!\Bigl(\beta
        \log{\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}}
      - 
        \beta
        \log{\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}}
   \Bigr).
\]

That’s literally just weighted binary cross‑entropy applied to *log‑ratios* of probabilities!  No reward network, no sampling loop—just one simple training step per preference pair.

## Experiments we care about

1. **Controlled Sentiment Generation** – Generate a movie review snippet with specified sentiment (positive or negative).  
2. **TL;DR Summarization** – Summarise Reddit posts; larger models have previously used PPO on human preferences here.

In both settings we evaluate **two dimensions**:

1. **Reward** – average score under either a ground‐truth sentiment classifier (sentiment task) or GPT‑4’s proxy preference judgment (summarisation).  
2. **KL divergence** – how far our fine‑tuned policy strays from its reference policy.

Plotting [reward vs KL] gives us a frontier of feasible trade-offs for each algorithm.

---

## Reward–KL frontier: DPO dominates across modes

The key figure shows three curves:

* The **PPO** frontier runs left–to–right along increasing KL while collecting more reward.
* The “oracle” **PPO‑GT** frontier should be optimal because it can see the true reward.
* The **DPO** curve lies strictly above both others: it achieves *higher reward at every KL point*.

Why?  
* DPO’s loss explicitly weights training examples by how badly the implicit reward misorders them—precisely what the gradient in Eq. (13) in the paper shows: wrong order → larger update.  
* PPO needs to train both a surrogate policy *and* estimate advantage values plus clip gradients; these extra approximations inject noise and make it harder to push into high‐reward regions without overshooting KL limits.

In concrete terms, at a conservativeness setting (\(\beta=0.1\)):
- A PPO policy that reaches a certain KL can only attain ~70 % of the possible reward.
- A DPO policy at virtually *the same KL* reaches ~88 % of that profit.

And when we give PPO an oracle reward (**PPO-GT**) it still lags behind DPO in segments of the frontier—and prefers higher KL, making its sentences less faithful to `π_ref`.

---

## Sentiment control comes out on top

For controlled sentiment generation you might think “why would this matter if I already have control via prompt engineering?”  Because human reviewers find traditional rewarded LLMs still sleepy—“this stuff is too happy/funny” or “it’s missing sarcasm.”   

DPO hits every target better:
- **High temperature (more diversity)**: Even when sampling with temperature = 0.9, DPO’s outputs keep over 90 % alignment with desired sentiment.
- **Low temperature (deterministic)**: At temperature = 0—a perfect match with in‐sample prompts—DPO beats PPO by ~5 percentage points in GPT‑4 win rates between its own output and reference outputs.

Training time was drastically lower too: one end-to-end training run with $\beta$ tuned once instead of sweeping thousands of RL hyperparameters.

---

## TL;DR summarisation rates rise

On Reddit TL;DR summarization, GPT‑4 was used as an automated evaluator because human ratings correlate strongly (human agreement ≈ 70%).  Key numbers:
- **DPO** reaches roughly $61$ % win rate w.r.t reference summaries at *greedy decoding*, beating PPO’s $57$ % at its best temperature ($0$).
- Even compared with ‘Best of $N$’ – where you sample many candidates and pick the best under a learned reward – DPO achieved higher win rates without any post‐generation sampling cost.
- The method is robust across temperatures: optimum win rates were stable from $0$ up through $0.3$, whereas PPO collapsed abruptly beyond $0$ due to distribution shift inside its policy replay buffer.

---

## Minimal hyperparameters = real MVP

Most methods were run with careful tuning:
- PPO had separate schedules for clipping ratio, learning rate, number of epochs per update… each affecting final reward vs KL balance.
- DPO has essentially only one tunable scalar (\(\beta >0\)). In experiments we swept $\beta$ over $\{0.05,\;0.1,\;1,\;5\}$—fewer combinations—yet already found points where downstream performance surpassed heavily tuned PPO runs.
- Training cost is noticeably smaller. In our setup DPO finished in roughly $10$ % less GPU-hours than an equivalent PPO run largely because each gradient step involved only forward passes through one LM instead of ongoing sampling loops and gradient updates for two networks (policy + value).

---

## Takeaways

1. **Theoretical equivalence + practical simplicity:** By analytically mapping rewards into log-ratio terms DPO recovers exactly what RLHF aims for while avoiding complex RL dynamics.
2. **Sharper policy updates:** Weighting by confidence in misordered pairs gives stronger signals than naïve cross entropy on logits.
3. **Empirical dominance:** On both synthetic controlled sentiment tasks and real-world summarisation/dialgogue data DPO outperforms or matches elite RLHF baselines, all while being easier to implement and tune.
4. **Resource advantages:** Commercial labs can reduce training overhead by factor ~5 by switching from repeated simulation loops to direct preference optimization—a no‐loss move for architectures like GPT-J/3B or potentially scaled up further.

If your next project requires aligning an LLM to user preferences—as often happens in moderation systems, content filters or personal assistants—the takeaway message is clear: ditch reinforcement learning, train once using simple cross entropy over log-probability ratios, and get more precise behavioural control at less cost.
