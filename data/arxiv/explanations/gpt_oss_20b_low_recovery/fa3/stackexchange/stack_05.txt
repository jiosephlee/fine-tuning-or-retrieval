\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does incoherent processing help with outlier values in FP8 attention?
Question:
The authors propose multiplying Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix M before quantizing to FP8, claiming that this spreads out outliers. Could someone explain mathematically why this does not alter the attention output? Also, what is the cost of generating and applying M, and how is it fused with rotary embeddings as claimed?
Answer:
**How the “incoherent processing” trick works – and why it doesn’t change attention**

1. **The core idea**  
   In the FP8 version of FlashAttention‑3 the query and key tensors are first multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix  
   \[
     \tilde{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M},\qquad
     \tilde{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M},
   \]
   before these matrices are quantised to FP8.  Because $\mathbf{M}$ is orthogonal we have  
   \[
      \tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\top}
        = (\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^{\top}
        = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{\top}
        = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}.
   \]
   The dot‑product scores – and, consequently, all downstream quantities (softmax, weighted sum with $V$, gradients) – remain exactly the same.  The trick is thus mathematically lossless; it merely re‑expresses the same scores in a different basis.

2. **Why it helps with outliers**  
   FP8 has only 3 mantissa bits and 4 exponent bits; a few huge entries (the “outliers” that often arise in large‑scale transformers) can dominate the quantised representation and cause large relative errors.  By premultiplying with $\mathbf{M}$, each element of $\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}$ or $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}$ becomes a random linear combination of the original entries.  This spreads the magnitude of the outliers over many positions, reducing the likelihood that any single quantised value is excessively large.  The result is a more uniform distribution of the entries that the FP8 format can accommodate, which directly translates into lower quantisation error in the subsequent dot‑product and softmax.

3. **Cost of generating / applying \(\mathbf{M}\)**  
   The authors use a *structured* orthogonal matrix – the product of a Hadamard matrix and a diagonal matrix of random $\pm1$ signs.  Applying this to a vector takes $O(d\log d)$ operations (a fast Walsh–Hadamard transform).  In practice each threadblock processes its own chunk of $\mathbf{Q}$ or $\mathbf{K}$ once per forward pass, so the extra work is modest compared to the two large GEMMs that dominate the kernel.  Empirically the authors report that the extra FLOPs are a negligible fraction (on the order of 1–2 %) of the overall runtime, while the gain in accuracy is substantial.

4. **Fusion with rotary embeddings**  
   Rotary (RoPE) embeddings are essentially a per‑token phase rotation of the $d$‑dimensional query and key vectors:
   \[
     \mathbf{Q}\;\leftarrow\;\operatorname{RoPE}(\mathbf{Q}),\qquad
     \mathbf{K}\;\leftarrow\;\operatorname{RoPE}(\mathbf{K}).
   \]
   Because RoPE is a linear operation, it can be absorbed into the final matrix multiplication that applies $\mathbf{M}$.  Concretely, the kernel first applies RoPE to the input tensors, then immediately multiplies the result by the orthogonal matrix in the same warp‑group and thread‑buffer that already does the ldmatrix/stmatrix transpose.  Thus no extra memory traffic is needed and the overall launch overhead is unchanged.  
   The paper’s Section 3.2 (“Accuracy: block quantization and incoherent processing”) notes that “multiplying both \$\mathbf{Q}\$ and \$\mathbf{K}\$ with \$\mathbf{M}\$ before quantising does not change the attention output, and it can be fused with the rotary embedding at no extra computational cost.”

5. **Summary of benefits**  
   * **Zero‑change accuracy** – mathematically exact on the dot‑products.  
   * **Reduced quantisation noise** – spreads out extreme values, lowering RMSE by up to roughly 2.6× in their experiments.  
   * **Low overhead** – fast Hadamard transform, inexpensive to fuse with existing ad‑hoc kernels (rotary, transpose).  
   * **Hardware friendliness** – the operation is expressible as a sequence of warp‑level matrix‑load instructions and a small shuffle‑/permutation‑step, which the Hopper Tensor Cores can execute efficiently.

So, in short, the “incoherent processing” trick simply re‑bases the Q/K matrices without changing the final attention scores, while the random mixing phase mitigates the extreme‑value problem that plagues FP8 quantisation.  The extra cost is a tiny, linear‑ith‐log‑d matrix multiplication per token that can be fused with the existing memory‑transfer and rotary‑embedding steps, yielding a net gain in accuracy with negligible runtime penalty.
