\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why is the accumulator layout different from the operand layout in FP8 WGMMA, and how do you reconcile them?
Question:
Section 3.2.2 describes a mismatch between the layout of the FP32 accumulator and the FP8 operand when performing two successive WGMMA operations. Could we walk through a small example (e.g., a 4×4 matrix) showing the register arrangement for the accumulator of the first GEMM and the operand of the second GEMM, and then illustrate the byte‑permutations needed to convert one into the other?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Hopper the WGMMA instruction works with two kinds of operands:

* **+k‑major** operands that are laid out in *inner* dimension first (i.e. consecutive in K).
* **accumulators** that are kept in registers in a “row‑major” order (outer dimension first).

For FP16 the instruction accepts either layout, so a regular Tensor‑core GM‑A product can be followed immediately by a second one.  
For FP8 the story is different: the second operand must be *k‑major*, but the accumulator from the first WGMMA is still in “row‑major” order.  
If we simply feed the accumulator of the first GEMM into a second GEMM, the operand will be mis‑aligned and the HW will produce garbage.

Below we work through a concrete example with a 4×4 matrix (B = 4, K = 4).  
All entries are kept in **FP8** for the second GEMM, but the accumulator is in **FP32** after the first GEMM.

---

### 1.  What the registers look like after the first GEMM

After the first WGMMA  
\[
\mathbf{S}= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top
\]
the 4×4 product is stored in the accumulator register file in *row‑major* order.  
For a single warp we have 32 threads; each thread owns 4 FP32 registers that hold a contiguous row of `S`.  
If we write the registers row by row we get:

| Thread | r0 | r1 | r2 | r3 |
|--------|----|----|----|----|
| 0 | \(\,s_{00}\) | \(\,s_{01}\) | \(\,s_{02}\) | \(\,s_{03}\) |
| 1 | \(\,s_{10}\) | \(\,s_{11}\) | \(\,s_{12}\) | \(\,s_{13}\) |
| 2 | \(\,s_{20}\) | \(\,s_{21}\) | \(\,s_{22}\) | \(\,s_{23}\) |
| 3 | \(\,s_{30}\) | \(\,s_{31}\) | \(\,s_{32}\) | \(\,s_{33}\) |

(The layout is the same for any warp; the example simply shows one war‑group.)

---

### 2.  What the second WGMMA requires

The second GEMM that we want to execute is the “soft‑max scaling” part  
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{P}} \leftarrow \mathrm{diag}(\exp (m_i^{old}-m_i))^{-1}\; \mathbf{O}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathbf{O} \leftarrow \mathbf{O} + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}\;\mathbf{V}.
\]

The first operand of that WGMMA must be **k‑major**: consecutive entries in the *K* (inner) dimension must be stored contiguously in registers.  
In other words, the register file must look like

| Thread | r0 | r1 | r2 | r3 |
|--------|----|----|----|----|
| 0 | \(\,s_{00}\) | \(\,s_{10}\) | \(\,s_{20}\) | \(\,s_{30}\) |
| 1 | \(\,s_{01}\) | \(\,s_{11}\) | \(\,s_{21}\) | \(\,s_{31}\) |
| 2 | \(\,s_{02}\) | \(\,s_{12}\) | \(\,s_{22}\) | \(\,s_{32}\) |
| 3 | \(\,s_{03}\) | \(\,s_{13}\) | \(\,s_{23}\) | \(\,s_{33}\) |

(Notice that each thread now owns a *column* of `S`; the key dimension has been permuted.)

---

### 3.  The byte‑permutation that fixes the mismatch

The HW offers a “byte‑permute” instruction (`permutebytes` in PTX) that can reorder the 128‑bit content of each register arbitrarily.  
For our 4×4 example we need to move the entries of each register in the order

\[
\{\,d_0,\,d_1,\,d_4,\,d_5,\,d_2,\,d_3,\,d_6,\,d_7\,\},
\]

where `d0`…`d7` are the eight 8‑bit FP8 values that occupy a 32‑bit register after the first WGMMA.  
Applying this byte‑permutation to every thread’s register converts the row‑major layout into a k‑major one:

* `d0` (s00) stays at the front,
* `d1` (s01) stays next,
* `d4` (s10) moves to third,
* `d5` (s11) moves to fourth,
* `d2` (s02) moves to fifth,  
  … and so on.

After this permutation the registers encode the matrix in the shape required by the second WGMMA.

---

### 4.  How this is used in FlashAttention‑3

1. **First GEMM** (`Q × Kᵀ`):  
   The accumulator is implicitly formed in the row‑major order shown in §1.

2. **Byte‑permute**:  
   Immediately after the first GEMM completes, we issue a `permutebytes` on the accumulator registers to obtain the k‑major layout needed for the second GEMM.

3. **Optional transpose of V**:  
   While the accumulator is being permuted, the producer threads load the next `V` tile into SMEM and use `ldmatrix`/`stmatrix` to transpose it to the same k‑major layout that matches the permuted accumulator.

4. **Second GEMM** (`∼P × V`):  
   With both operands now in the same layout, the second WGMMA can execute without any software‑side rearrangement.

Because the permutation is a single warp‑wide instruction, it costs only a handful of cycles and does not affect occupancy.  The overall effect is that two successive WGMMA operations on FP8 can be fused in one kernel without any stalls.

---

### 5.  Takeaway

The mismatch between the FP32 accumulator layout and the FP8 operand layout is a hardware‑specific quirk of Hopper’s WGMMA.  In FlashAttention‑3 it is resolved by a quick, warp‑wide byte‑permute that reshapes the accumulator into a k‑major form, after which the second GEMM can proceed.  This trick keeps the pipeline fully asynchronous, allows the use of FP8 Tensor Cores, and is essential to achieve the reported 1.2 PFLOPs/s speed‑up.
