Chapter 1: # Foundations of Attention and GPU Computing  

In contemporary deep‑learning frameworks, the Transformer architecture has become the workhorse behind large language models, vision encoders, speech recognizers, and many other sequential and multimodal systems. At the heart of this architecture lies the self‑attention mechanism, an operation that is both mathematically elegant and computationally demanding. Modern deep‑learning workloads almost invariably run on graphics processing units (GPUs), whose execution model and instruction set were originally designed for graphics but have evolved into powerful general‑purpose accelerators.  

This chapter lays the groundwork for understanding how the mathematical structure of attention intertwines with the concrete realities of GPUs.  The presentation will follow the notation introduced by the FlashAttention‑3 paper, using LaTeX to express every mathematical idea.  We cover the Transformer’s attention machinery, the dual forward and backward passes, and the rich GPU memory and execution hierarchy—including recent advances such as the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), the warp‑group matrix multiply (WGMMA) instruction, and low‑precision Tensor Cores that support FP8, BF16, and other formats.  By the end of this chapter, a reader will be equipped to relate the abstract formulas of attention to the low‑level operations exploited by FlashAttention‑3.

## Transformer Architecture and the Role of Attention  

The original Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} introduced a novel way of encoding sequence information without recurrent or convolutional layers.  Instead of processing tokens sequentially, every token attends to every other token, forming a dense, pairwise interaction graph.  The source of this power is the scaled dot‑product attention, which computes how much each token influences each other token’s representation.  

Formally, consider a batch of $B$ sequences, each of length $N$, and a hidden dimension $D$ (the dimension of the full hidden state in the Transformer block).  Before entering an attention layer, each token’s hidden state $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is linearly projected into three vectors:
\[
    q_i = W^Q h_i,\quad k_i = W^K h_i,\quad v_i = W^V h_i ,
\]
where $W^Q,\, W^K,\, W^V \in \mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ are learnable weight matrices.  In practice, the model is divided into $H$ independent “heads”, each operating on a reduced dimension $d = D/H$.  All the equations below refer to the tensors associated with a particular head; the final outputs are then concatenated across heads and projected back to the hidden dimension.  

For a single head, let $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ denote the matrices of all query, key, and value vectors, respectively.  The fundamental attention operation is defined as a weighted sum:
\[
    \mathbf{O} \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}\!\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top} \right)\mathbf{V}\!,
\]
where $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ with $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is the unnormalized attention score matrix and $\mathbf{P}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$ are the normalized attention weights.  The softmax is applied row‑wise; each row of $\mathbf{S}$ is mapped to a probability distribution over the key positions, thus quantifying how much each query attends to each key.

The attention mechanism is called a *self‑attention* when the same sequence provides $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$.  Masking schemes, such as causal masking in autoregressive language models, can be applied to $\mathbf{S}$ before the softmax to enforce monotonicity in the output, but the basic workflow remains the same.

## Multi‑Head Self‑Attention: Forward and Backward Passes  

A full multi‑head self‑attention (MHA) layer applies the above operation in parallel for each head, then concatenates the per‑head outputs:
\[
    \mathbf{O}^{\text{MHA}} \;=\; \big[ \mathbf{O}_1;\,\mathbf{O}_2;\,\dots;\,\mathbf{O}_H \big] W^O ,
\]
with $W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$ projecting back to the hidden dimension.  Because all heads operate independently, the matmuls for different heads can be fused or batched, which is crucial for GPU efficiency.

The forward pass involves three sub‑computations per head:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\qquad$   (matrix‑multiply of $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ and $\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$).
  \item $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \qquad$    (row‑wise reduction with exponential and normalisation).
  \item $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}\qquad$           (matrix‑multiply of $\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ and $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$).
\end{enumerate}
All three operations are embarrassingly parallel over tokens and heads and lend themselves to highly efficient GPU kernels.

The backward pass, crucial for training, propagates gradients $\partial\phi/\partial \mathbf{O}$—denoted $\mathbf{dO}$—back to the inputs $\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}$ and the weight matrices.  The chain rule yields:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{dV} &= \mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{dO}, \label{eq:dV}\\
  \mathbf{dP} &= \mathbf{dO}\mathbf{V}^{\top}, \label{eq:dP}\\
  \mathbf{dS} &= \mathrm{dsoftmax}\bigl(\mathbf{dP}\bigr), \label{eq:dS}
\end{align}
where the gradient of the softmax is:
\[
  \mathrm{dsoftmax}(\mathbf{dP}) \;=\; (\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{\top})\mathbf{dP}
\]
evaluated row‑wise on the original softmax output $\mathbf{p}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{s})$.  
Finally,
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{dQ} &= \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}\,\mathbf{K},      \label{eq:dQ}\\
  \mathbf{dK} &= \alpha\,\mathbf{dS}^{\top}\,\mathbf{Q}. \label{eq:dK}
\end{align}
These equations reinforce the symmetry between forward and backward passes: the backward kernel also contains two matrix‑multiplications interleaved with a softmax back‑prop call, mirroring the forward flow of computation.  In practice, the backward pass often doubles the number of GFLOPs executed, hence any GPU optimisation that reduces forward cost can have an even larger impact on training throughput.

## Memory Hierarchy of Modern GPUs (HBM, L2, SMEM, Registers)

GPUs possess a deep memory hierarchy designed to offer an increasing capacity at the expense of bandwidth.  Understanding this hierarchy is pivotal when designing kernels that minimize global‑memory traffic, the dominant performance bottleneck in attention due to the $\mathcal{O}(N^2d)$ scaling of the score matrix.

| Level | Typical Size | Typical Bandwidth (peak) | Access Latency | Notes |
|-------|--------------|-------------------------|----------------|-------|
| Global Memory (HBM) | 80–400 GB | ~5–20 TB/s | ~200 ns | Off‑chip, shared among all SMs, sparse caching in L2 |
| L2 Cache | ~4–8 GB | 5–10 TB/s | ~50 ns | GPU‑wide, L2 learns to cache high‑traffic patterns |
| Shared Memory (SMEM) | 48–96 KB per SM | 12–20 TB/s | ~5 ns | Programmer‑managed, banked, used for tiling |
| Registers (RMEM) | 192–256 words per thread | 400 GB/s (per SM) | ~1 ns | The fastest, private to each thread |

**High Bandwidth Memory (HBM).**  The 80 GB/s on Hopper H100 gives a theoretical per‑GPU memory bandwidth of ~900 GB/s at 225 GB/s per channel.  However, practical bandwidth is often limited by streaming patterns and memory alignment.  Therefore, reducing the number of global‑memory loads or writes is crucial.

**L2 Cache.**  The GPU’s on‑chip L2 cache is shared across all streaming multiprocessors (SMs).  It provides a “spill‑over” resource that can smooth out bursty access patterns but is still an order of magnitude slower than SMEM.

**Shared Memory (SMEM).**  Each SM manages its own instance of SMEM, typically 48–96 KB.  This memory is the prime candidate for block‑level tiling: a threadblock loads a small tile of $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{K}$, or $\mathbf{V}$ into SMEM, then performs multiple operations on that tile before evicting it.  The key to FlashAttention‑3 is to eliminate the intermediate global‑memory materialisations of the full $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ tensors; instead, the algorithm only stores a small tile of each at any time in SMEM or registers.

**Registers.**  Each thread houses up to 256 32‑bit registers.  Different parts of the kernel may need more or less register pressure.  In the context of FlashAttention‑3, the 2‑stage and 3‑stage pipelines introduce extra temporaries (e.g., $\mathbf{S}_\text{cur}$, $\mathbf{S}_\text{next}$, row‑wise maximums) that consume registers; proper register allocation is crucial to avoid spilling back to local memory.

## Thread, Warp, and Block Organization on the GPU  

The CUDA programming model organizes parallel work in a hierarchy of logical execution units that dovetails with the physical execution structure of the GPU.

| Level | Size | Ownership | Schedule | Example in Attention |
|-------|------|-----------|----------|----------------------|
| Thread | 32-gate | Single thread | Executes one instruction per cycle | Loads one element of $\mathbf{Q}$ into register |
| Warp | 32 threads | Warp group | Executes SIMD instructions together | Computes one row of $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$ via WGMMA |
| Warp‑Group (WPG) | 4 contiguous warps | Group of 4 warps | Operates as a unit; receives one tensor argument for WGMMA | A consumer warpgroup that performs a WGMMA on a tile of $\mathbf{S}$ |
| Threadblock (CTA) | Variable, e.g., 64 warps | All threads in a CTA | Runs on a single SM; scheduled on SMs | Holds a block of $\mathbf{Q}_i$ (size $B_r\times d$) |
| Cluster | 8 CTAs | One kernel launch | Groups CTAs for inter‑SM communication | Hopper’s cooperative thread array (CTA) cluster spreads a 2048‑token sequence over many CTAs |

- **Threads** are the smallest unit; each thread can load one or a few scalar values.
- **Warps** execute instructions in lockstep; all 32 threads in a warp run the same instruction simultaneously.  This architectural feature is exploited by the WGMMA, which interprets a warp as a matrix‑multiplication kernel: each thread contributes a partial product to a tile of the result.
- **Warp‑Groups** (Hopper’s terminology) allow an entire set of warps to share the same instruction stream, crucial for the Tensor Cores.  The WGMMA instruction is a *warp‑group‑wide* matrix multiply that can read operands from SMEM or registers, produce output into registers, and write results back asynchronously.
- **Threadblocks (CTAs)** bundle warps together; they share registers and SMEM.  The FlashAttention kernels map a block $\mathbf{Q}_i$ onto a CTA, giving the CTA the responsibility of computing the corresponding $\mathbf{O}_i$ tile.
- **Clusters** (Hopper’s super‑CTA groups) allow synchronization and data sharing across CTAs using the new cooperative group primitives.  Though not strictly required for FlashAttention‑3, cluster‑level cooperation can be leveraged for scaling to longer sequences with many CTAs.

## Asynchrony: Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) and Warp‑Group Matrix Multiply (WGMMA)  

From a performance standpoint, the most dominating cost in attention is the movement of data between memory tiers and the specialized arithmetic units that perform matrix multiplications.  Modern GPUs, particularly NVIDIA’s Hopper architecture, expose dedicated hardware blocks that can concurrently execute memory transfers and arithmetic, enabling *asynchrony*—the ability to launch an operation and continue doing useful work before the operation is finished.  

### Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA)  

The TMA is a dedicated engine that handles multi‑dimensional memory copies between global memory and SMEM.  Unlike classic `__ldg` loads, which block the issuing thread until data arrives, TMA launches a *stream* that runs in parallel with the rest of the kernel.  The kernel specifies the source and destination pointers, the shape and stride of the tile, and a *stream descriptor* that captures the layout.  After enqueueing the stream, the kernel can continue to issue other instructions.  When the data is needed, the kernel either blocks or polls for completion.  This mechanism is a foundation of FlashAttention’s tiling strategy: `$ \mathbf{K}_j, \mathbf{V}_j$` can be transferred to SMEM in the background while the kernel already processes the previous block.

### Warp‑Group Matrix Multiply (WGMMA)  

WGMMA is the instruction that powers the Tensor Cores in Hopper.  It hijacks the 32‑thread warp as a full matrix‑multiply engine, operating on tiles of size up to $128\times128$ in FP16 or FP8 precision (depending on the operand format).  The interface is simple:

```
WGMMA A_tile, B_tile, Acc_col
```
where `A_tile` and `B_tile` can be sourced from either SMEM or registers.  The operation is *asynchronous*: the instruction sets a flag indicating that the multiplication will finish at a later time, and the CPU’s control flow can proceed.  It is only when a later instruction accesses the accumulator that the kernel must wait for the multiplication to complete.  WGMMA’s asynchrony is key to the *producer‑consumer pipeline* of FlashAttention: the producer warps load data into SMEM via TMA, the consumer warps issue WGMMA, and they happen in parallel.

**Key points about WGMMA:**
- **Precision support:** WGMMA can target FP16, BF16, FP8, or even lower formats (FP4 in Blackwell).  FP8 operations double the throughput of FP16 on Hopper, but the layout constraints are stricter (see next section).
- **Operand layout constraints:**
  - For FP16 WGMMA, both operands may come from either *mn‑major* or *k‑major* layout.  
  - For FP8 WGMMA, only *k‑major* layout is accepted for the first operand, and the accelerator uses *k‑major* for the second operand by default.  
  - The accumulator lives in FP32 registers.  Consequently, when the first operand is in `mn‑major` layout, the per‑thread registers store the rows of the first operand, while the second operand is interpreted as columns.

- **Asynchronous behaviour:** The instruction returns control immediately.  The kernel typically inserts a `barrier` or `wait` before using the result, which automatically synchronizes the stream and continues execution without stalling the entire SM.

## Low‑Precision Tensor Cores: FP8, BF16, and Hardware Layout Constraints  

The past decade has seen a pronounced push toward lower precision in deep learning, motivated by the empirical observation that many models can be trained and inferred using 16‑bit or mixed‑precision arithmetic without a noticeable loss of accuracy.  NVIDIA’s successive GPU generations have extended this trend with BF16 in Ampere and FP8 in Hopper.

### FP8 Overview

FP8 is a floating‑point format with 8 bits: 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits, and 3 mantissa bits in the variant used by Hopper (often called e4m3).  This format delivers a narrow dynamic range and a coarse quantisation step, but it enables *double* the throughput of FP16 tensor cores because each 8‑bit element occupies half the storage and is processed more aggressively.  The throughput advantage is visible in Hopper H100, where FP8 WGMMA can reach 1.2 PFLOPs/s, roughly twice the FP16 rate.

### Layout Constraints

Because the WGMMA is highly optimised to read operands laid out in a particular tile ordering, its operand layout constraints become a source of architectural friction.

Consider a GEMM of the form $A(m\times k)\times B(n\times k)^{\top}$.  In SMEM, an *mn‑major* layout stores rows of $A$ contiguously (i.e., $A_{\text{row}_i, k}$ varies on the outer loop), whereas a *k‑major* layout stores columns contiguously (the inner loop is over $k$).  The WGMMA accepts both layouts for FP16 operands, but only the *k‑major* layout for the first operand when targeting FP8.  This limitation forces a programmer to either transpose $A$ before loading it into SMEM or rely on an in‑kernel transpose that therefore incurs extra register shuffling.

In addition, the *accumulator* is stored in FP32 registers but has a layout that is implied by the operand's tiling.  When chaining two FP8 WGMMAs, as FlashAttention‑3 does:

1. $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\quad$ (FP8 WGMMA)
2. $\tilde{\mathbf{P}} = \mathrm{exp}(\mathbf{S})$ (softmax, still in FP32)
3. $\mathbf{O} = \tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}\quad$ (second FP8 WGMMA)

The result of the first WGMMA is an FP32 accumulator tile, which must be laid out in a way that the second WGMMA can consume.  The Flips in the register ordering (e.g., $\{d0,d1,d4,d5,d2,d3,d6,d7\}$) are a lightweight byte permutation that reorders the accumulator to match the input expectations of the second WGMMA.  This transformation is usually cheaper than performing a full data copy between SMEM and registers.

### Bottom‑Line Impact on FlashAttention‑3

FlashAttention‑3 resolves the layout constraints by:
- Performing an in‑kernel transpose of $\mathbf{V}$ while it is being loaded from GMEM, producing a $k$‑major layout in SMEM.
- Using a *byte‑permutation* after the first FP8 WGMMA to re‑order the FP32 accumulator into the correct layout for the second FP8 WGMMA.
- Adopting *block‑wise* quantisation to keep per‑block scaling factors that mitigate the coarse dynamic range of FP8.

These decisions, while subtle, are indispensable for both the *latency* (avoiding stalls) and the *accuracy* (reducing quantisation error) of the algorithm.
