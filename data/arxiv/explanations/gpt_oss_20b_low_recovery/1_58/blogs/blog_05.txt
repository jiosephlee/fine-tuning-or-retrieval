# Scaling Laws Revisited: 1‑Bit LLMs Across Model Sizes

## Why a New Scaling Law Matters

Large Language Models (LLMs) have exploded in size over the past few years. Training a 30 B‑parameter model today is still ten‑fold cheaper than training a 3 B model the year before, but even with that acceleration most deployers still run into the classic “more parameters = more compute & memory” trap. Until recently, the only way to shave costs was to quantize weights and activations for *inference*, but the underlying training frictions stayed the same.

Enter **BitNet b1.58**: a 1‑bit Transformer that stores every weight in the ternary set \(\{-1, 0, +1\}\). In practice, this is equivalent to a *1.58‑bit* representation—one bit for the sign, one extra “zero” bit, plus a small scaling factor. The consequence is a catastrophic reduction in memory bandwidth, floating‑point operations, and, hence, energy. The authors of the paper showed that starting at a 3 B scale, a BitNet b1.58 model can match or even exceed the perplexity of a full‑precision (FP16) LLaMA baseline, *without* any floating‑point arithmetic during matrix multiplication.

Because the performance of BitNet b1.58 scales with size, the authors discovered a new **scaling equivalence** that maps 1‑bit models to their 16‑bit cousins in a way that preserves both accuracy and hardware efficiency. This redefines how we think about “Larger = Better” in the age of low‑precision LLMs.

| **13 B b1.58** | 3 B FP16 | 13 B b1.58 runs ~2.5× faster, uses ~3.5× less memory, and consumes ~70× less energy than 3 B FP16. |
| **30 B b1.58** | 7 B FP16 | 30 B b1.58 matches the perplexity of 7 B FP16 while being 3–4× faster and ~3× lighter on GPU cards. |

These numbers come directly from the paper’s exhaustive GPU memory, latency, energy, and throughput measurements on A100 hardware with state‑of‑the‑art kernels. They give us a clean recipe: **if you need the performance of a 3 B FP16 model, run a 13 B BitNet b1.58 instead; if 7 B FP16 is your sweet spot, pick 30 B b1.58; and if 13 B FP16 is what you want, crank up to 70 B b1.58.**

## Picking the Right Model for Your Budget

### Scenario 1 – Edge or Mobile Deployment

On a mobile device, the memory bill is often the top‑line cost. A 3 B FP16 model will likely overrun 8 GB of RAM even if you use quantization. The 13 B b1.58 model, however, stores **all 13 B parameters in a 1.58‑bit format**, which in practice takes roughly \(13\,\text{B}\times1.58~\text{bits} \approx 3.3~\text{GB}\) on disk and about \(1.4~\text{GB}\) of on‑device RAM once loaded. That’s enough headroom for an 8‑GB mobile CPU to keep the model resident, while still delivering the quality of a 3 B FP16 LLM.

### Scenario 2 – Cloud‑scale Inference

If you run hundreds of queries per second on a GPU farm, you care almost exclusively about throughput. The bit‑wise addition in BitNet b1.58 means the kernel is over **four times faster** than its FP16 counterpart on the same hardware. Using a 30 B b1.58 for tasks that historically required a 7 B FP16 gives you both a throughput win and a **3× drop in memory consumption** on each GPU, allowing more concurrent batches or the deployment of an extra model per node.

### Scenario 3 – Research & Development

Training interest is where the real game changes. Training a 70 B b1.58 on 2 T tokens showed *better* zero‑shot accuracy than a 13 B FP16 trained on the same data, with no loss in perplexity. So if your research budget is limited, you might train a 30 B or 70 B b1.58 model on a handful of GPUs and still outperforms a 7 B or 13 B FP16 trained on the same pipeline.

## Implications for Future LLM Work

### 1. **Re‑thinking Scaling Laws**

Traditional scaling laws in LLMs (see, e.g., Kaplan et al.) map *accurate performance* to *model size* with a power‑law trend, assuming a fixed precision. The introduction of 1‑bit weights uncouples *efficiency* from *size*: you can “double-sized” without a proportional cost blow‑up. Models that previously fell below the “threshold” where adding capacity improved accuracy now *stay* in that sweet spot because the obtainment cost per parameter drops dramatically.

### 2. **Hardware Co‑design**

The term “1.58‑bit” is a signal to hardware architects: matrix multiplications no longer need floating‑point units—just integer addition and a tiny lookup for the sign and zero masks. The paper already alludes to a new generation of **LPUs (LLM Processing Units)** that would favor binary word–level parallelism, many fewer memory ports, and aggressive on‑chip caching of the zero‑masked weights. This opens the door for chips that beat GPUs in terms of energy‑per‑token by an order of magnitude, directly benefiting cloud accelerators and dynamic inference services.

### 3. **Long‑Sequence and MoE Synergies**

Because the only activations that remain in a higher precision are the “raw” input tokens and the embedding layer, the bulk of the KV‑cache memory can be shrunk to 8‑bit or even 4‑bit. This means longer contexts become economically feasible. Moreover, a 1‑bit *Mixture-of-Experts* model would be a perfect fit; the weight reduction drastically cuts inter‑chip bandwidth, making MoE practical on a single chip.

### 4. **Open‑Source & Ecosystem Integration**

BitNet b1.58 already factors into popular tooling: it uses LLaMA‑like components, removes biases, and relies on RMSNorm & SwiGLU—all of which are well‑supported by HuggingFace and vLLM. The only barrier now is the lack of mature 1‑bit kernels in the mainstream libraries, which will likely come as a matter of pace once this research gains traction.

## How to Start Using BitNet b1.58

1. **Get the checkpoints** – The authors have released the 1‑bit model weights for 13 B and 30 B variants. They come in a custom format that can be loaded directly into the BitLinear layer.
2. **Switch to the BitLinear layer** – Replace all `nn.Linear` calls in your Transformer with `BitLinear`. The forward pass is identical; the only difference is the activation of the ternary weights.
3. **Tune the quantization** – The `absmean` quantizer scales by the average absolute value of the matrix. Adjust `epsilon` if you run into saturation issues on specific layers.
4. **Measure** – Use a GPU profiling tool (e.g., Nsight) or the `FasterTransformer` benchmark to verify latency and memory improvements. You should see a 2–4× speed‑up and 3–4× memory drop.

## Bottom Line

The **1‑bit scaling law** in BitNet b1.58 flips the classic LLM trade‑off on its head. By moving to a ternary weight representation, we free up compute and memory budgets, allowing us to push models to 70 B while still remaining within the cost envelope of a 13 B FP16 baseline. For practitioners, this means:

- **Get more accuracy per dollar**: Run bigger models without paying a linear price in GPU hours.
- **Run on less hardware**: Fit more queries into the same GPU cluster or deploy larger models on mobile CPUs.
- **Future‑proof your pipelines**: As hardware evolves to favor integer‑only operations, BitNet b1.58 already sits at the intersection of software and hardware innovation.

In a world where every watt saved counts, 1‑bit LLMs are not just a curiosity—they represent a new point in the efficiency frontier that will shape the next wave of intelligent applications. If you’re building or scaling LLMs, the message is clear: look past the *number of parameters* and look toward *bits per parameter*.
