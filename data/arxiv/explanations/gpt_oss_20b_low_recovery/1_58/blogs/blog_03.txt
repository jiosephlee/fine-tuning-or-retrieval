# Why 1‑Bit LLMs Beat 16‑Bit Models in Speed and Memory

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become the workhorse behind everything from chatbots to code‑generation tools. Yet the bigger the model, the more it costs to store, run, and power. A recent paper, *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*, shows that you can shrink every weight of a transformer to just *{-1, 0, +1}* – a 1‑bit representation – and still match, or even beat, the performance of a full‑precision 16‑bit baseline.  
Below we unpack the numbers, explain what they mean in everyday terms, and show when a 1‑bit model is the clear winner.

---

## 1. What Are 1‑Bit LLMs?

In a standard transformer the weights are 16‑bit floating‑point numbers (FP16). Replacing ``nn.Linear`` with a custom **BitLinear** layer that keeps each weight in the ternary set {−1, 0, +1} reduces the *information depth* from 16 bits to **1.58 bits** (the extra 0 worth gives us “1.58” in binary).  

During inference Only *integer addition* is required for the mat‑mul that dominates LLM cost; no floating‑point multiplication is needed. This leads to:

| **Operation** | **FP16** | **BitNet b1.58** |
|---------------|----------|-------------------|
| MAC (Multiply‑Accumulate) | 16‑bit arithmetic + 16‑bit MAC | 8‑bit addition + 1‑bit multiply (trivial) |
| Memory per weight | 2 bytes | ≈0.25 bytes (1 bit) |

---

## 2. Empirical Gains – From 3 B to 70 B

| **Model size** | **Speed‑up** | **Memory‑red.** | **Latency** | **Throughput** |
|----------------|--------------|-----------------|-------------|----------------|
| 3 B | 2.7× faster | 3.55× lighter | 35 ms/token (FP16) → 13 ms/token (1‑bit) | *+60 %* |
| 3.9 B | 2.4× faster | 3.32× lighter | 13 ms → 5 ms | *+70 %* |
| 7 B | 3.0× faster | 3.1× lighter | 5 ms → 1.5 ms | *+90 %* |
| 13 B | 3.7× faster | 2.8× lighter | 1.5 ms → 0.4 ms | *+110 %* |
| 70 B | 4.1× faster | 2.3× lighter | 0.4 ms → 0.1 ms | *+190 %* |

> **Latency** is the time taken to produce each token.  
> **Throughput** is how many tokens per second can be generated once the pipeline is full (cache‑warm).

The speed‑up scales with model size because *nn.Linear* – the part that benefits most from the 1‑bit chemistry – constitutes a larger fraction of operations in bigger models.

---

## 3. Energy Savings – 70‑Fold Reduction

Figure 1 (conceptual) shows the energy breakdown per mat‑mul on a 7 nm ASIC:

```
                    ┌───────────────────────┐
                    │  FP16 LLaMA (16‑bit)  │
                    │  ⇢  FP16 mul + add   │
  ╭──────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────╮
  │  BitNet b1.58 (1‑bit)         │  ⇢  INT8 add + 1‑bit mul (negligible) │
  └──────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Using an energy‑cost model, **BitNet b1.58** meets a *71×* reduction in the arithmetic–operation energy for matrix multiplication compared to FP16 on a 7 nm node. As models grow, the energy advantage widens because the share of linear layers rises.

---

## 4. Accuracy – When 1‑Bits Match the Full‑Precision

### Perplexity (WikiText2 & C4)

Perplexity is a language‑model‑specific “surprise” metric; lower is better.

| Model (depth) | FP16 | 1‑bit |
|---------------|------|-------|
| 3 B | 12.10 | 12.12 |
| 3.9 B | 11.60 | 11.58 |
| 7 B | 11.25 | 11.26 |
| 13 B | 10.90 | 10.91 |

The gap closes at **3 B**: perplexity values are statistically indistinguishable.

### Zero‑Shot Accuracy

A second test is “zero‑shot” performance on a battery of NLU benchmarks (ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ).  
Figure 2 (interactive plot) shows the mean accuracy versus model size.

```
  Accuracy
    ^
 100%|   ● (1‑bit 70B)
     |   ├─► FP16 70B
  90%|   │   ● (1‑bit 13B)
     |   │   ├─► FP16 13B
  80%|   │   │   ● (1‑bit 3.9B)
     |   │   │   ├─► FP16 3.9B
  70%|   │   │   │   ● (1‑bit 3B)
     │   │   │   │   ├─► FP16 3B

```

> **Key takeaway**: A 1‑bit **3 B** model *outperforms* a 3 B FP16 baseline in almost every task while being cheaper to run. By 13 B, the 1‑bit variant matches or exceeds the FP16 counterpart with a **~20–35 %** throughput boost.

---

## 5. Practical Implications

| Scenario | Why 1‑bit Helps |
|----------|----------------|
| **Edge & Mobile** | With *8 ×* less memory and *70×* less arithmetic energy, a 1‑bit LLM can run in the same SRAM budget that currently limits 3 B FP16 models to 1 B. |
| **Large‑Scale Serving** | A 70 B 1‑bit model costs *the same RAM* as a 13 B FP16 model but delivers *nearly double* inference throughput. Even in a GPU‑cluster, the same GPU can host *11×* more instances. |
| **Multi‑GPU Sparse MoE** | 1‑bit MoE removes the need to shard the weight matrix across chips – all weights fit on a single chip, eliminating cross‑chip communication. |

## 7. Takeaway

- A *deterministic* ternary weight set keeps the modeling power of transformers intact.  
- **1‑bit LLMs** are demonstrably *faster*, *smaller*, and *more energy‑efficient* than FP16 models starting at 3 B.  
- For every 10‑fold increase in parameter count, a 1‑bit model matches or outperforms a 10‑fold smaller FP16 model.  
- Scaling out to 70 B shows *quadratic* gains in latency and memory, turning a table‑top LLM into a server‑cluster‑grade machine in terms of efficiency.

> **Bottom line:** If you’re deploying LLMs at scale or on constrained hardware, consider switching to a 1‑bit architecture. The math, empirics, and hardware evidence all point the same direction – a *tiny alphabet* yields *big performance*.
