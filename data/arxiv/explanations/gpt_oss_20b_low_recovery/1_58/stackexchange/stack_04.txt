\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the scaling law for 1.58‑bit LLMs differ from the traditional scaling law for FP16 models?
Question:
In the results section, the authors provide equivalences such as a 13B BitNet b1.58 outperforming a 3B FP16 LLM. What is the theoretical basis for this scaling shift? Is it simply because of reduced computational cost allowing larger models, or is there a mathematical relationship between bits per weight, model size, and perplexity that the paper derives?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “new scaling law” that the paper talks about is not an entirely new empirical curve for *per‑parameter* performance – it is the realization that the cost of a model now scales with *bits per weight* as well as with its size.  
In the traditional FP16 world the effective “parameter budget” is just *the number of floating‑point parameters* \(P\): a 3 B‑parameter FP16 LLM and a 13 B‑parameter FP16 LLM differ only by a constant factor in the number of parameters (≈ 4.3×).  
With 1.58‑bit weights the same *numeric* size (e.g., a 3 B‑parameter model) occupies only \(0.0785\) B (because \(3\times10^9\times1.58/8\approx0.59\,\text{GB}\) instead of ≈ 19 GB).  In other words, a 1.58‑bit model can host many more *effective* parameters for the same resource budget.

---

### 1.  How the scaling shift is expressed

The paper derives an implicit “equivalence mapping” between FP16 and BitNet b1.58 that is illustrated in the paragraph *“BitNet b1.58 is enabling a new scaling law…”*.  
The authors measured latency, memory usage and energy for a set of BitNet b1.58 models of size 3–70 B and compared them against the same metrics for FP16 LLaMA baselines.  For each FP16 model size \(S_{\mathrm{FP16}}\) they found a **larger BitNet** size \(S_{\mathrm{B58}}\) that achieves the *same* or better performance *with less cost*.  The concrete equivalences reported are:

- 13 B BitNet b1.58 ≈ 3 B FP16  
- 30 B BitNet b1.58 ≈ 7 B FP16  
- 70 B BitNet b1.58 ≈ 13 B FP16  

This is a *data‑driven shift* of the scaling law: the “effective” size that the hardware can support for a given budget grows roughly 4× (3 B → 13 B) while the *performance* does not degrade.

---

### 2.  Why the shift occurs – a mathematical intuition

Let  

\[
\Delta_{\text{bits}}=\text{bits per weight}
\]

For an FP16 model \(\Delta_{\text{bits}}^{\text{FP16}}=16\).  
For BitNet b1.58 \(\Delta_{\text{bits}}^{\text{B58}}=1.58\).  
If a hardware platform can process \(M\) bits of weight per second (roughly the same because the *number of arithmetic operations* is unchanged, only the **data movement** changes), the *effective* number of parameters it can handle in a given time scales as

\[
P_{\text{effective}} \propto \frac{M}{\Delta_{\text{bits}}}.
\]

Therefore

\[
P_{\text{effective}}^{\text{B58}} \approx \frac{16}{1.58}\;P_{\text{effective}}^{\text{FP16}}
\approx 10.1\times P_{\text{effective}}^{\text{FP16}} .
\]

In practice the authors observe a factor ≈ 4–5, because the per‑parameter computational cost is not perfectly linear (the quantized kernels are lighter and easier to parallelize, activation bit‑width is 8 bit, etc.).  Still, the *order‑of‑magnitude* gain in parameter budget is the key.

---

### 3.  Not just a cost advantage, but a capacity advantage

Because the *same* 3 B‑parameter budget can now host a 13 B‑parameter effective network, the empirical scaling law observed for FP16 models (e.g., perplexity improving roughly as some power‑law of \(P\)) can now be satisfied with *smaller* raw parameter counts.  In other words, the 1.58‑bit model uses the available compute‑memory budget more efficiently, allowing us to step further along the FP16 scaling curve without consuming more hardware resources.  

The paper does not present a closed‑form equation like \( \text{PPL} \approx a \cdot P^{-b} \) for BitNet b1.58; instead it shows that for every FP16 model that hits a given perplexity, a 1‑58‑bit model of roughly four times the parameter count achieves a **Pareto‑improved** point in the space of
\[
(\text{perplexity}, \; \text{latency}, \; \text{memory}, \; \text{energy}).
\]

---

### 4.  Connection to broader scaling ideas

Traditional LLM scaling laws (e.g. Kaplan et al., 2020) focus on relationships such as  
\[
\text{PPL} \approx A \cdot P^{-\alpha}
\]
where \(P\) is the number of parameters.  They implicitly assume a constant *parameter bit‑width*.  When we change \(\Delta_{\text{bits}}\), the *effective* \(P\) that can be fit into a given hardware budget changes, so the **effective scaling curve** shifts.  
The paper’s equivalence list is therefore a concrete example of how changing the *quantization regime* reshapes the resource‑performance trade‑off while staying within the same asymptotic scaling regime.

---

### Bottom line

1. The scaling shift is empirically derived from measurements of latency, memory and energy for BitNet b1.58 versus FP16 baselines.  
2. Mathematically it stems from the fact that the *effective* number of parameters that a given hardware budget can handle scales inversely with the number of bits per weight.  
3. The equivalences (13 B → 3 B, 30 B → 7 B, 70 B → 13 B) show that a 1.58‑bit model can achieve the same or better performance while consuming 2–4× less resources, effectively moving the traditional scaling law curve to the left.  

Thus the “new scaling law” is not about a new functional form for performance versus size, but about a new *resource‑efficient mapping* that allows larger effective models under the same hardware budget.
