\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is the intuition behind the `absmean` quantization function used for 1.58‑bit weights?
Question:
The paper defines $\tilde{W} = \text{RoundClip}\bigl(\frac{W}{\bar{|W|} + \tau}, -1, 1\bigr)$ where $\bar{|W|} = \frac{1}{nm}\textstyle\bigl\rvert W \bigr\rvert$. I understand that this normalises the weights before clipping to \\{-1,0,1\\}, but why does normalising by the average absolute value improve quantisation compared to, say, a fixed global scale or per‑column scaling? Does it help preserve the dynamic range across layers, and how does the small $\tau$ (epsilon) affect stability?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *BitNet b1.58* the weights are forced to the ternary set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
The paper calls the mapping

\[
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},-1,1\right),
\qquad  
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|
\]

the **absmean** quantization function.  
Below is an intuitive and technical explanation of why the *average‑absolute* scaling is used, what benefits it brings compared with a fixed global scale or per‑column scaling, and the role of the small epsilon \(\varepsilon\).

---

### 1. Why scale by the *average absolute value*?

1. **Layer‑wise dynamic range preservation**

   A transformer layer contains many attention heads or MLP sub‑blocks, each of which tends to develop a different weight distribution after training.  
   The average absolute value \(\gamma\) of a weight matrix captures *how big* the typical magnitude is inside that particular layer, without imposing an arbitrary global scale.  
   By dividing by \(\gamma\), we “normalize’’ the layer’s weights so that most of them fall in the interval \([-1,1]\).  
   After the subsequent `RoundClip`, the most significant values become ±1, while the rest collapse to 0.  
   Thus every layer keeps an equal chance to use the full ternary range; a layer whose weights would otherwise be very small does not get overwhelmed by a global high scale, and a layer with very large weights does not saturate the range.

2. **Robustness to training dynamics**

   During training the distribution of weights can shift (e.g., due to weight decay or adaptive optimizers).  
   Using the up‑to‑date mean absolute value means the quantization automatically adapts to these shifts, keeping the ternary mapping roughly centered around zero.  
   A fixed global scale would either over‑compress some layers (making them effectively constant) or under‑compress others (causing many weights to saturate at ±1).

3. **Simplicity vs. complexity**

   A more fine‑grained per‑column or per‑channel scaling would give slightly higher fidelity but would require storing an additional scalar per column, increasing memory and kernel complexity.  
   The absmean approach keeps the scaling factor per *matrix* only, matching the architecture’s existing `nn.Linear` semantics and avoiding extra memory traffic.

---

### 2. What happens if we used a fixed global scale or per‑column scaling instead?

| Scaling scheme | Effect on quantization | Practical complications |
|----------------|------------------------|-------------------------|
| **Fixed global** | All layers are forced to share one scale. Layers with very different magnitudes end up being either *prematurely clipped* (when the global scale is large) or *too sparse* (when the global scale is small). | Needs careful tuning; can degrade performance dramatically. |
| **Per‑column** | Each column (i.e., each output dimension) gets its own scale, which can capture subtle differences. | Requires storing and broadcasting \(n\) scalars per weight matrix; increases code complexity and memory jumps. |
| **Average‐abs** | Provides a compromise: captures the overall spread of a layer but keeps kernel minimal. | Uses only one scalar per weight matrix; the quantization remains hardware‑friendly. |

The paper’s experiments show that the absmean method yields perplexity and zero‑shot performance comparable to full‑precision models while keeping the model size and runtime dramatically smaller. The modest overhead of a single scalar per layer is negligible compared to the benefits.

---

### 3. Intuition behind the small epsilon \(\varepsilon\)

1. **Numerical stability**

   If a weight matrix were all zeros (which can happen early in training or in some residual connections), \(\gamma\) would be zero and division would be undefined.  
   Adding a tiny \(\varepsilon\) (e.g., \(10^{-5}\)) guarantees a non‑zero denominator so the quantizer always behaves.

2. **Avoiding extreme scaling**

   Even when \(\gamma\) is very small, the division could blow the values up, leading to an overwhelming number of ±1 outcomes and a loss of information.  
   The epsilon acts as a hard cap: the effective scale becomes \(\min(\gamma,\varepsilon)\), preventing runaway amplification of tiny weights.

3. **Gradual decay**

   Because \(\varepsilon\) is much smaller than typical \(\gamma\) values, it has negligible influence during normal operation.  
   Only in degenerate cases does it bite, ensuring stability without sacrificing expressiveness.

---

### 4. Connection to broader quantization ideas

The absmean approach is a form of *per‑layer mean absolute value (MA) scaling*, a family of techniques used throughout low‑bit quantization research (e.g., GPT‑Q, AdaRound, and many post‑training schemes).  
It sits between fully asymmetric per‑tensor scaling (most expressive but heavy) and symmetric fixed‑point scaling (fast but brittle).  
By preserving each layer’s dynamic range while keeping the quantizer simple, it aligns well with hardware constraints: a single scalar per kernel can be fused into the GEMM read‑out, and the ternary weights can be merged with integer addition in the inference engine.

---

### 5. Bottom line

- **Average‑absolute scaling** adapts the ternary mapping to each layer’s own weight distribution, avoiding the pitfalls of a global scale and the overhead of per‑column scaling.  
- It keeps the quantizer lightweight, so the back‑end can fuse the process into a fast integer GEMM.  
- The tiny epsilon protects against pathological cases without affecting normal training dynamics.  

In short, the absmean trick is a practical, layer‑aware, and hardware‑friendly way to turn full‑precision weight matrices into ternary (\(-1,0,+1\)) representations with little loss in model quality.
