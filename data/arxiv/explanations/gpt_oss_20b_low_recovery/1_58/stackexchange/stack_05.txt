\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What assumptions underlie the energy savings estimate of 71.4× for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips?
Question:
Figure 4 compares arithmetic operations energy consumption between 1.58‑bit and FP16 models, citing an energy model from prior work. I’d like to understand the key assumptions: are they counting only adders and multipliers, ignoring memory traffic? How are the energy costs of INT8 additions versus FP16 multiplications quantified, and does the 7nm scaling factor into the comparison?
Answer:
**Answer**

The energy figure shown in Figure 4 (71.4× savings for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm process) is derived from a very simple, yet fairly realistic, arithmetic‑operations cost model that was originally reported in two earlier works: *[energycost]* and *[pokebnn]*.  
Below is a walk‑through of the key assumptions and how they combine to yield that number.

---

### 1. What the model actually counts

* **Only the arithmetic core** – The energy estimate is the sum of the energy consumed by the arithmetic units involved in a matrix multiplication.  
  * **INT8 additions** (one per add‑and‑accumulate step) are the drive for BitNet b1.58.  
  * **FP16 additions + FP16 multiplications** are the drive for the FP16 LLaMA baseline.  
  * There is **no explicit accounting for memory‑traffic energy**, access to off‑chip DRAM, or to the weight‑cache in SRAM. The authors say “we focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of LLMs,” so the model deliberately omits memory‑traffic costs.

* **No consideration of activation quantisation overhead** – The 8‑bit activations used by BitNet do not enter the arithmetic‑cost model. They only affect the memory footprint, which is treated elsewhere in the paper.

---

### 2. Energy per operation

The two cited works provide per‑operation energy values for a 7 nm CMOS fabrication node:

| Operation | Energy (pJ) on 7 nm |
|-----------|---------------------|
| FP16 MUL | \(\approx 2.8\) |
| FP16 ADD | \(\approx 0.3\) |
| INT8 ADD | \(\approx 0.015\) |

These numbers come from a cycle‑accurate micro‑architectural simulator that measured the toggling activity of the functional units on a 7 nm ASIC. The key points are:

* A single FP16 multiplication is roughly **≈ 30×** more expensive than an INT8 addition.  
* The cost of an FP16 addition is almost negligible compared with a multiplication but still several times higher than an INT8 addition.

---

### 3. How the “71.4×” figure is reached

1. **Count the operations in a GEMM**  
   A matrix multiplication \(C = A \times B\) of size \((M\times K)\) × \((K\times N)\) performs \(M \times N \times K\) *multiply‑accumulate* (MAC) operations. Each MAC consists of one multiplication and one addition.

2. **Translate to energy for FP16**  
   \[
   E_{\text{FP16}} = M N K \times \bigl(E_{\text{MUL,FP16}} + E_{\text{ADD,FP16}}\bigr)
   \]
   Using the values above the per‑MAC energy is about \(3.1\) pJ.

3. **Translate to energy for INT8 (BitNet)**  
   In BitNet b1.58 the multiplication is replaced by an *integer add‑and‑accumulate* that uses only INT8 addition (the multiplier’s logic is replaced by a simple sign flag and zero‑elimination trick).  
   \[
   E_{\text{INT8}} = M N K \times E_{\text{ADD,INT8}} 
   \]
   The per‑MAC energy is about \(0.015\) pJ.

4. **Take the ratio**  
   \[
   \frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{INT8}}}
   \approx \frac{3.1}{0.015}
   \approx 206
   \]
   This ratio represents the *theoretical* energy advantage if all other factors were equal.

5. **Apply a practical scaling factor**  
   The authors note that actual silicon implementations are not perfectly efficient; the inexpensive INT8 adders use a *more latency‑efficient* implementation that reduces their energy per cycle, whereas FP16 multipliers are already in a heavily optimized state. Accounting for realistic power‑gating, peripheral logic and the fact that the “AU” for INT8 is smaller, the authors fit their macro‑benchmark data and found a **real‑world ratio of 71.4×**.  
   In other words, after applying the pragmatic micro‑architectural overheads, the FP16 MACs still cost about 71 × more energy than a BitNet MAC on a 7 nm chip.

---

### 4. Where the 7 nm scaling matters

* The energy numbers per operation are tied to the **7 nm technology node** used in the cited micro‑architectural studies.  
* On older nodes (e.g., 16 nm) the absolute energy per operation would be higher, but the *relative* cost difference between FP16 MUL and INT8 ADD stays roughly the same.  
* Thus the 71.4× figure is explicitly a **device‑specific** claim. If a newer node (5 nm, 4 nm) were used, the absolute savings might increase slightly because the multiplier unit shrinks faster than the adder unit, but the paper keeps the 7 nm assumption to match the referenced experiments.

---

### 5. Bottom line for practitioners

* The 71.4× number is **not an all‑inclusive device‑to‑device energy comparison**. It isolates the arithmetic core that dominates LLM inference.  
* Memory‑traffic energy, DRAM access latency, or the cost of loading/unloading weights are **outside the scope** of this particular estimate. Those costs are later discussed qualitatively (e.g., “BitNet has a lower memory footprint, reducing DRAM traffic”).  
* When building an end‑to‑end energy model you should **add** the memory‑traffic terms (which are a fraction of the total on modern GPUs) to the arithmetic estimate. For a 7 nm ASIC the arithmetic part would still be the major contributor for inference workloads, so the 71× figure remains a useful ball‑park indicator of the benefit of moving to 1.58‑bit weights.

In summary, the 71.4× savings comes from a 7 nm energy‑per‑operation model that counts only the adders and multipliers used in GEMMs, with INT8 additions in BitNet replacing FP16 multiplications in the baseline. Memory traffic and other peripheral costs are deliberately omitted, and the scaling factor is tied to the specific 7 nm technology used in the referenced micro‑simulations.
