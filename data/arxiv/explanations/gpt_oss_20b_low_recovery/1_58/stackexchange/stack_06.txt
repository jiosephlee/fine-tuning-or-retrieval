\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the 1.58‑bit quantisation affect the training dynamics compared to full‑precision training?
Question:
The paper shows that training from scratch with 1.58‑bit weights and 8‑bit activations can reach similar perplexities as FP16 models. Does the quantisation introduce significant bias in gradients? How do the authors handle gradient scaling or rescaling during training, and is the `absmean` quantisation applied during back‑propagation as well?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
During training BitNet b1.58 uses a *straight‑through estimator* (STE) for the quantization routine. The weights are openly quantized before the forward pass, but for the backward pass the gradient is simply copied from the quantized weights (`∂L/∂Ŵ = ∂L/∂W`). No extra gradient scaling or rescaling is performed; the same 1.58‑bit quantization that appears in the forward pass is *not* re‑applied to the gradients. The only scaling that matters is the “abs‑mean” factor γ used to bring the weight magnitudes into the \{-1,0,1\} range—this factor is recomputed at every weight‑update step, effectively redefining the scaling of the discrete weights while the gradient remains in full precision.

---

### 1. Does the quantization introduce bias in the gradients?

Quantizing the forward tensors to discrete values inevitably creates a *dead‐weight* effect: small updates that would have been absorbed in 16‑bit are clamped to 0 when they cross a quantization boundary. However, BitNet b1.58 sidesteps most of this bias by

1. **Using a *soft* scaling λ = γ (the mean absolute value)** – before rounding, each weight is divided by γ, so the dynamic range of the discrete set stays centred around the current statistics of the weight matrix. This keeps most significant gradients within the range where rounding to an adjacent ternary value is still informative.

2. **Applying the straight‑through estimator** – the gradient flowing into the quantized weight is taken as if the quantization were an identity function. In practice this means the optimizer sees the true gradient with respect to the *real* weight (not the quantized weight), and the update step is computed in full precision. The discrete weight is only *re‑quantized* after the step. Thus the training dynamics are governed by the full‑precision gradient, not by a biased discrete gradient.

Because of these mechanisms the accumulated bias over many updates is kept small, which is why the authors observe perplexities comparable to FP16 training.

---

### 2. How do the authors handle gradient scaling or rescaling during training?

The only form of scaling that appears in the training loop is the **abs‑mean factor γ**. The training procedure is:

1. Compute the weight‑scale γ from the current weight matrix (equation (3) in the paper).  
2. Quantize the weights using the `RoundClip` function.  
3. Forward‑propagate with the ternary weight matrix.  
4. Compute the loss and back‑propagate *as if* the weights were continuous:  
   \[
   \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} \;\text{(full‑precision gradient)}
   \]
5. Update the *full‑precision* weight matrix with the optimizer, typically AdamW.  
6. Repeat.

No extra multiplicative factor is applied to the gradient; the scaling of the weight (γ) does not appear in the backward pass. The only time the gradient is “rescaled” is indirectly: after the weight update, γ is recomputed and a new quantized weight matrix is derived. This re‑scaling does not tamper with the original gradient computed in step 4.

---

### 3. Is `absmean` quantization used during back‑propagation?

No. The `absmean` quantization is applied **only to the forward tensors** (weights and activations). Once the backward pass begins, the algorithm treats the quantization step as a non‑differentiable barrier and replaces its derivative with one that is identity (STE). Thus the gradient with respect to the quantized weight is simply the gradient with respect to the underlying full‑precision weight. The same holds for activations: they are quantized for the forward pass only; the backward pass propagates gradients through the un‑quantized pre‑activation values.

---

### 4. Why does this work for large‑scale LLMs?

The heavy part of LLM training is the matrix multiplication in the linear layers. In FP16 the multiplication and accumulation dominate the memory traffic and power budget. By constraining weights to \{-1,0,1\}, the authors remove the multiplication entirely; the matrix product becomes an *integer addition* that can be performed with a 2‑bit kernel. Because the forward values are exact integers, the activations are only residual (8‑bit), the full‑precision gradients can still be computed without any numerical explosion, and the optimizer can make fine‑grained updates in the continuous weight space. The *abs‑mean* scaling keeps the ternary weights in a reasonable dynamic range so that the quantization error does not grow unbounded.

This combination of an exact forward quantization, STE‑based backward pass, and per‑forward‑step scaling is what allows BitNet b1.58 to match FP16 perplexities while training from scratch on 100 B tokens.

---

### Bottom line

- Quantization is applied only in the forward pass.  
- The backward pass uses a straight‑through estimator; the gradients are *not* scaled or re‑quantized.  
- The only scaling factor that changes during training is the mean‑absolute‑value γ that normalises the weights before quantization.  
- Because the gradient update operates on the underlying full‑precision weight, the bias introduced by the ternary quantization is kept minimal and does not degrade training performance.
