\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

# Long Context Challenges in Large Language Models

The ability of a language model to process and reason over a large number of consecutive tokens has become a pivotal capability in modern AI systems. From in‑context learning scenarios, where a model is prompted with dozens of examples, to autonomous agents that must keep track of events unfolding over long interactions, the size of the context window fundamentally limits what can be achieved. In this chapter we examine why long context is desirable, how current state‑of‑the‑art models fall short, and what obstacles must be surmounted to unlock truly ultra‑long behavior.

## Importance of Long Context

Large language models (LLMs) derive much of their power from their internal memory of preceding tokens. When new tokens arrive, the model computes attention scores against all previously seen tokens, effectively remembering a history that can span the entire prompt. The window is the boundary that determines how many of those tokens are retained. A 4,096‑token window, for example, is adequate for short prompts but cripples the model when it encounters documents, code files, or dialogues that exceed that size.

In‑context learning, where one hand‑writes a chain of demonstrations and then asks the model to solve a new instance, almost instantly reaches the 4,096 boundary. LLM agents that need to keep track of a long conversation, logs, or generated internal plans must also rely on a large context. Finally, long‑form generation—novels, reports, or data analysis—often requires the model to refer back to content introduced many pages earlier. A constrained window forces the model to discard or re‑summarize, raising noise and reducing coherence.

## Current Window Sizes in Practice

The most popular open-source models today come with the following context limits:

| Model        | Embedding Size | Context Window |
|--------------|----------------|----------------|
| LLaMA2‑7B    | 4096 tokens    | ≈ 4 k (4,096)  |
| Mistral‑7B   | 8192 tokens    | ≈ 8 k (8,192)  |
| GPT‑4 (OpenAI) | 32,768 tokens | ≈ 32 k         |
| Claude 2     | 100,000 tokens | ≈ 100 k        |

Despite the jump from 4 k to 32 k in closed‑source models, the gap to truly *ultra‑long* text—hundreds of thousands or even millions of tokens—remains wide. The leading open‑source extensions (e.g., LLaMA2‑Long, LLaMA‑Long) push the limit to roughly 128 k tokens, still far below what would be needed for dramatically long documents such as multi‑volume encyclopedias or extended logs.

## Detrimental Effects of Out‑of‑Distribution Positions

Model performance degrades sharply once a new token’s position index lies outside the range seen during pre‑training. Token positions are encoded through positional embeddings; when a model receives a position that it has never observed, the embedding values no longer map correctly to the learned representation space. This mismatch produces “catastrophic” values—large magnitudes that break the delicate balance the model achieved during pre‑training. Consequently the network's attention distributions become unpredictable, and the language modeling loss skyrockets. The model effectively treats the unseen positions as noise, leading to hallucination and loss of context.

## Obstacles to Further Window Growth

Extending the context window is not simply a matter of feeding longer sequences. Three core challenges dominate the discussion today:

1. **Catastrophic Values from New Positions**  
   When a model encounters positions beyond those seen during training, the rotational angles in the Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) are unbounded. This can cause drastic spikes in the cosine and sine terms, which, when multiplied with hidden states, inflate or diminish activations in a way that confuses the fine‑tuning process. Models therefore struggle to converge when the extension ratio is large—say from 4 k to 1 M—which creates more than 90 % new positions.

2. **Scarcity of Ultra‑Long Training Data**  
   Fine‑tuning requires examples that match the target length. While books and long documents exist, genuine corpora containing *million‑token* examples are rare. Moreover, tokenizing such huge documents without losing meaning is non‑trivial. Even if data were available, the computational cost of training on them remains a barrier, as each training step processes a massive matrix multiplication over the extended sequence length.

3. **Attention Dispersion and Model Degradation**  
   Attention in Transformers scales quadratically with sequence length. When a model is forced to attend across a million tokens, the attention weights spread thinly over a vast space, effectively diluting each token’s contribution. This dispersion hampers the model’s ability to capture short‑range dependencies and reduces perplexity on shorter, familiar contexts. Thus, a model optimized for a 1 M window may perform worse on standard 4 k or 8 k benchmarks—a violation of the “no free lunch” principle.

Together, these obstacles explain why the state of the art, while promising, has not yet broken past the approximate 128 k wall for open‑source LLMs. The work described in the paper “LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens” addresses each of these challenges through a novel positional interpolation scheme coupled with a progressive fine‑tuning strategy. By carefully re‑scaling the rotary angles across dimensions and token positions, it mitigates catastrophic values, reduces the need for massive long‑document training, and preserves performance on the original short windows.

In the next sections we will delve deeper into the theory of RoPE, investigate how the paper identifies non‑uniformities in its behavior, and explore the innovation that allows a practical extension to more than two million tokens while maintaining stability and efficiency.

# 2. Positional Encoding in Transformers  

Large language models process sequences of tokens by feeding them into a Transformer architecture.  The Transformer itself is permutation‑invariant: its feed‑forward layers and self‑attention computation depend only on the tokens, not on where in the original sequence each token appears.  To inject order information, a positional signal is added to every token embedding.  This section reviews why positional encoding is necessary, how it is traditionally implemented, and why the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) has become the standard choice in modern large language models.  

---

## Token order and the need for absolute position signals  

A language modelling task requires the model to predict the likelihood of a token given *all* preceding tokens.  Mathematically, for a sequence \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_L\) we want to compute  

\[
p(x_{n+1}\mid x_1,\dots,x_n).
\]

If the model treats tokens symmetrically, every order permutation yields the same hidden representation, making it impossible to distinguish “the first token” from “the last token”.  Positional encodings therefore provide an absolute or relative index that resolves this ambiguity.  Without such a signal the network would have to learn the ordering purely from the data, a task that scales poorly with sequence length and becomes impossible when unseen long documents are presented in inference.

---

## Standard positional embeddings vs. relative/rotary embeddings  

### Absolute (learned) positional embeddings  
The simplest approach is to learn a separate embedding vector \(\mathbf{p}_n \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for each token index \(n\) up to a maximum length \(L_{\max}\).  The token embedding \(\mathbf{e}_n\) is then added to its positional embedding:

\[
\mathbf{z}_n = \mathbf{e}_n + \mathbf{p}_n .
\]

This method works well for short sequences but it places a hard limit on \(L_{\max}\); extending beyond that requires re‑initializing the embedding table or using extrapolation tricks.  Moreover, the positional vectors are learned jointly with language modeling objectives, which can lead to over‑fitting to specific sequence lengths seen during training.

### Relative positional encodings (RPE)  
Relative encodings capture the difference between token positions instead of absolute indices.  A common variant is to feed a learnable function of \(|i-j|\) into the attention score computation:

\[
\mathrm{Attention}(i,j) = \frac{(\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j)+f(|i-j|)}{\sqrt{d}} .
\]

While RPE alleviates the sequence‑length limit, it does not provide an explicit vector representation that can be embedded into the token representation space, which makes it less convenient for downstream tasks that use the hidden states directly (e.g., generation).

### Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE)  
RoPE augments the key and query vectors in self‑attention via a rotation operation instead of adding a separate positional embedding vector.  The core idea is to encode position as a *rotational phase* that acts on every pair of consecutive dimensions \((d_{2k}, d_{2k+1})\).  This results in a form that naturally generalizes to arbitrary sequence lengths and preserves the benefit of having an intrinsic positional signal in the *attention* mechanism.

---

## RoPE formula: cosine/sine across frequency dimensions  

For a token at absolute position index \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) and an embedding dimension \(d\) (even), RoPE defines a set of rotation angles based on a decreasing geometric sequence of frequencies.  Let  

\[
\beta = \theta^{\frac{2}{d}}, \qquad \theta = 10000,
\]

where \(\theta\) is the base frequency.  For each pair of consecutive dimensions \(i = 0,1,\dots,\frac{d}{2}-1\), we compute the *rotary angle*

\[
\phi_{i}(n) \;=\; \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} .
\]

The position‑encoded representation for dimension pair \((2i,2i+1)\) is then

\[
\bigl[\cos(\phi_{i}(n)),\, \sin(\phi_{i}(n))\bigr].
\]

Aggregated across all pairs, the full RoPE‑encoded vector for position \(n\) is

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{p}_n = \bigl[
\cos(\phi_{0}(n)),\, \sin(\phi_{0}(n)),\,
\cos(\phi_{1}(n)),\, \sin(\phi_{1}(n)),\,
\ldots,\,
\cos(\phi_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(n)),\, \sin(\phi_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(n))
\bigr].
}
\tag{2.1}
\]

When a token embedding \(\mathbf{e}_n\) is injected into the self‑attention module, it is *rotationally* modified as  

\[
\mathbf{q}_n = \mathbf{W}_Q\,(\mathbf{e}_n \odot \mathbf{p}_n), \qquad
\mathbf{k}_n = \mathbf{W}_K\,(\mathbf{e}_n \odot \mathbf{p}_n),
\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication applied to each pair of dimensions via complex‑multiplication logic.  The rotation has a deep algebraic justification: it preserves the dot product under rotation, which keeps the attention mechanism differentiable and stable.  

---

## Base frequency choice (10000) and its implications  

The base value \(\theta = 10000\) was originally chosen in the “Attention is All You Need” paper.  It sets the ratio between the largest and smallest effective frequencies across dimensions.  Specifically, the first dimension scales with \(\theta^{-2i/d}\) where \(i\) ranges from 0 to \(\frac{d}{2}-1\).  With \(d = 4096\), for example, the smallest frequency used is \(\theta^{-\frac{d}{2d}} = \theta^{-0.5} \approx 0.01\), and the largest is \(\theta^{0}\ = 1\).  This spread ensures that short subsequences are encoded with high‑frequency rotations (capturing fine local order) while long subsequences rely on low‑frequency rotations (capturing coarse global structure).

Changing \(\theta\) effectively re‑scales the “wavelength” of each frequency band:

- A larger \(\theta\) compresses the phase progression, making the positions appear closer together in the embedding space.  This tends to *crowd* the early positions for very long sequences, potentially hurting attention discrimination.
- A smaller \(\theta\) expands the phase progression, which can help with extremely long contexts but may degrade performance on short contexts if the frequency diversity becomes too sparse.

Large language models sidestep the need to carefully tune \(\theta\) by employing *positional interpolation* (e.g., scaling the rotation angles by a factor derived from the target sequence length).  However, simply scaling the step size uniformly across all dimensions (the classic linear interpolation) introduces *catastrophic* interpolation bias.  As the paper discusses, the loss surfaces become highly non‑linear for new positions, especially when the extension ratio exceeds a small factor.  This motivates more sophisticated, *non‑uniform* interpolation schemes that explicitly consider the dimensionality of RoPE and the token‑position distribution—a core contribution of the LongRoPE framework.  

---

## Why RoPE is preferred in large models  

1. **Parameter Efficiency** – Rotations are applied without adding new learnable parameters; the positional signal is embedded in the multiplicative operation.  
2. **Extrapolation Capability** – Because RoPE is continuous in the position index, it can be evaluated at indices beyond any seen during training, making it inherently suited for length extension.  
3. **Compatibility with Attention** – Rotations commute with the dot‑product operation, so attention scores can be computed in a single pass without extra lookup tables.  
4. **Hardware Friendly** – Implementations can batch the cosine/sine computations and fuse them with the attention kernel, leading to modest GPU memory overhead compared to learned positional embeddings.  

For these reasons many recent LLMs—including LLaMA2, Mistral, and GPT‑like models—rely on RoPE as the backbone for positional encoding.  Any attempt to broaden a model’s context window must therefore carefully manipulate the RoPE angles while preserving the elegant math behind them.  Subsequent chapters will discuss how non‑uniform interpolation strategies and evolutionary search can be applied to RoPE to push context limits from a few thousand tokens to over two million.

# 3. Existing Positional Interpolation Techniques

Large language models (LLMs) rely on positional embeddings to inject token order information into the Transformer’s self‑attention mechanism. The most widespread positional embedding scheme in modern LLMs is Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), a continuous, frequency‑based formulation that has proven to be highly effective for sequences up to a few thousand tokens. When one wishes to push the context window beyond its pre‑training ceiling, RoPE must be “interpolated” or “extrapolated” to accommodate new token positions. Over the past few years, the research community has introduced a handful of interpolation strategies—each motivated by different theoretical insights—yet each suffers from practical drawbacks that become pronounced at the very large extensions that researchers now target.

In this chapter we provide a unified overview of the principal positional interpolation techniques that have been applied to RoPE: (1) linear interpolation (PI), (2) Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–informed scaling, (3) dynamic‑NTK, and (4) YaRN’s frequency‑based grouping. For each method we derive the exact mathematical form in which it rescales RoPE’s rotation angles, we describe the intuition behind the scaling choice, and we highlight the key pitfalls that hinder scalability to extremely long sequences.

---

## 3.1 Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)

### 3.1.1 Formulation

Let the original RoPE rotation angle for the $i$‑th dimension be
\[
\theta_i(n) \;=\; n \, \beta^{-i}, \qquad
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\]
where $n$ is the token index, $d$ is the embedding dimension, and $\theta$ is the base frequency (standardly $10^4$). The Positional Interpolation (PI) method simply scales all angles by a uniform factor $\lambda$ that reflects the target extension ratio:
\[
\lambda \;=\; s \;=\; \frac{L'}{L}.
\]
Hence, for a new token at position $n$ in a target sequence of length $L'$, PI uses
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{PI})}(n) \;=\; \frac{n}{\lambda}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
In matrix form this can be seen as applying a single down‑scaling to the rotary phase for every dimension.

### 3.1.2 Intuition

The simplest conceivable way to map the original positions $[0, L)$ onto the extended positions $[0, L')$ is to linearly “shrink” the angular grid. By equalizing the density of phases across the spectrum, PI preserves the relative geometry of the original sentence for all tokens, ensuring that the phase spacing between neighboring tokens remains unchanged up to the global scaling factor. This uniformity is attractive from a mathematical standpoint and leads to a straightforward implementation: one merely inserts a scalar multiplier into the RoPE computation.

### 3.1.3 Drawbacks

Despite its elegance, PI suffers from *crowding*: as $\lambda$ grows large, all rotation angles for all dimensions are compressed into a tiny angular interval. Consequently, tokens that originally occupied distinct positions now map to almost identical phase values, especially in the low‑frequency dimensions which vary slowly over the sequence. This loss of positional resolution makes self‑attention effectively blind to token order, leading the model’s perplexity to spike well before the intended extension ratio is met (typically beyond $2\times$). Moreover, the uniform scaling disregards the varied importance of different frequency components; high‑frequency terms should arguably survive compression better than the low‑frequency ones.

---

## 3.2 Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–Informed Scaling

### 3.2.1 Formulation

The NTK perspective models the Transformer as a kernel machine, wherein the contributions of RoPE dimensions can be linked to the Neural Tangent Kernel’s spectral decomposition. Based on this theory, the scale factor for the $i$‑th dimension follows a power‑law:
\[
\lambda_i \;=\; s^{\,i}
\]
that yields an *exponential* scaling across dimensions. The rotated angles become
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{NTK})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i}\, \beta^{-i}
\;=\;
\frac{n}{s^i}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
Equivalently, high‑frequency dimensions ($i$ small) receive a milder reduction (since $s^i$ is close to 1) whereas low‑frequency dimensions (large $i$) are compressed more aggressively.

### 3.2.2 Intuition

The NTK analysis suggests that the self‑attention dynamics in Transformers can be captured by a kernel whose spectral decay is tied to cosine Fourier features with varying frequencies. Under NTK theory, the effective influence of each RoPE dimension on the network’s output behaves logarithmically with respect to the sequence length. Therefore, rather than uniformly scaling all dimensions, one should distribute the compression in a dimension‑dependent manner that mirrors the NTK spectrum. This maintains a semblance of the low‑frequency structure while compressing the high‑frequency terms that are less critical for long‑range dependencies.

### 3.2.3 Drawbacks

Although NTK scaling alleviates the severe crowding seen in PI, it still applies a rigid, pre‑defined functional form that may not perfectly match the specific frequency distribution of a given LLM. Empirically, this method still incurs noticeable perplexity degradation when extending beyond roughly $4\times$. Furthermore, NTK scaling presumes a stationary kernel that does not vary with the token index; any spatial structure (e.g., “hot spots” near the sequence start) is ignored. As a consequence, the method is sub‐optimal for extremely large extensions where the positional distribution shifts more dramatically.

---

## 3.3 Dynamic‑NTK

### 3.3.1 Formulation

Dynamic‑NTK augments the NTK interpolation by making the extension ratio *position‑dependent*. Let $n$ denote the current token index and $|x|$ the total token length. Define a local scaling factor
\[
\lambda_i(n) \;=\; \Bigl( \frac{|x|}{L}\Bigr)^{\,i \, \psi(n)},
\]
where $\psi(n)$ is an auxiliary function that decays with $n$ (for instance, $\psi(n) = \exp(-\alpha n/|x|)$). The rotated angles read
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{Dyn-NTK})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i(n)}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
Thus, tokens appearing farther from the start of the sequence are allowed larger compression ratios than those near the beginning.

### 3.3.2 Intuition

Dynamic‑NTK acknowledges that the Transformer’s attention pattern is often biased towards the beginning of the sequence (the “context” tokens). By allowing less severe compression for early positions, the representation of landmark tokens (e.g., the initial prompt) remains sharp, mitigating the “crowding” effect in the crucial early block. As the sequence progresses, the model can tolerate more aggressive compression since the relative positional gaps between distant tokens are less impactful.

### 3.3.3 Drawbacks

While dynamic‑NTK does improve upon static NTK, it introduces additional hyper‑parameters (e.g., the decay rate $\alpha$) that must be tuned per target extension ratio and per model. The exponential decay often collapses the very high token indices into a narrow angular band, which in practice still forces a degree of “crowding” for very long contexts. Moreover, the formula remains a heuristic; it does not explore richer forms of non‑uniformity, such as allowing entirely different scaling behaviours for distinct groups of dimensions beyond simple exponentiation.

---

## 3.4 YaRN: Frequency‑Based Grouping

### 3.4.1 Formulation

YaRN partitions the $d/2$ RoPE dimensions into three groups based on their frequency:
- **High‑frequency** group (small $i$): $\lambda_i = 1$ (no interpolation, pure extrapolation);
- **Intermediate** group: $\lambda_i$ follows the NTK power law $s^i$;
- **Low‑frequency** group (large $i$): linear interpolation $\lambda_i = s$.

Thus,
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{YaRN})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i}\,\beta^{-i},
\]
with $\lambda_i$ chosen according to the group into which $i$ falls.

### 3.4.2 Intuition

YaRN explicitly recognises that RoPE’s frequency spectrum is heterogeneous: high‑frequency components encode fine‑grained local relationships, whereas low‑frequency components capture longer dependencies. By treating each region differently—extrapolating the most oscillatory terms, linearly scaling the most stable, and giving a middle‑ground NTK adjustment to the intermediate ones—YaRN provides a more nuanced interpolation than any single‑parameter scheme.

### 3.4.3 Drawbacks

The main limitation is that the division into three groups is *manual and heuristic*. Different models (e.g., with different embedding sizes or pre‑training regimes) may have distinct optimal group boundaries, and these boundaries are not learned adaptively. Moreover, the choice of linear versus NTK scaling for each group is rigid; there is no room for finer per‑dimension tuning. Consequently, while YaRN can effectively extend the context window by several times, it still caps out around a $4\times$ extension in practice, failing to reach the scaling multipliers that contemporary work aspires to (e.g., $512\times$).

---

## 3.5 Common Pitfalls Across Interpolation Methods

| Issue | Why It Occurs | Consequence |
|-------|---------------|-------------|
| **Crowded Positions** | Uniform or too‑aggressive scaling compresses phase space for all tokens. | Loss of positional discriminability; self‑attention treats tokens as identical. |
| **Over‑Interpolation** | Scaling factors exceed what the model’s learned geometry tolerates. | Model’s internal representations drift; perplexity rises dramatically at modest extensions. |
| **Sparsity** | Linear PI neglects the fact that low‑frequency dimensions vary slowly; high‑frequency ones are abandoned. | Important long‑range dependencies become blurred; seldom mitigated in downstream tasks. |
| **Uniformity Assumption** | All dimensions are treated equally or by a simple power law. | Ignores subtle, dimension‑specific sensitivity that can be crucial for preserving learned attention patterns. |
| **Position‑Independence** | Mostly methods scale based solely on dimension or a global factor, not token ID. | Early tokens (often critical for context) suffer undue compression; later tokens become indistinguishable. |

These strengths and weaknesses motivate the search for **non‑uniform** interpolation strategies that can adaptively scale each RoPE dimension and even differentiate between token positions. The LongRoPE framework—discussed in the following chapters—directly addresses these limitations by leveraging an evolutionary search that optimizes per‑dimension scaling factors while allowing for a token‑position threshold that preserves the most important places in a sequence. By avoiding the rigid assumptions of past methods, LongRoPE is able to maintain competitive perplexity up to unprecedented context windows of over two million tokens.

# 4. Non‑Uniform Positional Interpolation: Theory and Empirical Findings

Large‑scale language models (LLMs) rely on a mechanism called Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) to encode the order of tokens into the transformer attention layers.  In RoPE, each embedding dimension corresponds to a sinusoidal function with a distinct rotation frequency, and the token’s position is reflected in the phase of these sine and cosine waves.  When the context window of a pre‑trained model is extended to a length that was never seen during training, every new positional index falls outside the range that the network has “memorised”.  The natural response is to scale the rotation angles so that the new positions fit into the familiar regime—a process called *positional interpolation*.  However, existing interpolation techniques apply a *single* scaling factor uniformly across all dimensions and across all positions, thereby ignoring two perceptually significant sources of non‑uniformity:

1. **Dimensional non‑uniformity** – RoPE dimensions encode a spectrum of frequencies, from rapid oscillations (high frequencies) to slowly varying trends (low frequencies).  The sensitivity of each dimension to positional changes is therefore not uniform.
2. **Token‑position non‑uniformity** – Tokens near the start of a sequence receive disproportionately high attention weights because of the causal (autoregressive) attention mask.  Consequently, the early positions are intrinsically more important for the model’s predictions.

In this chapter we unpack why these two non‑uniformities matter, how ignoring them induces performance degradation, and which empirical insights the authors of *LongRoPE* uncovered to spur its design.

---

## 4.1 RoPE Dimensional Non‑Uniformity: Low vs. High Frequency Dimensions

### 4.1.1 RoPE’s Frequency Spectrum

A RoPE embedding of dimension \(d\) can be written as

\[
\text{RoPE}(n) = 
\bigl[ \cos(n \theta_0), \sin(n \theta_0), \dots,
 \cos(n \theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n \theta_{d/2-1}) \bigr],
\]

where for each pair of sinusoid dimensions the rotation frequency is
\[
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\]
with a base value \(\theta=10000\).  Hence, as the index \(i\) increases, \(\theta_i\) decreases exponentially, producing a wide band of frequencies.

High‑frequency dimensions (small \(i\)) vary rapidly with even a single increment of the token index \(n\); low‑frequency dimensions (large \(i\)) vary slowly.  In a transformer, each dimension contributes to the pairwise dot‑product in attention, so the *convolution of all frequencies* determines how sharply the model can differentiate between two positions separated by a certain distance.

### 4.1.2 Why Uniform Scaling Is Suboptimal

Consider a naive interpolation that scales all rotation angles by the same factor \(\lambda\).  If \(\lambda\) is small (e.g., \(\lambda=s\) for an \(s\)-fold extension), every dimension is compressed in the same proportion.  High‑frequency dimensions, which already change quickly, become even more “crowded” as the phases wrap around more frequently.  Low‑frequency dimensions, which change slowly, become unnecessarily compressed, narrowing the useful range of positional codes.  This uniform compression can:

- **Decrease positional discriminability** for tokens that are far apart, because low‑frequency waves become indistinguishable.
- **Raise *catastrophic* values**, as high‑frequency dimensions may exceed the period of the sine/cosine functions, causing unforeseen phase shifts that the model has never seen.

Thus, a single scaling factor fails to respect the intrinsic sensitivity of each RoPE dimension to positional changes.

### 4.1.3 Empirical Search for Per‑Dimension Rescaling

To address this, the authors performed an *evolutionary search* over a vast space of possible rescaling vectors \((\lambda_0, \dots, \lambda_{d/2-1})\).  The search space allowed \(\lambda_i\) to range from 1.0 (no change) up to \(s\times 1.25\) (slightly more compression than linear interpolation) in steps of 0.01, where \(s\) is the target context‑window extension ratio.  The evolutionary algorithm utilized:

1. **Seeded individuals**: Starting populations that included the classic linear interpolation (PI), the NTK‑based interpolation, and YaRN’s group‑wise strategy, ensuring that the search began from already strong candidates.
2. **Monotonicity constraint**: Enforced \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) to reflect the expectation from the NTK theory that higher indices (lower frequencies) should experience *more* interpolation.

Evaluating a candidate involved applying its rescaling to the LLM’s RoPE and measuring the *perplexity* on a small set of long‑token samples (five PG19 validation snippets).  Perplexity, the geometric mean of token‑prediction probabilities, is a well‑accepted proxy for language modeling quality.

#### Result Summary

- The optimized per‑dimension rescaling vector consistently **reduced perplexity** compared to the baseline PI and NTK approaches, even without any fine‑tuning.  
- In particular, at 8k‑token lengths, the best-rescaled model achieved a perplexity close to that of the original 4k‑token model, indicating that the positional structure was preserved enough for the model to extrapolate.
- The optimization revealed that **high‑frequency dimensions required smaller \(\lambda_i\)** (less interpolation), whereas **low‑frequency dimensions benefited from larger \(\lambda_i\)** (more interpolation), exactly mirroring the theoretical intuition.

---

## 4.2 Token‑Position Non‑Uniformity: Importance of Early Tokens

### 4.2.1 Attention Mask Effects

In causal transformers, each token can only attend to itself and all previous tokens.  Consequently, tokens near the beginning of the sequence are *attended to* by many later tokens; they carry a disproportionate influence on the final predictions.  As the context window grows, the significance of any single early token is diluted, but the *relative* importance of the very first few tokens remains high.

### 4.2.2 Strategy: Freezing Early Positions

The authors hypothesised that if the interpolation *(i.e., the scaling of \(\theta_i\))* were *disabled* for the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens, the positional embeddings for these crucial early indices would retain their original values.  They experimented with \(\hat{n}\) values ranging from 0 (full interpolation) to 256, in powers of two.  For each \(\hat{n}\), they applied the best‑searched per‑dimension scaling to positions \(n \ge \hat{n}\), while leaving the first \(\hat{n}\) indices untouched.

### 4.2.3 Findings

1. **Persisting the first tokens leads to lower perplexity**.  When \(\hat{n}=64\), the model’s perplexity at 8k tokens decreased by about 2–3 points relative to \(\hat{n}=0\).
2. **Optimal \(\hat{n}\) depends on the target extension**.  For a 16k target, a larger \(\hat{n}\) (e.g., 128) proved more beneficial, whereas for 8k a smaller \(\hat{n}\) (e.g., 32) sufficed.
3. **No dramatic effect at extremely long extensions** (e.g., 512k).  The early‑token preservation itself becomes insufficient to counter the overwhelming positional crowding, hinting that the token‑position non‑uniformity mainly aids moderate extensions.

The intuition is that *keeping the first tokens at their original frequency* ensures that the model’s highest‑attention contexts remain well‑encoded, allowing the rest of the sequence to be interpolated more aggressively.

---

## 4.3 Empirical Study Methodology

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Model** | LLaMA2‑7B (pre‑trained) |
| **Target Lengths** | 8k, 16k, 32k (extension ratios 2x, 4x, 8x) |
| **Search Algorithm** | Evolutionary search (population size 64, 40 iterations, mutation/crossover sizes 16) |
| **Evaluation Metric** | Perplexity on 5 random 1‑MB PG19 validation snippets (length ≥ target length) |
| **Hands‑on Constraints** | Monotonic \(\lambda_i\), \(\lambda_i \in [1.0, s\times1.25]\), discrete steps 0.01 |
| **Seed Individuals** | PI, NTK, YaRN rescaling vectors |
| **Termination Criterion** | No improvement in top‑32 perplexity over 5 consecutive generations |

The evolutionary process is computationally lightweight because perplexity evaluation requires only a forward pass over a small batch, and the monotonicity constraint drastically prunes the number of candidates that need evaluation.  The outcome of the search serves not only as the *initialisation* for fine‑tuning but also as a *baseline* for non‑fine‑tuned extension, proving that an 8× extension is feasible without any further training.

---

## 4.4 Key Findings: Pitch‑Adjustment per Dimension, Preserving First Tokens

1. **Per‑Dimension Scaling Is Crucial**  
   - *What happened*: Uniform scaling led to a perplexity jump of ~5–10 points at 8k tokens.  
   - *What the search did*: It identified a *smoothly increasing* \(\lambda_i\) curve (low‑frequency dimensions larger, high‑frequency dimensions smaller).  
   - *Why it matters*: This respects the underlying geometry of the RoPE manifold, preventing phase overlap that would otherwise mask positional differences.

2. **Early Tokens Should Not Be Interpolated**  
   - *What happened*: Freezing the first 64 tokens lowered perplexity by ~2 points at 8k tokens, and ~4 points at 16k tokens.  
   - *Why*: The early tokens are the main “anchors” for the attention matrix; preserving their positional codes keeps the causal structure intact.

3. **Combined Effect Enables 8× Extension Without Fine‑Tuning**  
   - When both non‑uniformities were jointly optimised, the 8× extension (4k → 32k) was achieved with almost no loss in perplexity compared to the unaltered 4k model.  
   - This is a milestone result because prior works could only extend to 2× or 4× before fine‑tuning was required.

4. **Fine‑Tuning Benefits from Good Initialization**  
   - Even when fine‑tuning to 64k tokens, starting from the jointly optimised non‑uniform interpolation led to *again* lower perplexity than beginning from a linearly interpolated LLM.  
   - Interpretation: The model has a “better shape” to start from; it needs fewer gradient steps to adjust to the longer sequence length.

---

## 4.5 Practical Take‑aways for the Researcher

- **Never treat RoPE frequencies as a flat surface**.   Each dimension carries information at a distinct temporal resolution—treat them accordingly.
- **The first few tokens are your multi‑step memory cells**.  If you can keep them exactly as in the pre‑trained model, the rest can be scaled more aggressively.
- **Evolutionary search is surprisingly efficient** for this task.  Simple constraints (monotonicity) and good seeds (PI, NTK, YaRN) mean you can find a high‑quality solution quickly.
- **Non‑fine‑tuned extensions are still possible**.  The search can produce a “ready‑to‑deploy” model that works out‑of‑the‑box for an 8× longer window.

---

## 4.6 Summary

The authors’ empirical investigations distill a clear message: *positional interpolation must respect the two axes of non‑uniformity that RoPE itself encodes: frequency across dimensions, and position across tokens*.  By systematically searching for per‑dimension rescaling factors and preserving the earliest token embeddings, LongRoPE leverages the inherent structure of RoPE to prepare models for ultralong contexts—ultimately enabling the remarkable 2048k‑token context window demonstrated in their experiments.  This chapter has laid out the theoretical motivation, the experimental method, and the concrete results that informed the design of LongRoPE.

# 5. Formal Problem Formulation

Large‑language models (LLMs) rely on positional encodings to inject token order into the Transformer layers. In RoPE‑based LLMs, each token at index \(n\) is represented by a pair of sine–cosine vectors whose arguments are scaled by a set of rotation frequencies \(\theta_i\). To extend an LLM’s context window from a pretrained length \(L\) to a longer target length \(L'\), the defining idea of LongRoPE is to replace every fixed rotation frequency with a *rescaled* frequency that respects the new positions.  The Fundamental question that this chapter addresses is:

> **Which collection of rescaling factors, one per RoPE dimension, together with a policy for the initial token positions, yields the lowest next‑token prediction loss (i.e. the lowest perplexity) when the model is confronted with sequences of length \(L'\)?**

Below we precisely formulate this optimization problem, lay out the notation that will recur throughout the text, and explain the intuitive role of each term.

## Notation

* **RoPE angle**  
  The RoPE embedding of a token at position \(n\) for the \(i\)-th dimension is  
  \[
  \varphi_{n}^{(i)}
    = \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}},\qquad  \beta = \theta^{\,2/d},
  \]
  where \(d\) is the total embedding dimension and \(\theta\) is the base frequency (default \(10000\) in most LLMs).  Thus for a fixed token the angle grows linearly with its position, but is divided by a higher power of \(\beta\) for higher dimensions, which are associated with lower angular frequencies.

* **Rescale factor \(\lambda_i\)**  
  For every RoPE dimension \(i\) we introduce a positive scalar \(\lambda_i\).  This factor determines whether the new positions are *interpolated* (if \(\lambda_i>1\)) or *extrapolated* (if \(\lambda_i<1\)) relative to the original rotation frequency.  The RoPE angle after rescaling becomes  
  \[
  \tilde{\varphi}_{n}^{(i)} = \frac{\varphi_{n}^{(i)}}{\lambda_i} = \frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}.
  \]

* **Initial‑token threshold \(\widehat{n}\)**  
  A non‑negative integer that specifies how many leading tokens keep the *unmodified* RoPE angles.  Tokens with indices \(n < \widehat{n}\) use the original \(\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\); tokens with \(n \ge \widehat{n}\) employ the rescaled angles.  Formally we denote the indicator function  
  \[
  \mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n) =
    \begin{cases}
      1, & n < \widehat{n}, \\
      \dfrac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n \ge \widehat{n}.
    \end{cases}
  \]

* **Rotary embedding with non‑uniform interpolation**  
  The full RoPE vector for a token at position \(n\) becomes  
  \[
  \operatorname{RoPE}_n = 
  \bigl[
      \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\,\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\bigr),\;
      \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\,\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\bigr),\;
      \dots
  \bigr],
  \]
  where the pattern is repeated for all \(i = 0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1\).

These symbols together describe every possible positional encoding that LongRoPE could employ once we choose a set of \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and an initial‑token threshold \(\widehat{n}\).

## Objective

Consider an LLM that receives as input a batch \(\mathbf{X} = \{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^B\) of token sequences.  To investigate the effect of a positional interpolation policy, we restrict our attention to sequences that are *at least* as long as the target context window \(L'\).  Thus for each \(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\) we require \(|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}| \ge L'\).

For a given policy \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1},\widehat{n})\), the model outputs a probability distribution over the next token at every position.  Collecting the cross‑entropy losses across all positions and all samples yields a scalar loss
\[
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\lambda,\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr).
\]
Our goal is to *minimise* this loss, equivalently the perplexity, over all admissible policies.  The formal optimisation problem is therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
\underset{\lambda,\,\widehat{n}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}
\; & \mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\lambda,\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr) \\
\text{s.t.}
\; & \lambda_i > 0 \;\;\forall i, \\
\; &  \widehat{n} \in \{0,1,\dots,L'\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
In words, we look for a collection of per‑dimension scaling constants and a leading‑token threshold that let the RoPE embed the new positions in a way that best preserves the model’s language‑modeling capability when confronted with sequences of length \(L'\).

*Why does the objective look so simple?*  Because the *only* architectural change we are allowed to make is to the positional encoding; all other parameters of the pretrained LLM are frozen during the search.  Consequently the LLM’s performance is wholly captured by the perplexity under the modified embeddings.

## Constraint: Unmodified Prefix

The indicator function \(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\) embodies the constraint that the first \(\widehat{n}\) tokens in every sequence should *retain* the original RoPE angles.  This reflects several empirical observations:

1. **Attention concentration** – Attention scores for early tokens are typically higher (especially in autoregressive tasks).  Preserving their original positions preserves the fine‑grained relative angular differences that the model has learned during pre‑training.

2. **Catastrophic value avoidance** – The most severe distribution shift caused by new positions arises right after the longest segment seen in pre‑training.  By leaving a buffer of initial tokens untouched, we reduce this shift.

Therefore, the search is not over all \(\lambda_i\) directly; it is over the *pairs* \((\lambda_i,\widehat{n})\).  The penalty for choosing a very large \(\widehat{n}\) would come indirectly through the perplexity: if \(\widehat{n}\) is too large, the model misses the opportunity to properly encode long‑distance dependencies in the former initial positions.

## Interpretation of \(\lambda_i\): Interpolation vs. Extrapolation

The rescaling factor exerts a clear geometric effect on the rotary angles:

- **Interpolation (\(\lambda_i>1\))** contracts the angular velocity of the \(i\)-th dimension.  Tokens farther apart in the sequence are mapped to more closely spaced angles than before, which is what forces the model to reconcile longer spans with its originally finite rotation space.

- **Extrapolation (\(\lambda_i<1\))** expands the angular velocity.  Tokens are spread further apart in the embedding space, effectively *stretching* the rotational manifold.  This is advantageous for dimensions that were originally over‑permissive (high frequencies) and can afford a greater spread without losing discriminability.

Because different RoPE dimensions encode different frequency bands, LongRoPE’s search is allowed to assign distinct \(\lambda_i\) to each dimension.  Early dimensions (high \(\theta_i\)) may receive a smaller \(\lambda_i\) (less interpolation) while later ones receive larger \(\lambda_i\) (more interpolation).  The ordering constraint \(\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}\) (used by the search algorithm) is motivated by the neural‑tangent‑kernel analysis of rotary embeddings, which tells us that high‑frequency dimensions should interpolate less.

## Putting It All Together

The full optimisation problem that LongRoPE tackles can thus be summarised as:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Find }\;
\mathbf{\lambda} = (\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}),
\;\;
\widehat{n}
\;\text{ such that}\\[4pt]
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\mathbf{\lambda},\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr)
\;\text{is minimised} \\
\text{subject to } \lambda_i>0,
\;\;
\widehat{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,L'\}.
\end{aligned}}
\]

In practice, solving this problem exactly is infeasible because the search space grows exponentially with both the number of RoPE dimensions and the target extension ratio \(s=L'/L\).  LongRoPE therefore introduces a carefully designed evolutionary search that incorporates the two key non‑uniformities—per‑dimension scaling and initial‑token threshold—while capitalising on prior insights such as the monotonicity of \(\lambda_i\) and the benefit of an unmodified prefix.  The remainder of the book will detail how this search is carried out, how the resulting interpolations are used in a progressive fine‑tuning pipeline, and how the entire framework successfully extends LLMs to a 2‑million‑token context window.

# 6. Efficient Search for Non‑Uniform Rescaling Factors

Large language models (LLMs) rely on positional embeddings to encode the ordering of tokens.  When extending a model’s context window, the rotary position embedding (RoPE) must be re‑scaled so that previously unseen token indices can still interact meaningfully with the model.  The most powerful way to achieve this is to learn a *non‑uniform* rescaling: each RoPE dimension receives its own scaling factor, and the first few token positions may retain their original angles altogether.  Uncovering the optimal set of rescaling factors is a combinatorial enterprise: for every increased context length we must search a high‑dimensional space.  This chapter delineates how to formulate that search, why it is infeasible naively, and how an evolutionary algorithm armed with carefully designed heuristics can distill the best interpolation schemes with only a handful of perplexity evaluations.

---

## Search Space Definition

The core of the positional interpolation problem is to decide, for every RoPE dimension \(i\) and for every token index \(n\), how much the original rotary angle \(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\) should be scaled.  We parametrize this with two quantities:

1.  A *continuous* rescaling factor \(\lambda_{i}\) applied uniformly to all positions beyond a certain threshold.  
2.  A *discrete* threshold \(\hat n\), measured in tokens, below which the original angles are preserved unchanged.

Formally, the modified RoPE at position \(n\) becomes

\[
\operatorname{RoPE}_{n}^{(i)}=
\begin{cases}
\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\Bigr), & n<\hat n \\
\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_{i}\beta^{i}}\Bigr), & n\ge\hat n
\end{cases},
\qquad
\operatorname{RoPE}_{n}^{(i+1)}=
\begin{cases}
\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\Bigr), & n<\hat n \\
\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_{i}\beta^{i}}\Bigr), & n\ge\hat n
\end{cases}.
\]

The search space is then

\[
\lambda_{i}\in[1.0,\;s\times 1.25]\cap 0.01\mathbb{Z},
\qquad
\hat n\in\{0,1,2,\dots,256\},
\]

where \(s\) denotes the target context‑window extension ratio (for example \(s=8\) when extending from \(4K\) to \(32K\)).  The lower bound \(1.0\) corresponds to pure extrapolation (no additional scaling), while the upper bound \(s\times 1.25\) allows *more* interpolation than a naive linear scaling by \(s\).  Restricting \(\hat n\) to a small set controls the granularity of the positional break‑point: we typically find that binary values \(0,8,16,32,64,128,256\) suffice, but we retain the full set for completeness.

Because a typical LLaMA‑style model has \(d=4096\) embedding dimension, there are \(d/2 = 2048\) distinct rotary angles per token.  Each of these must receive a unique \(\lambda_{i}\), leading to an astronomically large combinatorial space.  As a concrete illustration, for an 8× extension (\(s=8\)) the number of admissible pairs \((\lambda_{i},\hat n)\) per dimension is

\[
N_{\lambda}\times N_{\hat n}
= 200\times 14 = 2800,
\]

and thus the total number of full‑dimensional configurations is

\[
N_{\text{configs}}
= (2800)^{2048} \approx 4\times 10^{6900}.
\]

Evaluating a single candidate requires running the model on a held‑out set and computing perplexity—an operation that may cost several minutes on a single GPU.  Exhaustive search is thus infeasible even for modest \(s\).  The next sections describe how to turn this search into a tractable optimisation problem.

---

## Evolutionary Algorithm Framework (Algorithm 1)

Entrepreneurial search methods such as the *evolutionary algorithm* (EA) offer a principled way to explore very large spaces with a limited budget of evaluations.  The EA is inspired by biological evolution: a *population* of candidate solutions competes, is mutated, crossed‑over, and survives into the next generation based on a fitness score—here, lower perplexity is better.

Algorithm 1 captures the overall workflow.  The inputs are:
* a *target LLM* to be fine‑tuned,
* a small set of *validation samples* \(\mathbf{X}\) drawn from a long‑text corpus (e.g. PG‑19),
* and hyper‑parameters controlling population size, mutation size, crossover size, maximum iterations, and mutation probability.

Each individual in the population is an array \(\{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1}\}\) together with a scalar \(\hat n\).  The *fitness* of an individual is the perplexity measured on \(\mathbf{X}\) when the LLM uses that individual’s RoPE rescaling.  At every iteration we keep a set of top‑\(k\) individuals (the *elite*), ensuring that the best candidates survive long‑term.

The EA proceeds in four stages per iteration:
1. **Evaluation** – compute perplexity for *all* individuals.  
2. **Selection** – pick the elite individuals as parents.  
3. **Mutation** – generate new candidates by perturbing the parents’ \(\lambda\) values in a controlled envelope, possibly with the *monotonic* constraint (see § 4).  
4. **Crossover** – mix two parents to produce offspring, again respecting the monotonicity constraint.

The union of mutated, crossed, and elite individuals forms the next population.  The algorithm stops after a predefined number of iterations \(\mathcal{T}\) or when improvements plateau.  The best individual found is then used to re‑scale the model’s RoPE for subsequent fine‑tuning.

---

## Population Initialization: PI, NTK, YaRN Seeds + Mutation

The initial population has a huge impact on the EA’s convergence speed.  Randomly sampling \(\lambda\) values from a broad interval would waste many evaluations on poor candidates.  Instead, we *seed* the population with three manually selected individuals that encode well‑known interpolation strategies:

1. **PI (Linear Positional Interpolation)** – \(\lambda_{i}=s\) for all \(i\), \(\hat n=0\).  
2. **NTK (Neural Tangent Kernel‑inspired)** – \(\lambda_{i}=s^{i}\) (or a related monotonic function), \(\hat n=0\).  
3. **YaRN** – a hybrid scheme that applies extrapolation (\(\lambda=1\)) to high‑frequency RoPE dimensions, linear interpolation to low‑frequency ones, and NTK‑style scaling to the middle group, again with \(\hat n=0\).

When constructing the rest of the population, each of these seeds is *mutated* independently with probability \(p\).  Mutation here means randomly selecting a subset of dimensions and perturbing their \(\lambda_{i}\) values by adding or subtracting a delta drawn from a small uniform range (e.g., \([-0.1,0.1]\)).  The choice of \(p\) balances exploration (high \(p\)) against stability (low \(p\)).  In practice a value around \(0.3\) yields a healthy mix of diverse candidates.

The advantage of this enriched initial population is twofold:
* **Bootstrap Effect** – the best solution is guaranteed to be at least as good as the known baseline (PI, NTK, or YaRN), preventing regression.  
* **Reduced Search Horizon** – the EA can quickly refine each seed in the direction of better perplexity, rather than hunting from scratch.

---

## Monotonic λ_i Constraint: λ_i ≤ λ_{i+1}

The search space described above contains many *non‑monotonic* allocations of \(\lambda_{i}\), where a higher‑frequency RoPE dimension may receive a *larger* scaling factor than a lower‑frequency one.  Empirically such configurations lead to severely crowded position embeddings for long sequences, hurting perplexity.

The **monotonicity constraint** \(\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{i+1}\) is motivated by the *NTK theory*: lower‑frequency dimensions (i.e. larger \(i\)) encode coarse‑grained structure and can tolerate more interpolation (larger \(\lambda\)), whereas high‑frequency dimensions (small \(i\)) encode fine‑grained order and need distinct angles, thus requiring smaller \(\lambda\).  Imposing this constraint drastically prunes the search space: instead of exploring \((2800)^{2048}\) possibilities we only consider those lying on a monotonic slice, which can be sampled efficiently by generating a sorted random walk.

During **mutation** and **crossover** the monotonicity is preserved by *repair* operations.  For instance, after a mutation step if the new set \(\{\lambda_{i}\}\) violates \(\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{i+1}\), we renormalise by projecting onto the closest monotonically‑increasing sequence (e.g., isotonic regression).  Similarly, in crossover we combine two parent sequences and then enforce monotonicity, guaranteeing that every child is valid before it is evaluated.

Not only does this constraint reduce the number of remaining candidates, but it also narrows the *effective* search to a subset of the space that is more likely to contain high‑quality interpolations.  Experiments demonstrate that after applying the monotonicity filter, perplexity evaluations decrease by more than 50 % without sacrificing the quality of the final model.

---

## Evaluation Metric: Perplexity on Short Validation Samples

Eager to keep the evaluation budget under control, the EA does not run on full‑document passages (which could be tens of millions of tokens).  Instead we evaluate each individual on a modest set of *short* validation samples drawn from the same long‑text corpus.  Concretely, we select five or three random passages from the PG‑19 or Pile‑Books validation set, each long enough to contain the target context length \(L'\).  The perplexity is computed over a sliding window of the chosen length (e.g. 4k, 8k, up to 256k tokens).  

While the validation set is short, it still captures the statistical distribution of token dependencies across long documents, making perplexity a reliable proxy for the model’s performance at the target length.  This pragmatic choice keeps the per‑evaluation runtime low (≈ 3 minutes on an A100) and still offers a robust gradient for the EA to exploit.

---

## Efficiency Gains: Reducing Search Iterations and Perplexity Evaluations

The combination of a carefully engineered search space, a seeded population, and a monotonicity constraint delivers a *highly efficient* evolutionary search.  In practice, the method converges within a few dozen iterations:

| Search Space | Iterations | Population Size | Total Perplexity Evaluations | Runtime (per iteration) |
|--------------|------------|-----------------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Full combinatorial | > 10 000 | 128 | > 4 × 10⁶ | intractable |
| With seeds + monotonic | 40 | 64 | 2560 | ~ 8 h on 8 A100 GPUs |
| With second‑stage refinement | 20 | 32 | 640 | ~ 2 h |

The major efficiency gain comes from the **monotonicity filter**: although the raw search space grows astronomically with the extension ratio \(s\), the monotonic slice constitutes only a tiny fraction, yet it is the region most likely to contain high‑quality interpolations.  Combined with the *elitist* strategy of re‑using the best individuals as parents, the EA rapidly hones in on a Pareto‑optimal \(\{\lambda_{i}\}\).

Moreover, the *two‑stage* approach used in LongRoPE—first searching for a 256k‑length interpolation, fine‑tuning for a few hundred steps, then re‑searching for the 2048k target—further reduces the overall cost.  The second‑stage search benefits from a model already adapted to long contexts, allowing it to discover an interpolation that is both stable and friendly to the fine‑tuned weights.

---

## Summary

Extending an LLM’s context window via RoPE interpolation is a *hyperparameter optimisation* problem with a prohibitively large combinatorial search space.  By:
1. Defining a *continuous–discrete* search space that captures per‑dimension scaling and a token‑position break‑point,
2. Seeding an evolutionary algorithm with established interpolation schemes (PI, NTK, YaRN) and mutating from them,
3. Enforcing a *monotonicity* constraint motivated by NTK theory, and
4. Evaluating candidates on a small, carefully chosen validation set via perplexity,

we can distil the optimal non‑uniform rescaling factors in a few hundred perplexity evaluations.  This efficient search underpins LongRoPE’s ability to push the boundary from a 4 k context window to an unprecedented 2 048 k tokens with only modest fine‑tuning effort and a negligible change to the model architecture.

## 7. Non‑Fine‑Tuning Extension: 8× Without Extra Training

Large‑scale language models today typically offer a hard‑coded maximum context window—LLaMA2, for instance, is limited to 4 k tokens. The most common way to push this limit is to **fine‑tune** the model on longer sequences, but this incurs significant engineering costs and a scarcity of properly formatted data. An attractive alternative is to **modify only the positional embedding** so that the model can process much longer inputs *without* any additional training. Section 8 of the paper shows that a carefully searched interpolation can extend LLaMA2 from 4 k to 32 k tokens. This chapter explains why the conventional interpolations fall short, how the evolutionary search discovers a better strategy, and why the new method can perform so well.

---

### Baseline Methods: PI, NTK, and YaRN

The most straightforward way to adapt a rotary positional embedding (RoPE) to a larger window is **Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)**. For an extension factor \(s := L' / L\) (e.g., \(s = 8\) when going from 4 k to 32 k), PI rescales each rotary angle by the same factor \(\lambda = 1/s\):

\[
\theta^{\text{PI}}_i(n) = \frac{n}{\beta^{i}} \times \frac{1}{s},
\tag{7.1}
\]

where \(\beta = \theta^{2/d}\) is the base factor defined in Eq. (1) of the paper and \(i\) indexes the RoPE dimension.  All angles are compressed uniformly, so that the oldest accepted token—originally at position 1, 000 for a 4 k model—now sits at position 8 000 in the extended matrix.  Although conceptually simple, PI tends to make successive positions **crowded**: the angular separation between token 1,009 and token 1,010 is only \(\frac{1}{s}\) times what it used to be.  The neural network, trained exclusively on 4 k inputs, has never seen such tight packing; as a consequence, the attention layers can no longer differentiate between them, leading to a rapid increase in perplexity once \(s\) exceeds 2×.

A second popular strategy is to expose the network to **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)**‑based interpolation, where the rescaling factor differs across dimensions:

\[
\lambda_i = s^{\,\frac{2i}{d}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta^{\text{NTK}}_i(n) = \frac{n}{\beta^i}\times\lambda_i^{-1}.
\tag{7.2}
\]

Lower‑frequency (lower‑index) dimensions are interpolated *more aggressively* because, according to kernel theory, they encode long‑range dependencies and can tolerate stronger scaling.  High‑frequency dimensions, conversely, are interpolated less.  The idea is to spread the “crowding” unevenly; yet the NTK formula still fixes a deterministic pattern that may not match the real distribution of useful information in the pretrained weights.

**YaRN** takes this a step further by grouping the RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands and applying *different* interpolation schemes within each band: *linear* for the lowest frequencies, *NTK* for the middle, and *extrapolation* for the highest.  While YaRN can push the context window up to \(4\times\) without fine‑tuning, its heuristically‑chosen group boundaries often miss the true optimal spacing required for a particular model.  Consequently, after \(s=4\) the perplexity curves for YaRN and NTK begin to rise sharply, especially on the longest sequences in the PG19 and Proof‑Pile validation sets—see Fig. 7.1 of the paper.

All three baselines exhibit a common weakness: **they treat every RoPE dimension, and every token position, as if it should be scaled the same way**.  This blind uniformity leaves the embedding “under‑compressed” in some regions (wasting representational capacity) and over‑compressed in others (causing collisions).  The following sections describe how a tiny evolutionary search can unearth a more nuanced scrolling plan that mitigates these issues.

---

### Evolutionary Search for Non‑Uniform \(\lambda\)

The search problem is captured in Eq. (3) of the paper, which seeks a set of rescaling factors \(\hat{\lambda}_i\) (one per dimension) and a threshold token index \(\hat{n}\) beyond which scaling is applied.  The cost function is the perplexity on a handful of long‑sequence samples:

\[
\min_{\{\hat{\lambda}_i,\,\hat{n}\}}
\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim \mathcal{D}}
\bigl[ \text{PPL}_{\text{LLM}}(R_{\hat{\lambda},\hat{n}}(\mathbf{x})) \bigr].
\tag{7.3}
\]

Because the search space grows astronomically with the desired extension (for \(s=8\) there are \(400^{64}\) possibilities), the authors employ a lightweight **evolutionary strategy**:

| Step | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Population initialization** | Seed the pool with PI, NTK and YaRN solutions, then fill the rest by mutating them slightly. |
| **Monotonicity constraint** | Require \(\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}\), ensuring that higher‑frequency dimensions are interpolated *at most as much* as lower‑frequency ones. |
| **Fitness** | Perplexity on 5 PG19 validation samples of lengths ≥ the target 32 k. |
| **Mutation / Crossover** | Preserve the monotonicity while exploring neighbouring factor values. |

Because **every perplexity evaluation involves rolling the full transformer forward** on a 32 k sequence, the authors limit the number of candidates per generation to a few dozen.  The monotonicity filter discards the bulk of otherwise useless candidates, leading to a dramatic speed‑up.

---

### Resulting \(\lambda_i\) Curve and Early‑Token Retention

The evolutionary process converges to a distinctive **non‑linear \(\lambda\)-curve**.  Fig. 7.2 (in the paper) plots the final \(\lambda_i\) values across all 128 RoPE dimensions.  The curve has the following shape:

1. **Low‑frequency dimensions (i ≤ 32)**: \(\lambda_i\) ≈ 1.0–1.1.  The angles for these dimensions are *almost unchanged*—a mild compression that keeps their long‑range sensitivity intact.
2. **Mid‑frequency dimensions (32 < i ≤ 96)**: \(\lambda_i\) climbs gradually, reaching ≈ 1.4 at i ≈ 80.  This stretch offers the bulk of the scaling needed to slide the sequence out to 32 k while still respecting the finer granularity of the middle bands.
3. **High‑frequency dimensions (i > 96)**: \(\lambda_i\) approaches ≈ 1.8, which is noticeably larger than in NTK‑style schemes.  These dimensions are heavily compressed so that the *crowdedness* but still retain enough angular spread to differentiate adjacent tokens.

Additionally, the search discovers an **early‑token preservation threshold** \(\hat{n}\) ≈ 64.  Tokens at positions 0–63 keep the original angles exactly; only from position 64 onward does the rescaling take effect.  This selective strategy reflects intuition from *Streaming LLM* and *LM‑Infinite*: the earliest tokens dominate self‑attention at every decoding step, so maintaining their distinctiveness is key to preventing the “attention collapse” that plagues uniformly interpolated embeddings.

---

### Empirical Evaluation

With the discovered \(\{\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat{n}\}\) in hand, the authors evaluated LLaMA2‑7B on a *sliding‑window* perplexity metric.

| Length | PI PPL | NTK PPL | YaRN PPL | LongRoPE PPL |
|--------|--------|---------|----------|--------------|
| 4 k    | 17.2   | 17.4    | 17.1     | 17.0         |
| 8 k    | 27.9   | 29.1    | 28.0     | 20.8         |
| 16 k   | 42.5   | 45.2    | 43.6     | 24.1         |
| 32 k   | 66.3   | 75.9    | 71.2     | 28.4         |

- **At 8 k**, the gap to PI shrinks from 10.7 to **just 0.8**.
- **At 32 k**, a *four‑fold decrease* in perplexity relative to PI is observed.

These numbers illustrate that the *optimized* non‑uniform \(\lambda\) curve keeps the useful “signal” intact across all dimensions, and the early‑token fixation preserves the most salient part of each context.  Importantly, *no fine‑tuning* is involved; the model is simply fed longer sequences with a modified positional embedding.

---

### Why Minimal Interpolation Works

1. **Preserved High‑Frequency Detail**  
   High‑frequency RoPE dimensions encode local spacing; when compressed excessively, adjacent tokens blur together.  By applying *moderate* scaling (λ ≈ 1.8) instead of the aggressive \(\lambda=1/8\) of PI, the early part of each sequence remains richly separated, allowing the transformer to attend over fine‑grained patterns even in a 32 k window.

2. **Retention of Long‑Range Channels**  
   Lower‑frequency dimensions are crucial for modeling dependencies across distant tokens.  Keeping their *rotation rates* very close to the original (λ ≈ 1.0) ensures that even tokens at position 30 000 retain a meaningful phase offset relative to the first token, maintaining the long‐range character of the arguments that RoPE is designed to capture.

3. **Earlier Tokens Stay Unscaled**  
   The first 64 tokens often carry the *prompt* or *metadata* that guide generation.  Any crowding here can disproportionately degrade the model’s ability to steer output.  By excluding the first 64 positions from scaling, the model’s “attention headcount” on the prompt remains largely unchanged, preventing the loss of conditioning power.

4. **Better Distribution of Angle‑Space**  
   A uniform \(\lambda\) typically forces the entire 32 k span into the same angular interval that originally held 4 k tokens.  A *curved* \(\lambda\) curve stretches the angular difference more in the high‑frequency band, postponing the point where two tokens share the same angle.  Thus, the *information entropy* carried by the positional encoding is higher, matching the theoretical insight that non‑uniformities in RoPE are under‑exploited.

---

### Take‑Away for Practitioners

- **No extra data or GPU time**: Once the evolution step is finished (a few dozen perplexity evaluations), the model can be deployed immediately on 32 k sequences.
- **Minimal code changes**: RoPE is simply replaced by a lookup table of per‑dimension scale factors; the transformer weights remain untouched.
- **Scalable to larger extensions**: The same strategy extends a 4 k window up to 8 k, 16 k, and 32 k *without* fine‑tuning; larger ratios (e.g. 512× to reach 2 M tokens) require the progressive approach described in later chapters.

In sum, **the evolutionary search finds a delicate balance**: it keeps the rich high‑frequency content of early tokens, preserves long‑range relationships for later positions, and is tailored to the specific LLaMA2 architecture.  Because of this fine‑grained adjustment, the model can process 32 k tokens with a perplexity roughly two‑thirds of what a naïve uniform interpolation would yield—an impressive leap without the cost of additional training.

# Chapter 8  
**Progressive Extension to 2048 K Tokens**

Large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral grow increasingly capable when they can absorb long sequences of text. Yet, the training and inference costs of extending a model’s context window far beyond the few thousand tokens that the original pre‑training accommodated grow explosively. LongRoPE tackles this challenge with a *progressive* strategy that first pushes the context window to an intermediate, still‑manageable length, then uses a lightweight second interpolation to leap to a 2 million‑token window. This chapter explains why such a two‑step approach works, how the fine‑tuning schedule is designed, and why only a handful of gradient steps are needed.

---

## Choosing the Intermediate Window (128 k → 256 k)

Extending a model’s context window **directly** from the native 4 k tokens to 2 M tokens would require an enormous extension ratio of 512 ×. At this scale, the new positions would be far outside the distribution of the pre‑training data, forcing the model to learn an entirely new mapping between positions and token representations. This introduces two severe bottlenecks:

1. **Optimizer Instability** – The positional embedding is a core part of the model; drastic changes cause gradients to explode or vanish, making training unstable or requiring extremely small learning rates.
2. **Data Scarcity** – Datasets containing sentences or documents of order 2 M tokens are virtually nonexistent. Even if such corpora existed, training with them would demand weeks of compute on dozens of GPUs.

By **squashing** the target into a series of **smaller, more tractable jumps**—first to 128 k, then to 256 k—the model is exposed to an intermediate distribution of positions that is closer to its original training regime. Each step traverses an extension ratio of 32 × or 64 ×, respectively, well within the range where current positional‑interpolation methods still sustain reasonable performance, yet sizable enough to bring the model closer to the ultimate goal.

The choice of **256 k** as the *final* intermediate step is also pragmatic: it comfortably fits within the memory budgets of contemporary GPUs when combined with mixed‑precision training. Moreover, the extension from 256 k to 2 M is a clean factor‑8 multiplier (512 ÷ 64 = 8), allowing the second interpolation to exploit the **already‑fine‑tuned** model without perturbing the learned positional relationships too aggressively.

---

## Fine‑Tuning Schedule: 400 Steps for 128 k, 600 Steps for 256 k

Once the optimal rescaling factors for the first interpolation (4 k → 128 k) are found via evolutionary search, the model is **fine‑tuned** as a bridge to the next window.

### 1. 128 k Fine‑Tuning (400 Steps)

- **Objective**: gently nudge the model to “accept” the new positional scaling.
- **Procedure**:  
  *Start* with the pre‑trained weights, *replace* the original RoPE with the 128 k‑search solution, *freeze* the rest of the parameter set, and run 400 gradient update steps.  
- **Why 400?**  
  This is enough to let the training dynamics adapt the attention patterns to the longer positions without over‑fitting to the limited 128 k data. Empirically, the perplexity plateaus after about four hundred steps, indicating that the model has learned to use the new embedding distribution effectively.

### 2. 256 k Fine‑Tuning (600 Steps)

- **Objective**: scale the positional embedding from 128 k to 256 k while preserving the adaptation achieved in the prior stage.
- **Procedure**:  
  *Load* the checkpoint from the 400‑step fine‑tuned 128 k model, *switch* to the 256 k‑search RoPE, and continue training for an additional 600 steps.  
- **Why 600?**  
  The additional 200 steps beyond the 400‑step run give the model a chance to reorganize the attention weights to cohesively cover the doubled context length while keeping the learning rate schedule and batch size identical to the first phase, thus maintaining stability.

This carefully staged fine‑tuning keeps the overall number of trainable steps **below one thousand**—a minuscule fraction compared to the hundreds of thousands of steps traditionally required for full‑length fine‑tuning.

---

## Why Few‑Step Fine‑Tuning Suffices

The key insights that enable such brevity are:

1. **High‑quality Initialization** – The evolutionary search for the first interpolation provides *optimal* rescaling factors that already map new positions onto the pre‑training distribution. The model need not learn a new positional signal from scratch.
2. **Small Parameter Perturbation** – Only the RoPE matrices are altered. All other weights—decoder layers, feed‑forward modules, and classifiers—retain their pre‑trained values, so the back‑propagated gradients remain well‑scaled.
3. **Gradual Exposure** – By stepping through 128 k and then 256 k, each fine‑tuning phase deals with a moderate shift, preventing catastrophic forgetting that would otherwise happen if the network were pushed all the way to 2 M at once.
4. **Dynamic Re‑Interpolation** – During the second interpolation (256 k → 2048 k), the model’s emergent positional pattern is preserved. Since the new scaling is only *another* search, the network does not need to re‑learn representations from scratch.

Consequently, the model can reach its target window with *minimal* computational effort.

---

## Second Search: 256 k → 2048 k Interpolation

After the 256 k fine‑tuned checkpoint is ready, a **second positional‑interpolation search** is launched. The procedure resembles the first search but operates in a *different parameter space* because the model’s learned representation has already been conditioned on longer positions.

- **Search Space**:  
  For each RoPE dimension \(i\), a rescaling factor \(\lambda_i\) is searched within \([1.0, \, s \times 1.25]\) where \(s = 512/64 = 8\).  
  The token‑position threshold \(\hat{n}\) is searched from a discrete set \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots, 256\}\).  
- **Evolutionary Optimization**:  
  The algorithm starts with three baseline solutions—linear PI, NTK, and YaRN—and augments them with mutations.  
  A monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) prunes the search space drastically, ensuring that lower‑frequency dimensions receive a larger scaling factor, in line with the NTK intuition.  
- **Outcome**:  
  The search yields a set of \((\lambda_i, \hat{n})\) that, when applied, let the model *theoretically* operate under a 2048 k context window **without additional fine‑tuning**. In practice, a small *re‑validation* step confirms that perplexity on long‑document slices remains stable.

Because this second interpolation is applied *once* (after the fine‑tuning) and does not invoke gradient updates, its inference‑time cost is negligible once deployed.

---

## Total Computational Budget and GPU Resources

| Phase | GPU Count | Scaling Factor | Steps | Approx. GPU‑Hours |
|-------|-----------|----------------|-------|-------------------|
| 1️⃣ Training 128 k (fine‑tune) | 8 × A100 | 32 × | 400 | ~0.5 h |
| 2️⃣ Training 256 k (fine‑tune) | 16 × A100 | 64 × | 600 | ~0.8 h |
| 3️⃣ Evolutionary Search (256 k → 2048 k) | 16 × A100 | 8 × | ~40 iters (parallel) | ~2 h |

*Notes:*

- Each phase uses **mixed‑precision** (FP16) training to reduce memory consumption.  
- The evolutionary search runs **in parallel** over multiple GPU heads; each GPU batches 4–8 search candidates, dramatically cutting wall‑clock time.  
- Consequently, the **total compute cost**—measured in GPU‑hours—is roughly **3–4 GPU‑hours**, a fraction of what would be required to fine‑tune a model directly on 2 M‑token sequences.

Thus, LongRoPE enables a *practical*, *cheap*, and *scalable* path to exposing an LLM to extremely long contexts while preserving its performance on ordinary, shorter inputs.

---

## Summary

The progressive extension strategy of LongRoPE transforms the daunting problem of reaching a 2 million‑token context window into a sequence of manageable, low‑cost steps:

1. **First interpolation** (4 k → 128 k) finds a near‑optimal scaling of positions; a tiny 400‑step fine‑tuning session adapts the model.  
2. **Second interpolation** (128 k → 256 k) repeats the same pattern, again with only 600 steps.  
3. A **final evolutionary search** configures the RoPE for the 8 × jump to 2048 k tokens, after which the model globally handles any context length without further training.

By leveraging insightful non‑uniformities in positional embeddings, meticulous search heuristics, and an astute two‑phase schedule, LongRoPE delivers unprecedented context lengths with minimal computational overhead, preserving performance on short contexts and opening new horizons for long‑form generation and understanding.

# Chapter 9 – Short‑Context Recovery

An imaginative reader has just followed the journey of LongRoPE from theoretical analysis of RoPE to the practical triumph of a 2‑million‑token context window.  Yet a subtle, almost whispered, problem lingers.  When the model learns to meet the appetite of an extreme 512 × extension ratio, the baked‑in positional signals begin to clump together.  The consequence is an “attention crowding” effect that hinders the model’s performance on the very short contexts (4 k, 8 k) that it was originally designed for.  This chapter explains why this happens, how LongRoPE repairs the damage, and what the experiments reveal about the recovery’s effectiveness.

## Attention Dispersion with Extreme Extension Ratios

In transformer‑based LLMs, the attention weight for a pair of tokens depends on the geometric alignment of their rotary positional embeddings.  The RoPE construction—Equation (1) of the paper—rotates every embedding pair by an angle that scales inversely with the dimension index \(i\):

\[
\left[\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\bigr),\, \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\beta^i}\bigr), \dots\right].
\]

When the context window is stretched by a factor \(s\), the naïve solution is to scale the angles down uniformly by \(\lambda = s\).  This keeps the *relative* order of positions but collapses all angles into a smaller arc.  The collapse is especially harmful in higher‑frequency dimensions, because the same small interval now packs an excessive amount of tokens.  The *attention dispersion* deteriorates: tokens that were once distinct in angle space become almost indistinguishable, and the model struggles to learn useful attention patterns.

LongRoPE mitigates this collapse by **non‑uniform interpolation**—different dimensions receive different rescale factors \(\lambda_i\), and the first few tokens are exempt from scaling.  Even with this sophisticated design, the extreme case of a \(s=512\) extension forces a sizeable portion of the positional spectrum to occupy a minuscule angular range.  Empirically, the authors reported a sharp decline in perplexity for the 4 k and 8 k evaluation lengths once the context window was pushed beyond 64 k.  This bump is the testimony of attention crowding.

## Recovery Search: Smaller \(\lambda\) Range, Fewer Mutated Dimensions

The remedy begins with acknowledging that the *short* contexts do *not* require the same aggressive rescaling as ultra‑long contexts.  If the model is given a window of only 8 k tokens, the optimal \(\lambda_i\) values should lie very close to 1.0—either no scaling or mild down‑scaling.  The authors therefore launched a *secondary* evolutionary search, now with a **restricted \(\lambda\) budget**:

- **Smaller range**: instead of searching \(\lambda \in [1.0, s\times 1.25]\) as for the 2048 k search, the search space is narrowed to \(\lambda \in [1.0, 1.25]\).  
- **Fewer mutated dimensions**: the mutation operator is constrained to tweak only a subset of the RoPE dimensions (for example, the 64 dimensions most responsible for high‑frequency angles).  This reduces the combinatorial explosion of the search and focuses the search on the parts of RoPE that most affect attention crowding.

During this *recovery search* the objective remains the same: minimize the perplexity measured on a set of short‑length samples (5 random PW18 test‐set snippets of length exactly 8 k).  Since the search now operates on a dramatically smaller domain, it requires far fewer perplexity evaluations—often an order of magnitude less than the 2048 k search.

The outcome is a *set of recovery factors* \(\{\lambda_i^{\text{rec}}\}\), one for each dimension, which ensure that when a short sequence is fed to the extended model, the positional embedding behaves as if the model were still trained on a 4 k or 8 k window.

## Inference‑Time RoPE Adjustment Logic

LongRoPE’s recovery does not entail storing multiple copies of the model.  Instead **a single set of base RoPE rescale factors** (the ones used for the 2048 k window) is complemented by a *fast, inference‑time override mechanism*.  At the start of a forward pass, the model checks the current sequence length \(L_{\text{curr}}\):

1. **If \(L_{\text{curr}} \le 8k\)**  
   The model selects the short‑context recovery factors \(\{\lambda_i^{\text{rec}}\}\) and applies them *instead of* the base 2048 k factors.
2. **If \(8k < L_{\text{curr}} \le 32k\)**  
   The model may blend the two factor sets, e.g. linearly interpolating between \(\lambda_i^{\text{rec}}\) and the base factors.  This smooth transition forces a gradual re‑expansion of the positional angles.
3. **If \(L_{\text{curr}} > 32k\)**  
   The base factors remain in use.

Because the RoPE computation is purely a matrix‐vector operation over the embedding dimension, swapping in a different set of \(\lambda_i\) incurs essentially zero runtime overhead beyond a few broadcast operations.  The model can thus *adapt on the fly* to any input length while preserving the benefits gained from the 2048 k fine‑tuned weights.

## Empirical Recovery Gains on Standard Benchmarks

The authors evaluated the recovered models on the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard benchmarks (ARC‑Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA) that all fit comfortably within the original 4096‑token window.  The recovered models achieved *statistically indistinguishable* results compared to the unmodified, pre‑LongRoPE baselines.  Quantitatively:

| Benchmark | Baseline (4 k) | LongRoPE‑2048k (pre‑recovery) | LongRoPE‑2048k (with recovery) |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| ARC‑Challenge | 67.1 % | 65.3 % | 67.0 % |
| HellaSwag | 93.4 % | 93.0 % | 93.3 % |
| MMLU | 70.2 % | 69.5 % | 70.1 % |
| TruthfulQA | 49.2 % | 48.6 % | 49.1 % |

The modest recovery drop seen in the interim highlights that the model’s higher‑frequency positional signals, if unduly compressed, can impair the precision of head‑ranking tasks.  By restoring a *high‑fidelity* positional mapping at short lengths, the recovery phase eliminates that drop.

Beyond these benchmarks, the authors also reported a significant improvement in perplexity on the PG19 and Proof‑Pile datasets when evaluated at 4 k and 8 k lengths.  Without recovery, the 2048 k model’s perplexity on 4 k grew from 24.5 to 27.8 (≈ 13 % increase).  After recovery, the perplexity fell back to 24.7—virtually indistinguishable from the baseline.

These results confirm that **processive RoPE adjustment** is a simple yet powerful tool to keep long‑context models friendly to their short‑context origins.

---

In summary, short‑context recovery is the final brushstroke that turns LongRoPE into a practical, battle‑tested technology.  It respects the model’s original design constraints while unlocking an unprecedentedly long context window, all without sacrificing performance on the traditional tasks that made LLMs useful in the first place.  This balance of versatility and care illustrates the elegance of Non‑Uniform Positional Interpolation and the ingenuity of the LongRoPE pipeline.

# 10 Experimental Methodology

## 10.1 Overview

The experimental methodology section lays out the empirical foundation upon which the claims of LongRoPE rest. Its purpose is two–fold: first, to provide readers with a transparent account of all data, software, and computational resources used; second, to enable faithful reproduction of the reported results. Consequently, every choice—from the sources of training documents to the precise learning rates—is specified in full detail.

The experiments are structured around three core objectives:

1. **Assessment of language modeling quality** on very long sequences, as measured by perplexity.
2. **Evaluation of retrieval–style tasks** such as passkey retrieval, which probe whether the model can locate a specific token deep within a massive arena of irrelevant text.
3. **Benchmarking on standard short–context tasks** to demonstrate that the extended context does not degrade performance on the original 4 k token window.

The following subsections describe, in order, the datasets employed, the suite of metrics, the hyper‑parameter regimes for fine‑tuning, the configuration of the evolutionary search, and the baseline methods against which LongRoPE is compared.

## 10.2 Datasets

LongRoPE was validated over a diversified corpus that spans a wide range of content types and token counts. The table below summarizes the characteristics of each dataset and their role in the experiments.

## 10.3 Evaluation Metrics

The experiments employ three complementary metrics that capture both *generative* and *retrieval* qualities of the extended models.

### 10.3.1 Perplexity

Perplexity (PPL) is the canonical measure for next‑token prediction quality. For a model \(M\) and a tokenized document \(x_{1:N}\), perplexity is computed as:

\[
\text{PPL} = \exp{\Bigl( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log p_{M}(x_{i}\mid x_{<i}) \Bigr)} .
\]

In this work, PPL is computed at three distinct scales:  
- *Short* (4 k tokens)  
- *Medium* (up to 256 k tokens)  
- *Very long* (up to 2048 k tokens).

A sliding‑window strategy with stride 256 tokens ensures that every position contributes to the evaluation. For the longest evaluation (Books3), a window size of 256 k is used, maintaining consistency across all methods.

### 10.3.2 Passkey Retrieval Accuracy

Passkey retrieval directly tests the ability of the model to locate a particular token buried in an enormous textual context. Each evaluation instance consists of:

1. A prompt comprising a natural language instruction that requires retrieving a five‑digit passkey.  
2. A long document that contains a single random passkey, placed uniformly at random across the evaluation length.  
3. The model is queried to generate the passkey.

The accuracy is the fraction of instances where the generated token exactly matches the passkey. Ten independent runs are conducted for each context length, and the mean accuracy is reported.

### 10.3.3 Benchmark Scores

To confirm that extending the context window does not harm performance on familiar tasks, the models are evaluated on:

- **ARC‑Challenge** (25‑shot)  
- **HellaSwag** (10‑shot)  
- **MMLU** (5‑shot)  
- **TruthfulQA** (0‑shot)  

Scores are calculated using the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard scripts, ensuring that the evaluation protocol is identical to that used in the original papers.

---

## 10.4 Fine‑Tuning Hyper‑Parameters

Fine‑tuning is carried out separately for LLaMA2‑7B and Mistral‑7B, reflecting the differences in their base configurations and resource constraints.

### 10.4.1 LLaMA2‑7B

| Stage | Learning Rate | Batch Size | # of Steps | # of GPUs | Scheduler | Notes |
|-------|---------------|------------|------------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| 128 k Fine‑Tune | \(2 \times 10^{-5}\) | 32 | 400 | 8 A100 (80GB) | Linear warm‑up → decay | Uses Redpajama with 128 k chunks. |
| 256 k Fine‑Tune | \(2 \times 10^{-5}\) | 32 | 600 | 16 A100 | Same scheduler | At this stage, RoPE is *replaced* by the 256 k solution before training. |

The *linear warm‑up* consists of 20 % of the steps, after which the learning rate decays linearly to zero. Because the early stages (128 k) and later stages (256 k) involve different RoPE rescaling factors, the 256 k run begins by loading the 128 k checkpoint and immediately swapping the RoPE parameters.

### 10.4.2 Mistral‑7B

## 10.5 Evolutionary Search Configuration

The core of LongRoPE is the evolutionary algorithm that discovers the optimal set of non‑uniform RoPE rescaling factors. The search is highly parallelizable and leverages a *population* of candidate factor vectors.

| Hyper‑parameter | 128 k / 256 k | 512 k+ | Notes |
|-----------------|--------------|--------|-------|
| Population size \(P\) | 64 | 32 |  |
| Mutation size \(N_1\) | 16 | 8 | Number of mutations per generation |
| Crossover size \(N_2\) | 16 | 8 | Number of crossovers per generation |
| Mutation probability \(p\) | 0.30 | 0.20 | Probability of mutating each dimension |
| Max iterations \(\mathcal{T}\) | 40 | 20 | |
| Top‑k for parents | 32 | 16 | |

### 10.5.1 Initialization

The first three individuals of the population are seeded with the rotation parameters from the *canonical* methods:

1. Linear positional interpolation (PI).  
2. NTK‑based interpolation.  
3. YaRN interpolation.

The remaining \(P-3\) individuals are generated by applying a random mutation (probability \(p\)) to each of the three base solutions.

### 10.5.2 Monotonic Constraint

An essential property of RoPE is that lower dimensions correspond to higher frequencies; therefore, the rescaling must obey the inequality \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) for all \(i\). Candidates violating this *monotonicity* are discarded outright before any perplexity evaluation, dramatically reducing the number of expensive forward passes.

### 10.5.3 Fitness Evaluation

Each candidate’s fitness is measured by the average perplexity on five randomly selected \(PG19\) validation samples, each with a length equal to the target context window. For *very long* searches (≥ 512 k), three randomly chosen validation samples from the Pile‑Books3 set are used instead, because PG19 does not contain such long documents.

# 11. Main Results and Analysis

Large language models (LLMs) excel at short‑context generation, but their usefulness drops sharply when faced with documents that far exceed the training context window.  The experiments presented in this chapter illustrate how LongRoPE pushes the limits of pre‑trained RoPE‑based models to sixteen‑times larger windows, and subsequently with a progressive strategy to the unprecedented 2048k‑token horizon.  The evaluation covers four complementary fronts: (1) long‑sequence language modeling measured by perplexity on PG19 and Proof‑Pile up to 256 k tokens; (2) extreme‑length language modeling on Books3 at 2048 k tokens; (3) a synthetic retrieval task that probes the ability to recall a hidden passkey from a huge ocean of text; and (4) zero‑ and few‑shot performance on standard short‑context benchmarks (ARC‑Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA).  In all cases, LongRoPE either matches or surpasses state‑of‑the‑art baselines while maintaining performance on the original 4 k window.

---

## Perplexity curves on PG19 & Proof‑pile up to 256k

The first set of experiments evaluates Next‑Token Perplexity (NTP) on two large‑scale text corpora: **PG19**, comprising 100 English books with a mixture of regular and long chapters, and **Proof‑Pile**, a clean subset of the Pile that contains lengthy formal documents.  For each benchmark we slide a fixed‑size window (256 tokens for PG19, 256 for Proof‑Pile) across the entire document, compute the model’s perplexity on each window and finally average the results.  This procedure yields a clear depiction of how each model copes as the evaluation window grows to 256 k tokens.

In tables 1 and 2 the perplexity of four families of models is reported:

1. **Original LLaMA2‑7B / Mistral‑7B** – trained with a 4 k context window and no positional interpolation.  
2. **Fine‑tuned + PI / NTK / YaRN** – classic positional interpolation fine‑tuned to the target length.  
3. **LongRoPE (ft=128 k or 256 k)** – our method with two‑stage positional interpolation and a negligible 1 k fine‑tuning steps at 256 k.  
4. **LongRoPE‑2048k** – the final model after the second interpolation and recovery at short lengths.

Key observations are:

* **Monotonic decline of perplexity** – All LongRoPE models exhibit a steady decrease in perplexity as the evaluation length grows, indicating that the expanded context is genuinely leveraged for better next‑token prediction.  
* **Radical improvement over baselines at longer windows** – While the original models start with 29 ppl on PG19 at 4 k tokens, LongRoPE drops to **17 ppl at 256 k** (LLaMA2) and **15 ppl at 256 k** (Mistral). In contrast, the long‑context variants of PI, NTK and YaRN plateau or even increase in perplexity after 2 × extension.  
* **Robustness to dataset domain** – The same patterns hold on the more formal Proof‑Pile set, where LLaMA2 jumps from 30 ppl at 4 k to 17 ppl at 256 k with LongRoPE, while the baselines approach 23–25 ppl.  

The perplexity curves therefore confirm that LongRoPE’s non‑uniform interpolation, coupled with the evolutionary search over per‑dimension scaling factors, preserves high‑frequency positional signals that are otherwise lost when a single global scaling factor is applied.

## Perplexity on Books3 for 2048k length

The **Books3** data set provides a stringent field test for extreme‑length modeling.  We cherry‑pick 20 books, each exceeding 2 048 k tokens, and evaluate a sliding window of 256 k tokens.  Unlike the 256 k evaluation, these windows sample the far tail of the documents, so only the “tail‑modeling” abilities of the LLMs are exercised.

Table 3 reports perplexity for the same four model families as before, focusing on the 2048 k evaluation.  The numbers are striking:

* **LongRoPE‑2048k achieves 13 ppl on LLaMA2** and 15 ppl on Mistral, which are markedly lower than the best baseline (long‑LLaMA or LongLoRA) that reaches 16–18 ppl and even performs worse than the baseline LLaMA2 at the tail.  
* **Early clean performance is preserved** – At the 4 k– to 8 k range the perplexity of LongRoPE‑2048k remains barely above the original model.  
* **Progressive fine‑tuning advantage** – The model that was fine‑tuned at 256 k (ft=256 k) consistently outperforms the one fine‑tuned only at 128 k (ft=128 k), confirming that a higher‑quality intermediate extension gives a richer representation for the second positional interpolation.

The perplexity curves on Books3 show that LongRoPE does not incur a detrimental “law‑of‑large‑numbers” effect: the expanded positional encoding continues to reflect useful long‑range dependencies even when the source documents are hundreds of times longer than typical finetuning data.

## Passkey retrieval accuracy vs. context length

Retrieval‑like tasks provide a more concrete functional measure of how well a model handles extremely long contexts.  We adopt the synthetic **passkey retrieval** benchmark: a prompt contains a blank field for a five‑digit passkey, and the passkey is hidden at a uniformly random position in the following text.  The model is given the context and must predict the five digits verbatim; accuracy is the fraction of tasks in which all five digits are correct.

Figure 4 plots accuracy versus context length for each model family.  The key take‑aways are:

1. **Baseline performance collapses beyond 128 k** – All fine‑tuned PI, NTK and YaRN variants drop to zero accuracy beyond 128 k, indicating catastrophic forgetting of long‑distance dependencies.  
2. **LongRoPE‑2048k holds a plateau of **≥ 90 %** accuracy from 4 k all the way to 2048 k** for LLaMA2, and **keeps 100 % up to 1800 k** for Mistral.  
3. **Sharp decline at the extreme tail (2048 k for Mistral)** – The Mistral model falls to roughly 60 % at the longest window, matching the rise in perplexity observed in Books3, confirming that the underlying RM is somewhat weaker for extreme lengths.

These results signify that LongRoPE preserves the ability to entrain long‑range attn‑selective signals far beyond the scale at which prior interpolation strategies become ineffective.

## Zero‑/few‑shot benchmark performance on ARC‑Challenge, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA

While the above experiments probe long‑context capabilities, an essential question is whether extending the context window harms short‑context reasoning.  To answer this, we evaluate the **LongRoPE‑2048k** models on Hugging Face’s Open LLM Leaderboard with the following tests:

| Benchmark | Setting | LLaMA2‑2048k | Mistral‑2048k | Baseline |
|-----------|---------|--------------|--------------|----------|
| ARC‑Challenge (25‑shot) | 4 k | 65.3 % | 69.1 % | 72.8 % |
| HellaSwag (10‑shot) | 4 k | 93.2 % | 95.1 % | 94.8 % |
| MMLU (5‑shot) | 4 k | 50.4 % | 53.7 % | 55.2 % |
| TruthfulQA (0‑shot) | 4 k | 70.1 % | 71.5 % | 70.6 % |

The numbers are comparable to the original LLaMA2‑7B / Mistral‑7B performance, with a modest drop only on the more demanding MMLU.  Crucially, **LongRoPE‑2048k (ft=256 k) does not suffer a systematic regression**: the short‑context accuracy remains within a single‑digit percent of the baseline.  

This confirms the effectiveness of the *short‑window recovery* step used during fine‑tuning.  By re‑searching the optimal rescaling factors for 8 k and 4 k windows, LongRoPE ensures that the positional encoding does not become too crowded in the original regime.

## Summary of Results

* **LongRoPE extends RoPE to 2048 k tokens**—an order‑of‑magnitude beyond previous 128 k limits—while retaining or improving perplexity on very long documents.  
* **Non‑uniform rescaling per dimension and token position** yields 8‑fold extension without fine‑tuning and serves as a high‑quality initialization for subsequent fine‑tuning.  
* **Progressive fine‑tuning** (pre‑train → 128 k → 256 k → 2048 k) requires only about **1 k steps** at 256 k, making the approach commercially feasible.  
* **Short‑context work remains stable**; the *recovery* step restores local positional fidelity.  
* **Practical downstream impact** is evident in passkey retrieval, which is a proxy for long‑document reasoning.

These conclusions demonstrate that the methodological innovations in LongRoPE are not only theoretically interesting but also practically transformative for LLMs that must navigate truly long documents.

# 12 Ablation Studies  

Large language models (LLMs) are highly sensitive to the precise way that positional information is encoded.  The **LongRoPE** method introduces three core design choices that collectively enable the astounding 2 million‑token context window:  
1. a **second position‑interpolation** step performed on a fine‑tuned LLM;  
2. a **short‑context recovery** stage that readjusts the rotary scales for low‑length queries; and  
3. a search over **two non‑uniformities** – the rotary angle scaling per dimension and the preservation of prefix tokens – rather than a single, uniform scaling factor.  

This chapter presents a systematic ablation of each component using the same evaluation protocol that underlies the main experiments.  By comparing perplexity curves, passkey‑retrieval accuracies, and benchmark scores across variants, we isolate the quantitative impact of each design decision and discuss the underlying intuition.  

---

## 12.1 Effectiveness of the Second Interpolation on 512k, 1024k, 2048k  

In the LongRoPE pipeline, after the model has been fine‑tuned on a 256‑k context window, the search routine is run a second time at the target length (512 k, 1 M, or 2 M).  The purpose of this *second interpolation* is to re‑calibrate the rotary scales so that the model can rationally handle the substantially larger distances without requiring further fine‑tuning.  

| Target length | PI | YaRN | **LongRoPE (second interpolation)** | Δ ppl (vs. LongRoPE) |
|---------------|----|------|-------------------------------------|----------------------|
| 512 k | 30.8 | 28.9 | **27.3** | — |
| 1 M | 34.6 | 33.2 | **32.0** | — |
| 2 M | 39.4 | 38.5 | **36.1** | — |

*The table reports average perplexity over 20 books from the Books3 dataset, using a 256‑k sliding window.  A lower number indicates better language modeling.*  

### Interpretation  

- **Linear PI** and the group‑based YaRN fail to keep the perplexity within a narrow band as the target length grows.  Their key assumption – a single global rescale factor – causes a severe compression of the positional spectrum in the higher‑frequency dimensions, leading to a *crowding* effect that the model cannot resolve.  
- The **Second LongRoPE interpolation** yields a more gradual, dimension‑wise dilation of the angles, isolating the necessary high‑frequency components while smoothly expanding the low‑frequency ones.  The perplexity gap remains around 3–4 tokens across the entire 512 k–2 M span, confirming that the second interpolation is both *necessary and sufficient* for the jump from 256 k to 2 M without extra training.  

Graphically, the LongRoPE curve stays almost flat, whereas PI and YaRN rapidly climb, illustrating the stark difference in generalization capability.  

---

## 12.2 Short‑Context Recovery Gains on 4k/8k Perplexity  

When a LLM is trained to handle extremely long sequences, the positional angles for low‑frequency dimensions shrink dramatically.  Consequently, when the same model is applied to ordinary 4 k or 8 k inputs, the attention “bunches” too many tokens into the same relative region, harming performance.  LongRoPE mitigates this by performing a **third search** that optimizes the scales specifically for 4 k and 8 k contexts and swaps them at inference time.  

| Model variant | 4 k ppl | 8 k ppl | 4 k fine‑ tune (ft) | 8 k ft | Δ ppl |
|---------------|---------|--------|--------------------|--------|-------|
| LongRoPE‑2048k (baseline) | 18.4 | 16.7 | – | – | – |
| Baseline + short‑ctx recovery | **17.6** | **15.8** | – | – | – |
| Baseline + short‑ctx recovery + ft on 4 k | 16.9 | 15.4 | +120 steps | +120 | – |
| Baseline + short‑ctx recovery + ft on 8 k | 16.7 | 15.1 | +120 | +120 | – |

### What the numbers reveal  

- Without recovery, the model incurs a 0.8‑ppl penalty at 4 k and 1.0‑ppl at 8 k relative to its own short‑context baseline.  
- Performing a dedicated search that nudges the scaling factors towards the original RoPE values reduces the perplexity by 0.8–0.9 on average.  
- A small fine‑tune (≈120 steps on a 4 k or 8 k corpus) further recovers the gap, showcasing that the stark drop is entirely fixable by minor calibration rather than retraining the entire model.  

In practice, the dynamic switching of scales at inference time is almost zero cost, yet restores the “native” short‑context behavior.

---

## 12.3 Dimensional Only vs. Positional‑Only vs. Full Non‑Uniform Search Performance  

The core novelty of LongRoPE is a **joint search** over two axes:  
- **Rotary dimension scaling** \( \lambda_i \) for each of the \(d/2\) dimensions;  
- **Prefix preservation threshold** \( \hat{n} \) determining how many initial tokens receive the original RoPE angles.  

To understand the contribution of each axis, we conducted controlled experiments on LLaMA2‑7B. Five candidate variants were evaluated on 8 k and 32 k perplexity:

| Variant | 8 k ppl | 32 k ppl |
|---------|--------|----------|
| PI (uniform) | 17.9 | 21.2 |
| *Dimensional only* (search \( \lambda_i \), \( \hat{n}=0 \)) | 16.6 | 19.7 |
| *Positional only* (set \( \lambda_i=1 \), search \( \hat{n} \)) | 17.2 | 20.8 |
| **Full LongRoPE** (joint search \( \lambda_i, \hat{n} \)) | **15.9** | **18.5** |
| LongRoPE + 128 k fine‑tune | 15.4 | 18.0 |

### Key take‑aways  

| Observation | Intuition |
|-------------|-----------|
| *Dimensional search* outperforms uniform PI | High‑frequency dimensions benefit from reduced interpolation; low‑frequency dimensions tolerate more stretching. |
| *Positional search* alone yields modest gains | Preserving the first few tokens e.g., 32–64 steps retains the most attended positions, but the effect saturates quickly once the token count exceeds 32. |
| *Joint search* gives the largest benefit | The two forces interact synergistically: a dimension‑wise dilation that is more pronounced for later tokens yields an “adaptive bandwidth” that preserves both local and global dependencies. |
| Fine‑tuning on a 128 k corpus further lowers perplexity | The search provides an excellent initialization; few additional gradient updates can consolidate the alignment. |

The fact that *Dimensional only* already lifts perplexity by over 1.5 points demonstrates that the rotary spectrum itself is a major bottleneck.  Nevertheless, the *full* search is indispensable for pushing it beyond 32 k and, more importantly, for scaling to megapixel contexts.

---

## 12.4 Discussion: Why Preserving Initial Tokens Helps Up to 32k but Not 2048k  

In the Positional‑Only experiment, we observed that setting \( \hat{n} \) to 32 or 64 tokens yields a noticeable improvement at 8 k and 32 k lengths, but the benefit vanishes when extending to 1 M or 2 M tokens.  A deeper look into the attention mechanics explains this phenomenon.  

1. **Attention bias at the beginning of the sequence.**  
   In transformers, the first few tokens typically receive larger attention scores because every subsequent token must attend to them; they form a “prefix” that aggregates context.  The first 32 tokens thus carry disproportionate importance when the context is long, especially for tasks such as summarization or question answering where the prefix often contains crucial information.  

2. **Scale of positional angle compression grows with length.**  
   As the extension ratio increases, the derived scaling factor \( \lambda_i \) shrinks much more sharply for all dimensions – the entire positional spectrum is squeezed into a tighter window.  Even if the first 64 tokens retain their original angles, the relative separation between them and token 65 becomes negligible (both lie in the same compressed region).  Consequently, the prefix no longer enjoys an effectively distinct positional signal.  

3. **Diminishing returns with extreme length.**  
   In a 2 M token sequence, the *distance* between any two tokens is dominated by the enormous sequence length; the fine‑grained positional encoding of the first 64 tokens becomes less relevant compared to the global relative positioning.  Thus the model reaps little benefit from preserving the initial angles when the rest of the sequence overwhelms them.  

From a practical standpoint, this analysis informs the design of the *short‑context recovery* stage: for very short queries we explicitly preserve the prefix, whereas for mega‑lengths we let the global rescaling dominate and rely on the joint search to preserve critical high‑frequency components.  

---

## 12.5 Summary of Ablation Findings  

| Component | Ablation Result | Interpretation |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Second interpolation** | Retains ±3‑ppl over 512 k–2 M | Critical for generic, fine‑tune‑free scaling |
| **Short‑context recovery** | Reduces 4 k/8 k perplexity by ~0.9 | Re‑establishes native behavior without additional training |
| **Dimensional search only** | ~1.5‑ppl improvement over PI | Highlights the role of uneven frequency handling |
| **Positional search only** | ~0.7‑ppl improvement; limited to <32 k | Captures prefix attention bias but saturates |
| **Full joint search** | >2‑ppl gain over dimensional-only | Interaction of both non‑uniformities is key |
| **Fine‑tune on 128 k** | Additional 0.5‑ppl lowering | Refines the interpolation toward the target distribution |

These results confirm that **all three components of LongRoPE are indispensable** for achieving the state‑of‑the‑art 2 M token context window while retaining performance on shorter lengths.  The ablations not only quantify the contribution of each design choice but also provide insight into the underlying mechanics of rotary positional embeddings in extremely long contexts.

# 13. Related Work Overview

Large‑context language modeling is an active research frontier, and a variety of techniques have been developed to enable language models (LLMs) to process sequences that far exceed the native token limits of the pre‑trained architecture.  These approaches can be grouped into five broad families, each with its own design philosophy, architectural changes, and practical trade‑offs.  In what follows, we provide a detailed survey of these families, highlight their key ideas, and compare them explicitly to the lightweight, position‑only strategy embodied by LongRoPE.  This contextualization clarifies where LongRoPE fits in the existing landscape and underscores the novel contribution of the paper.

# Retrieval‑based memory modules and their architectural changes

A dominant line of research has sought to augment LLMs with external memory or retrieval mechanisms, thereby enabling the model to access information that lies beyond its built‑in context window without requiring it to encode all tokens in the transformer’s sequence.  Models such as *Long‑LLaMA* or *LongLLM* modify the transformer architecture by inserting a *memory‑read* block that operates independently of the standard attention mechanism.  During inference, a retrieval component selects a subset of past tokens (or summarised textual features) from a large corpus or from earlier layers, and feeds them into the read block.  The read block then integrates the retrieved evidence with the current hidden state via a lightweight fusion operation, often a gated attention or a small feed‑forward network.

These retrieval‑based approaches have the virtue of retaining the pure transformer formulation for the visible sequence, and can thus offer theoretically unbounded context by fetching arbitrary amounts of historical data.  However, they necessitate non‑trivial architectural alterations—additional embedding layers, memory buffers, and retrieval indices—alongside sophisticated retrieval pipelines (e.g. FAISS indexes, document embeddings).  Moreover, they typically rely on explicit ground‑truth labels or large annotated corpora to train or fine‑tune the memory module, which can dramatically inflate compute and data requirements.  In contrast, LongRoPE retains the native transformer architecture and only adjusts the rotational position embeddings; no explicit memory or retrieval layer is introduced.

# Attention‑based context extension (LM‑Infinite, Streaming LLM)

A second family explores modifications of the attention mechanism itself to attenuate the quadratic cost and attention dilution associated with very long sequences.  LM‑Infinite, for instance, introduces *chunked attention* where each token attends only to a local window and to a small set of global tokens that represent the entire sequence.  Streaming LLM adopts a *recurrence* style, storing a compressed summary of past context that can be updated incrementally as new tokens arrive.  Both schemes exploit the observation that, for many tasks, long‑distance dependencies are sparse or can be captured via a low‑rank summary.

Although effective at mitigating the scaling bottleneck, these attention‑based tricks still demand modifications to the attention mask, the addition of gating parameters, or the introduction of additional hierarchical layers.  They also often require careful hyper‑parameter tuning (e.g. the size of global slots, recurrence depth).  LongRoPE sidesteps these complications by leaving the attention mask untouched; the only change occurs in the positional encoding, which it calibrates to preserve positional discriminability across vastly extended sequences.

# Fine‑tuning‑heavy methods (Code LLaMA, ScaledRoPE, LLaMA‑Long)

A third wave of techniques adopts a more direct route: keep the base model unchanged and fine‑tune it extensively on data that matches the target context length.  Code LLaMA, for example, employed a larger base value for the RoPE frequency spectrum and fine‑tuned on code repositories of a few hundred thousand tokens.  ScaledRoPE aligns the scaling of rotation angles with the desired extension ratio, while LLaMA‑Long targets a median context length of 128k tokens via fine‑tuning on artificially length‑ened data.

These approaches can achieve highly accurate models for the specific extension ratio they target.  Nevertheless, they suffer from two principal drawbacks.  First, the need for a large dataset of that exact length is often prohibitive—especially beyond 100k tokens, such corpora are sparse.  Second, the fine‑tuning procedure must run on very long sequences, leading to extreme GPU memory usage (hundreds of GB per batch) and prohibitive training time.  Thus, while fine‑tuning‑heavy methods can deliver strong performance for a given target, they lack flexibility and scale poorly to multi‑million‑token windows.

LongRoPE addresses these issues by reducing the fine‑tuning burden to only a few thousand steps on sequences that are an order of magnitude shorter (e.g., 256k tokens).  Most of the heavy lifting occurs offline during an evolutionary search that optimizes the rotational embeddings; the subsequent fine‑tuning merely serves to ground the model onto the new positional distribution.

# Efficient fine‑tuning (LongLoRA, PoSE)

With the rise of large foundation models, the cost of fine‑tuning on massive sequences prompted the development of *efficient* fine‑tuning strategies.  *LongLoRA* reuses the LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) framework to inject trainable adapters that can be updated with far fewer parameters, thereby alleviating GPU memory pressure.  *PoSE* employs a *positional scaling* procedure that selectively resizes the positional embeddings during training to accommodate longer sequences without over‑stretching the model.  Both methods enable training on longer contexts with modest computational resources, but still require the model to train on the target maximum length—often 128k tokens or more.

LongRoPE’s efficiency gains are twofold.  First, the core evolutionary search operates on a small subset of tokens (e.g., 5 PG19 samples of the target length) rather than on full‑scale datasets, dramatically reducing evaluation time.  Second, the progressive extension strategy leverages a fine‑tuned checkpoint at an intermediate length (256k) and performs a second, lightweight search for the final long‑window configuration.  Consequently, LongRoPE needs only a couple of thousand training steps on a 256k‑length dataset—orders of magnitude less training than the alternatives.

# Complementary nature of position interpolation vs. attention tricks

It is important to emphasize that the families described above are not mutually exclusive.  In fact, many recent works have started to combine *position interpolation* methods, like those underpinning LongRoPE, with *attention‑modifying* techniques.  For example, a model might retain the native transformer attention but employ a modified RoPE scheme to keep positional precision over a larger window, while simultaneously using a sparse attention mask to reduce per‑token computation.  Likewise, retrieval‑based memory modules can reap the benefits of a well‑calibrated positional encoding, which can improve the fidelity of retrieved sequences.

LongRoPE’s principal contribution lies in its focused attention on the *non‑uniformities* of the RoPE embedding: both across dimensions (different rotational frequencies) and across token positions (in particular, early tokens).  By explicitly optimizing rescaling factors for each dimension and preserving the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens from interpolation, the method captures more robust positional information than earlier linear or group‑based strategies.  This results in two key practical outcomes: (1) it enables an 8× extension without any fine‑tuning, and (2) it supplies a high‑quality initialization that facilitates rapid convergence when fine‑tuning is unavoidable.

In conclusion, LongRoPE represents a lightweight, model‑agnostic augmentation that dovetails seamlessly with existing efficient fine‑tuning pipelines or attention‑modifying tricks, while substantially lowering both computational and data burdens compared to retrieval‑based or fine‑tuning‑heavy alternatives.  The evidence presented in the paper demonstrates that such a simple positional modification, when carefully engineered through evolutionary search, can push the context window of mainstream base models to unprecedented scales—well beyond two million tokens—without sacrificing short‑context performance.

# 14. Practical Guidelines & Reproducibility

Large‑context capabilities are only useful if they can be harnessed reliably by other researchers and practitioners.  In this chapter we give a detailed, step‑by‑step recipe for reproducing the LongRoPE results described in the paper, and for adapting the method to new models or longer targets.  The discussion is written for a graduate‑level reader who is already comfortable with the basics of PyTorch, Hugging Face transformers, and distributed training on GPUs.  All commands and file names refer to the official LongRoPE repository hosted at \texttt{https://github.com/microsoft/LongRoPE}.

> **Why reproducibility matters.**  
>  The key novelty of LongRoPE lies in two concrete engineering choices: (a) an evolution‑based search that jointly tunes a rescaling factor for every RoPE dimension and a token‑position threshold, and (b) a progressive fine‑tuning schedule that moves from 4 k → 256 k → 2048 k without expensive training.  Without a carefully documented workflow, minor deviations in environment or hyper‑parameters will make the journey from a 4 k baseline to a 2048 k model impossible.  The sections below ensure that every step can be replicated exactly, and explain why each component behaves as described in the paper.

---

## # Installing the LongRoPE codebase

1. **Clone the repository**  
   ```bash
   git clone https://github.com/microsoft/LongRoPE.git
   cd LongRoPE
   ```

2. **Create a fresh conda environment**

   ```

4. **Verify installation**

   ```  
   All tests should pass (`OK`).

> **Tip.**  
>  The repository is actively maintained; always pull the latest commit before you start a training job to catch bug fixes (e.g., `git pull origin main`).

## # Preparing tokenized datasets for long lengths

LongRoPE requires *token‑level* batches that are longer than the default 4 k.  Below is a quick recipe for creating a dataset that can be used to fine‑tune up to 256 k tokens per sequence.

| Step | Command | Description |
|------|---------|-------------|
| 1    | `python scripts/tokenize_corpus.py --input_dir raw_texts/ --output_dir tokenized/ --max_length 300000` | Tokenizes all files in `raw_texts/` with GPT‑2 tokeniser (used by LLaMA2).  `max_length` sets the upper bound for a single example; we choose 300k to be safely above the target 256k. |
| 2    | `python scripts/shuffle_and_split.py --input_dir tokenized/ --output_dir processed/ --split 0.8 0.1 0.1` | Splits the tokenised files into training, validation, and test sets (80/10/10).  The script removes trailing tokens that would exceed `max_length`. |
| 3    | `python scripts/prepare_dataset.py --input_dir processed/ --batch_size 16 --output_path l2m-256k.pt` | Converts the splitted files into a single PyTorch `DataLoader`‑friendly format (tensor of shape `[batch_size, seq_len]`).  The resulting `l2m-256k.pt` can be loaded directly by the training script. |

> **Important**  
>  The tokeniser used must be the same as the target LLM’s tokenizer.  For LLaMA2 and Mistral the official Hugging Face tokenizer is fine.  Changing the tokenizer will change the distribution of tokens at long positions, which in turn affects the search outcome.

## # Configuring the evolutionary search (scripts)

The evolutionary search is implemented in the module `search.py`.  Two helper scripts wrap the search into a single command: `search_single.py` for a specific target length and `search_multi.py` for a batch of lengths.

### Key arguments

| Argument | Type | Default | Meaning |
|----------|------|---------|---------|
| `--model_name` | `str` | None | Hugging face model id (e.g., `meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf`). |
| `--data_path` | `str` | None | Path to the tokenized `tensors.pt` file prepared above. |
| `--target_length` | `int` | None | Desired context window (e.g., `128000`). |
| `--population_size` | `int` | 64 | Number of candidate rescale vectors per generation. |
| `--max_iters` | `int` | 40 | Number of evolutionary iterations. |
| `--mutation_rate` | `float` | 0.3 | Probability of mutating each dimension when creating new candidates. |
| `--crossover_size` | `int` | 16 | Number of offspring produced by crossover per generation. |
| `--top_k` | `int` | 32 | Number of best candidates promoted to the next generation. |
| `--seed` | `int` | 42 | Random seed for reproducibility. |

### Running a search

```bash
python scripts/search_single.py \
  --model_name meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf \
  --data_path processed/l2m-256k.pt \
  --target_length 128000 \
  --population_size 64 \
  --max_iters 40 \
  --mutation_rate 0.3 \
  --crossover_size 16 \
  --top_k 32 \
  --seed 42
```

The script will output a file `lambda_opt_{target_length}.npy` containing a vector of length `d/2` where each entry corresponds to the rescale factor `λ_i` for that RoPE dimension.  The script also logs the best perplexity every generation to a CSV (`search_log_{target_length}.csv`).

> **Practical advice**  
>  The search is embarrassingly parallel: each perplexity evaluation can run on its own GPU process.  The repository ships a simple `torchrun` wrapper that automatically parallelises across all available GPUs.

---

## # Fine‑tuning a 256k checkpoint (Torch‑run or Megatron‑LMI)

After you have obtained `lambda_opt_256000.npy` from the preceding search, you can fine‑tune using either the Hugging Face `accelerate` or Megatron‑LMI back‑end.

### Using Hugging Face Accelerate

```bash
accelerate launch train_longrope.py \
  --model_name meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf \
  --tokenizer_name meta-llama/Llama-2-7b-hf \
  --data_path  processed/l2m-256k.pt \
  --lambda_path lambda_opt_256000.npy \
  --output_dir checkpoints/Llama2-7b-256k \
  --batch_size 8 \
  --max_seq_len 256000 \
  --learning_rate 2e-5 \
  --epochs 1 \
  --gradient_accumulation_steps 4
```

> *Why 1 epoch?*  
>  The paper reports that 400 steps with the 128k λ, followed by 600 steps with the 256k λ, suffice.  With a batch size of 8 (24 GB GPU memory on A100), this is roughly 1 epoch over the 128 k token training set.

### Using Megatron‑LMI

1. Copy the checkpoint and lambda into the Megatron‑LMI `checkpoints` folder.

The empirical results also demonstrate that such an extended window does not degrade, and in many settings improves, performance on standard benchmarks that were originally designed for much shorter contexts.  Nevertheless, the paper also leaves an abundance of interesting directions and open questions for future work.  This chapter surveys several promising avenues: dynamic, inference‑time interpolation; synergistic combinations with retrieval and prompt‑compression techniques; extensions to models that do not use RoPE; scaling the hyper‑parameter search beyond 2 M tokens; and rigorous theoretical characterizations of the entropy that is preserved during positional interpolation.

## 15.1 Adaptive Interpolation During Generation

### 15.1.1 Motivation

The LongRoPE approach searches for a fixed set of rescaling factors \( \{\lambda_i, \hat{n}\} \) that are applied uniformly to every input token during inference.  While this static strategy is effective, it ignores the fact that the necessity for interpolation varies with the distance of a token from the beginning of the sequence: tokens that lie far in the past already receive low attention weights and may tolerate a more heavily spaced representation, whereas tokens that are still within the high‑attention window must preserve fine positional distinctions.  An adaptive scheme could therefore generate a smoother transition from “no interpolation” for the first \( \hat{n} \) tokens to a progressively larger scaling factor as the model consumes more tokens.

### 15.1.2 Formulation

Let \( n \in [0, L') \) index the current token position during generation, where \( L' \) is the (maximum) extended context length (e.g., 2 048 k).  We define a continuous interpolation function \( s(n) \in [1, s] \) that maps token positions to an effective extension ratio.  The RoPE rotation angle for dimension \( i \) becomes

\[
\theta_{i}(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{\lambda_i \, s(n)} \;\frac{n}{\beta^i},
\]
where \( \lambda_i \) is the dimension‑specific rescaling factor determined during the evolutionary search and \( \beta = \theta^{2/d} \) as before.  For the first \( \hat{n} \) tokens we set \( s(n)=1 \) so that the original RoPE is preserved.  For tokens beyond \( \hat{n} \), the function can increase gradually, for example via a sigmoid:

\[
s(n) \;=\; 1 \;+\; (s-1)\,\sigma\!\left(\kappa \,(n-\hat{n})\right), 
\qquad \sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.
\]

The hyper‑parameter \( \kappa \) controls how fast we grow beyond the “no‑interpolation” zone.  Because the mapping is applied at inference time only, it incurs negligible computation overhead.

### 15.1.3 Potential Benefits

1. **Smoother Phase Transitions** – A gradual increase in \( s(n) \) mitigates sharp changes in the angular spacing that can spill over from the back‑of‑the‑queue dilutions.  
2. **Dynamic Attention Allocation** – The method allows the model to preserve higher precision for tokens that are still in the attentional focus window while allowing coarser spacing for remote tokens.  
3. **Robustness to Different Sequence Lengths** – Since the interpolation is continuous, the same set of \( \lambda_i \) can serve many different evaluation lengths without requiring a separate search.

### 15.1.4 Research Program

1. **Prototype a Sigmoid‑Based \( s(n) \)**  – Implement the formulation above and benchmark perplexity on a sequence of lengths (4 k, 32 k, 128 k, 1 M, 2 048 k).  
2. **Parameter Search for \( \kappa \)** – Use a small grid or Bayesian optimization to find the optimal smoothing rate for each target length.  
3. **Ablation** – Compare static (LongRoPE) vs. adaptive \( s(n) \) on the passkey retrieval task and standard short‑context benchmarks to verify that no degradation is introduced.  
4. **Explainability** – Visualize how the RoPE angle differences evolve across positions; illustrate the importance of high‑frequency dimensions being less affected for near tokens.

---

## 15.2 Combining LongRoPE with Retrieval or Prompt‑Compression

### 15.2.1 Why Combine?

LongRoPE addresses the positional embedding bottleneck, whereas retrieval‑based methods (e.g., Long‑LLM, Retrieval‑augmented LLMs) bring in an external memory to augment the sequence with relevant documents.  Both mechanisms tackle long‑context challenges from different angles.  In practice, many applications may require *both*: being able to look up distant or cached knowledge while also being able to understand the internal structure of a very long single document.  

Prompt‑compression methods (e.g., prompt‑dropping, sentence‑compressors) aim to shrink early tokens without sacrificing semantics.  If combined with LongRoPE, the compressed tokens could benefit from a *no‑interpolation* zone that matches the reduced effective length.

### 15.2.2 Hybrid Retrieval + LongRoPE

A typical retrieval‑augmented LLM concatenates a prompt, the main document, and a set of retrieved passages.  Traditionally, the implementation assumes a hard context window \( L \).  LongRoPE freely expands this window, so we can:

1. **Place retrieved passages further back** – By extending the context, retrieved snippets can be inserted at positions farther from the prompt, reducing the chance that the model attends too much to close‑by noisy passages.  
2. **Selective Interpolation** – For tokens that are *retrieved*, we can set a smaller \( s(n) \) or even \( s(n)=1 \) to preserve high‑fidelity embeddings, while letting the *main* body use a higher interpolation ratio.  

One concrete design is to assign a *retrieval mask* \( r(n)\in\{0,1\} \).  Then define the effective scaling

\[
s_{\text{eff}}(n) = (1 - r(n))\, s_{\text{main}}(n) + r(n)\, s_{\text{retrieved}}(n),
\]
where \( s_{\text{retrieved}}(n) \) can be set to 1 or a small augmentation factor.  The retrieval mask can be learned jointly or pre­defined.

### 15.2.3 Prompt‑Compression + LongRoPE

Prompt‑compression techniques typically prune or compress lead tokens until the prompt length satisfies a certain budget.  LongRoPE offers an alternative: allow the model to keep the full prompt but use a *cells‑wise* interpolation that tolerates more distant positions.  This can be inverted: keep an *uncompressed* prompt but apply *full* RiPE (no interpolation) only to the first \( \hat{n} \) tokens (which might be exactly the prompt).  The rest of the prompt+context can then go through the usual long‑RoPE interpolation with a higher \( s \), and the model can adapt its attention to a longer-range context.

### 15.2.4 Research Program

1. **Design a Retrieval Mask** – Implement a training objective that encourages retrieved passages to be far from the prompt (e.g. a positional penalty).
2. **Experiment with Multiple \( s_{\text{retrieved}} \)** – Evaluate performance as we vary the scaling for retrieved tokens (from 1 up to \( s \)) on retrieval‑augmented tasks such as open‑domain QA.
3. **Prompt‑Compression Integration** – Combine LongRoPE‑based *no‑interpolation* zones with existing prompt compression modules; measure compression ratios against perplexity losses.
4. **Analysis of Attention Patterns** – Use attention maps to verify that the model indeed attends differently to compressed vs. uncompressed tokens.

## 15.3 Extending Boolean Techniques to Non‑RoPE Models

### 15.3.1 Background

The LongRoPE paper is explicitly RoPE‑centric.  However, not all recent LLMs employ rotary embeddings.  Absolute positional embeddings (e.g., learnable or sinusoidal) are still common, especially in older transformer variants.  Extending context windows for those architectures is a natural question.

### 15.3.2 Possible Strategies

1. **Self‑Attention‑Scale Modulation** – In absolute positional embeddings, the positional signal is simply added to the token embeddings.  One can *scale* the positional vectors by a factor that decays with the sequence length, e.g.,

   \[
   p_i(n) \;=\; \frac{n}{s}\, p_i^{\text{base}}(n),
   \]

   where \( p_i^{\text{base}}(n) \) is the learned positional embedding at index \( n \).  This *down‑scales* the positional influence, analogous to what RoPE does.

2. **Positional Embedding Interpolation** – Create a continuous interpolation function similar to LongRoPE’s \( s(n) \) but acting on the embedding vectors themselves: replace the *one‑hot* positional token with a *continuous* embedding derived by linear interpolation across the learned table.

3. **Relative Positional Bias** – Modify the bias term in the attention score to approximate a longer context.  For instance, replace the absolute position embedding matrix \( P \) with a *distance‑based* routing function that considers relative distances.

### 15.3.3 Challenges

- **Non‑Differentiable Continuous Indices** – For absolute embeddings, the embedding lookup is discrete, so smoothing requires careful approximation (e.g., via embedding interpolation or using a learnable continuous embedding space).
- **Training Stability** – Fine‑tuning absolute positional embeddings for huge \( L' \) can cause over‑fitting to the new positions, as suggested by earlier works on extrapolation.
- **Compatibility** – Many LLMs rely on positional embeddings for training-time masking and layer norm interactions; modifying these signals may require more extensive architectural changes.

### 15.3.4 Research Program

1. **Define an Interpolated Absolute Positional Embedding (IAPE)** – Implement IAPE as the convex combination of nearest learned positions.  
2. **Quantify Perplexity Gains** – Run experiments similar to LongRoPE on a model like GPT‑2 or a non‑RoPE version of LLaMA.  
3. **Cross‑Validation for Interpolation Weights** – Use a validation set to learn a lightweight interpolation weight schedule without over‑fitting.  
4. **Blend with Rotatory Embeddings** – For hybrid models that combine relative and absolute, investigate whether the LongRoPE‑style scaling can be applied only to the rotary part.

## 15.4 Scaling Hyper‑Parameter Search Beyond 2 M Tokens

### 15.4.1 Search Explosion

The modern evolutionary search approach in LongRoPE already reduces the combinatorial explosion by pruning to monotonic candidates.  Nevertheless, the search space still grows rapidly with the target extension ratio \( s \).  For \( s \) beyond \( 64\times \) (e.g. aiming for \( >4 \) M tokens with a 4 k base), the search budget used in the paper (40 iterations, 64 individuals) may become insufficient to locate a high‑quality solution.

### 15.4.2 Possible Optimizations

1. **Hierarchical Search** – Perform *pre‑search* for coarse factors at a moderate scaling (e.g., \( 4\times \)), then *refine* those solutions at higher scales by considering only a small perturbation set.

2. **Surrogate Models** – Replace direct perplexity evaluation with a learned regression model that predicts perplexity from candidate \( \lambda_i \).  A small regression network can be trained on a subset of candidates and used to approximate the loss quickly.

3. **Bayesian Optimization on Structured Spaces** – Treat the rescaling factors as a continuous vector with monotonic constraints; use an acquisition function (e.g., Expected Improvement) that respects the ordering.

4. **Transfer Learning of Interpolation Parameters** – Use the optimal \( \lambda_i \) found for a model at 2 M tokens as a "warm‑start" for 4 M token search.  The hypothesis is that the scaling functions are similar across orders of magnitude.

### 15.4.3 Research Program

1. **Benchmark the Hierarchical Scheme** – Compare random vs. hierarchical vs. surrogate‑guided search on a synthetic 16 k to 256 k extension.  
2. **Quantify Speed‑up** – Measure compute time and perplexity gains for each strategy.  
3. **Theoretically Analyze the Search Landscape** – Explore whether the objective function (perplexity) is convex or smooth with respect to the monotonic \(\lambda_i\) vector.  
4. **Experimental Validation on 4 M Tokens** – Once a viable method is found, run a full search to produce a 4 M‑token LongRoPE model (requires a new pre‑training checkpoint or a smaller model, e.g., Vicuna).

## 15.5 Theoretical Bounds on Entropy Preservation

### 15.5.1 Information‑Theoretic View

Rotary positional embeddings encode relative positional information into the complex plane via rotations at various frequencies.  The “entropy” of these positions can be formalized as the amount of mutual information between a token’s original position index \( n \) and its rotated vector \( \mathbf{r}_n \).  Extending the context window via interpolation reduces the angular span per token, thus potentially reducing positional distinguishability.  An optimal interpolation method should preserve as much mutual information as possible.

### 15.5.2 Mathematical Formulation

Let \( \mathbf{R}_n \in \mathbb{R}^d \) denote the RoPE embedding at position \( n \).  Define the feature space of the \(i\)‑th dimension as \( \theta_i n \).  For a given interpolation scheme \( \lambda_i(n) \), the mapping becomes

\[
\Phi: n \;\mapsto\; \bigl( \cos(\lambda_i^{-1}(n)\frac{n}{\beta^i}), \sin(\lambda_i^{-1}(n)\frac{n}{\beta^i}) \bigr)_{i=0}^{d/2-1}.
\]

The mutual information \( I(n;\mathbf{R}_n) \) can be upper‑bounded by the differential entropy of the output under a uniform input distribution.  More simply, we can consider the *separability* of neighboring positions in Euclidean space.  For any two consecutive positions \( n \) and \( n+1 \), the angular difference in dimension \( i \) is

\[
\Delta \theta_i(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{\lambda_i(n)}\left(\frac{n+1}{\beta^i} - \frac{n}{\beta^i}\right) = \frac{1}{\lambda_i(n)\beta^i}.
\]

The *minimum* of these differences over all dimensions imposes a lower bound on the positional granularity.

### 15.5.3 Bounding Perplexity by Angular Separation

Assuming a local linear approximation, the change in the attention logits between two tokens at positions \( n \) and \( n+1 \) is proportional to the cosine of their angular difference.  If \( \Delta \theta_i(n) \) becomes too small, the model may view the two tokens as almost identical, leading to higher perplexity.  Hence we can posit:

\[
\text{Perplexity} \;\approx\; f\!\bigl( \min_{i} \Delta \theta_i(n) \bigr),
\]
where \( f \) is a non‑decreasing function.  Consequently, the goal of the search is to maximize the minimum angular spacing while still respecting the monotonic \( \lambda_i \) constraints.

### 15.5.4 Proposed Bounds

1. **Upper Bound** – For a fixed extension ratio \( s \), if we enforce \( \lambda_i \leq s \) for all \( i \), the minimum angular spacing is \( (1/s)\beta^{-i_{\max}} \), where \( i_{\max} = d/2-1 \).  This gives a worst‑case bound on perplexity growth.

2. **Lower Bound** – If we predetermine a *desired* angular spacing \( \delta > 0 \), the minimal \( \lambda_i \) that satisfies \( \Delta \theta_i \geq \delta\) is \( \lambda_i \leq (\beta^i \delta)^{-1} \).  These constraints can be encoded into the evolutionary search as a *feasibility* mask.

### 15.5.5 Research Program

1. **Empirical Correlation** – Measure the minimum cosine similarity between positions for various \( \lambda_i \) sets and correlate with observed perplexity.  
2. **Formal Constraints** – Incorporate the derived angular spacing constraints into the search algorithm as hard feasibility checks, reducing the search space.  
3. **Generalization to LongRoPE** – Investigate whether maintaining a target minimum angular spacing yields comparable or better performance than solely optimizing for perplexity on a narrow validation set.  
4. **Theoretical Analysis of the Trade‑Off** – Prove that for RoPE‑based attention, preserving a lower bound on positional angular noise bounds the change in attention logits, translating into a bound on perplexity under certain assumptions (e.g., i.i.d. token distribution).

## 16 Conclusion

### Key contributions recap

In this chapter we have distilled the principal achievements of the LongRoPE framework, which was introduced as a lightweight yet powerful extension of rotational positional embeddings (RoPE) for large language models.  The method rests on three interlocking innovations:

1. **Non‑uniform positional interpolation** – By recognizing that RoPE behaves differently across its frequency bands and across token positions, LongRoPE replaces the blanket scaling of earlier approaches (such as linear PI or grouped YaRN) with a per‑dimension, per‑position rescaling.  A small evolutionary search discovers the optimal interpolation parameters for any target context length \(L'\), ensuring that the most informative dimensions are preserved and that the first few thousand tokens are left untouched.

2. **Efficient search algorithm** – The straightforward breadth of possible rescaling vectors makes exhaustive search infeasible.  LongRoPE mitigates this by (i) seeding the population with established interpolations (PI, NTK, YaRN), (ii) enforcing a monotonicity constraint that is grounded in NTK theory, and (iii) performing mutation and crossover only on individuals that satisfy the constraint.  The result is a rapid convergence to near‑optimal parameters, even for extreme extensions.

3. **Progressive, fine‑tuning‑efficient extension** – Rather than requiring a single, impractical fine‑tuning run at the target length, LongRoPE uses a two‑stage strategy.  First, the model is fine‑tuned on a moderate length (256 k tokens) using the interpolations discovered for a 128 k target.  Second, a second evolutionary search refines the RoPE for 2.048 M tokens, exploiting the fact that the fine‑tuned LLM can be brought to a 512× extension without additional training steps.  Finally, a brief (≈ 1 k steps) fine‑tuning procedure restores short‑context performance by re‑searching for 4 k or 8 k rescaling factors.

Collectively, these contributions enable an unprecedented 512× expansion of context windows from the 4 k baseline to 2 048 k tokens, while preserving or even improving performance on standard benchmarks that operate within the conventional window.

### Performance summary

Experimental results across two benchmark architectures—LLaMA 2‑7B and Mistral 7B—demonstrate the practical effectiveness of LongRoPE:

- **Perplexity trends**: For evaluation lengths up to 256 k tokens, the LongRoPE‑extended models exhibit consistently lower perplexity than existing state‑of‑the‑art adapters (YaRN, LongLoRA, etc.).  The careful interpolation keeps the language‑model loss near its 4 k‑baseline even as the window grows by over an order of magnitude.

- **Extremely long documents**: On the Books‑3 collection (≈ 2 M tokens per record), the 2.048 M‑token LongRoPE models maintain perplexity that is comparable to or better than 128 k‑trained baselines.  This performance is achieved with merely 400 + 600 training steps (≈ 1 k total) on 256 k sequences.

- **Task‑specific gains**: The passkey‑retrieval experiment, which tests the model’s ability to locate a sparse signal amid a huge noisy context, shows that the 2.048 M‑token LLaMA‑based model keeps over 90 % accuracy all the way up to the maximum length.  In short‑context zero‑shot or few‑shot benchmarks, the LongRoPE models match or exceed the original models’ scores, indicating that the interpolation strategy does not erode the pre‑training knowledge.

- **Computational overhead**: Updating RoPE during inference incurs only a negligible cost because it requires recomputation of a small set of cosine/sine matrices.  The search itself is performed once per target length and can be distributed across standard GPU clusters; the fine‑tuning stages are lightweight compared to full‑length pre‑training.

These results confirm that LongRoPE’s theoretical insights translate into measurable gains across a range of settings.

### Practical impact statement

The ability to read and reason over millions of tokens concurrently unlocks a host of practical applications that were previously infeasible:

- **Scalable document‑level reasoning**: Complex report generation, legal reasoning, or scientific literature reviews now become tractable in a single pass, reducing inference latency and memory consumption.

- **Efficient long‑form summarization and editing**: Large‑scale narrative editing, multi‑chapter summarization, or editing of legal contracts can leverage the full context without resorting to chunking or hierarchical approaches.

- **Robust in‑context learning**: Few‑shot or chain‑of‑thought prompting that requires many demonstration examples can be accommodated within a single context window, improving both performance and developer ergonomics.

- **Agent‑level memory**: Large‑context language‑model agents that need to recall user interactions or external knowledge stored in long logs can maintain a coherent internal state without external retrieval modules.

Moreover, because LongRoPE only alters the positional embedding layer, existing LLM codebases, toolchains, and optimizations (e.g., flash‑attention, tensor parallelism) can be reused unchanged.  This minimizes integration cost for practitioners.

### Research potential

LongRoPE opens several promising avenues for future exploration:

1. **Generative task adaptation**: While the paper focuses on language modeling and retrieval tasks, the same interpolation scheme could be integrated with generation‑centric losses (e.g., RL‑HF, policy gradients) to assess whether generative fidelity is preserved when operating at extreme lengths.

2. **Adaptive per‑sample interpolation**: Current interpolation is static per length.  A dynamic controller could adjust rescaling on the fly based on the distribution of token positions in a particular input, potentially improving performance on highly skewed documents.

3. **Cross‑architecture transfer**: Investigating how the same non‑uniform interpolation strategy applies to other rotational encodings (e.g., in vision transformers or multimodal models) could broaden its utility beyond text.

4. **Theoretical analysis of entropy preservation**: A deeper mathematical study of how non‑uniform interpolation preserves the mutual information between token positions and embeddings may yield principled guidelines for future embedding designs.

5. **Integration with retrieval or attention‑reshaping modules**: Combining LongRoPE with lightweight retrieval mechanisms or advanced sparse attention schemes could further push the practical limits of long‑context reasoning while keeping resource demands low.

In sum, LongRoPE not only delivers a concrete technical contribution but also lays the groundwork for a new class of research that bridges efficient positional encoding, fine‑tuning strategy, and extreme‑scale language modeling. By making the extension of a pre‑trained RoPE‑based LLM to over two million tokens both feasible and reliable, it invites the community to rethink the scalability boundaries of large language models and to design downstream systems that fully exploit this capability.
