\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 5: # 5. Formal Problem Formulation

Large‑language models (LLMs) rely on positional encodings to inject token order into the Transformer layers. In RoPE‑based LLMs, each token at index \(n\) is represented by a pair of sine–cosine vectors whose arguments are scaled by a set of rotation frequencies \(\theta_i\). To extend an LLM’s context window from a pretrained length \(L\) to a longer target length \(L'\), the defining idea of LongRoPE is to replace every fixed rotation frequency with a *rescaled* frequency that respects the new positions.  The Fundamental question that this chapter addresses is:

> **Which collection of rescaling factors, one per RoPE dimension, together with a policy for the initial token positions, yields the lowest next‑token prediction loss (i.e. the lowest perplexity) when the model is confronted with sequences of length \(L'\)?**

Below we precisely formulate this optimization problem, lay out the notation that will recur throughout the text, and explain the intuitive role of each term.

## Notation

* **RoPE angle**  
  The RoPE embedding of a token at position \(n\) for the \(i\)-th dimension is  
  \[
  \varphi_{n}^{(i)}
    = \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}},\qquad  \beta = \theta^{\,2/d},
  \]
  where \(d\) is the total embedding dimension and \(\theta\) is the base frequency (default \(10000\) in most LLMs).  Thus for a fixed token the angle grows linearly with its position, but is divided by a higher power of \(\beta\) for higher dimensions, which are associated with lower angular frequencies.

* **Rescale factor \(\lambda_i\)**  
  For every RoPE dimension \(i\) we introduce a positive scalar \(\lambda_i\).  This factor determines whether the new positions are *interpolated* (if \(\lambda_i>1\)) or *extrapolated* (if \(\lambda_i<1\)) relative to the original rotation frequency.  The RoPE angle after rescaling becomes  
  \[
  \tilde{\varphi}_{n}^{(i)} = \frac{\varphi_{n}^{(i)}}{\lambda_i} = \frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}.
  \]

* **Initial‑token threshold \(\widehat{n}\)**  
  A non‑negative integer that specifies how many leading tokens keep the *unmodified* RoPE angles.  Tokens with indices \(n < \widehat{n}\) use the original \(\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\); tokens with \(n \ge \widehat{n}\) employ the rescaled angles.  Formally we denote the indicator function  
  \[
  \mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n) =
    \begin{cases}
      1, & n < \widehat{n}, \\
      \dfrac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n \ge \widehat{n}.
    \end{cases}
  \]

* **Rotary embedding with non‑uniform interpolation**  
  The full RoPE vector for a token at position \(n\) becomes  
  \[
  \operatorname{RoPE}_n = 
  \bigl[
      \cos\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\,\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\bigr),\;
      \sin\!\bigl(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\,\varphi_{n}^{(i)}\bigr),\;
      \dots
  \bigr],
  \]
  where the pattern is repeated for all \(i = 0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1\).

These symbols together describe every possible positional encoding that LongRoPE could employ once we choose a set of \(\{\lambda_i\}\) and an initial‑token threshold \(\widehat{n}\).

## Objective

Consider an LLM that receives as input a batch \(\mathbf{X} = \{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^B\) of token sequences.  To investigate the effect of a positional interpolation policy, we restrict our attention to sequences that are *at least* as long as the target context window \(L'\).  Thus for each \(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\) we require \(|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}| \ge L'\).

For a given policy \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1},\widehat{n})\), the model outputs a probability distribution over the next token at every position.  Collecting the cross‑entropy losses across all positions and all samples yields a scalar loss
\[
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\lambda,\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr).
\]
Our goal is to *minimise* this loss, equivalently the perplexity, over all admissible policies.  The formal optimisation problem is therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
\underset{\lambda,\,\widehat{n}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}
\; & \mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\lambda,\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr) \\
\text{s.t.}
\; & \lambda_i > 0 \;\;\forall i, \\
\; &  \widehat{n} \in \{0,1,\dots,L'\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
In words, we look for a collection of per‑dimension scaling constants and a leading‑token threshold that let the RoPE embed the new positions in a way that best preserves the model’s language‑modeling capability when confronted with sequences of length \(L'\).

*Why does the objective look so simple?*  Because the *only* architectural change we are allowed to make is to the positional encoding; all other parameters of the pretrained LLM are frozen during the search.  Consequently the LLM’s performance is wholly captured by the perplexity under the modified embeddings.

## Constraint: Unmodified Prefix

The indicator function \(\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\widehat{n};\,n)\) embodies the constraint that the first \(\widehat{n}\) tokens in every sequence should *retain* the original RoPE angles.  This reflects several empirical observations:

1. **Attention concentration** – Attention scores for early tokens are typically higher (especially in autoregressive tasks).  Preserving their original positions preserves the fine‑grained relative angular differences that the model has learned during pre‑training.

2. **Catastrophic value avoidance** – The most severe distribution shift caused by new positions arises right after the longest segment seen in pre‑training.  By leaving a buffer of initial tokens untouched, we reduce this shift.

Therefore, the search is not over all \(\lambda_i\) directly; it is over the *pairs* \((\lambda_i,\widehat{n})\).  The penalty for choosing a very large \(\widehat{n}\) would come indirectly through the perplexity: if \(\widehat{n}\) is too large, the model misses the opportunity to properly encode long‑distance dependencies in the former initial positions.

## Interpretation of \(\lambda_i\): Interpolation vs. Extrapolation

The rescaling factor exerts a clear geometric effect on the rotary angles:

- **Interpolation (\(\lambda_i>1\))** contracts the angular velocity of the \(i\)-th dimension.  Tokens farther apart in the sequence are mapped to more closely spaced angles than before, which is what forces the model to reconcile longer spans with its originally finite rotation space.

- **Extrapolation (\(\lambda_i<1\))** expands the angular velocity.  Tokens are spread further apart in the embedding space, effectively *stretching* the rotational manifold.  This is advantageous for dimensions that were originally over‑permissive (high frequencies) and can afford a greater spread without losing discriminability.

Because different RoPE dimensions encode different frequency bands, LongRoPE’s search is allowed to assign distinct \(\lambda_i\) to each dimension.  Early dimensions (high \(\theta_i\)) may receive a smaller \(\lambda_i\) (less interpolation) while later ones receive larger \(\lambda_i\) (more interpolation).  The ordering constraint \(\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}\) (used by the search algorithm) is motivated by the neural‑tangent‑kernel analysis of rotary embeddings, which tells us that high‑frequency dimensions should interpolate less.

## Putting It All Together

The full optimisation problem that LongRoPE tackles can thus be summarised as:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Find }\;
\mathbf{\lambda} = (\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{\tfrac{d}{2}-1}),
\;\;
\widehat{n}
\;\text{ such that}\\[4pt]
\mathcal{L}\bigl(\text{LLM};\,\text{RoPE}(\mathbf{\lambda},\widehat{n}),\,\mathbf{X}\bigr)
\;\text{is minimised} \\
\text{subject to } \lambda_i>0,
\;\;
\widehat{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,L'\}.
\end{aligned}}
\]

In practice, solving this problem exactly is infeasible because the search space grows exponentially with both the number of RoPE dimensions and the target extension ratio \(s=L'/L\).  LongRoPE therefore introduces a carefully designed evolutionary search that incorporates the two key non‑uniformities—per‑dimension scaling and initial‑token threshold—while capitalising on prior insights such as the monotonicity of \(\lambda_i\) and the benefit of an unmodified prefix.  The remainder of the book will detail how this search is carried out, how the resulting interpolations are used in a progressive fine‑tuning pipeline, and how the entire framework successfully extends LLMs to a 2‑million‑token context window.
