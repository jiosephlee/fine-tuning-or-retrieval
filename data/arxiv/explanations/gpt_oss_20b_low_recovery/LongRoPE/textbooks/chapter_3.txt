\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Existing Positional Interpolation Techniques

Large language models (LLMs) rely on positional embeddings to inject token order information into the Transformer’s self‑attention mechanism. The most widespread positional embedding scheme in modern LLMs is Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), a continuous, frequency‑based formulation that has proven to be highly effective for sequences up to a few thousand tokens. When one wishes to push the context window beyond its pre‑training ceiling, RoPE must be “interpolated” or “extrapolated” to accommodate new token positions. Over the past few years, the research community has introduced a handful of interpolation strategies—each motivated by different theoretical insights—yet each suffers from practical drawbacks that become pronounced at the very large extensions that researchers now target.

In this chapter we provide a unified overview of the principal positional interpolation techniques that have been applied to RoPE: (1) linear interpolation (PI), (2) Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–informed scaling, (3) dynamic‑NTK, and (4) YaRN’s frequency‑based grouping. For each method we derive the exact mathematical form in which it rescales RoPE’s rotation angles, we describe the intuition behind the scaling choice, and we highlight the key pitfalls that hinder scalability to extremely long sequences.

---

## 3.1 Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)

### 3.1.1 Formulation

Let the original RoPE rotation angle for the $i$‑th dimension be
\[
\theta_i(n) \;=\; n \, \beta^{-i}, \qquad
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\]
where $n$ is the token index, $d$ is the embedding dimension, and $\theta$ is the base frequency (standardly $10^4$). The Positional Interpolation (PI) method simply scales all angles by a uniform factor $\lambda$ that reflects the target extension ratio:
\[
\lambda \;=\; s \;=\; \frac{L'}{L}.
\]
Hence, for a new token at position $n$ in a target sequence of length $L'$, PI uses
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{PI})}(n) \;=\; \frac{n}{\lambda}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
In matrix form this can be seen as applying a single down‑scaling to the rotary phase for every dimension.

### 3.1.2 Intuition

The simplest conceivable way to map the original positions $[0, L)$ onto the extended positions $[0, L')$ is to linearly “shrink” the angular grid. By equalizing the density of phases across the spectrum, PI preserves the relative geometry of the original sentence for all tokens, ensuring that the phase spacing between neighboring tokens remains unchanged up to the global scaling factor. This uniformity is attractive from a mathematical standpoint and leads to a straightforward implementation: one merely inserts a scalar multiplier into the RoPE computation.

### 3.1.3 Drawbacks

Despite its elegance, PI suffers from *crowding*: as $\lambda$ grows large, all rotation angles for all dimensions are compressed into a tiny angular interval. Consequently, tokens that originally occupied distinct positions now map to almost identical phase values, especially in the low‑frequency dimensions which vary slowly over the sequence. This loss of positional resolution makes self‑attention effectively blind to token order, leading the model’s perplexity to spike well before the intended extension ratio is met (typically beyond $2\times$). Moreover, the uniform scaling disregards the varied importance of different frequency components; high‑frequency terms should arguably survive compression better than the low‑frequency ones.

## 3.2 Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–Informed Scaling

### 3.2.1 Formulation

The NTK perspective models the Transformer as a kernel machine, wherein the contributions of RoPE dimensions can be linked to the Neural Tangent Kernel’s spectral decomposition. Based on this theory, the scale factor for the $i$‑th dimension follows a power‑law:
\[
\lambda_i \;=\; s^{\,i}
\]
that yields an *exponential* scaling across dimensions. The rotated angles become
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{NTK})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i}\, \beta^{-i}
\;=\;
\frac{n}{s^i}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
Equivalently, high‑frequency dimensions ($i$ small) receive a milder reduction (since $s^i$ is close to 1) whereas low‑frequency dimensions (large $i$) are compressed more aggressively.

### 3.2.2 Intuition

The NTK analysis suggests that the self‑attention dynamics in Transformers can be captured by a kernel whose spectral decay is tied to cosine Fourier features with varying frequencies. Under NTK theory, the effective influence of each RoPE dimension on the network’s output behaves logarithmically with respect to the sequence length. Therefore, rather than uniformly scaling all dimensions, one should distribute the compression in a dimension‑dependent manner that mirrors the NTK spectrum. This maintains a semblance of the low‑frequency structure while compressing the high‑frequency terms that are less critical for long‑range dependencies.

### 3.2.3 Drawbacks

Although NTK scaling alleviates the severe crowding seen in PI, it still applies a rigid, pre‑defined functional form that may not perfectly match the specific frequency distribution of a given LLM. Empirically, this method still incurs noticeable perplexity degradation when extending beyond roughly $4\times$. Furthermore, NTK scaling presumes a stationary kernel that does not vary with the token index; any spatial structure (e.g., “hot spots” near the sequence start) is ignored. As a consequence, the method is sub‐optimal for extremely large extensions where the positional distribution shifts more dramatically.

## 3.3 Dynamic‑NTK

### 3.3.1 Formulation

Dynamic‑NTK augments the NTK interpolation by making the extension ratio *position‑dependent*. Let $n$ denote the current token index and $|x|$ the total token length. Define a local scaling factor
\[
\lambda_i(n) \;=\; \Bigl( \frac{|x|}{L}\Bigr)^{\,i \, \psi(n)},
\]
where $\psi(n)$ is an auxiliary function that decays with $n$ (for instance, $\psi(n) = \exp(-\alpha n/|x|)$). The rotated angles read
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{Dyn-NTK})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i(n)}\, \beta^{-i}.
\]
Thus, tokens appearing farther from the start of the sequence are allowed larger compression ratios than those near the beginning.

### 3.3.2 Intuition

Dynamic‑NTK acknowledges that the Transformer’s attention pattern is often biased towards the beginning of the sequence (the “context” tokens). By allowing less severe compression for early positions, the representation of landmark tokens (e.g., the initial prompt) remains sharp, mitigating the “crowding” effect in the crucial early block. As the sequence progresses, the model can tolerate more aggressive compression since the relative positional gaps between distant tokens are less impactful.

### 3.3.3 Drawbacks

While dynamic‑NTK does improve upon static NTK, it introduces additional hyper‑parameters (e.g., the decay rate $\alpha$) that must be tuned per target extension ratio and per model. The exponential decay often collapses the very high token indices into a narrow angular band, which in practice still forces a degree of “crowding” for very long contexts. Moreover, the formula remains a heuristic; it does not explore richer forms of non‑uniformity, such as allowing entirely different scaling behaviours for distinct groups of dimensions beyond simple exponentiation.

## 3.4 YaRN: Frequency‑Based Grouping

### 3.4.1 Formulation

YaRN partitions the $d/2$ RoPE dimensions into three groups based on their frequency:
- **High‑frequency** group (small $i$): $\lambda_i = 1$ (no interpolation, pure extrapolation);
- **Intermediate** group: $\lambda_i$ follows the NTK power law $s^i$;
- **Low‑frequency** group (large $i$): linear interpolation $\lambda_i = s$.

Thus,
\[
\theta_i^{(\text{YaRN})}(n) \;=\;
\frac{n}{\lambda_i}\,\beta^{-i},
\]
with $\lambda_i$ chosen according to the group into which $i$ falls.

### 3.4.2 Intuition

YaRN explicitly recognises that RoPE’s frequency spectrum is heterogeneous: high‑frequency components encode fine‑grained local relationships, whereas low‑frequency components capture longer dependencies. By treating each region differently—extrapolating the most oscillatory terms, linearly scaling the most stable, and giving a middle‑ground NTK adjustment to the intermediate ones—YaRN provides a more nuanced interpolation than any single‑parameter scheme.

### 3.4.3 Drawbacks

The main limitation is that the division into three groups is *manual and heuristic*. Different models (e.g., with different embedding sizes or pre‑training regimes) may have distinct optimal group boundaries, and these boundaries are not learned adaptively. Moreover, the choice of linear versus NTK scaling for each group is rigid; there is no room for finer per‑dimension tuning. Consequently, while YaRN can effectively extend the context window by several times, it still caps out around a $4\times$ extension in practice, failing to reach the scaling multipliers that contemporary work aspires to (e.g., $512\times$).

## 3.5 Common Pitfalls Across Interpolation Methods

| Issue | Why It Occurs | Consequence |
|-------|---------------|-------------|
| **Crowded Positions** | Uniform or too‑aggressive scaling compresses phase space for all tokens. | Loss of positional discriminability; self‑attention treats tokens as identical. |
| **Over‑Interpolation** | Scaling factors exceed what the model’s learned geometry tolerates. | Model’s internal representations drift; perplexity rises dramatically at modest extensions. |
| **Sparsity** | Linear PI neglects the fact that low‑frequency dimensions vary slowly; high‑frequency ones are abandoned. | Important long‑range dependencies become blurred; seldom mitigated in downstream tasks. |
| **Uniformity Assumption** | All dimensions are treated equally or by a simple power law. | Ignores subtle, dimension‑specific sensitivity that can be crucial for preserving learned attention patterns. |
| **Position‑Independence** | Mostly methods scale based solely on dimension or a global factor, not token ID. | Early tokens (often critical for context) suffer undue compression; later tokens become indistinguishable. |

These strengths and weaknesses motivate the search for **non‑uniform** interpolation strategies that can adaptively scale each RoPE dimension and even differentiate between token positions. The LongRoPE framework—discussed in the following chapters—directly addresses these limitations by leveraging an evolutionary search that optimizes per‑dimension scaling factors while allowing for a token‑position threshold that preserves the most important places in a sequence. By avoiding the rigid assumptions of past methods, LongRoPE is able to maintain competitive perplexity up to unprecedented context windows of over two million tokens.
