\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why are the rescale factors allowed to exceed the extension ratio s in the search space?
Question:
The search space allows λ_i to go up to s × 1.25, which means more interpolation than simple linear scaling. How does increasing λ_i beyond the naive interpolation ratio help preserve positional information, and under what circumstances does this happen?
Answer:
**Why can the searched rescaling factors λᵢ exceed the raw extension ratio s, and when does this extra interpolation help?**

---

### 1.  What “exceeding s” really means

The naive linear‑interpolation (PI) scheme simply rescales every rotary angle by the same factor s = L′/L. In the notation of the paper this is

\[
\lambda_i \;=\; s \qquad\forall i ,
\]

so each RoPE dimension is shrunk by s.  
LongRoPE lets each λᵢ vary freely in the interval  

\[
1.0 \;\leq\; \lambda_i \;\leq\; s \times 1.25 .
\]

A value greater than s therefore relaxes the scaling: the rotary angle is *less* shrunk than the nominal PI factor. In other words, the positional embedding is “pushed out” farther in cosine‑sine space than the plain linear scheme would allow.

---

### 2.  Why an “extra” interpolation can preserve information

Rotary position embeddings encode relative position as an angle that grows linearly with the token index \(n\).  
When we extend the context window, new positions appear at large `n`. If we keep the original growth rate, the new angles would quickly leave the principal interval \([0,2\pi)\) and wrap around, causing many distant tokens to look very close to each other in the embedding space. This crowding makes it harder for the model to differentiate near‑by tokens—especially those that multiply contribute to attention scores.

The non‑uniformity formalised in Eq. (3) of the paper says that **low‑frequency (high‑index) RoPE dimensions are more tolerant of interpolation**: they already encode slowly varying positional signals and can absorb a larger scaling factor without sacrificing information. Conversely, **high‑frequency dimensions need finer angular resolution** and therefore must be interpolated more aggressively.

Let us illustrate with a toy example. Suppose a dimension has frequency \(f=1/1000\) (so \(\theta_i=2\pi f\)). With a target ratio \(s=4\) (extending from 4 k to 16 k), linear PI would set \(\lambda_i=4\), shrinking the angle by a factor of 4. The new angle for token \(n=12000\) would be

\[
\frac{n}{\beta^i}\lambda_i^{-1}
  \;=\; \frac{12000}{\beta^i}\cdot\frac{1}{4}
  \;=\; 3000/\beta^i,
\]

which may already lie in the wrapped region. If we use a slightly larger \(\lambda_i = 4.5\), the angle becomes \( \approx 2667/\beta^i\), i.e. 12.5 % farther away from the wrap point. The two tokens now sit at distinct places in the Hilbert space, and the attention mechanism can distinguish them better.

In a multi‑dimensional RoPE, the overall positional representation is a product of cosines across all dimensions. The *information entropy* of the representation—i.e. the amount of distinguishable positional patterns—depends on how the **collection** of angles is spread out. By giving higher‑frequency dimensions a *larger* λᵢ, we spread their angles more aggressively and prevent the high‑frequency portion from collapsing into a dense cluster. The aggregate entropy rises, and the model can keep learning reasonable positional cues without needing to fine‑tune for every new length.

---

### 3.  When the benefit shows up

The paper’s ablation in Table 5 (see “Extension case 1 – λₛ only”) demonstrates that even with a fixed λ across all dimensions, allowing λ to go beyond s reduces perplexity compared to strict PI. However, the *greatest* improvements are seen when both non‑uniformities are exploited:

1. **Non‑uniform λᵢ across dimensions** – the core idea that different frequencies have different optimal scales.
2. **Token‑position threshold \( \hat{n}\)** – keeping the first few tokens exact (no interpolation) because they dominate attention.

When the target extension is modest (e.g. 2–8×), only the λᵢ non‑uniformity is crucial. As the extension grows (32×, 64×, 512×), the tokenizer‑position threshold starts to matter: the very first few tokens carry most of the semantic load of a long document, and interpolating them would produce a severe loss of positional fidelity.

Thus, the “exceeding s” regime is particularly useful when:

- The extension ratio is large enough that a single global λ = s would push many angles into the wrapped region, causing heavy crowding.
- The model still needs to distinguish close tokens early in the sequence (often true at very long ranges where long‑dependency modeling is critical).

---

### 4.  Intuitive takeaway

Think of the RoPE angles as a stretched rubber band:  
- **Low‑frequency bands** are thick and elastic—they can be pulled further without snapping.  
- **High‑frequency bands** are thin and stiff—they break if stretched too far.

When we extend the context, we’re effectively pulling the band further. Letting λᵢ exceed s corresponds to *treating the thick bands more aggressively*, so they keep their ability to tell apart far‑away tokens, while we keep the delicate bands close to their original shape to preserve fine positional detail.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

Allowing λᵢ to be larger than the raw extension ratio s gives the optimizer room to **balance positional discrimination across frequency bands**. This extra degree of freedom mitigates the “crowding” problem that plagues simple linear interpolation, especially at large extensions, and is essential for LongRoPE to achieve a 512× stretch (4 k → 2048 k) while still keeping short‑context performance nearly intact.
