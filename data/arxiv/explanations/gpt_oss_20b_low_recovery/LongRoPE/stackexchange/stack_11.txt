\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does increasing the starting token threshold τ̂ help at very long extensions but not at 2048k?
Question:
The ablation study indicates that keeping the first tokens uninterpolated benefits performance at 16k and 32k but becomes less effective at 2048k. What is the theoretical explanation for this diminishing return as the extension ratio grows?
Answer:
**Why keeping the first few tokens unchanged gives a big boost at 16 k/32 k but almost nothing at 2048 k**

When we extend the context length from the original 4 k to a longer window, we have to *re‑map* the rotary angles of all new positions back into the range that the model was trained on.  
In the simplest (linear) interpolation we multiply every rotary angle by a single factor \( \lambda = 1/s\) where \(s=L'/L\).  
If we keep the first \(\hat{n}\) positions untouched, we are effectively applying \(\lambda=1\) to the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens and the smaller \(\lambda\) to all later tokens.  
Why does this matter?

| **Effect of keeping the first tokens untouched** | **Magnitude of the effect** | **How it scales with \(s\)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher absolute angles for the very first tokens** | The first tokens usually receive the largest attention scores (they are the left‑most query tokens in every module that looks backward). By leaving their angles unchanged we preserve the *relative angular spread* between the first token and its neighbours, which helps the attention heads to focus on them. | When \(s\) is small (e.g., \(s=4\) or \(8\)) the angular spread is still large enough so that preserving it for the first few tokens noticeably **reduces the “crowding” problem** that linear interpolation causes. |
| **Relative angular resolution in later tokens** | For the rest of the sequence, the angular spread shrinks as we apply a larger \(\lambda\). The larger \(s\) is, the smaller the spacing between consecutive angles becomes. | At \(s=512\) (2048 k), the spacing between every *second* token is already on the order of a few degrees. Even if the first 256 tokens keep their original spacing, this accounts for only \(\frac{256}{2048\cdot 10^3}\approx 0.01\%\) of the sequence. The crowding effect dominates everywhere else, so the marginal benefit of keeping the first tokens is negligible. |
| **Practical optimization landscape** | The evolutionary search for the optimal \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) has to navigate a search space that grows exponentially with \(s\). For very large \(s\), the contribution of a fixed \(\hat{n}\) becomes so small that many candidate solutions with different \(\hat{n}\) values produce almost the same perplexity. Consequently, the search gives little incentive to push \(\hat{n}\) higher. | In the experiments reported in Table 4 of the paper, the optimal \(\hat{n}\) for 16 k is around 128–256, whereas for 2048 k it drops to 0 (i.e., the best solution is simply to interpolate *all* positions). This empirical observation is exactly what the theory predicts. |

**Putting it together**

The key intuition is that keeping the first tokens unchanged is a *local* modification that combats the “crowding” of attention vectors in the *early* part of the sequence. Crowding is the primary reason why pure linear interpolation shows a sharp drop in performance as we increase \(s\). When the extension ratio is modest, a handful of untouched tokens can significantly relieve this crowding. When \(s\) becomes huge (512 × for 2048 k), the crowding is already pervasive; the head attention is spread over a million positions, and the relative advantage of preserving the first 256 angles becomes vanishingly small. In other words, the *fraction* of tokens that benefit from being untouched shrinks to almost zero, so the effect on overall perplexity disappears.

This reasoning is consistent across all the ablation plots in the paper: at 16 k and 32 k the curves for “keep 256 tokens” lie noticeably lower than the pure PI curve, whereas at 2048 k the two curves virtually overlap. The theoretical explanation therefore matches the empirical evidence: the diminishing return is a consequence of the relative magnitude of the “un‑interpolated slice” shrinking as the context window grows.
