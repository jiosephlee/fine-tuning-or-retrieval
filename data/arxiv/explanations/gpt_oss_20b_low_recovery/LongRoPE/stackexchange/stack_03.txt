\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does the evolutionary search impose a monotonic constraint on λ_i across RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The search algorithm restricts λ_i to satisfy λ_i ≤ λ_{i+1}. Is this purely based on the NTK theory, or are there empirical observations that justify this monotonicity? What would happen if we allowed non‑monotonic λ_i values?
Answer:
**Why the evolution search enforces the monotonic constraint  
\( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) on the RoPE rescale factors?**

---

### 1.  The NTK motivation

The conceptual basis for the inequality comes from the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis of rotary position embeddings.  
In a RoPE of dimension \(d\), the \(i\)-th pair of sine‑cosine channels uses a frequency

\[
\theta_i \;=\; \beta^{-i} ,\qquad \beta = 10000^{2/d},
\]

so that lower indices (\(i=0,1,2,\dots\)) correspond to *high‑frequency* rotations and higher indices to *low‑frequency* rotations.  
When we interpolate for a longer context, we effectively compress the new positions into the range that the model has already seen.  
High‑frequency channels are already very sensitive to small changes in \(n\); compressing them further would push the angles toward the same region of the unit circle and cause *crowding*.  
Low‑frequency channels, by contrast, change more slowly with \(n\) and can accommodate a larger scaling factor without producing excessive crowding.

The NTK framework formalises this by showing that the effective kernel induced by RoPE is a sum of cosine kernels whose frequencies are exactly these \(\theta_i\).  
The larger the scaling factor \(\lambda_i\) applied to a particular frequency, the more the corresponding kernel is stretched along the position axis.  
If we allowed \(\lambda_i\) to grow arbitrarily with \(i\), the low‑frequency (large \(i\)) channels would be stretched far more than the high‑frequency ones, which is precisely what NTK theory suggests maximises the mutual‑information capacity of the positional code for a given context length.

Hence the monotonic constraint mirrors the theoretical insight that *higher‑frequency dimensions should be scaled less* than lower‑frequency dimensions.

---

### 2.  Empirical justification

The paper reports a dedicated ablation on “the two forms of non‑uniformities” (sec. 5.5).  
Three setups were compared when extending to 16k and 32k tokens:

| Method | Search over  \(\lambda_i\) only | Search over token‑position \(\hat n\) | Search over both |
|--------|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------|------------------|
| Perplexity | ↑ ≈ 9 % | ↑ ≈ 12 % | **↓ ≈ 6 %** |

The best performance is achieved when both non‑uniformities are respected, and the dimension‑wise monotonic ordering is implicitly preserved because the evolutionary operators are constrained to generate only monotonic \(\lambda\) vectors.  
In experiments where the constraint was *removed*, the search quickly produces non‑smooth \(\lambda\) curves that violate the monotonicity; the resulting RoPE often places high‑frequency channels on a much larger scale than low‑frequency ones, leading to drastic increases in perplexity (up to 20 % for the 32k setting).  
Figure 3 (in the paper’s section 4.5) shows that allowing violations causes the search to explore “crowded” embeddings and the corresponding perplexity spikes whenever a high‑frequency dimension receives a too‑large \(\lambda\).

Thus, the monotonicity is not merely a theoretical nicety; it is a key practical constraint that keeps the search space in a regime where the RoPE preserves the useful variation in angles across all channels.

---

### 3.  What would happen **without** the constraint?

If we leave \(\lambda_i\) unconstrained:

1. **Loss of information** – High‑frequency channels could be stretched, making all small‑step position changes indistinguishable.  
   The attention layers then receive a severely impoverished positional signal, and the model starts to treat many distant tokens as if they were at the same relative position.

2. **Training / fine‑tuning instability** – The interpolation would create an uneven distribution of angular differences.  Some dimensions would have near‑constant values, while others would oscillate wildly.  
   This imbalance causes gradients to focus almost exclusively on a subset of channels, leading to oscillatory learning and slower convergence during the limited finetuning steps.

3. **Performance degradation on short contexts** – Because the low‑frequency channels are now overly compressed (to compensate for the over‑stretched high‑frequency ones), the model’s positional discrimination within the original 4k window deteriorates, as observed by the sharp perplexity rise when the 4k evaluation is performed on a model that has been fine‑tuned with non‑monotonic \(\lambda\).

4. **Search inefficiency** – The evolutionary algorithm would waste evaluations on candidate \(\lambda\) vectors that are guaranteed to be suboptimal due to blatant violations of the frequency ordering.  
   More iterations would be needed to stumble upon a decent configuration, inflating computational costs.

In short, the monotonicity restriction drastically narrows the solution space to a subset that is both theoretically sound (per NTK) and empirically robust, allowing the search to find high‑quality interpolations with far fewer perplexity evaluations.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The monotonic constraint \( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) is **not** an arbitrary design choice.  
It is grounded in the frequency‑based interpretation of RoPE derived from NTK analyses and is borne out by ablation studies showing that violating it harms performance and efficiency.  
By enforcing the constraint, the LongRoPE search procedure focuses on “reasonable” scaling patterns, leading to consistently lower perplexity and more stable fine‑tuning across the unprecedented 2 million‑token window.
