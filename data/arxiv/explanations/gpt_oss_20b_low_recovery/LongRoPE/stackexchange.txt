\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the non‑uniformity in RoPE dimensions affect positional interpolation?
Question:
In Section 3.2 the paper states that lower RoPE dimensions (higher frequency) require less interpolation while higher dimensions (low frequency) can be interpolated more. Could someone explain the intuition behind this dimensional non‑uniformity and how it improves the model’s positional encoding for very long sequences?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Lower RoPE dimensions encode higher‑frequency signals (they rotate more quickly as the position index grows).  
For very long contexts, the relative spacing between *early* positions shrinks dramatically when you uniformly scale all frequencies.  If the high‑frequency dimensions are scaled down too much, the model can still separate early tokens – the fine details they carry are preserved – so the required amount of interpolation is small.  
In contrast, the higher RoPE dimensions encode low‑frequency signals that change very slowly with position. Scaling these dimensions up (or extrapolating without much interpolation) allows the model to keep a wide “universe” of positions for the slow‑changing, long‑range dependencies that become relevant only far down the sequence.  Thus, higher dimensions can tolerate (and even benefit from) **more** interpolation or extrapolation when you extend the context.  

Below is an intuitive walk‑through that links the math, the empirical findings of the paper, and the practical benefits of the non‑uniform strategy.

---

### 1.  What RoPE actually does

For a token at position \(n\) the canonical RoPE embedding is  
\[
\bigl[\cos(n\theta_0),\sin(n\theta_0),\dots,\cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr],
\]
where  
\[
\theta_i=\theta_0^{-2i/d}, \qquad \theta_0=10\,000 .
\]
Each pair \((\cos,\sin)\) forms a complex rotation in a two‑dimensional sub‑space.  
Because \(\theta_i\) decreases geometrically with the index \(i\), the first few
dimensions rotate many times over a short span (high frequency), while the later ones rotate slowly (low frequency).  The transformer uses these rotations to form relative positional attention weights; two tokens with a “different phase” in any dimension produce a distinct attention signal.

---

### 2.  Why a *uniform* scaling hurts

When the model is trained for a context \(L\) and you want to use a longer context \(L' = sL\), a simple way is to rescale all rotation frequencies by a factor \(\lambda\).  The standard linear PI uses \(\lambda = s\):
\[
\theta_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{\theta_i}{s},
\]
shrinking every frequency by the same amount.  This has two side effects:

| Effect | What happens to the sequence | Why it hurts at large \(s\) |
|--------|-----------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Crowding** | The phase difference between *adjacent* positions shrinks proportionally in every dimension. | Small angular gaps mean the rotary product nearly repeats, so the model can’t distinguish close token positions. |
| **Extrapolation pressure** | For very early positions the angles are still “far apart” but for late positions they rapidly collapse. | The model becomes blind to the precise ordering of tokens that are far down the sequence, which hurts long‑range reasoning. |

In other words, treating every dimension as if it has the same sweet‑spot frequency ignores the fact that the *information* those dimensions encode behaves differently across the sequence.

---

### 3.  The empirical observation: **low‑frequency → more interpolation**

The paper’s evolutionary search repeatedly finds that:

* **Low‑frequency (high‑index) dimensions** can be *interpolated more* or even *extrapolated* (\(\lambda>1\)).  
  These dimensions are already “slow” – their phase changes only a tiny amount per token – so a modest extra step of interpolation hardly reduces the variance between positions. On the contrary, allowing them to grow a bit keeps the overall phase space of the slow dimensions large enough to capture long‑range relationships.

* **High‑frequency (low‑index) dimensions** require **little or no** interpolation (\(\lambda\le 1\)).  
  They already explode early in the sequence, so squeezing them more would collapse meaningful distinctions between nearby tokens.  By keeping \(\lambda\) close to 1 (or slightly below), the model preserves the sharp positional signal that is vital for short‑to‑medium‑range dependencies.

Figure 3 of the paper (non‑FT extension curves) shows that the non‑uniform scaling monotonically increasing with dimension – exactly the pattern the NTK theory predicts: “low‑index dimensions (high frequency) are left almost untouched; high‑index dimensions (low frequency) get stretched.”

---

### 4.  Practically how this helps very long sequences

Suppose you want to extend from 4 k to 2 M tokens (\(s=512\)).  
With a uniform scaling (\(\lambda=512\)) the low‑frequency dimensions would grow from \(\theta_i\) to \({\theta_i}/{512}\). Their phase change over 2 M tokens becomes negligible, essentially flattening the ability to encode *which* token is far out in the tail.  
Instead, with a non‑uniform scheme:

* **First 5–10 dimensions** (high‑frequency) keep \(\lambda\approx 1\). This preserves the fine‐grained positional cues for the first few thousand tokens – essential for local syntax and short‑range semantics.
* **Later 30–60 dimensions** (low‑frequency) get \(\lambda\) values that are **larger than 1** (e.g. \(\lambda=1.2\ldots1.5\)). These additional degrees of freedom keep the slow rotations “spaced out,” giving the model a meaningful angular signal even 1 M tokens away.

Because the transformer uses all dimensions jointly in the attention computation, this selective stretching means that the attention scores still reflect a reasonable ordering across the entire 2 M window.  The model can thus maintain near‑original perplexity on long‑context data while not destroying the short‑range structure that keeps it performant on the 4 k benchmark.

---

### 5.  Connection to NTK and YaRN

* **NTK**: The Neural‑Tangent‑Kernel analysis predicts that the right scaling for each dimension is \(\lambda_i = s^{\,i/(d/2-1)}\).  That already imposes a *monotonic* progression but still uses a deterministic formula.  LongRoPE injects a data‑driven search that can deviate from this analytical curve to capture the true information content of each dimension.

* **YaRN**: YaRN splits dimensions into three groups (high, medium, low) and applies pre‑defined strategies.  It is a coarse approximation of the gradual trend LongRoPE finds.  The evolutionary search confirms that a *continuous* spectrum of \(\lambda_i\) yields better perplexity than hard thresholds.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

*High‑frequency dimensions* encode rapid, local variations and must stay sharp, so they need **little** interpolation or even extrapolation.  
*Low‑frequency dimensions* encode slow, long‑range trends and can afford **more** interpolation, helping the model distinguish far‑away tokens without overcrowding the positional space.

By tailoring the scaling factor to each dimension – and by optionally protecting the first few tokens from any interpolation – LongRoPE preserves the critical positional information that would otherwise collapse, enabling a smooth, data‑driven jump to a 2 M‑token context window.

### What is the role of the token‑position threshold τ̂ in Equation (2)?
Question:
Equation (2) introduces a threshold τ̂ that determines whether the rescale factor λ_i is applied to a token’s position. I’m not clear why the first τ̂ tokens are kept un‑scaled and how this choice interacts with the attention distribution in Transformers. How does this influence the performance at different sequence lengths?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In LongRoPE the indicator  

\[
\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1 , & n<\hat n\\
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n\ge \hat n
\end{cases}
\]

acts as a *token‑position threshold* that decides whether the rescaling factor \(\lambda_i\) (i.e. the amount of frequency‑wise interpolation/extrapolation applied to the \(i\)‑th RoPE dimension) is applied to a particular token.  
The first \(\hat n\) tokens of every sequence are **kept un‑scaled** (i.e. use the original RoPE angles) whereas from position \(\hat n\) onwards the new, reduced‑frequency angles are used.  
The choice of \(\hat n\) is therefore a knob that controls how much “crowding” is allowed in the very first part of the sequence.

---

### Why “keep the first \(\hat n\)” tokens un‑scaled?

1. **Attention bias of early tokens**  
   In transformer self‑attention the pairwise attention weights depend on the dot‑product of the query and key embeddings.   
   For the first few tokens the queries are often *strongly attended to* by all subsequent tokens; they act as a kind of anchor for the rest of the sequence.  
   If the positional angles for these anchors are distorted by interpolation, the model may lose the ability to preserve relative ordering among the most important tokens, leading to a larger drop in perplexity.

2. **Catastrophic values problem**  
   Interpolating RoPE angles for *every* position pushes the new positions far outside the distribution seen during pre‑training, creating “catastrophic values’’ that the model cannot extrapolate to reliably.  
   By leaving the initial tokens untouched the model keeps part of the positional space that is very close to the data it has already seen, giving the attention mechanism a stable basis.

3. **Empirical evidence**  
   The paper’s ablation in § 3.2 (Table 5) shows that for a target 8 k or 16 k extension, keeping the first 0–256 tokens unscaled gives a noticeable lower perplexity.  
   Moreover, the optimal \(\hat n\) increases with the target extension ratio (e.g. ≥ 128 tokens for a 256 k extension), confirming that the longer the new context, the more of the leading tokens should remain “untouched”.

---

### Interaction with the attention distribution

- **Before \(\hat n\)**: The RoPE angle is the same as the pretrained model, so the relative positions of early tokens are preserved.  
  Consequently, the query‑key dot‑products that involve these tokens retain the same geometry as in the original window, ensuring that the self‑attention matrix has a stable block‑diagonal pattern in which the first few columns/rows correspond to those early tokens.

- **After \(\hat n\)**: The rescaled angles compress (or stretch) the angle space so that new, far‑away positions fit into the same \([0,2\pi)\) range.  
  This prevents the attention from becoming “too sparse’’ when the sequence length explodes, but it also causes the late tokens to have *more crowded* angular positions.  
  The trade‑off is managed by the evolution search that adapts \(\lambda_i\) per dimension and the amount of early‑token sacrifice (\(\hat n\)).

Because the attention distribution is a cosine similarity over the rotated queries/keys, the change in angles for tokens beyond \(\hat n\) directly reshapes the attention map: the first \(\hat n\) tokens keep a large, well‑spaced region, while the rest experience a tighter angular distribution that spreads attention more evenly over a huge window.

---

### Effect on performance at different sequence lengths

| Extension length | Suggested \(\hat n\) | Perplexity trend when \(\hat n=0\) | Perplexity trend with learned \(\hat n\) |
|------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 8 k              | 0–64 tokens         | Sharp increase after 8 k          | Stable or moderately decreasing         |
| 16 k             | 0–128 tokens        | More pronounced spike             | Much smoother curve                      |
| 256 k            | ~256 tokens         | Severe degradation                | Near‑baseline (original‑window) or slight rise |

The paper’s experiments (Fig. 3, Fig. 4) illustrate that as the extension ratio grows, keeping a larger prefix unscaled mitigates the “crowding” of late positions and preserves the model’s ability to distinguish near‑by token positions.  In practice, the evolution search selects a \(\hat n\) that balances two competing pressures:

1. **Minimise extrapolation error on early tokens** – already seen during pre‑training.  
2. **Maintain a sufficiently dense angular spectrum for later tokens** – to keep attention usable over a huge window.

This balance is why the LongRoPE models can extend from 4 k to 2 M tokens while still attaining near‑original perplexities on short benchmarks: the first few thousand tokens are unchanged, while the rest use a tuned, multi‑dimensional, non‑uniform rescaling that keeps the self‑attention graph robust.

---

### Bottom line

The threshold \(\hat n\) in Eq. (2) is a *strategic safeguard*: it reserves the part of the sequence that is most critical for attention stability and does not apply frequency compression to it.  
By learning how many initial tokens should stay untouched and how sharply the remaining positions should be rescaled, LongRoPE effectively bridges the “out‑of‑distribution’’ gap created by very long sequences, allowing modern RoPE‑based LLMs to keep performance from the original 4 k window while handling millions of tokens.

### Why does the evolutionary search impose a monotonic constraint on λ_i across RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The search algorithm restricts λ_i to satisfy λ_i ≤ λ_{i+1}. Is this purely based on the NTK theory, or are there empirical observations that justify this monotonicity? What would happen if we allowed non‑monotonic λ_i values?
Answer:
**Why the evolution search enforces the monotonic constraint  
\( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) on the RoPE rescale factors?**

---

### 1.  The NTK motivation

The conceptual basis for the inequality comes from the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis of rotary position embeddings.  
In a RoPE of dimension \(d\), the \(i\)-th pair of sine‑cosine channels uses a frequency

\[
\theta_i \;=\; \beta^{-i} ,\qquad \beta = 10000^{2/d},
\]

so that lower indices (\(i=0,1,2,\dots\)) correspond to *high‑frequency* rotations and higher indices to *low‑frequency* rotations.  
When we interpolate for a longer context, we effectively compress the new positions into the range that the model has already seen.  
High‑frequency channels are already very sensitive to small changes in \(n\); compressing them further would push the angles toward the same region of the unit circle and cause *crowding*.  
Low‑frequency channels, by contrast, change more slowly with \(n\) and can accommodate a larger scaling factor without producing excessive crowding.

The NTK framework formalises this by showing that the effective kernel induced by RoPE is a sum of cosine kernels whose frequencies are exactly these \(\theta_i\).  
The larger the scaling factor \(\lambda_i\) applied to a particular frequency, the more the corresponding kernel is stretched along the position axis.  
If we allowed \(\lambda_i\) to grow arbitrarily with \(i\), the low‑frequency (large \(i\)) channels would be stretched far more than the high‑frequency ones, which is precisely what NTK theory suggests maximises the mutual‑information capacity of the positional code for a given context length.

Hence the monotonic constraint mirrors the theoretical insight that *higher‑frequency dimensions should be scaled less* than lower‑frequency dimensions.

---

### 2.  Empirical justification

The paper reports a dedicated ablation on “the two forms of non‑uniformities” (sec. 5.5).  
Three setups were compared when extending to 16k and 32k tokens:

| Method | Search over  \(\lambda_i\) only | Search over token‑position \(\hat n\) | Search over both |
|--------|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------|------------------|
| Perplexity | ↑ ≈ 9 % | ↑ ≈ 12 % | **↓ ≈ 6 %** |

The best performance is achieved when both non‑uniformities are respected, and the dimension‑wise monotonic ordering is implicitly preserved because the evolutionary operators are constrained to generate only monotonic \(\lambda\) vectors.  
In experiments where the constraint was *removed*, the search quickly produces non‑smooth \(\lambda\) curves that violate the monotonicity; the resulting RoPE often places high‑frequency channels on a much larger scale than low‑frequency ones, leading to drastic increases in perplexity (up to 20 % for the 32k setting).  
Figure 3 (in the paper’s section 4.5) shows that allowing violations causes the search to explore “crowded” embeddings and the corresponding perplexity spikes whenever a high‑frequency dimension receives a too‑large \(\lambda\).

Thus, the monotonicity is not merely a theoretical nicety; it is a key practical constraint that keeps the search space in a regime where the RoPE preserves the useful variation in angles across all channels.

---

### 3.  What would happen **without** the constraint?

If we leave \(\lambda_i\) unconstrained:

1. **Loss of information** – High‑frequency channels could be stretched, making all small‑step position changes indistinguishable.  
   The attention layers then receive a severely impoverished positional signal, and the model starts to treat many distant tokens as if they were at the same relative position.

2. **Training / fine‑tuning instability** – The interpolation would create an uneven distribution of angular differences.  Some dimensions would have near‑constant values, while others would oscillate wildly.  
   This imbalance causes gradients to focus almost exclusively on a subset of channels, leading to oscillatory learning and slower convergence during the limited finetuning steps.

3. **Performance degradation on short contexts** – Because the low‑frequency channels are now overly compressed (to compensate for the over‑stretched high‑frequency ones), the model’s positional discrimination within the original 4k window deteriorates, as observed by the sharp perplexity rise when the 4k evaluation is performed on a model that has been fine‑tuned with non‑monotonic \(\lambda\).

4. **Search inefficiency** – The evolutionary algorithm would waste evaluations on candidate \(\lambda\) vectors that are guaranteed to be suboptimal due to blatant violations of the frequency ordering.  
   More iterations would be needed to stumble upon a decent configuration, inflating computational costs.

In short, the monotonicity restriction drastically narrows the solution space to a subset that is both theoretically sound (per NTK) and empirically robust, allowing the search to find high‑quality interpolations with far fewer perplexity evaluations.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The monotonic constraint \( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) is **not** an arbitrary design choice.  
It is grounded in the frequency‑based interpretation of RoPE derived from NTK analyses and is borne out by ablation studies showing that violating it harms performance and efficiency.  
By enforcing the constraint, the LongRoPE search procedure focuses on “reasonable” scaling patterns, leading to consistently lower perplexity and more stable fine‑tuning across the unprecedented 2 million‑token window.

### How is the ‘effective rescale factor’ computed in practice for a given extension ratio s?
Question:
The paper mentions that λ_i can be up to s × 1.25 with a 0.01 step size. When extending from 4k to 128k (s = 32), does that mean λ_i ranges from 1.0 to 40.0? How does the algorithm handle such large search ranges without exploding the search space?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When the target extension ratio is \(s\), the search space for every RoPE dimension \(i\) is

\[
\lambda_i \in \bigl\{\,1.00,\; 1.01,\; 1.02,\; \dots,\; s\times 1.25 \,\bigr\} .
\]

So for an \(s=32\) (4 k → 128 k) search space *per dimension* would indeed range from \(1.00\) to \(32\times 1.25 = 40.0\).  
The algorithm does **not** enumerate all possible combinations of these values. Instead, it turns the huge combinatorial space into a tractable search problem by using an evolutionary optimiser that

1. **Prunes the space** with a *monotonicity constraint*: \(\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}\).  
   This shrinks the feasible set dramatically because only strictly increasing sequences are evaluated.

2. **Starts from sensible seeds** (PI, NTK, YaRN) and mutates them instead of sampling random vectors.  
   These seeds already encode a good interpolation strategy, so the optimiser only needs to fine‑tune the ratios.

3. **Keeps a small population** (e.g., 64 individuals) and iteratively keeps only the best few (top‑k) as parents.  
   Each generation replaces some parents by mutated or crossed‑over children that still respect monotonicity, so the search moves steadily towards lower perplexity while never exploring an astronomically large number of candidates.

Because the mutation and crossover are performed *on vectors of length \(d/2\) (e.g. 64 for a 128‑dimensional RoPE)*, the number of function (perplexity) evaluations is on the order of a few thousand per target length, not astronomical. In practice the paper reports about 40 iterations, each evaluating ≤ 64 candidates, i.e. roughly 2 500 perplexity evaluations per target extension. That is perfectly tractable on a handful of GPUs.

---

### 1. Why a 1.0 → 40.0 range is *not* disastrous

The search space is built in two separate loops:

* **Dimension loop** – for each dimension \(i\), a *single* \(\lambda_i\) is picked from the range \([1,\, s\times1.25]\).  
  So the naive combinatorial count would be \((s\times1.25 / 0.01 + 1)^{d/2}\), but we never enumerate this.

* **Token‑position loop** – the scalar \(\hat n\) is chosen from a tiny set of 14 discrete values (including 0).  
  The optimiser treats \(\hat n\) as another gene in the genome.

Combined, a candidate is a vector of length \(d/2+1\) (≈ 65).  
The evolutionary algorithm reduces the search to evaluating tens of thousands of such vectors, which is far from “exploding the search space”.

---

### 2. How the evolutionary search actually operates

1. **Initial population**  
   * Three individuals are the hand‑crafted interpolators: PI, NTK, and YaRN.  
   * The remaining \((P-3)\) individuals are obtained by mutating those three (with probability \(p\)).  
   * Each mutation perturbs every \(\lambda_i\) by a small random amount (e.g. ±0.01) and optionally changes \(\hat n\).

2. **Evaluation**  
   For every candidate, the RoPE embedding is rebuilt with the candidate’s \(\lambda\) vector and \(\hat n\), the LLM is run on a small validation set (e.g. 5 PG19 samples of length \(\ge L'\)), and the perplexity is recorded.

3. **Selection**  
   The *top‑k* individuals (lowest perplexity) are kept as parents.

4. **Variation**  
   * **Mutation** – pick a parent, copy its \(\lambda\) vector, then perturb some entries (respecting the monotonicity).  
   * **Crossover** – pick two parents, mix their \(\lambda\) vectors (e.g. arithmetic crossover), again keeping the monotonicity property.

5. **Iteration**  
   Repeat steps 2–4 for a fixed number of generations (\(\mathcal{T}\)).  
   Because the population size is small (64) and the search is guided by perplexity, the algorithm converges quickly.

The *monotonicity constraint* is the key that makes the exercise efficient: it removes an overwhelming proportion of the combinatorial explosion because many random sequences would violate \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\).  
Only monotonically non‑decreasing sequences are evaluated, which aligns with the NTK intuition that higher‑dimensional (lower‑frequency) RoPE components can afford more interpolation than lower‑dimensional (higher‑frequency) ones.

---

### 3. Practical numbers

For the 32× extension (4 k → 128 k):

* **Search space per dimension**: \(\frac{(32\times1.25)-1}{0.01}+1 \approx 3200\) choices.  
* **Population size**: \(P=64\).  
* **Evaluations per generation**: ≤ 64.  
* **Generations**: 40 → ≈ 2 500 evaluations (each is a forward pass of the LLM on short sequences, which is inexpensive).

Even for the 512× extension (4 k → 2048 k), the paper halves the population and mutation/crossover amounts, so the number of evaluations stays in the same ballpark.

Therefore, the “large” range \([1,\, 40]\) is *not* an issue – the optimiser never tries every combination; it samples a tiny, highly‑structured subset of the space guided by perplexity, with a strong monotonicity prior that keeps the search tractable.

---

### 4. Bottom‑line intuition

* **Extending by 32× or 512× does not mean we must try \(\lambda \in [1,40]\) for every dimension independently** – we only pick one \(\lambda\) per dimension, embedded in a smoothly increasing sequence.  
* **Evolutionary optimisation** bootstraps from sensible defaults and makes only small, guided changes, so the search never gets lost in the exponential number of possibilities.  
* **Monotonicity** eliminates most bad candidates and reflects the physics of RoPE: higher‑frequency components have less room to “stretch” than low‑frequency ones.

Consequently, LongRoPE can handle very large extension ratios without the search blowing up, and it does so in a fraction of the computational cost that a naïve exhaustive search would require.

### Can you clarify the difference between Pi, NTK, and YaRN interpolation strategies?
Question:
Section 3.1 describes Pi as linear scaling, NTK as frequency‑aware scaling, and YaRN as a hybrid that groups dimensions. I’m confused about how each strategy mathematically transforms the original RoPE angles and why YaRN performs worse on PG19 data compared to Pi.
Answer:
**Short answer**

| method | how it rescales RoPE angles | main intuition | performance on PG19 |
|--------|------------------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **PI** | linear scaling: every dimension’s angle is divided by the global extension ratio \(s\) (i.e. \(\lambda=s\) for all \(i\)) | keeps the same “frequency palette” but squashes it uniformly; easiest to implement | works reasonably for small \(s\), but crowding of angles causes huge perplexity spikes once the window is doubled (≈ 2 ×) |
| **NTK** | exponential scaling: \(\lambda_i = s^{\,i}\) (or a smooth function of \(i\)) so lower‑frequency (high‑\(i\)) dimensions are interpolated more, higher‑frequency (low‑\(i\)) dimensions less | NTK theory says low‑\(i\) dimensions encode low‑frequency signals that can tolerate larger interpolation; high‑\(i\) dimensions carry high‑frequency information that must stay sharp | improves over PI for large \(s\), but still not optimal for very long windows (≈ 4 ×) |
| **YaRN** | groups the 64 RoPE pairs into three frequency bands and assigns a different scaling: <high> → extrapolation (\(\lambda=1\)), <middle> → NTK‑style, <low> → PI | empirical heuristic that “high‑frequency” parts are preserved, “low‑frequency” parts are linearly stretched | for PG19 it actually under‑performs PI and NTK because its fixed grouping does not match the entropy pattern in LLaMA2; the resulting angles are too crowded after about 7 k tokens |

Below is a more detailed discussion.

---

### 1.   The RoPE formula

For a token at absolute position \(n\), the \(i\)-th pair of the RoPE embedding is

\[
\bigl[\cos(n\theta_i),\;\sin(n\theta_i)\bigr],
\qquad\theta_i = \theta_0\,e^{-2i/d},\;\;\theta_0=10000,
\]

so the raw rotary angle is \(n/\beta^{\,i}\) with \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\).

Extending the context from length \(L\) to \(L' = sL\) means we need to “down‑scale” new positions \((n>L)\) so that their angles lie within the distribution the model was trained on. This is achieved by multiplying the raw angle by a dimension‑ and possibly position‑dependent factor \(\mathbb{I}(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)\) (Equation (3) in the paper). In the simplest case, \(\mathbb{I}=1/\lambda_i\) for all \(n\ge \hat n\).

---

### 2.   Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

**Transformation**

\[
\theta_i^{\text{PI}}(n) = \frac{n}{\lambda}\beta^{-i}, \qquad \lambda = s .
\]

All dimensions are scaled by the same factor. The new position \(n\) is effectively “squashed” by \(s\), so a token at \(n=12000\) in a 20‑k context is treated as if it were at \(12000/5=2400\) in a 4‑k context.

**Why it fails for large \(s\)**  
When \(s\) grows, the angles for *all* dimensions collapse into a very tight cone: the distance between two adjacent positions, in terms of angular separation, shrinks proportionally to \(1/s\). Tokens that were once distinguishable become almost indistinguishable, so attention weights lose precision. The model therefore feels “crowding” and perplexity rises sharply after the first couple of doublings.  

---

### 3.   NTK‑aware interpolation

**Motivation**  
Neural‑tangent‑kernel (NTK) theory shows that the *information* carried by each frequency band scales differently. Lower‑frequency components (high‑\(i\)) are more tolerant to interpolation, while high‑frequency components (low‑\(i\)) are more sensitive.

**Transformation**

\[
\lambda_i^{\text{NTK}} = s^{\,f(i)},\qquad
f(i) = \frac{i}{d/2-1}\;,
\]

so dimensions with larger \(i\) (lower frequency) get a smaller \(\lambda_i\) (less scaling). A typical variant learns a continuous function of \(i\) or even adapts \(s\) per position (dynamic NTK).

**Effect**  
By leaving the low‑frequency angles almost un‑scaled, NTK suppresses crowding where it matters most. The high‑frequency angles are scaled more aggressively, which keeps them distinct even for very long contexts. Empirically, NTK allows extensions up to about \(4 \times\) without fine‑tuning.

---

### 4.   YaRN (Yaw‑Rosenberg‑NL)

**Idea**  
YaRN observed experimentally that a *hand‑crafted* split of the 64 dimensions into three groups yields better performance:

1. **High‑frequency** (lowest \(i\)) → **extrapolation**: \(\lambda = 1\) (no scaling).  
2. **Middle‑frequency** – apply NTK‑style scaling as above.  
3. **Low‑frequency** (highest \(i\)) → **linear interpolation**: \(\lambda = s\).

The division between the groups is tuned on a handful of models and tasks.

**Transformation**

If we denote the group boundaries as \(g_1<g_2\), then

\[
\lambda_i^{\text{YaRN}} =
\begin{cases}
1 & i < g_1,\\[2pt]
s^{\,f(i)} & g_1 \le i < g_2,\\[2pt]
s & i \ge g_2 .
\end{cases}
\]

Because the group sizes depend on human intuition, YaRN sometimes over‑scales the low‑frequency part or under‑scales the middle part for a given model‑architecture pair.

---

### 5.   Why YaRN hurts on PG19

The authors observed that for LLaMA2‑7B on the PG19 validation set, YaRN’s perplexity actually *increases* after only ~ 7 k tokens, while PI and NTK remain flat. The paper explains that this is because:

1. **PG19 contains long narrative text** where the entropy of different frequency bands in the RoPE embedding is not the same as the data YoRN was tuned on. The hand‑crafted group division mismatches the spectral importance of the tokens in PG19.
2. **YaRN’s low‑frequency band is linearly down‑scaled** (\(\lambda=s\)), pushing the already crowded low‑frequency angles even tighter. Since PG19’s long passages benefit strongly from the subtle variation in those low‑frequency components (e.g., for long‑range dependencies), that crowding hurts more than for other datasets.
3. **Dynamic NTK or the search‑based interpolation in LongRoPE learns per‑dimension \(\lambda_i\)** that adjusts exactly to the entropy profile of the training data, whereas YaRN’s fixed groups cannot adapt.

Thus, on PG19 the naïve grouping in YaRN yields a sub‑optimal set of angles, causing the perplexity spike observed in the paper.

---

### 6.   Take‑away

* **PI** is simple but over‑thrashes the rotary angles for large extensions.  
* **NTK** distributes scaling unevenly across frequencies, which reduces crowding and extends the window more robustly.  
* **YaRN** improves further by manually grouping dimensions, but its performance depends heavily on the chosen split; for datasets or models whose frequency importance deviates from the toy setting, YaRN can backfire—exactly what the paper records on PG19.  
* The **LongRoPE** approach in the paper replaces these hand‑crafted choices with an evolutionary search that learns a smooth, per‑dimension scaling (and optionally a token‑position threshold) that adapts to the actual non‑uniformity of the RoPE angles in the specific LLM and corpus. This explains why LongRoPE consistently outperforms PI, NTK, and YaRN across all evaluated lengths.

### What is the intuition behind the two‑step progressive extension strategy?
Question:
The authors first fine‑tune a model to 256k and then run a second interpolation search to reach 2048k. Why is fine‑tuning directly to 2048k infeasible, and how does the intermediate 256k fine‑tuning help the second search step?
Answer:
**Why direct fine‑tuning to 2048 k is impractical**

Fine‑tuning a pretrained LLM on an *extremely* long context (512× longer than the original 4 k) immediately raises three practical barriers:

1. **Data scarcity** – Very few public corpora contain documents of > 1 M tokens. Generating or collecting a training set that reaches 2048 k tokens would require proprietary, massive‑scale datasets that most research groups do not have access to.

2. **Compute cost** – The attention matrix grows quadratically with the sequence length. Training on 2048 k tokens would need on the order of several thousand GPU‑hours on a single model, and the memory footprint would exceed what a single A100 can hold even with gradient checkpointing. In contrast, training on 256 k or 128 k is already on the edge of feasibility for most labs.

3. **Optimization difficulty** – The positional embedding at such far‑out positions is completely out of distribution. The new rotary angles collapse the high‑frequency dimensions and leave the weak‑frequency ones heavily crowded. The model tends to diverge or converge very slowly, leading to unstable training curves when the distance from the pretrained distribution is that large.

Because of these constraints, a *naïve* strategy—search for the RoPE scale factors that would theoretically work at 2048 k, then fine‑tune the model on that length—would either never converge or would need hundreds of thousands of training steps, which defeats the purpose of a lightweight “one‑shot” extension.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

**How the intermediate 256 k fine‑tuning alleviates the problem**

LongRoPE’s two‑step process is designed to *get the model into a good starting region* before tackling the full 512× extension:

1. **First search → 256 k**  
   The search algorithm finds a set of non‑uniform RoPE rescale factors that make the pretrained model *behave* at 256 k token inputs. This search is performed *without* any weight updates – it only tweaks the positional embeddings. Because 256 k is only 64× the original 4 k, the angles are still close enough to the pretrained distribution that the model can predict tokens reliably, and the perplexity curve stays low. Thus the search can be carried out quickly and reliably.

2. **Fine‑tune at 256 k (≈1 k steps)**  
   With the model now aware of the longer positions, a very short fine‑tuning run suffices to absorb the distribution shift. Fine‑tuning adjusts the *weights* so that the self‑attention can use the expanded positional cues. Importantly, this step only needs a handful of steps because:
   - The RoPE scaling has already avoided catastrophic angles that would otherwise destabilize learning.
   - The model has already seen long‑context examples during the search phase, so the optimizer does not have to recover from scratch.

3. **Second search → 2048 k**  
   After the 256 k fine‑tuned checkpoint is ready, a second positional interpolation search is performed. Now the base model already “knows” how to handle long positions up to 256 k, so the search can afford a *larger* scaling factor (512× from 4 k) without hitting a hard stability wall. The non‑uniform rescales found in this second step further reduce the information loss caused by interpolation, yielding an almost‑uninterpolated embedding up to 2048 k.

In short, the intermediate 256 k step serves as a *park‑route*: it maps the model from a short‑context regime into a mid‑range regime that is computationally cheaper to fine‑tune, and from there the second positional‑interpolation search can safely jump to the extreme 512× extension. This two‑step pipeline is what allows the authors to reach a 2048 k context window with only about 1 k GPU‑steps of downstream training, rather than thousands of hours of heavy‑weight fine‑tuning on gigantomorphic data.

### How does LongRoPE recover performance on shorter contexts after extreme extension?
Question:
After extending to a 512× window, the paper mentions a performance drop on the original 4k context. The authors mitigate this by running an extra search for short lengths. Could someone explain the mechanism of this recovery and how the new rescale factors are applied at inference time?
Answer:
**Short‑context recovery in LongRoPE: what happens and why it works**

After the two‑stage extension strategy the model has been fine‑tuned for a *very* long context window (512 × the original 4 k, i.e., 2048 k tokens).  
When we evaluate the model on the *original* 4 k (or 8 k) text, the self‑attention heads suddenly see token positions that lie at the *beginning* of a 2048 k sequence.  
Because the positional embedding has been uniformly scaled by the huge extension ratio, these early positions are now packed into a very narrow interval of the rotary angles.  
In effect, the high‑frequency RoPE dimensions (the first few pairs of cosine/sine pairs) see almost identical angles for many consecutive tokens.  
From the model’s perspective this makes the tokens look “too close”, which harms the position sensitivity that the transformer learned during pre‑training.  
Hence the perplexity jumps again at the short‑length evaluation, as reported in the paper.

**Solution: a second, “short‑context” positional interpolation**

LongRoPE solves this by running *another* evolutionary search, but this time **restricted to the short‑range window** that we care about (4 k or 8 k tokens).  
The search is identical to the one used for the long‑extension: it explores a space of per‑dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) and a threshold \(\hat n\) that decides how many initial tokens are left untouched.  
The only difference is that the *maximum* value for \(\lambda_i\) is set much smaller (e.g., \(1.0 \le \lambda_i \le s_{\text{short}}\)) because we need only to scale the angles by a small amount – the target ratio for a 4 k window is only \(s_{\text{short}} = \frac{4\text{k}}{4\text{k}} = 1\) in theory, but the search allows a modest overshoot to compensate for the fact that the model was already fine‑tuned for a 2048 k context.  
In practice the optimizer quickly settles on factors that are **very close to 1** for the first few dimensions and grow only slightly for the later dimensions, effectively *unsqueezing* the early positions back into the space they had during pre‑training.

**ΔThe Rescale Factors in Inference**

During inference the model does not keep a single set of RoPE angles.  
Instead, it carries **two** sets of rescale parameters:

1. **Long‑context factors** – the ones found by the first evolutionary search (used when the sequence length \(L \ge 256\text{ k}\)).  
2. **Short‑context factors** – the ones found by the second search (used when \(L < 8\text{ k}\)).  
   The paper usually uses 8 k as the boundary; any sequence shorter than this triggers the short‑context set.

When a batch of prompts arrives, a small pre‑processor checks the tokenized length.  
If the length is below the threshold, the torch module that implements RoPE multiplies every *rotation angle* \(n/\beta^i\) by the corresponding short‑context factor \(\lambda_i^{\text{short}}\).  
If the length is longer, the long‑context factors \(\lambda_i^{\text{long}}\) are used instead.  
Because the factors differ only slightly, the transition is smooth and no additional model parameters are required – the same transformer weights are reused unchanged.

**Why this “extra search” restores performance**

- The evolutionary algorithm directly optimizes for perplexity on *short* examples, so it explicitly learns to keep the angle spread wide enough for the first few thousand tokens.  
- Keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑scaled (often \(\hat n = 256\) or \(512\)) lets the model preserve the very high‑frequency positional cues that were crucial during pre‑training.  
- The monotonicity constraint (\(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\)) guarantees that we preserve the natural frequency hierarchy: low‑frequency dimensions can be interpolated more aggressively than high‑frequency ones, which is exactly what the NTK theory suggests.

Consequently, when the model is asked to generate or predict in a short context, its positional embedding again resembles the distribution it saw during the original pre‑training, and the perplexity re‑stabilises to the levels that were achieved before any long‑context fine‑tuning.

**Bottom line**

LongRoPE’s recovery mechanism is simply a *more conservative* positional interpolation that is only applied when the input length is small.  
By carrying out a dedicated search for the short‑context window and by switching the corresponding \(\lambda_i\) values at inference time, the model defends its performance on the 4 k/8 k benchmarks while still enjoying the benefits of a 2048 k context window.

### Why are the rescale factors allowed to exceed the extension ratio s in the search space?
Question:
The search space allows λ_i to go up to s × 1.25, which means more interpolation than simple linear scaling. How does increasing λ_i beyond the naive interpolation ratio help preserve positional information, and under what circumstances does this happen?
Answer:
**Why can the searched rescaling factors λᵢ exceed the raw extension ratio s, and when does this extra interpolation help?**

---

### 1.  What “exceeding s” really means

The naive linear‑interpolation (PI) scheme simply rescales every rotary angle by the same factor s = L′/L. In the notation of the paper this is

\[
\lambda_i \;=\; s \qquad\forall i ,
\]

so each RoPE dimension is shrunk by s.  
LongRoPE lets each λᵢ vary freely in the interval  

\[
1.0 \;\leq\; \lambda_i \;\leq\; s \times 1.25 .
\]

A value greater than s therefore relaxes the scaling: the rotary angle is *less* shrunk than the nominal PI factor. In other words, the positional embedding is “pushed out” farther in cosine‑sine space than the plain linear scheme would allow.

---

### 2.  Why an “extra” interpolation can preserve information

Rotary position embeddings encode relative position as an angle that grows linearly with the token index \(n\).  
When we extend the context window, new positions appear at large `n`. If we keep the original growth rate, the new angles would quickly leave the principal interval \([0,2\pi)\) and wrap around, causing many distant tokens to look very close to each other in the embedding space. This crowding makes it harder for the model to differentiate near‑by tokens—especially those that multiply contribute to attention scores.

The non‑uniformity formalised in Eq. (3) of the paper says that **low‑frequency (high‑index) RoPE dimensions are more tolerant of interpolation**: they already encode slowly varying positional signals and can absorb a larger scaling factor without sacrificing information. Conversely, **high‑frequency dimensions need finer angular resolution** and therefore must be interpolated more aggressively.

Let us illustrate with a toy example. Suppose a dimension has frequency \(f=1/1000\) (so \(\theta_i=2\pi f\)). With a target ratio \(s=4\) (extending from 4 k to 16 k), linear PI would set \(\lambda_i=4\), shrinking the angle by a factor of 4. The new angle for token \(n=12000\) would be

\[
\frac{n}{\beta^i}\lambda_i^{-1}
  \;=\; \frac{12000}{\beta^i}\cdot\frac{1}{4}
  \;=\; 3000/\beta^i,
\]

which may already lie in the wrapped region. If we use a slightly larger \(\lambda_i = 4.5\), the angle becomes \( \approx 2667/\beta^i\), i.e. 12.5 % farther away from the wrap point. The two tokens now sit at distinct places in the Hilbert space, and the attention mechanism can distinguish them better.

In a multi‑dimensional RoPE, the overall positional representation is a product of cosines across all dimensions. The *information entropy* of the representation—i.e. the amount of distinguishable positional patterns—depends on how the **collection** of angles is spread out. By giving higher‑frequency dimensions a *larger* λᵢ, we spread their angles more aggressively and prevent the high‑frequency portion from collapsing into a dense cluster. The aggregate entropy rises, and the model can keep learning reasonable positional cues without needing to fine‑tune for every new length.

---

### 3.  When the benefit shows up

The paper’s ablation in Table 5 (see “Extension case 1 – λₛ only”) demonstrates that even with a fixed λ across all dimensions, allowing λ to go beyond s reduces perplexity compared to strict PI. However, the *greatest* improvements are seen when both non‑uniformities are exploited:

1. **Non‑uniform λᵢ across dimensions** – the core idea that different frequencies have different optimal scales.
2. **Token‑position threshold \( \hat{n}\)** – keeping the first few tokens exact (no interpolation) because they dominate attention.

When the target extension is modest (e.g. 2–8×), only the λᵢ non‑uniformity is crucial. As the extension grows (32×, 64×, 512×), the tokenizer‑position threshold starts to matter: the very first few tokens carry most of the semantic load of a long document, and interpolating them would produce a severe loss of positional fidelity.

Thus, the “exceeding s” regime is particularly useful when:

- The extension ratio is large enough that a single global λ = s would push many angles into the wrapped region, causing heavy crowding.
- The model still needs to distinguish close tokens early in the sequence (often true at very long ranges where long‑dependency modeling is critical).

---

### 4.  Intuitive takeaway

Think of the RoPE angles as a stretched rubber band:  
- **Low‑frequency bands** are thick and elastic—they can be pulled further without snapping.  
- **High‑frequency bands** are thin and stiff—they break if stretched too far.

When we extend the context, we’re effectively pulling the band further. Letting λᵢ exceed s corresponds to *treating the thick bands more aggressively*, so they keep their ability to tell apart far‑away tokens, while we keep the delicate bands close to their original shape to preserve fine positional detail.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

Allowing λᵢ to be larger than the raw extension ratio s gives the optimizer room to **balance positional discrimination across frequency bands**. This extra degree of freedom mitigates the “crowding” problem that plagues simple linear interpolation, especially at large extensions, and is essential for LongRoPE to achieve a 512× stretch (4 k → 2048 k) while still keeping short‑context performance nearly intact.

### What are the practical GPU and memory requirements for a 2088k-long RoPE search?
Question:
The paper claims the search is efficient, but for a target context length of 2M tokens, what kind of hardware is needed? How many GPU hours does a typical search take, and what percentage of it is dominated by perplexity evaluation versus genetic operations?
Answer:
**Answer (Stack Exchange style)**  

LongRoPE’s claim “efficient search for a 2 M‑token context window” is largely an *engineering* claim: it refers to the cost of performing the evolutionary search that discovers the per‑dimension RoPE rescale factors. The actual fine‑tuning cost is very modest (≈ 1 k steps at 256 k length), and the inference cost is almost identical to a normal LLaMA‑style model.  

Below is a practical breakdown that should give you a rough sense of what to expect if you want to reproduce a 2048 k‑token work‑piece on a typical GPU farm.

---

### 1. Search cost (≈ 60 % of total GPU time)

The paper uses a *population‑based* evolutionary strategy (Algorithm 1). For a target window of 256 k (extension ratio \(s=64\)) the authors set:

| hyper‑parameter | value used in the paper |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| population size \(P\) | 64 |
| mutation size \(N_1\) | 16 |
| crossover size \(N_2\) | 16 |
| mutation probability \(p\) | 0.3 |
| iterations \(\mathcal{T}\) | 40 |
| samples per perplexity evaluation | 5 PG19 slices, each ≥ \(\ell\) tokens |

Perplexity is evaluated on those **5 8 k‑token PG19 snippets that are *padded* to the target length**.  
Each evaluation runs **forward‑only inference** on a single GPU (the authors used A100 80 GB). The inference for a 256 k sequence takes roughly 1.5 s on one A100. 40 iterations × 64 candidates = 2 560 evaluations, i.e. ≈ ~ 40 min CPU‑equivalent, but since the evaluations are embarrassingly parallel we can spread them over **4 A100s** and finish in ~ 10 min wall‑time.  

For the 512 × extension (2048 k) the search space is larger, so the authors **halve the population** ( \(P=32\) ) and keep the other settings. The number of evaluations is then 1 280, giving about 5 min per GPU. In practice the authors report ~ 45 min of GPU time for the full 2048 k search on 4 A100s, which is dominated by perplexity evaluation.  

Because the evolutionary algorithm has a *monotonicity constraint* on \(\lambda_i\) the search discards many candidates before the forward pass, saving ≈ 30 % of time.  

---

### 2. Fine‑tuning cost (≈ 30 % of total GPU time)

Once the rescale factors are found, LongRoPE fine‑tunes:

1. **128 k stage**: 400 steps on 8 A100 GPUs (global batch 32, LR \(2\times10^{-5}\)).  
2. **256 k stage**: 600 additional steps on 16 A100 GPUs.

A single training step for 128 k on the 6‑Gb model (LLaMA‑2‑7B) uses ~ 120 ms on a single A100. 400 steps ≈ 2.5 h × 8 GPUs ≈ 20 h GPU‑hours.  
For the 256 k stage the steps are twice as many but run on 16 GPUs; runtime is ~ 30 min × 16 GPUs ≈ 480 h GPU‑hours.  

Adding these together gives **≈ 500 GPU‑hours** of fine‑tuning (mostly dominated by the wide‑batch 256 k stage). This number is *independent of the search budget* – it will stay roughly the same if you use the same learning‑rate schedule and batch size.

---

### 3. Inference cost

Once the model and the RoPE factors are fixed, inference is identical to a standard LLaMA‑style model: **dense self‑attention on each token**. The sequence length of 2048 k incurs:

- Memory: **≈ 1.7 GB per 8 k tokens** (including activations and KV‑cache).  
  Therefore 2048 k tokens occupy ≈ 430 GB of KV‑cache, which **cannot fit in one GPU**.  
- The authors therefore use **chunked‑generation**: they generate in blocks of 8 k tokens, keeping a rolling 4 k context.  
  This adds a negligible overhead (few percent) compared to a single‑pass 4 k model.

The *search phase* and *fine‑tuning phase* are the only places where a special GPU architecture is required; inference can run on a single A100 as long as you stick to the chunk‑generation strategy or distribute across many GPUs.

---

### 4. Empirical summary for a single GPU farm

| Component | GPUs | GPU‑hours | Comments |
|----------|------|-----------|----------|
| Search (256 k) | 4 | 8 | 10 min wall‑time |
| Search (512 ×, 2048 k) | 4 | 1 | 5 min wall‑time |
| Fine‑tune 128 k | 8 | 20 | 2.5 h per GPU |
| Fine‑tune 256 k | 16 | 480 | 30 min per GPU |
| **Total** | – | **≈ 509 GPU‑hours** |  |
| Wall‑time | 20‑24 h | | (assuming no GPU idle) |

So, if you have a modest 16‑GPU A100 cluster you can finish the full pipeline in just under a day, with a total of roughly **500 GPU‑hours**.  

---

### 5. Dominant cost: perplexity evaluation vs. genetic ops

- **Perplexity evaluation**: each candidate requires a full forward pass. This is the *largest* fraction of the search budget because the model must process the entire 256 k/2048 k context. In the numbers above it accounts for ~ 90 % of the search GPU‑time.
- **Genetic operations** (mutation/crossover) are cheap: they are simple tensor operations (element‑wise additions/multiplications) and cost < 0.01 s per population on a GPU.  
  In practice they consume < 5 % of the search time and can even be done on the CPU.

So, the search is *perplexity‑driven*.

---

### 6. Practical tips

1. **Parallelize perplexity checks**: use 4–8 GPUs for the evolutionary loop so each GPU processes a different fraction of the population every iteration.  
2. **Cache and reuse**: the RoPE factors are reused across candidates that share the same active set (mutation only changes a few entries). Use a hash table to avoid recomputing perplexity for identical candidates.  
3. **Explain the 1 k step fine‑tuning**: the authors explicitly state “1 k fine‑tuning steps at within 256k training length”. Because the search already gives a good initialization, 1 k steps are enough to converge.  
4. **Check the monotonic constraint**: if you hand‑craft λ_i values it saves time; otherwise adopt the algorithm’s sorted mutation.  
5. **Hardware**: Any modern 80 + GB GPU (A100, H100) is suitable for inference – just split 2048 k into 256 k windows if you want streaming generation.  

---

**Bottom line**  
*The “efficient” claim refers to a short, GPU‑coarsely bounded evolutionary search that takes a few minutes per GPU; the majority of the overall cost is the fine‑tuning block (≈ 500 GPU‑hours on a 16‑GPU A100 cluster). Once fine‑tuned, inference stays on par with a standard LLaMA‑type model—only the generation strategy (chunk‑by‑chunk) changes.*

### How does the Cosine/Sine encoding formula in Equation (1) change after applying the searched rescale factors?
Question:
Equation (1) shows the standard RoPE formulation. After the search, each dimension gets its own λ_i and a token‑position threshold. Can someone write out the modified formula explicitly for a token at position n and dimension i to see the exact scaling applied?
Answer:
In the original RoPE formulation each dimension \(i\) of the rotary embedding for a token at position \(n\) is obtained by rotating the query‑key vectors by the “angle”

\[
\theta_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}, \qquad \beta=\theta^{\,{2/d}},
\]

so that the actual embedding is

\[
\bigl[\,\cos \theta_{0}(n),\;\sin\theta_{0}(n),\;\dots,\;
\cos \theta_{d/2-1}(n),\;\sin\theta_{d/2-1}(n)\,\bigr].
\]

To enlarge the context window the authors introduce *non‑uniform* rescaling factors that depend both on the RoPE dimension and on a token‑position threshold.  For every dimension \(i\) a scalar \(\lambda_i\) is learned (the paper uses the notation \(\hat \lambda_i\) but we will write \(\lambda_i\) for clarity) and a single token‑position threshold \(\hat n\) is chosen.  In the notation of Eq. (3) in the paper

\[
\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1          & n<\hat n,\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge \hat n .
\end{cases}
\]

Thus for the first \(\hat n\) tokens the original angle \(\theta_i(n)=n/\beta^{\,i}\) is used unchanged, while for every subsequent token the angle is multiplied by the factor \(1/\lambda_i\).  Putting this together, the *modified* RoPE angle for dimension \(i\) and position \(n\) becomes

\[
\tilde{\theta}_{i}(n)=
\mathbb{I}(\lambda_i,\hat n)\;\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}
=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}, & n<\hat n,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\;\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}, & n\ge \hat n .
\end{cases}
\]

The resulting positional embedding is therefore

\[
\boxed{
\Bigl[
\cos \tilde{\theta}_{0}(n),\;\sin \tilde{\theta}_{0}(n),\;
\dots,\;
\cos \tilde{\theta}_{d/2-1}(n),\;\sin \tilde{\theta}_{d/2-1}(n)
\Bigr]
}
\]

with the two hyper‑parameters \(\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\) and \(\hat n\) chosen by the evolutionary search described in Section 3 of the paper.

Intuitively the \(\lambda_i\) control how aggressively the rotation frequency is *scaled down* (when \(\lambda_i>1\)) or *left unchanged* (when \(\lambda_i=1\)).  Because low‑index dimensions encode higher angular frequencies, the search consistently finds smaller \(\lambda_i\) for them and larger values for higher‑index (lower‑frequency) dimensions, exactly the pattern suggested by the NTK theory.  The threshold \(\hat n\) reflects the observation that the first few tokens receive a disproportionately large attention weight, so keeping their original angular pattern intact helps preserve local coherence.  

In practice, one simply plugs the retrieved \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat n\) into the above equations when computing the rotary embeddings for any sequence.  The rest of the transformer architecture remains unchanged, making LongRoPE a drop‑in positional‑embedding upgrade that can be applied to any RoPE‑based LLM.

### Why does increasing the starting token threshold τ̂ help at very long extensions but not at 2048k?
Question:
The ablation study indicates that keeping the first tokens uninterpolated benefits performance at 16k and 32k but becomes less effective at 2048k. What is the theoretical explanation for this diminishing return as the extension ratio grows?
Answer:
**Why keeping the first few tokens unchanged gives a big boost at 16 k/32 k but almost nothing at 2048 k**

When we extend the context length from the original 4 k to a longer window, we have to *re‑map* the rotary angles of all new positions back into the range that the model was trained on.  
In the simplest (linear) interpolation we multiply every rotary angle by a single factor \( \lambda = 1/s\) where \(s=L'/L\).  
If we keep the first \(\hat{n}\) positions untouched, we are effectively applying \(\lambda=1\) to the first \(\hat{n}\) tokens and the smaller \(\lambda\) to all later tokens.  
Why does this matter?

| **Effect of keeping the first tokens untouched** | **Magnitude of the effect** | **How it scales with \(s\)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher absolute angles for the very first tokens** | The first tokens usually receive the largest attention scores (they are the left‑most query tokens in every module that looks backward). By leaving their angles unchanged we preserve the *relative angular spread* between the first token and its neighbours, which helps the attention heads to focus on them. | When \(s\) is small (e.g., \(s=4\) or \(8\)) the angular spread is still large enough so that preserving it for the first few tokens noticeably **reduces the “crowding” problem** that linear interpolation causes. |
| **Relative angular resolution in later tokens** | For the rest of the sequence, the angular spread shrinks as we apply a larger \(\lambda\). The larger \(s\) is, the smaller the spacing between consecutive angles becomes. | At \(s=512\) (2048 k), the spacing between every *second* token is already on the order of a few degrees. Even if the first 256 tokens keep their original spacing, this accounts for only \(\frac{256}{2048\cdot 10^3}\approx 0.01\%\) of the sequence. The crowding effect dominates everywhere else, so the marginal benefit of keeping the first tokens is negligible. |
| **Practical optimization landscape** | The evolutionary search for the optimal \(\lambda_i\) and \(\hat{n}\) has to navigate a search space that grows exponentially with \(s\). For very large \(s\), the contribution of a fixed \(\hat{n}\) becomes so small that many candidate solutions with different \(\hat{n}\) values produce almost the same perplexity. Consequently, the search gives little incentive to push \(\hat{n}\) higher. | In the experiments reported in Table 4 of the paper, the optimal \(\hat{n}\) for 16 k is around 128–256, whereas for 2048 k it drops to 0 (i.e., the best solution is simply to interpolate *all* positions). This empirical observation is exactly what the theory predicts. |

**Putting it together**

The key intuition is that keeping the first tokens unchanged is a *local* modification that combats the “crowding” of attention vectors in the *early* part of the sequence. Crowding is the primary reason why pure linear interpolation shows a sharp drop in performance as we increase \(s\). When the extension ratio is modest, a handful of untouched tokens can significantly relieve this crowding. When \(s\) becomes huge (512 × for 2048 k), the crowding is already pervasive; the head attention is spread over a million positions, and the relative advantage of preserving the first 256 angles becomes vanishingly small. In other words, the *fraction* of tokens that benefit from being untouched shrinks to almost zero, so the effect on overall perplexity disappears.

This reasoning is consistent across all the ablation plots in the paper: at 16 k and 32 k the curves for “keep 256 tokens” lie noticeably lower than the pure PI curve, whereas at 2048 k the two curves virtually overlap. The theoretical explanation therefore matches the empirical evidence: the diminishing return is a consequence of the relative magnitude of the “un‑interpolated slice” shrinking as the context window grows.
