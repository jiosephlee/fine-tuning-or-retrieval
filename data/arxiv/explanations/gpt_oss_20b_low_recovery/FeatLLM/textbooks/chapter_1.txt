\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: # Chapter Title

**Few‑Shot Tabular Learning with Large Language Models: From Challenges to Rule‑Based Feature Engineering**

---

## Chapter Description

In many scientific and industrial settings—finance, healthcare, or public policy—data come in the form of structured tables. A typical table contains dozens, sometimes hundreds, of features (columns) and each row represents a distinct instance. Building predictive models on such data usually requires a substantial amount of labeled examples; otherwise the models overfit or fail to capture the underlying relationships. Yet, in practice, labeling is expensive, time‑consuming, or downright impossible for sensitive domains. This chapter addresses the **few‑shot tabular learning** problem where only a handful of labeled samples are available.

Recent advances in **Large Language Models (LLMs)** have revealed a surprising ability to generalize to tasks beyond pure language processing. Trained on corpora that contain domain knowledge, statistics, and causal reasoning, LLMs possess a *prior knowledge* that can be tapped for tabular learning. Most existing research leverages LLMs to produce predictions for each new row—requiring a costly inference call per sample and often demanding fine‑tuning or enormous prompt sizes that do not scale well.

A compelling alternative is to treat LLMs as *feature engineers*. Instead of making end‑to‑end predictions, the LLM can be asked to extract logical **rules**—human‑readable conditions that link feature values to the target label. These rules can then be turned into binary features, allowing a lightweight downstream model to infer class probabilities. In this way, the LLM’s reasoning capabilities are used once to bootstrap a set of powerful features; subsequent inference is inexpensive and free of the LLM. This section divides the discussion into four key subtopics that unpack the motivations, limitations, knowledge sources, and the rule‑based strategy that underlies this novel approach.

---

## Motivation for Few‑Shot Learning in Tabular Domains

### Scarcity of Labels

Tabular datasets describe real‑world phenomena: credit risk scores, disease diagnoses, credit card fraud, or product quality. Acquiring ground‑truth labels entails human experts, costly tests, or long‑term observation. Consequently, the number of labeled rows is often restricted to a few dozen or even fewer—a scenario that defies traditional supervised learning pipelines that rely on abundant data to estimate a high‑dimensional parameter space.

### Overfitting and Spurious Correlations

With very few labeled examples, conventional machine learning models are prone to latch onto accidental patterns: a particular feature might correlate with the label in the small training set but not in the population. In the absence of many samples to confirm robustness, such patterns become overfitted and are unreliable at test time.

### Domain Prior Knowledge as a Remedy

In many applications, domain experts already possess *prior knowledge* about the variables. For instance, a cardiologist knows that hypertension and high cholesterol are strong risk factors for coronary artery disease. Rendering such knowledge formalously into a model is itself a significant challenge. The promise of LLMs lies in their exposure to vast amounts of medical literature, financial regulations, and other domain corpora, making them a natural candidate for injecting this prior knowledge into the learning process.

---

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Tabular Approaches

### End‑to‑End Inference Burden

Many recent works weave tabular rows into long text sequences, prompting an LLM to directly output a label. Each prediction then triggers a token‑generation operation that consumes compute resources and inference time. In industrial deployments with millions of predictions, this model is impractical.

### Dependence on Fine‑Tuning or Large Context Windows

Some strategies refine an LLM through **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning** (e.g., LoRA, IA3) or rely on the raw LLM’s network parameters. Such fine‑tuning requires either full access to the model weights or at least a training API—something that modern closed‑source services (OpenAI GPT‑4, Anthropic Claude) do not provide. Additionally, long tables can exceed the model’s prompt token limit. When the serialized table becomes too long, the LLM either truncates data or suffers performance loss, making these methods fragile for high‑dimensional datasets.

### Susceptibility to Spurious Correlations

When the LLM is trained or fine‑tuned on few labeled samples, it can easily *hallucinate* spurious logical relationships, especially if the prompt contains noisy or irrelevant columns. Because the LLM’s internal weights directly encode these patterns, correcting them later is difficult without re‑fine‑tuning.

### Inadequate Scaling to High‑Feature Tables

Empirical studies show that LLM‑based tabular methods break down on datasets with dozens or hundreds of features. The required prompting and tokenisation overhead become prohibitive, and the LLM’s internal capacity for representing subtle interactions diminishes as the feature space grows.

## Role of Prior Knowledge in Tabular Tasks

### Implicit Knowledge Embedded in Language Models

LLMs are trained on diverse corpora that naturally encode many domain facts. For example, the phrase “diabetes is associated with elevated blood glucose” appears countless times, allowing the model to internalise a causal link. When prompted with a set of features (e.g., “Glucose is 120 mg/dL”), the model can leverage this prior to answer questions about a diagnosis.

### Causal vs Correlational Reasoning

A key advantage of using prior knowledge is the ability to **distinguish causality** from incidental correlation. Because the LLM has seen causal statements in its training data, it can, through carefully crafted prompts, infer that *raising* a feature (like hypertension) *causes* an increased risk score rather than merely co‑occurring. This inference is essential when the few-shot data alone would not provide enough signals to tease apart such nuances.

### Personalization Through Prompt Engineering

By incorporating explicit feature definitions (e.g., “Age is measured in years”) and clarifying the *type* of each variable (continuous, categorical, ordinal), the prompt helps the LLM contextualise each feature appropriately. This reduces misinterpretations—such as treating a categorical variable as numeric—helping the model to extract meaningful rules.

## High‑Level Idea of Rule‑Based Feature Generation

### Two‑Stage Process

1. **Rule Extraction**: The LLM is underspecified to *generate a set of logical conditions* (rules) that separate the classes. Instead of predicting class probabilities directly, the LLM is asked to articulate statements like “If *Age* > 50 and *Blood Pressure* > 140, then the probability of *Disease A* is high.”  
   - **Prompt Design**: The prompt is split into a *basic information* part (task description, feature list, and a few labeled examples), a *reasoning instruction* that guides the model to first consider domain knowledge, then use the examples, and finally a *response instruction* that forces the output into a parseable format (e.g., JSON or a simple if‑then syntax).  
   - **Rule Quantity**: Fixing a number of rules (e.g., 10 per class) balances expressiveness and tractability, preventing an explosion of tokens that would exceed the context window.

2. **Feature Engineering**: The extracted rules are converted into *binary features* for every instance. Each new feature indicates whether the rule holds (`1`) or not (`0`). The entire dataset is thus mapped to a new binary matrix where rows are instances and columns are rules.

### Learning Rule Weights

Once the rules are in place, a lightweight **linear model** (or any simple probabilistic model) is trained on the few labeled samples to learn a *weight vector* over the new binary features. The dot‑product produces a *logit* for each class, followed by a softmax to yield class probabilities. Since the features are binary and rules are sparse, the resulting model is fast to evaluate and transparent: the weight of each rule indicates its influence on the prediction.

### Ensemble and Bagging

Because the LLM’s rule generation can vary depending on prompt noise, temperature, or the order of examples, the methodology generates multiple rule sets—each representing a *weak learner*. A **bagging** strategy selects random subsets of features or training instances for each learner, and the final prediction is an average over all ensembles. This technique reduces variance, mitigates overfitting, and effectively increases robustness against inherent stochasticity in LLM outputs.

### Advantages Over End‑to‑End LLM Inference

- **No Per‑Sample LLM Calls**: After the initial rule extraction phase, subsequent predictions are purely *model‑inference* with a lightweight linear classifier.  
- **Prompt Size Resilience**: By bagging subsets of features, the method stays within the LLM’s token limit even for large tables.  
- **Training‑Free LLM Access**: Only a handful of LLM queries are needed (one per ensemble), eliminating the need for parameter‑fine‑tuning and making the approach feasible with API‑only models.  
- **Interpretability**: The extracted rules are human‑readable, providing clear insight into which feature conditions drive predictions—an advantage in regulated domains like medicine or finance.

## Conclusion

Traditional few‑shot learning strategies struggle with tabular data due to limited labels, spurious correlations, and the high dimensionality of feature spaces. Large Language Models offer a powerful reservoir of prior knowledge but, if used naively, require expensive per‑sample inference and complex fine‑tuning. By reframing the role of the LLM—from direct predictor to *designer of logical rules*—we bridge the gap between rich domain knowledge and data‑efficient learning. The rule‑based feature generation framework enables robust, low‑latency inference that remains faithful to the LLM’s strengths while circumventing its drawbacks. This novel paradigm opens a new pathway for deploying large language models in data‑scarce, structured‑data settings, bringing both performance gains and interpretability to practical machine learning pipelines.
