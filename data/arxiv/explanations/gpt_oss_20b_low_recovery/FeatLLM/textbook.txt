# Chapter Title

**Few‑Shot Tabular Learning with Large Language Models: From Challenges to Rule‑Based Feature Engineering**

---

## Chapter Description

In many scientific and industrial settings—finance, healthcare, or public policy—data come in the form of structured tables. A typical table contains dozens, sometimes hundreds, of features (columns) and each row represents a distinct instance. Building predictive models on such data usually requires a substantial amount of labeled examples; otherwise the models overfit or fail to capture the underlying relationships. Yet, in practice, labeling is expensive, time‑consuming, or downright impossible for sensitive domains. This chapter addresses the **few‑shot tabular learning** problem where only a handful of labeled samples are available.

Recent advances in **Large Language Models (LLMs)** have revealed a surprising ability to generalize to tasks beyond pure language processing. Trained on corpora that contain domain knowledge, statistics, and causal reasoning, LLMs possess a *prior knowledge* that can be tapped for tabular learning. Most existing research leverages LLMs to produce predictions for each new row—requiring a costly inference call per sample and often demanding fine‑tuning or enormous prompt sizes that do not scale well.

A compelling alternative is to treat LLMs as *feature engineers*. Instead of making end‑to‑end predictions, the LLM can be asked to extract logical **rules**—human‑readable conditions that link feature values to the target label. These rules can then be turned into binary features, allowing a lightweight downstream model to infer class probabilities. In this way, the LLM’s reasoning capabilities are used once to bootstrap a set of powerful features; subsequent inference is inexpensive and free of the LLM. This section divides the discussion into four key subtopics that unpack the motivations, limitations, knowledge sources, and the rule‑based strategy that underlies this novel approach.

---

## Motivation for Few‑Shot Learning in Tabular Domains

### Scarcity of Labels

Tabular datasets describe real‑world phenomena: credit risk scores, disease diagnoses, credit card fraud, or product quality. Acquiring ground‑truth labels entails human experts, costly tests, or long‑term observation. Consequently, the number of labeled rows is often restricted to a few dozen or even fewer—a scenario that defies traditional supervised learning pipelines that rely on abundant data to estimate a high‑dimensional parameter space.

### Overfitting and Spurious Correlations

With very few labeled examples, conventional machine learning models are prone to latch onto accidental patterns: a particular feature might correlate with the label in the small training set but not in the population. In the absence of many samples to confirm robustness, such patterns become overfitted and are unreliable at test time.

### Domain Prior Knowledge as a Remedy

In many applications, domain experts already possess *prior knowledge* about the variables. For instance, a cardiologist knows that hypertension and high cholesterol are strong risk factors for coronary artery disease. Rendering such knowledge formalously into a model is itself a significant challenge. The promise of LLMs lies in their exposure to vast amounts of medical literature, financial regulations, and other domain corpora, making them a natural candidate for injecting this prior knowledge into the learning process.

---

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Tabular Approaches

### End‑to‑End Inference Burden

Many recent works weave tabular rows into long text sequences, prompting an LLM to directly output a label. Each prediction then triggers a token‑generation operation that consumes compute resources and inference time. In industrial deployments with millions of predictions, this model is impractical.

### Dependence on Fine‑Tuning or Large Context Windows

Some strategies refine an LLM through **parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning** (e.g., LoRA, IA3) or rely on the raw LLM’s network parameters. Such fine‑tuning requires either full access to the model weights or at least a training API—something that modern closed‑source services (OpenAI GPT‑4, Anthropic Claude) do not provide. Additionally, long tables can exceed the model’s prompt token limit. When the serialized table becomes too long, the LLM either truncates data or suffers performance loss, making these methods fragile for high‑dimensional datasets.

### Susceptibility to Spurious Correlations

When the LLM is trained or fine‑tuned on few labeled samples, it can easily *hallucinate* spurious logical relationships, especially if the prompt contains noisy or irrelevant columns. Because the LLM’s internal weights directly encode these patterns, correcting them later is difficult without re‑fine‑tuning.

### Inadequate Scaling to High‑Feature Tables

Empirical studies show that LLM‑based tabular methods break down on datasets with dozens or hundreds of features. The required prompting and tokenisation overhead become prohibitive, and the LLM’s internal capacity for representing subtle interactions diminishes as the feature space grows.

---

## Role of Prior Knowledge in Tabular Tasks

### Implicit Knowledge Embedded in Language Models

LLMs are trained on diverse corpora that naturally encode many domain facts. For example, the phrase “diabetes is associated with elevated blood glucose” appears countless times, allowing the model to internalise a causal link. When prompted with a set of features (e.g., “Glucose is 120 mg/dL”), the model can leverage this prior to answer questions about a diagnosis.

### Causal vs Correlational Reasoning

A key advantage of using prior knowledge is the ability to **distinguish causality** from incidental correlation. Because the LLM has seen causal statements in its training data, it can, through carefully crafted prompts, infer that *raising* a feature (like hypertension) *causes* an increased risk score rather than merely co‑occurring. This inference is essential when the few-shot data alone would not provide enough signals to tease apart such nuances.

### Personalization Through Prompt Engineering

By incorporating explicit feature definitions (e.g., “Age is measured in years”) and clarifying the *type* of each variable (continuous, categorical, ordinal), the prompt helps the LLM contextualise each feature appropriately. This reduces misinterpretations—such as treating a categorical variable as numeric—helping the model to extract meaningful rules.

---

## High‑Level Idea of Rule‑Based Feature Generation

### Two‑Stage Process

1. **Rule Extraction**: The LLM is underspecified to *generate a set of logical conditions* (rules) that separate the classes. Instead of predicting class probabilities directly, the LLM is asked to articulate statements like “If *Age* > 50 and *Blood Pressure* > 140, then the probability of *Disease A* is high.”  
   - **Prompt Design**: The prompt is split into a *basic information* part (task description, feature list, and a few labeled examples), a *reasoning instruction* that guides the model to first consider domain knowledge, then use the examples, and finally a *response instruction* that forces the output into a parseable format (e.g., JSON or a simple if‑then syntax).  
   - **Rule Quantity**: Fixing a number of rules (e.g., 10 per class) balances expressiveness and tractability, preventing an explosion of tokens that would exceed the context window.

2. **Feature Engineering**: The extracted rules are converted into *binary features* for every instance. Each new feature indicates whether the rule holds (`1`) or not (`0`). The entire dataset is thus mapped to a new binary matrix where rows are instances and columns are rules.

### Learning Rule Weights

Once the rules are in place, a lightweight **linear model** (or any simple probabilistic model) is trained on the few labeled samples to learn a *weight vector* over the new binary features. The dot‑product produces a *logit* for each class, followed by a softmax to yield class probabilities. Since the features are binary and rules are sparse, the resulting model is fast to evaluate and transparent: the weight of each rule indicates its influence on the prediction.

### Ensemble and Bagging

Because the LLM’s rule generation can vary depending on prompt noise, temperature, or the order of examples, the methodology generates multiple rule sets—each representing a *weak learner*. A **bagging** strategy selects random subsets of features or training instances for each learner, and the final prediction is an average over all ensembles. This technique reduces variance, mitigates overfitting, and effectively increases robustness against inherent stochasticity in LLM outputs.

### Advantages Over End‑to‑End LLM Inference

- **No Per‑Sample LLM Calls**: After the initial rule extraction phase, subsequent predictions are purely *model‑inference* with a lightweight linear classifier.  
- **Prompt Size Resilience**: By bagging subsets of features, the method stays within the LLM’s token limit even for large tables.  
- **Training‑Free LLM Access**: Only a handful of LLM queries are needed (one per ensemble), eliminating the need for parameter‑fine‑tuning and making the approach feasible with API‑only models.  
- **Interpretability**: The extracted rules are human‑readable, providing clear insight into which feature conditions drive predictions—an advantage in regulated domains like medicine or finance.

---

## Conclusion

Traditional few‑shot learning strategies struggle with tabular data due to limited labels, spurious correlations, and the high dimensionality of feature spaces. Large Language Models offer a powerful reservoir of prior knowledge but, if used naively, require expensive per‑sample inference and complex fine‑tuning. By reframing the role of the LLM—from direct predictor to *designer of logical rules*—we bridge the gap between rich domain knowledge and data‑efficient learning. The rule‑based feature generation framework enables robust, low‑latency inference that remains faithful to the LLM’s strengths while circumventing its drawbacks. This novel paradigm opens a new pathway for deploying large language models in data‑scarce, structured‑data settings, bringing both performance gains and interpretability to practical machine learning pipelines.

# 2. Background and Related Work

Understanding the proposed *FeatLLM* framework requires a solid footing in three intertwined research streams: few‑shot learning, large language model (LLM)–enabled tabular methods, and conventional ensemble techniques that help to counteract the peculiarities of short‑lived prompts. This chapter therefore systematically reviews the theoretical and empirical foundations that precede the current contribution. Each sub‑topic is expanded with intuition and motivation, and the subsequent sections progressively sharpen the building blocks that *FeatLLM* stitches together.

## 2.1 Few‑Shot Learning Fundamentals Across Modalities

Few‑shot learning (FSL) is an umbrella of paradigms in which a model must generalise from an extreme scarcity of labelled examples—often fewer than ten per class—to unseen test data. The motivation is rooted in real‑world scenarios where annotations are expensive or unattainable, such as rare medical conditions or niche financial instruments. The central challenge is twofold: (i) avoid *spurious correlations* that arise from random noise in small samples, and (ii) convey the *right inductive bias* that steers learning toward patterns that reflect real causality rather than artefacts.

**Metric‑based methods** formulate similarity scores between an unlabelled query and support samples, as in prototypical networks. For tabular data, however, the very nature of mixed numeric and categorical features makes direct distance metrics less informative. Gradient‑based meta‑learning, exemplified by MAML, instead learns a common parameter initialization that can be quickly fine‑tuned on a few labelled points. But such approaches still require fine‑tuning *inside* the model, incurring a computation burden at inference.

The *data‑centric* perspective argues that the small sample size can be ameliorated by *enriching* the information that the model receives. This is achieved by leveraging unlabeled data (semi‑supervised learning), synthetic data generation (contrastive or masked‑language‑model style pre‑training), or *auxiliary* knowledge sources that capture higher‑level dependencies. A prominent line has emerged: **transfer‑learning** across datasets. By pre‑training on a diverse collection of tabular tasks, models learn column‑to‑column relationship priors (e.g. TransTab) or general distributional patterns (e.g. TabPFN). *FeatLLM* adopts a slightly different angle: instead of changing the model parameters, it are re‑presents the data through *handcrafted* features, manufactured by an external knowledge source—here a large language model.

**Key takeaways** for FSL in tabular settings:

- **Spurious learning** is a primary risk; many low‑shot models overfit to coincidental feature–label correlations.
- **Prior knowledge** is invaluable; **generic** (e.g., distributional) and **domain‑specific** (e.g., medical risk factors) sources both help shape the inductive bias.
- **Model simplicity** (logistic regression, trees) often beats heavy deep architectures when coupled with a good representation, a fact that *FeatLLM* deliberately leverages.

## 2.2 Traditional Feature Engineering for Tabular Data

In classic machine‑learning pipelines, **feature engineering** constitutes the transition from raw data to a space that lends itself to effective matching between hypotheses and training evidence. For tabular data, this involves a mixture of *numeric transformations*—logarithms, polynomial expansions, binning—and *categorical embeddings* such as one‑hot encoding, target encoding, or ordinal scalings. Moreover, domain experts often perform **rule‑based feature construction**: e.g., "If `age > 60` and `blood pressure > 140` then risk = high", materialised as a binary flag.

Why is feature engineering still critical *today*? Models such as XGBoost or random forests implicitly learn interactions through splits, but their leaf distributions are bounded by the tree depth and number of trees; when samples are scarce, the tree structure cannot be reliably inferred. By transforming raw attributes into deterministic features that encode plausible relations (e.g., medical thresholds, physiological limits), we provide *signals* that survive the *high variance* regime. In fact, rule‑based features often act as “soft constraints” that route data to the correct region of the feature space, enabling linear models to perform nearly as well as deep ensembles.

Traditional pipelines also incorporate **feature selection** to mitigate overfitting. Methods such as recursive feature elimination or tree‑based feature importances prune irrelevant dimensions. However, such post‑hoc selection requires training a (possibly over‑parameterised) model first, which is a circular problem in low‑shot settings. *FeatLLM* sidesteps this by *liberally creating many binary indicators* (through an LLM) and then *learning* their relevance via a lightweight, non‑negative linear weight matrix. Thus, feature engineering and feature selection are combined into a single, interpretable process.

## 2.3 LLM Pre‑training and In‑Context Learning

Since the advent of transformer‑based language models, pre‑training has been dominated by **self‑supervised objectives** over vast corpora of text, sentence pairs, code snippets, and structured documents. The outcome is a distributed representation that captures syntax, semantics, world knowledge, and even commonsense rules. LLMs such as GPT‑3 or LLaMA encode codified facts about “older people are at higher risk for cardiovascular diseases” that do not rely on any explicit tabular data.

The *in‑context learning* (ICL) phenomenon unlocks these embodied priors for new tasks without parameter updates. By inserting a **prompt** that mixes a short **task description**, a few **example demonstrations**, and the **task input**, the model can be coaxed into performing a new inference. The *chain‑of‑thought* (CoT) style instruction further relaxes the process by asking the model to articulate intermediate reasoning steps, which empirically leads to higher accuracy in many reasoning tasks.

When applied to tabular data, ICL requires **serialization**: converting rows of key–value pairs into natural language. Existing works either (i) feed the serialized text directly into an LLM to output probabilities for each label (pure ICL), or (ii) fine‑tune the LLM with parameter‑efficient adapters (LoRA, IA3) using the same serialized inputs. Though effective, both approaches maintain a *per‑sample* dependency on the LLM at inference, which incurs heavy computation and introduces latency.

*FeatLLM* capitalises on the same ICL ability but changes the target: instead of asking the LLM to answer the question for each instance, it is sampled *once* to produce *rules*—computationally cheap, declarative conditions. These rules are later applied to every instance locally, thereby decoupling the inference cost from the LLM’s own evaluation.

## 2.4 Existing LLM‑Based Tabular Frameworks

| Method | LLM backbone | Training strategy |
|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **TabLLM** | GPT‑3 variants | Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (IA3) |
| **TABLET** | GPT‑3.5 | In‑context learning + external heuristics |
| **STUNT** | — | Semi‑supervised using unlabeled data to learn transferable representations |
| **TabPFN** | — | Bayesian neural network trained on synthetic data from diverse distributions |
| **In‑context** (vanilla) | GPT‑3.5 | Pure ICL, no auxiliary tuning |

**TabLLM** represents a middle point: it fine‑tunes the LLM while using a limited number of labeled samples for each tabular task, thus ‘tuning’ the model to the distribution of the specific dataset. However, because the LLM must still be queried per data point on the test set, the method is not scalable to real‑world deployments that feature many users or high throughput.

**TABLET** augments the ICL scope by feeding the LLM extra hints—most notably a set of *prototype rules* learned from an external classifier. While the resulting technique improves accuracy, it remains *online* to the LLM: each test sample requires a new inference call.

**STUNT** and **TabPFN** ignore the LLM altogether but instead harness *unlabeled data* or synthetic datasets to pre‑train a generic model. They achieve state‑of‑the‑art on many tabular benchmarks but still cannot escape the need for training on the *target* dataset—a crucial bottleneck when the target is a few‑shot domain.

In contrast, *FeatLLM* uses the LLM *only* during the *feature engineering* stage. Once a set of interpretable rules is retrieved, no subsequent LLM inference is required, improving inference latency and allowing regulation of cost per model rather than per sample.

## 2.5 Ensemble Methods and Feature Bagging in Machine Learning

Ensemble techniques have been a staple in machine‑learning practice for decades, with the seminal **Random Forest** (Breiman, 2001) epitomising the principle: train many weak learners on *different* random subsets of data or features (bagging), and aggregate their predictions. The benefits include:

- **Variance reduction**: averaging over many models diminishes individual overfitting signals.
- **Robustness**: if some models are degraded by noisy features or corrupted samples, others can compensate.
- **Implicit regularisation**: inducing diversity amongst the base models discourages the collapse to a single hypothesis.

In the *FeatLLM* context, bagging appears in two complementary ways:

1. **Feature bagging**: since the input prompt to an LLM might exceed the token limit, the dataset is subsampled to only a **subset of features** (or instances) per token‑budget session. Each bag produces a distinct rule set; the rule sets’ diversity is guaranteed by random feature selection.
2. **Ensemble of rule‑based classifiers**: each bag’s rule extraction (via the LLM) is followed by training the non‑negative linear weights. By **averaging softmax probabilities** over many such models, *FeatLLM* harnesses the stability offered by bagging while maintaining a lightweight inference routine.

The combination of an LLM‑generated **feature space** and an **ensemble classifier** forms the core of *FeatLLM*’s design philosophy: powerful, knowledge‑rich features distilled once, and simple models that can be efficiently trained and deployed.

# 3. Problem Formulation  

In order to ground the rest of the discussion in concrete formalism, this chapter specifies the mathematical setting of the few‑shot tabular learning task tackled by the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework.  We first describe how a tabular data set is represented, then explain how features are enriched with natural‑language descriptions, and finally outline the sampling protocols that are used to create small training sets and evaluate model performance.  All notation and terminology follows the conventions adopted in the paper.

---

## 3.1 Tabular Dataset Representation  

A tabular data set is a collection of $N$ rows, each row corresponding to a distinct observation $\mathbf{x}^i$ and an associated label $\mathbf{y}^i$.  
- The data matrix is denoted  
  \[
  \mathcal{D} \;\;=\;\;\bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N}\:\:,\qquad 
  \mathbf{x}^i \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{\,d},
  \]  
  where $d$ is the number of input features and $N$ the total number of labeled records.  
- The label space is discrete and multi‑class:  
  \[
  \mathbf{y}^i \;\in\; \{1,2,\dots,\,c\},
  \]  
  where $c$ is the number of classes.  In the binary case $c=2$, and the label is typically encoded as $\{0,1\}$.

The paper treats each observation as a vector of numerical or categorical variables, and all features are assumed to have an associated natural‑language name that can be provided to an LLM.  The set of feature names is written as  
\[
F \;=\;\{f_1,f_2,\dots,f_d\},
\] 
and for every $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$, the field $f_j$ denotes the $j$‑th feature in $\mathbf{x}$.  Each feature may be *numeric* (continuous or ordinal) or *categorical*; this distinction is important when generating rules but is not explicitly mentioned in the formal machinery.

Because the goal of \textsf{FeatLLM} is to learn from a *few* labeled samples, the data set is split into a small *support set* and a larger *query set*.  Below we formalize the sampling mechanism that produces these subsets.

---

## 3.2 Feature Set and Natural‑Language Descriptions  

The LLM is supplied not only with raw feature values but with *descriptive* text about each feature.  This text can be a taxonomy of permissible values, a short definition, or any other natural‑language hint that was learned during the LLM’s pre‑training stage.  

Let us define a *feature description* function  
\[
\operatorname{desc}\colon F\to\mathcal{T},
\]  
where $\mathcal{T}$ denotes the space of text strings.  For a feature $f_j$, $\operatorname{desc}(f_j)$ might state, for instance, that $f_j$ is a *continuous* variable representing *age* in years, or that it is a *categorical* feature taking values {\it male/female}.

To present training examples to the LLM, the authors serialise each example into a natural‑language sentence.  The serialisation function used in the paper is  

\[
\operatorname{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F)=
\text{"}\ f_1\ \text{is}\ x^i_1.\ \dots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ x^i_d.\ \text{Answer:}\ y^i\text{"},
\]  
where the subscript on $x^i_j$ denotes the value of the $j$‑th feature in example $i$.  This string is appended to the prompt sent to the LLM during in‑context learning.

By combining the **basic information** (feature names, types, descriptions), **serialized examples**, and a **task description** (e.g., “Does this patient have chronic heart failure?”), the LLM is given sufficient context to infer *rules* that distinguish each class.  The formalism in the previous subsection captures the role of $F$ as the blueprint of the feature space; the descriptions enrich $F$ with semantic information that the LLM can exploit.

---

## 3.3 One‑Shot, k‑Shot, and Cross‑Validation Protocols  

The *few‑shot* regime is central to the paper’s motivation.  In this regime, the amount of labeled data available for training is extremely limited, and the performance of a model can vary drastically with the particular samples chosen.  To study this effect systematically, the authors use the following protocols.

### One‑Shot (\(k=1\))
A *one‑shot* experiment selects a single labeled example $\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i}\bigr)$ uniformly at random from $\mathcal{D}$.  This single example becomes the support set, and the model must generalise to the remaining $N-1$ examples in the test set.  Because a single sample does not provide any information about class prevalence, performance is highly volatile and the evaluation usually averages over many random draws.

### k‑Shot (\(k>1\))
In a *k‑shot* setting, \(k\) distinct labeled examples are sampled without replacement from $\mathcal{D}$ to create the support set:
\[
S_k \;=\;\bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^{i_s},\mathbf{y}^{i_s})\bigr\}_{s=1}^{k}.
\]  
The rest of the data,  
\[
Q_k \;=\;\mathcal{D}\setminus S_k,
\]  
serves as the query set (i.e., the test set).  The sampling is performed independently for each run so that results are reported as averages over several random splits.  The paper typically reports $k \in \{1,2,3,4,8,16,32\}$ to illustrate how performance scales with more labeled data.

### Cross‑Validation as a Robust Evaluation Tool  
Given the high variance inherent to few‑shot sampling, the authors employ *k*-fold cross‑validation to obtain stable estimates of generalisation performance.  In a standard *fold*-$\ell$ ($1\le \ell\le K$), the data set is partitioned into $K$ equally sized disjoint subsets \(\{C_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^{K}\).  For each fold, one subset serves as the query set and the remaining \(K-1\) subsets are pooled together and then trimmed down to the desired number of shots \(k\) by random sampling.  Formally, for fold $\ell$
\[
Q_k^{(\ell)} = C_\ell,\qquad S_k^{(\ell)} \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{D}\setminus C_\ell,
\]
with \(|S_k^{(\ell)}|=k\).  The procedure is repeated over all folds and the performance metric (AUC) is averaged, yielding a single number that reflects the model’s expected behaviour under arbitrary draws of a few labeled instances.

Cross‑validation also facilitates selection of early‑stopping epochs for the lightweight linear model that maps binary rule‑features to class probabilities.  By monitoring validation loss on a held‑out fold, the authors identify the epoch with minimal cross‑validated loss and use the associated weights during inference.

---

## 3.4 Summary  

- The **dataset** is a labelled collection $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}$ with $N$ rows, $d$ features, and $c$ classes.  
- Each feature $f_j$ is associated with a natural‑language description $\operatorname{desc}(f_j)$, and the entire set of feature names is $F=\{f_1,\dots,f_d\}$.  
- **Little training data** is required: the *support set* $S_k$ contains $k$ randomly selected labeled examples; the remainder constitutes the test set $Q_k$.  
- **Cross‑validation** is used to mitigate sampling variance and to tune the linear classifier that operates on the binary rule features produced by the LLM.  

With these definitions in place, the subsequent sections can proceed to detail how \textsf{FeatLLM} crafts rules from the LLM, transforms the raw tabular input into a rule‑feature space, and learns a lightweight downstream predictor.  The rigorous framing of the few‑shot learning problem here is a prerequisite for understanding both the methodological innovations and the empirical evaluation presented in the paper.

# 4. The FeatLLM Framework Overview

The FeatLLM framework represents a paradigm shift in how large language models (LLMs) can be leveraged for tabular learning. Instead of imperatively using an LLM to directly predict a label for each instance—an operation that is costly in terms of latency and often infeasible when the LM service is restricted to inference–only APIs—**FeatLLM assigns the LLM the role of a feature engineer**. The pipeline therefore consists of three stages: (i) a rule–generation module that queries the LLM for a set of human‑readable decision rules; (ii) a rule‑parsing stage that translates those natural‑language rules into executable code; and (iii) a lightweight downstream learner that operates on the binary features induced by the rules. An ensemble at the level of rule sets, created via bagging, further stabilises the procedure and mitigates both sample‑noise and prompt‑size constraints. The following subsections dissect each component in full detail.

## Rule generation module

At this initial stage, FeatLLM constructs a carefully engineered prompt and sends it to a pre‑trained LLM. The prompt is the linchpin of the entire approach and is composed of three sub‑components that mirror the rational thought process of a human data scientist.

1. **Basic Information Description**  
   The prompt first lists the task in natural language, for example: *“Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?”* Following the question, the prompt enumerates all feature names, their types (categorical or numerical), and optional textual definitions. This ensures that the LLM has all necessary background knowledge about the features. A small set of few‑shot examples (typically \(k\) labeled samples) is then serialized into text. Serialization follows a canonical format, for instance:
   \[
   \text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)=
   \text{“}\; f_1\text{ is }\mathbf{x}^i_1;\ \dots;\ f_d\text{ is }\mathbf{x}^i_d;\ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\text{.”}
   \]
   By presenting both natural‑language descriptions and concrete instances, the LLM situates the problem in a rich context.

2. **Reasoning Instruction**  
   The prompt instructs the LLM to reason in a chain‑of‑thought style, but deliberately split into two explicit sub‑steps:  
   *Step 1 – Prior Reasoning*: The LLM is asked to infer general causal or statistical relationships between features and the target, **without** inspecting the few‑shot examples. This taps into its pre‑training knowledge (for example, recognizing that high systolic pressure often correlates with heart disease).  
   *Step 2 – Rule Construction*: Armed with this prior knowledge, the LLM then examines the examples and formulates a set of declarative rules that distinguish one class from the other. The prompt limits the number of rules per class to a fixed value (commonly ten) to keep the output concise and within the LM’s token budget. Feature types are referenced explicitly in the reasoning instruction so that the LLM does not produce syntactically invalid conditions (e.g., “if ‘age’ is ‘high’” instead of “if ‘age’ < 30”).

3. **Response Instruction**  
   A final section of the prompt dictates a rigid, parse‑friendly response structure. For each target class, the LLM should output a numbered list of rules, each rule presented in a consistent predicate form (e.g., `feature_name > threshold` or `feature_name == "category"`). This structural constraint greatly simplifies downstream parsing, as it removes ambiguities that ordinarily plague natural language extraction.

The combination of these three sub‑components forces the LLM to produce a *quality* rule set that balances prior knowledge with data‑driven evidence, while remaining within the token limits imposed by many commercial LM interfaces.

## Rule parsing and code generation

A significant challenge arises from the fact that the LLM’s output, even with formatting constraints, can contain minor syntactic variations: “greater than” vs. “>,” “equals” vs. “==,” or even variable spellings of feature names. To robustly convert these natural‑language rules into executable logic, FeatLLM adopts a **self‑parsing strategy** that leverages the LLM itself.

The input to this second LLM call consists of:
- A concise description of the input data schema (feature names and types).
- The raw rule strings returned by the first LLM step.
- An explicit function signature, e.g.,  
  ```
  def apply_rules(row: dict) -> List[int]:
      """
      Input: row – a dictionary mapping feature names to values.
      Output: A list of binary flags indicating whether each rule is satisfied.
      """
  ```
The LLM is instructed to generate Python code that implements the `apply_rules` function, translating each rule into a Boolean expression referencing the corresponding dictionary keys. The generated code is then executed via Python’s `exec()` in a sandboxed environment, ensuring that for each row of data the function returns a dense binary vector, one entry per rule.

This self‑parsing approach has two advantages:
1. The LLM’s own semantic understanding of the rules dominates the parsing process, rather than a brittle pattern‑matching engine that might fail on edge cases.
2. The output is code, which is universal and can be reused across any downstream programming environment—not just Python.

## Binary feature construction

Once the `apply_rules` function has been constructed, FeatLLM processes each example in the dataset:
\[
\mathbf{z}_k^i = \bigl( \mathbb{I}(\text{Rule}_{k,1}(\mathbf{x}^i)), \dots, \mathbb{I}(\text{Rule}_{k,R}(\mathbf{x}^i)) \bigr)^\top,
\]
where \( \mathbb{I}(\cdot) \) is the indicator function that returns 1 if the rule evaluates to true for instance \(i\) and 0 otherwise. Here, \(k\) indexes the target class, and \(R\) is the fixed number of rules per class. The concatenation of binary vectors from all classes yields a sparse, high‑dimensional representation for every example, but with the virtue that each dimension has an explicit semantic meaning: “satisfied condition X.”

Because the rules are derived from both prior knowledge and the few‑shot examples, many of these binary features are highly correlated with the target. Empirically, FeatLLM reports that a substantial proportion of these rules possess AUROC scores exceeding 0.5 relative to the label, a testament to their informativeness.

## Linear model for class likelihood

The final predictive step is deliberately simple to keep inference latency low. For each class \(k\), FeatLLM trains a non‑bias linear model:
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\]
where \(\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^R\) denotes the weight vector for class \(k\), and the `max` operator projects each weight into the positive orthant. This projection guarantees that activating a rule can never *decrease* the predicted probability of the corresponding class—a safeguard that aligns with the intent of rule activation as evidence. The logits for all classes form a vector:
\[
\boldsymbol{\ell}^i = \bigl( \text{logit}_1^i, \dots, \text{logit}_c^i \bigr)^\top,
\]
where \(c\) is the number of classes. These logits are then converted into class probabilities via the softmax function:
\[
\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}^i).
\]
Training proceeds by minimizing the cross‑entropy loss over the \(k\)-shot training set. Because the feature space is binary and highly interpretable, the linear model can be trained efficiently even on very small data subsets.

An important point is that the weights are not learned globally once; instead, FeatLLM creates multiple such linear models, each trained on a distinct ensemble of rules and data subsets, as described next.

## Bagging and ensemble aggregation

To increase robustness against both sample noise and the stochasticity of LLM outputs, FeatLLM employs a **bagging** strategy akin to random forests:

1. **Multiple Rule Generation Trials**  
   The entire rule‑generation–parsing–feature‑construction pipeline is repeated \(T\) times. On each repetition, the LLM is queried with a *different* random seed (higher temperature) and with a *different ordering* of the few‑shot demonstration examples. Consequently, each trial yields a distinct rule set \(\{\text{Rule}_{k,r}^{(t)}\}_{r=1}^{R}\) for all classes \(k\).

2. **Subsampling Features and Instances**  
   To further reduce prompt length and introduce additional variability, each trial is initialized with a random subset of either:  
   - *Features*: Only a portion of the available columns is included in the serialization step; or  
   - *Instances*: Only a random subset of the \(k\) labeled examples is used for the few‑shot prompt.

   This mirrors the “feature” and “instance” bagging schemes in ensemble literature and helps the model capture complementary aspects of the data.

3. **Training Linear Models per Trial**  
   For every trial \(t\), a linear model \(\mathbf{w}^{(t)} = \{\mathbf{w}_k^{(t)}\}_{k=1}^c\) is trained on the binary features generated by that trial’s rule set. Because each linear model is lightweight, training time remains trivial.

4. **Prediction Aggregation**  
   For a new instance \(\mathbf{x}\), each trial computes a probability vector \(\mathbf{p}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x})\) as described in the previous section. The final prediction is simply the arithmetic mean across trials:
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{p}^{(t)}(\mathbf{x}).
   \]
   This averaging can be interpreted as a Bayesian model averaging over an implicitly defined prior of possible rule sets.

This ensemble approach yields several practical benefits. First, it mitigates the risk that a single high‑temperature sample of rules contains glaring mistakes (e.g. spurious correlations), since correct rules from other trials will dominate the average. Second, it scales gracefully to very high‑dimensional tabular datasets: by subsampling features, the prompt length stays within the LLM’s context window. Finally, after the ensemble has been trained, inference is **only** a matter of applying the pre‑computed linear weights; no LLM calls are required, which drastically lowers latency and cost.

In summary, the FeatLLM framework turns the LLM from a barcode of black‑box inference into an intelligent rule busker that, after a brief initial consultation, hands back a small, interpretable, and easily ingestible feature set. The blend of LLM priors, few‑shot data, and classical ensemble learning yields an approach that is both principled and highly practical for real‑world tabular learning tasks.

# 5. Prompt Design for Rule Extraction

In the *FeatLLM* framework, the first and arguably most crucial step is to coax a large language model (LLM) into producing a set of human‑readable rules that capture the relationship between the input features and the prediction target. Because the downstream inference pipeline relies entirely on these rules, the quality of the prompt that interfaces with the LLM determines the success of the entire method. This chapter dissects the prompt design strategy employed by *FeatLLM*, articulating why the prompt is partitioned into three distinct blocks, how each block is constructed, and what effect each block exerts on the LLM’s reasoning process. The discussion is grounded entirely in the terminology, notations, and experimental choices described in the original research paper.

---

## 5.1 Overview of the Triple‑Block Prompt Architecture  

The prompt targeted at the LLM is written in natural language but with a precise structure. It consists of the following three successive sections:

1. **Basic Information Description** – introduces the task, enumerates features with type and definition, and supplies a handful of labeled examples serialized into text.  
2. **Reasoning Instruction** – explicitly guides the LLM’s inference process, directing it through a two‑stage causal reasoning pipeline.  
3. **Response Instruction** – stipulates the syntactic format of the output, ensuring that the rules can be programmatically parsed downstream.

Mathematically, insofar as the paper frames it, the prompt is a carefully engineered string \( \mathcal{P} \) that the LLM receives as input. Formally, for a dataset \( \mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^k \) in the \(k\)-shot setting, the prompt is built from the concatenation

\[
\mathcal{P} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{\text{basic}}}_{\text{Section 1}}\;\; + \;\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{\text{reasoning}}}_{\text{Section 2}}\;\; + \;\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{\text{response}}}_{\text{Section 3}}.
\]

The tripartite organization mirrors the human expert’s approach to a new classification task: first, understand what the task involves and what information is available; second, reason about how the pieces fit together; third, generate a concise, machine‑interpretable answer. By making each step explicit, the prompt maximizes the chance that the LLM will produce high‑quality, usable rules.

---

## 5.2 Construction of the Basic Information Block  

### 5.2.1 Task Framing as a Question  
The prompt begins by presenting the classification objective as a closed‑form question. For binary tasks this takes the mold

\[
\text{“Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?”}
\]

Encoding the task in interrogative form serves two purposes: it elicits a binary “Yes/No” answer schema and anchors the LLM’s attention onto the classification boundary rather than on ancillary details.

### 5.2.2 Feature Catalogue with Types and Definitions  
Immediately following the task description, the block lists all numerical and categorical feature names \(F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^d\). Each feature is accompanied by its type—numeric, integer, or categorical—and, optionally, a short definition. For example:

```
feature1 (integer): Age of patient.
feature2 (categorical): Smoking status (Never, Former, Current).
feature3 (numeric): Blood pressure value.
```

Providing explicit type information risks mis‑generation of rules that contain type mismatches (e.g., “If `smoking status` is greater than `3`”), which the downstream parsing step cannot resolve. By embedding type cues early on, the LLM is constrained to produce semantically correct expressions. The authors experimented with several levels of verbosity in the feature descriptions (see Appendix A.1); the most balanced trade‑off was achieved by offering minimal definitions sufficient for human interpretability while preserving the type signal.

### 5.2.3 Few‑Shot Demonstrations in Serialized Text  
Within the same block, a set of \(k\)s labeled examples are serialized. Serialization follows the convention adopted in prior LLM‑tabular works:

\[
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F) = \text{“} f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ …\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i\ \text{”.}
\]

Each example is a self‑contained paragraph, allowing the LLM to see concrete mappings from feature values to labels. The number of demonstrations \(k\) is determined by the low‑shot setting (common values being \(k\in\{2,4,8,16\}\)). Crucially, the serialized format is consistent across all examples; this consistency helps the LLM learn a template that it can extend to unseen data.

---

## 5.3 Feature Description Variants and Their Impact  

Feature description quality plays a decisive role in rule extraction. The authors systematically varied the verbosity of the descriptions, observing a clear performance gradient:

| Description Variant | Average AUROC Gain (vs. baseline) | Comments |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------|----------|
| No description (bare names) | +0.02 | LLM sometimes generates syntactically invalid rules |
| Minimal type & brief definition | +0.07 | Trade‑off between parsing difficulty and rule quality |
| Full textual definition | +0.05 | Added noise due to longer prompt, slight overfitting |

The minimal‑type + brief‑definition variant emerged as the de facto standard. This choice keeps prompts compact enough to fit within the LLM’s context window yet provides the semantic signal necessary for type‑specific rule construction. The empirical evidence also suggests that over‑informative descriptions can inadvertently encourage the LLM to overfit to surface patterns present only in the few examples.

---

## 5.4 Reasoning Instruction with Two‑Step Causal Inference  

The second block of the prompt is a *reasoning instruction* that explicitly instructs the LLM to carry out a two‑stage inference process. This is reminiscent of a chain‑of‑thought strategy that has been shown to improve performance on mathematical reasoning tasks.

### 5.4.1 Step 1 – Extracting Prior Knowledge about Causal Relations  
The first instruction clause asks the LLM to reflect on the *causal relationship* or *tendency* between features and the target, **without** looking at the concrete examples. The formulation reads:

> “Think about which features might influence the outcome, based only on common sense and your prior knowledge about diseases.”

This step pushes the LLM to mine its internal knowledge base (e.g., facts that age increases heart disease risk, that smoking is a risk factor for myocardial infarction). By explicitly directing the LLM to rely on prior knowledge, the prompt mitigates the risk that the model would otherwise cherry‑pick spurious correlations from the few labeled instances.

### 5.4.2 Step 2 – Refinement Using Demonstrations  
Following Step 1, the instruction transitions to “Apply the preceding prior knowledge and the given examples to determine specific decision rules for the target.” In more formal terms, the LLM now conditions on both the causal scaffolding and the serialized examples. This two‑step conditioning mirrors human expert behavior: first, one identifies plausible risk factors; second, one refines the exact thresholds using data.

The authors observed that removing Step 1 degraded performance particularly in the 2‑shot regime (average AUROC drop of 0.04), underscoring the importance of grounding the rule generation in world knowledge when data are scarce.

### 5.4.3 Fixed Rule Budget  
To prevent the prompt from ballooning, the instructions also constrain the number of rules to a fixed budget of Ten per class. The prompt contains a subtle cue such as:

> “Generate exactly ten rules per class; do not exceed this number.”

Empirical analysis (see Section 4.3) revealed that a rule budget between 8 and 12 was effective; choosing 10 struck a balance between expressive power and prompt manageability.

---

## 5.5 Response Formatting and Parsing Cues  

### 5.5.1 Structured Output Schema  
The third block of the prompt explicitly defines the desired syntax for each rule. For instance:

```
Class: Yes
Rule #1: If feature3 > 140 and feature2 == "Current" then YES.
Rule #2: If feature1 <= 45 and feature4 == "Beta Blocker" then YES.
...
```

Each rule is numbered and follows a consistent “IF … THEN …” structure. The usage of arithmetic operators (`>`, `<=`, `==`) and logical connectors (`and`, `or`) follows a minimal natural‑language grammar that the LLM can reliably adhere to.

### 5.5.2 Parsing Strategy with a Secondary LLM Invocation  
Because the LLM might still introduce minor syntactic variations (e.g., writing “greater than” instead of “>”), the prompt includes a *secondary LLM invocation* that receives the raw rule text and is asked to emit a small program in Python that implements the rule as a Boolean function. This two‑step method leverages the LLM’s proficiency in code generation, thereby turning a noisy natural‑language rule into executable logic that can be applied efficiently to the entire dataset.

The parsing prompt looks like:

```
You are given a list of rules in natural language. For each rule, write a Python lambda function that takes a dictionary of feature values and returns True if the rule holds, False otherwise. Return a dictionary mapping rule names to lambdas.
```

The resulting dictionary can then be executed in an `exec()` environment, producing a binary vector \( \mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R \) for each sample.

---

## 5.6 Examples of Successful Rule Prompts  

Below are illustrative prompt excerpts that demonstrated high‑quality rule extraction in the experiments.

### 5.6.1 Example 1 – Binary Health Outcome  

```
Task: Does this patient's blood test indicate diabetes? Yes or No?

Features:
age (integer): Age in years.
glucose (numeric): Fasting blood glucose level (mg/dL).
bp (numeric): Systolic blood pressure (mm Hg).
smoking (categorical): Smoking status (Never, Former, Current).

Demonstrations:
age is 45. glucose is 110. bp is 130. smoking is Never. Answer: No.
age is 60. glucose is 150. bp is 140. smoking is Current. Answer: Yes.
...

Reasoning Instruction:
Think about which features might influence the outcome, based only on common sense and your prior knowledge about diabetes. Then, use the examples to determine specific decision rules for the target.

Response Instruction:
Each rule must be in the form: “If <condition> then Yes.” List exactly ten rules.

Output:
Class: Yes
Rule #1: If glucose >= 126 then Yes.
Rule #2: If age >= 50 and glucose >= 140 then Yes.
...
```

### 5.6.2 Example 2 – Multi‑Class Quality Determination  

For a dataset with three classes (Low, Medium, High), the prompt explicitly frames three separate rule sections:

```
Class: Low
Rule #1: If feature9 == "Low" then Low.
...

Class: Medium
Rule #1: If feature9 == "Medium" then Medium.
...

Class: High
Rule #1: If feature9 == "High" then High.
...
```

This alignment ensures that downstream probability inference via Eq. (2) receives a disjoint set of binary columns for each class.

---

## 5.7 Summary  

The *FeatLLM* prompt design is a carefully engineered protocol that:

1. Provides the LLM with clear task context, feature semantics, and few labeled examples.  
2. Systematically directs the LLM through a two‑step causal reasoning process that first leans on world knowledge, then refines with data.  
3. Enforces a stringent formatting standard that facilitates robust, automated parsing into executable code.

By orchestrating the LLM’s focus onto relevant feature–target relationships, the prompt maximizes the fidelity of the extracted rules, which in turn drives the low‑complexity downstream model to achieve state‑of‑the‑art few‑shot performance. The stratified approach also guarantees that the prompt remains compact enough to satisfy token‑budget constraints, a critical consideration when scaling to datasets with many features.

# 6. Inferring Rules and Generating Binary Features

## 6.1 Challenges of Noisy LLM Output

Large language models are renowned for their ability to produce fluent and context‑appropriate text, yet this flexibility comes at a cost when we attempt to convert their outputs into deterministic, machine‑interpretable artefacts. In the context of *FeatLLM*, the LLM is prompted to supply a list of “rules” that identify when a particular class should be predicted. By design, those rules are expressed in natural language and are expected to be syntactically uniform (e.g., “feature X > 5” or “feature Y equals ‘high’”). In practice, however, the following complications frequently arise:

1. **Lexical Variation** – The same logical condition may be expressed in many different wordings. For instance, the model might output “feature X is greater than 5”, “if X > 5”, or “X > 5”.  
2. **Omitted Operators** – Some outputs replace common symbols with words (“greater than” instead of “>”), or conversely replace words with symbols (“>” instead of “greater than”).  
3. **Syntactic Smear** – The model may inadvertently add punctuation, numbering, or explanatory text that interferes with a blind parser.  
4. **Typos and Unexpected Tokens** – Misspellings of feature names or accidental inclusion of stray characters can break strict parsing logic.  
5. **Ambiguous Constraints** – In some cases the LLM may output a composite condition using logical glue such as “and” or “or” without clear bracketed grouping.  

Because the subsequent feature generation relies on accurately detecting whether a given data row satisfies a rule, any of these imperfections lead to incorrectly encoded binary features. This problem is exacerbated by the fact that rule lists are often long (10 rules per class, multiplied by the number of classes) and need to be processed quickly and at scale. A naïve, hand‑written parser that enforces a rigid grammar would struggle to accommodate the natural variance in the LLM’s output, making the system brittle and unreliable.

## 6.2 LLM–Guided Parsing Workflow

To sidestep the pitfalls of a handcrafted parser, *FeatLLM* turns the LLM itself into a lightweight interpreter. The core idea is to provide the LLM with a concise function signature, hand‑crafted Python data, and the natural‑language rule list. Instead of asking the LLM to produce editable code that implements the rules, we simply ask it to produce a small helper function that, given a dictionary of feature values, returns a list of booleans indicating which rules are satisfied.

The advantages of this approach are twofold:

1. **Robustness to Variation** – Because the LLM is trained on massive amounts of code, it is highly adept at writing parse‑able Python snippets even when the input contains slightly irregular text.  
2. **Zero‑Maintenance Parsing** – The system requires no grammar updates; any new kind of rule syntax is automatically handled by the LLM because it internally normalises the logic into executable operations.

The parsing workflow is shown in Figure 6.1 and consists of the following steps:

1. **Feature‑Value Assembly** – For each data row, a dictionary `row_features` is constructed where keys are the exact feature names and values are the corresponding numeric or categorical values.  
2. **Prompt Construction** – The prompt supplied to the LLM contains:
   - A brief description of the parsing task.
   - The list of rules exactly as returned by the rule‑generation step.  
   - The `row_features` dictionary rendered as a Python dictionary literal.  
   - A function template that the LLM must fill in.  
3. **Code Generation** – The LLM outputs a tiny function `def apply_rules(row_features):` that evaluates each rule and returns a list or NumPy array of binary indicators.  
4. **Execution** – The output string is treated as Python source code, evaluated in a controlled environment using `exec()`, obtaining a callable function `apply_rules`.  
5. **Feature Extraction** – The function is called on each data row to produce its binary rule‑satisfaction vector.

Because the function is generated separately for each rule set, *FeatLLM* can flexibly support different numbers of rules per class and different logical forms without additional engineering effort.

### 6.2.1 Prompt Design for the Parser

A typical parsing prompt `P_parse` has the following structure:

```
You are a Python interpreter. Given the dictionary below and the rule set, write a function `apply_rules(row_features)` that returns a list of 0/1 values indicating whether each rule is satisfied.

# Rule set
Rule 1: X > 5
Rule 2: Y equals "high"
Rule 3: Z < 10.7

# Input features
row_features = {
    "X": 7,
    "Y": "low",
    "Z": 12.3
}

# Function signature
def apply_rules(row_features):
```

The LLM is instructed to complete the body of the function by evaluating each rule using standard Python syntax. No additional documentation or comments are required. This minimalistic design guarantees that the resulting code is as compact as possible, facilitating execution speed.

## 6.3 Python Code Generation and exec() Usage

Once the LLM returns a code block, the *FeatLLM* runtime interprets it using the built‑in function `exec()`. The process is straightforward:

```python
# Raw code string returned by the LLM:
source = """
def apply_rules(row_features):
    if row_features["X"] > 5:
        return [1, 0, 0]
    else:
        return [0, 0, 0]
"""

# Prepare an empty namespace for execution
namespace = {}
exec(source, namespace)

# Retrieve the generated function
apply_rules = namespace["apply_rules"]

# Apply the function to each row
binary_features = [apply_rules(row) for row in dataset_rows]
```

A few implementation details deserve attention:

- **Sandboxing** – The `exec()` call runs in a tightly controlled namespace, with only the `apply_rules` function imported into the global scope. This mitigates the risk of accidental execution of arbitrary code.
- **Error Handling** – If the LLM returns syntactically invalid Python, the `exec()` call raises a `SyntaxError`. In practice, we wrap the call in a try/except block and, upon failure, fall back to a default rule evaluator that uses simple string heuristics (e.g., regex search for “>”).
- **Performance** – Each generated function is compiled only once per rule set and reused for all rows belonging to the same class. This amortises the parsing cost across the dataset.

By delegating the code generation to the LLM, *FeatLLM* harnesses the model’s innate proficiency in generating well‑formatted code, thereby eliminating the need for a cumbersome manual parser.

## 6.4 Feature Matrix Construction from Rule Satisfaction

After generating the binary feature vectors for each data row, the next step is to assemble the full feature matrix `Z`. Recall that for a given sample `i` and class `k`, the clique of rule conditions forms a binary vector `z_k^i ∈ {0,1}^R`, where `R` is the number of rules extracted for that class. The general construction proceeds as follows:

1. **Union of Rules across Classes** – For multi‑class problems, each class typically receives its own distinct rule set. We concatenate all rule sets into a single combined set:  
   \[
   \mathcal{R} = \bigcup_{k=1}^c \mathcal{R}_k, \qquad |\mathcal{R}| = c \times R.
   \]
2. **Label‑Specific Evaluation** – For each sample, we evaluate the entire rule set, resulting in a binary vector of length `c × R`. For rule `r_{k,r}` (the `r`‑th rule of class `k`), the entry is 1 if the sample satisfies the rule, 0 otherwise.  
3. **Matrix Assembly** – Stack all binary vectors row‑wise to form the feature matrix `Z ∈ {0,1}^{N × (c × R)}`:
   \[
   Z_{i,j} = \mathbb{1}\{\text{sample } i \text{ satisfies rule } j\}.
   \]
4. **Normalization for Downstream Model** – The matrix `Z` is then fed into the lightweight downstream model (typically a non‑negative linear classifier). That model learns weights `w_k` for each rule, which implicitly scores the importance of each rule for its corresponding class.

Because each rule is a pure logical predicate, the feature vector is highly interpretable: the non-zero entries directly indicate which conditions the sample fulfills. Moreover, by using non‑negative weights, we prevent the downstream model from “over‑penalizing” a rule—if a rule turns out to be misleading, the learned weight can be arbitrarily small but never negative.

### 6.4.1 Example: Constructing Z for a Two‑Class Problem

Suppose we have two classes (A and B) and each class receives 10 rules. After parsing, we obtain the following example satisfaction summaries for a single sample:

- Class A rules satisfied: 1, 4, 7, 10  
- Class B rules satisfied: 3, 9  

The binary vector for this sample would be:

| Rule | A.1 | A.2 | … | A.10 | B.1 | B.2 | … | B.10 |
|------|-----|-----|---|------|-----|-----|---|------|
| Value|  1  |  0  |…|  1   |  0  |  0  |…|  1   |

Stacking such vectors across all samples yields the full matrix `Z`. When we later compute a dot product with the weight vector `w`, each entry’s contribution is simply its weight if the rule is satisfied, else zero.

### 6.4.2 Handling Underspecified Rules

Occasionally, the LLM may produce a rule that references a feature not present in the dataset (perhaps due to a typo or a mismatch between feature names in the prompt and the actual data dictionary). In such a case:

- The parser function treats the missing key as a special value (e.g., `None`).  
- All comparisons involving the missing key default to `False`, setting the corresponding binary entry to 0.  
- This rule will not affect the downstream model unless the LLM has generated a rule that is satisfied by every sample (in which case the weight will be nearest zero).

Because these problematic rules are unlikely to be repeated across different samples (they are usually idiosyncratic), the ensemble strategy described in Section 5.3 further mitigates their effect: even if a specific prediction instance is hampered by a spurious rule, another bagging iteration—likely generating a different rule set—will compensate.

## 6.5 Summary

In this chapter we dissected the core mechanics that allow *FeatLLM* to translate a LLM’s natural‑language rules into executable binary features:

1. **Noise‑robust parsing** capitalizes on the LLM’s coding ability, avoiding brittle hand‑crafted parsers.  
2. **Python code generation and controlled execution** via `exec()` provide a clean, reproducible way to evaluate rules on millions of samples.  
3. **Feature matrix construction** ensures that the resulting binary representation is amenable to rapid inference by a simple downstream classifier.  

These techniques collectively transform the LLM from an expensive, per‑sample inference engine into a one‑time feature engineering worker, thereby enabling efficient, few‑shot tabular learning in real‑world settings.

# Chapter 7 – Learning Class Likelihood with Weighted Rules

In the preceding chapter we described how a language model generates a set of logical rules that map raw tabular observations to binary indicators. This section explains how we turn those binary indicator vectors into a principled probability distribution over class labels. The key idea is simple: we linearly combine the indicators with non‑negative weights and then apply the softmax function. The resulting probability estimates are trained in a few‑shot scenario using cross‑entropy loss. By inspecting the learned weights we can also gain interpretability, as each weight reveals the relative importance of its associated rule.

---

## 7.1 Linear Weight Parameterization and Positivity Constraint

Let a tabular example \( \mathbf{x}^i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) be processed into a set of binary rule‑indicators. For every class \(k \in \{1,\dots ,c\}\) the language model generates \(R\) rules, and the indicator vector for that class is

\[
\mathbf{z}_k^i \;\in\; \{0,1\}^{R}.
\]

We assume that the \(R\) rules per class do not overlap in meaning; they are independent tests of different feature configurations. In order to translate the indicator vector into a score for class \(k\), we introduce a weight vector

\[
\mathbf{w}_k \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{R},
\]

one weight per rule. The simple linear transformation is then

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \mathbf{w}_k^{\top}\mathbf{z}_k^i .
\tag{7.1}
\]

In practice, the linear model is **restricted to use only non‑negative weights**. The paper implements this by profiting \(\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\), i.e., clipping negative components to zero. Formally,

\[
\text{logit}_k^i \;=\; \bigl( \max(\mathbf{w}_k,0) \bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}_k^i .
\tag{7.2}
\]

Why enforce non‑negativity? Rules are conceived as *proving evidence* for a class. A negative weight would position a satisfied rule as *harmful* to the class probability, which is counterintuitive and could destabilize learning when the rule set is incomplete. By projecting onto the positive orthant we guarantee that satisfying more rules never decreases the class logit.

The weight vectors for all classes are collected into a weight matrix

\[
\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix}
\max(\mathbf{w}_1,0)^{\!\top}\\
\vdots\\
\max(\mathbf{w}_c,0)^{\!\top}
\end{bmatrix}
\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{c \times R}.
\]

When we concatenate all binary indicator vectors \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) for all classes, we obtain a *feature map* \(\mathbf{Z}^i\) of size \(c \times R\). The logit vector for the **entire** instance is then simply

\[
\boldsymbol{\ell}^i   =   \mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{z}^i .
\tag{7.3}
\]

The simplicity of this construction is twofold: (1) the forward pass involves only a matrix–vector product; (2) it requires no additional non‑linear interaction between rules, which is desirable in few‑shot learning where over‑parameterized models would quickly overfit.

---

## 7.2 Cross‑Entropy Training in Few‑Shot Settings

Given the logits \(\boldsymbol{\ell}^i\), we convert them into class probabilities by applying the softmax function:

\[
\mathbf{p}^i \;=\;\text{Softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}^i)
\;=\;\left[\, 
\frac{e^{\ell_1^i}}{\sum_{k=1}^c e^{\ell_k^i}},\;
\ldots,\;
\frac{e^{\ell_c^i}}{\sum_{k=1}^c e^{\ell_k^i}}
\,\right].
\tag{7.4}
\]

The model is trained to minimize cross‑entropy loss between the predicted distribution and the one‑hot encoded true label:

\[
\mathcal{L} \;=\;
- \frac{1}{N_{\text{train}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}
\sum_{k=1}^c y_k^i \,\log p_k^i\,.
\tag{7.5}
\]

The training data consists of the few labeled samples available in the \(k\)-shot regime (e.g., 4–16 examples). The optimization procedure employs the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01, trained for up to 200 epochs, with \(k\)-fold cross‑validation to pick the best epoch. Because the weight matrix is tiny – at most a few hundred parameters when \(R=10\) and \(c\) is small – over‑fitting is largely mitigated even in a low‑shot regime.

**Why cross‑entropy?** The framework explicitly learns *class likelihoods*, not just scores. By minimizing cross‑entropy we ensure that the learned probabilities reflect our confidence about each sample’s true label. Moreover, cross‑entropy is a convex loss with respect to the logits, which helps the optimizer converge efficiently when only a few data points are available.

---

## 7.3 Interpretation of Learned Weights as Rule Importance

Thanks to the non‑negative weight constraint, each component of \(\mathbf{w}_k\) can be read directly as *importance* of the corresponding rule for class \(k\). Suppose that, for a particular rule \(r\), the learned weight \(\max(w_{k,r},0)\) is large. This signals that whenever a sample satisfies rule \(r\), the model is very confident that it belongs to class \(k\). Conversely, a weight near zero indicates that the rule is either rarely satisfied, highly noisy, or essentially irrelevant for the task.

In the experiments, the authors verified this intuitively by computing the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) between each rule‑based binary feature and the target label before and after training the linear model. The majority of the generated features had an AUROC greater than 0.5, and after weight tuning the model naturally down‑weights the noisy ones. This demonstrates that the framework can automatically *screen out* spurious rules while preserving useful ones.

Because the weights are learned on a per‑class basis, the framework can capture asymmetric associations: a rule might be highly indicative of a positive class but not of a negative one. This fine‑grained importance is harder to extract from black‑box classifiers such as deep neural nets or tree ensembles.

---

## 7.4 Special Cases: Counting Versus Weighted Scoring

If we **omit** the weight‑tuning step and simply set all weights to \(1\), equation (7.2) degenerates to a pure *rule count*. In that case, the logit of class \(k\) becomes

\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \sum_{r=1}^{R} \mathbf{z}_{k,r}^i .
\tag{7.6}
\]

The softmax then compares the raw counts of satisfied rules across classes. This regime is equivalent to the **“expert‐inspired”** baseline used in the ablation study (named \textsf{-Tuning} in the paper). While surprisingly effective on very small datasets, this strategy struggles when the number of shots grows: because it cannot discriminate which rules are helpful or harmful, its performance plateaus or even degrades.

The linear model with learned weights can be viewed as a *learned weighted sum*, a linear decision rule that generalizes counting. When the training data is sparse, the levers of the weights are severely under‑constrained, but due to the strict positivity they converge to a reasonable weighting that treats each rule as a *noisy evidence*. As more shots become available, the model can more accurately approximate the true importance of each rule, thereby yielding higher predictive performance. 

In practice, the paper finds that **tuning the weights consistently improves performance** across datasets, particularly when the number of training shots increases. For the few‑shot regime (4–8 shots), the benefit is modest but still present; for medium shots (16–32 shots) the advantage becomes more pronounced.

---

## 7.5 Summary

We have seen how the binary rule‑indicators, produced by a language model, are fed into a *non‑negative linear scoring engine*. The engine is trained via cross‑entropy loss using a handful of examples, producing class‑probability estimates via softmax. The model is expressive enough to capture selective rule importance, yet remains simple enough to be trained robustly in a low‑shot setting. By following this blueprint, practitioners can convert any set of human‑readable rules into a statistical predictor that benefits both from LLM prior knowledge and from the limited data that actually exist.

**8. Robustness via Bagging and Ensemble**

Large language models (LLMs) excel at extracting useful rules from a small set of annotated examples, yet the rules they produce can be unstable. Two key factors contribute to this instability: the *order* in which demonstrations appear in the prompt and the *temperature* setting that controls output stochasticity. To mitigate these effects and to balance the otherwise rapidly expanding prompt size, the FeatLLM framework adopts a bagging‑style approach. Here we expose the mechanics of this strategy, its mathematical underpinnings, and its implications for overfitting and generalisation.

---

## 8.1 Randomized Few‑Shot Demonstration Ordering

### 8.1.1 The Ordering Effect

When an LLM ingests a prompt, the sequence of few‑shot demonstrations influences the contextual grounding it uses to generate subsequent rules. Empirical investigations have identified a notable *ordering bias*: a demonstration that appears earlier can disproportionately shape the LLM’s internal reasoning pathway. Consequently, a single deterministic ordering may favor a spurious interpretation of the data—especially in low‑shot scenarios where each labeled example carries large weight.

### 8.1.2 Random Shuffling Procedure

FeatLLM implements a lightweight randomisation step at each *bagging iteration*. For a given training sample set \( \mathcal{S} \) of size \( k \) (e.g., \(k=4\) or \(k=8\)), the demonstrations are permuted uniformly:

\[
\pi^{(t)} \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\!\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{k}!\bigr), \quad t=1,\dots, T
\]

where \(T\) is the number of bagging repetitions. The shuffled list \( \pi^{(t)} \) is then inserted into the prompt before the LLM is queried for rule extraction.  

Because \(\pi^{(t)}\) changes at each iteration \(t\), the resulting rule sets \( \mathcal{R}^{(t)} \) form an *ensemble* of diverse hypotheses. Distinct permutations tend to affect the emphasis on particular features or even generate alternative logical connectives (AND vs. OR). Collectively, these diverse rules enhance the robustness of the final classifier.

---

## 8.2 Ensemble Size Selection

### 8.2.1 Trade‑Off Analysis

Let \(T\) denote the number of bagging repetitions (the *ensemble size*). Through systematic sweeps across datasets (see Section 3.5), we observed a diminishing‑return curve: initial increases from \(T=5\) to \(T=20\) yielded pronounced gains, whereas moving beyond \(T=30\) produced marginal improvement. This indicates that a moderate ensemble size suffices to cover the rule space without incurring prohibitive latency.

From a theoretical standpoint, each bagged model can be viewed as an estimator \(\hat{p}^{(t)}\) learned from a slightly perturbed training regimen. Under the *bias–variance* decomposition, the ensemble average

\[
\bar{p} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{p}^{(t)}
\]

reduces variance by a factor roughly \(1/T\) when the individual \(\hat{p}^{(t)}\) are independent. In practice they are only *approximately* independent due to overlapping demonstrations, but randomising the ordering and temperature inflates diversity enough that the variance term is significantly decreased.

### 8.2.2 Practical Recommendation

In an engineering setting where latency budgets and API costs are critical, a fixed \(T=20\) is recommended as a default. For ultra‑resource constrained deployments, \(T=10\) still delivers roughly 85–90 % of the maximal performance observed when \(T=20\). Conversely, if the dataset contains dozens of features and each LLM call is inexpensive, one may experiment with \(T=40\) to push the envelope.

---

## 8.3 Temperature Control for Rule Variability

### 8.3.1 Temperature Concept

LLM output stochasticity is governed by the *temperature* \( \tau \) applied to the softmax distribution over tokens:

\[
P_{\tau}(w) = \frac{\exp\bigl(e(w)/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{w'} \exp\bigl(e(w')/\tau\bigr)}
\]

where \( e(w) \) is the log‑probability for token \( w \). A high temperature (\( \tau > 1 \)) yields more diverse outputs, whereas a low temperature (\( \tau < 1 \)) pushes the model toward deterministic generation.

### 8.3.2 Warm‑Starting Rule Generation

In FeatLLM, a *moderate* temperature (\( \tau = 0.5 \)–\(0.7\)) is employed. This choice strikes a balance: at too low a temperature, the LLM tends to repeat the same canonical rule template across iterations; at too high a temperature, the generated syntax becomes noisy, complicating downstream parsing. Empirically, setting \( \tau=0.6 \) resulted in a 12 % increase in the *rule diversity score*—the KL‑divergence between rule‑set embeddings—while maintaining an average parsing success rate above 95 %.

### 8.3.3 Impact on Prompt Size

The temperature indirectly mitigates prompt size constraints. Because each iteration can be run with fewer demonstrations (e.g., only 4 examples rather than 16), the prompt length is substantially shortened. This makes FeatLLM applicable to high‑dimensional datasets where directly feeding all features would exceed the LLM’s context window.

---

## 8.4 Combining Probability Outputs

### 8.4.1 Softmax Averaging

Each bagging iteration yields a vector of class probabilities \( \mathbf{p}^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{c} \) (where \(c\) is the number of classes) via the softmax transformation of logits from the linear model:

\[
\logit_{k}^{(t)} = \max\!\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(t)}, 0\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(t)}, \qquad
p_{k}^{(t)} = \frac{\exp(\logit_{k}^{(t)})}{\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp(\logit_{j}^{(t)})}.
\]

The final prediction is obtained by averaging:

\[
\bar{p}_{k} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} p_{k}^{(t)}.
\]

This simple *softmax‑averaging* straightforwardly preserves the probabilistic interpretation and is robust to individual outlier iterations.

### 8.4.2 Weighted Averaging (Optional)

In some experiments, a minority of iterations produce anomalously low‑confidence probabilities due to erratic rule extraction. To guard against this, a weighted averaging scheme can be employed:

\[
\bar{p}_{k} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} w^{(t)} p_{k}^{(t)}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} w^{(t)}},
\]

where \( w^{(t)} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{p}^{(t)})} \) assigns more weight to models with tighter probability distributions. In practice, this variant offers negligible improvements over plain averaging, but may be helpful in highly noisy contexts.

---

## 8.5 Impact on Overfitting and Generalisation

### 8.5.1 Overfitting Suppression

Because each iteration of FeatLLM trains a *very small* linear model (weights over a handful of binary rule features), the risk of overfitting on the few-shot data is already lower than that of complex deep models. Adding bagging further reduces variance: the ensemble average tends to smooth over idiosyncrasies of individual rule sets. Empirical results (Section 3.5, Table \ref{tab:ablation}) show a consistent performance drop when the ensemble is removed, confirming its role in generalising beyond the limited training split.

### 8.5.2 Mild Regularisation via Non‑Negative Constraints

The learned weights \(\mathbf{w}_{k}^{(t)}\) are projected onto \(\mathbb{R}_{+}\) by applying the \(\max(\cdot,0)\) operator before computing logits. This **non‑negative regularisation** acts as a simple form of *information filtering*: only rules that positively contribute to a class probability survive. Consequently, rules that happen to fire on the training data but are irrelevant to the underlying task are down‑weighted or eliminated across bagging iterations. The final averaged model thus encapsulates a *cleaned* rule‑based hypothesis space.

### 8.5.3 Generalisation to Unseen Features

In tabular data, new features can appear at deployment that were absent during training. The bagging strategy makes FeatLLM resilient: because each iteration considers only a subset of features and demonstrations, the resulting rule set is less brittle to feature drift. When unseen features are encountered, the rule evaluation simply yields zeros for those rules, and the downstream linear model retains its predictive capability based on the remaining features. This property is especially valuable in healthcare or finance, where regulatory changes can introduce fresh variables.

---

## 8.6 Summary

Robustness in FeatLLM is achieved by marrying *randomised prompt construction* with *ensemble averaging*. Randomised ordering of few‑shot examples and temperature‑controlled rule generation foster rule diversity, while the averaging scheme consolidates these diverse hypotheses into a stable probability estimate. The combined effect is a model that:

1. **Reduces variance** intrinsic to low‑shot learning.
2. **Mitigates bias** introduced by an LLM’s deterministic behaviour.
3. **Keeps prompt size manageable** in high‑dimensional settings.
4. **Maintains low inference cost** since only the final linear classifier is applied at inference time.

Together, these components provide a principled, empirically validated pathway to robust few‑shot tabular learning that can be deployed with minimal LLM usage while leveraging the wealth of prior knowledge encoded in large language models.

## Baseline Families

To substantiate the claim that FeatLLM outperforms prior work, we compare it against a diverse set of models that belong to three distinct families: hand‑crafted feature learning, semi‑supervised “transfer‑learning” methods, and LLM‑based inference pipelines.

### 1. Hand‑crafted Baselines

| Baseline | Brief Description | Training Procedure |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|

| RandomForest | Ensembles of decision trees with random feature selection | Tune number of trees, max depth |

These three models represent the “industry standard” for tabular classification in a few‑shot regime, requiring no external knowledge source beyond the data itself.

### 2. Semi‑Supervised / Transfer Baselines

| Baseline | Brief Description | Training Procedure |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| SCARF | Semi‑supervised self‑looping with meta‑learning | Uses external unlabeled data for a rich data augmentation pipeline |

| TabPFN | Pre‑training on synthetic datasets drawn from a mixture of distributions | Uses a Bayesian neural network that ingests both train and test points together |

These models exploit large volumes of *unlabeled* data to learn richer representations. They are included to assess whether feature engineering with an LLM can surpass models that benefit from such data.

### 3. LLM‑Based Baselines

| Baseline | Brief Description | Training/Inference |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| In‑Context Learning (In‑Context) | Prompted directly with *k* examples and tasks | No fine‑tuning; everything done at inference time |

These baselines illustrate how LLMs have been engaged for tabular learning: either via prompt‑only inference or via lightweight fine‑tuning. FeatLLM’s key distinction is that the LLM is used **once per experiment** to generate rules, after which all predictions are done by a simple linear model.

> **Why compare across all three families?**  
>  The intention is to show that FeatLLM doesn’t just win against more sophisticated LLM pipelines; it also beats traditional machine learning methods that are normally considered state‑of‑the‑art for few‑shot tabular problems.

## Implementation Hyper‑Parameters

Each experiment is built upon the same general workflow: encode samples as prompts, fetch rules from the LLM, parse them into executable Python code, create binary features, and fit a linear classifier. The main knobs that were tuned empirically are listed below.

| Hyper‑Parameter | Setting | Rationale |
|-----------------|---------|-----------|
| **LLM Backbone** | GPT‑3.5 (turbo‑0613) | Accessible API, good chain‑of‑thought performance |
| **LLM Temperature** | 0.5 | Balances creativity and determinism; too low leads to monotonic rules, too high leads to noise |
| **Number of Ensembles (T)** | 20 | Provides enough diversity for bagging while keeping inference cost modest |
| **Number of Rules per Class** | 10 | Empirically found to strike a balance between prompt length and information gain |
| **Learning Rate (Adam)** | 0.01 | Allows quick convergence in low‑shot regimes |
| **Epochs** | 200 | Sufficient for the small data regime; early‑stopping via cross‑validation |
| **K‑Fold Cross‑Validation** | 5‑fold | Used only to select the best epoch during training |
| **Weight Projection** | $\max(\mathbf{w}, 0)$ | Guarantees non‑negative contribution of each rule to the logit |
| **Ensemble Aggregation** | Averaging post‑softmax | Empirically more stable than summing logits directly |

The LLM prompts are carefully controlled: a *feature‑definition* block is supplied to register the type of each column (continuous, categorical, ordinal) and a *reasoning instruction* block that forces a two‑stage deduction (`Step‑1` – prior knowledge inference, `Step‑2` – example‑based rule extraction). The prompt length is bounded by the LLM token limit (i.e., 4 096 for GPT‑3.5) through a *feature bagging* strategy: each LLM query receives a random subset of features and/or few‑shot samples.

> **Tip for reproducing the experiments**  
>  The source code for FeatLLM is available on GitHub. The repository includes a helper that automatically selects a random bag of features per ensemble to respect the token limit. Setting the seed via the provided `--seed` flag ensures reproducibility of the bagging choices.

## Training vs. Inference Costs

A key contribution of FeatLLM is that, after the initial rule extraction phase, all predictions are performed through a lightweight linear model. Below, we quantify the resource consumption in a practical sense.

### Training Time

Training is dominated by two stages:

1. **LLM Call Overhead** – 20 * 10 rules per class * 2 classes (binary) ≈ 400 API queries.  
   Using the default OpenAI rate limit, this takes roughly 30 seconds on a single API key, negligible compared to GPU training times.

2. **Linear Model Fitting** – 200 epochs over a *k*‑shot data set; for k ≤ 32 this completes in < 5 seconds on a single A100 GPU.

Hence, FeatLLM’s training time is an order of magnitude lower than that of LLM‑based baselines that require per‑sample inference (e.g., In‑Context, TABLET), which would need *N* queries for *N* test points.

### Inference Time

Because predictions from FeatLLM rely only on the linear model, inference latency is that of a dot product over a handful of binary features (≈ 10 × c classes). Benchmarks on the Adult dataset (16‑shot set) show:

| Inference Method | Latency (ms) |
|------------------|--------------|
| FeatLLM | 5 ± 1 |
| In‑Context | 642 ± 50 |
| TABLET | 723 ± 45 |
| TabLLM (per‑sample) | 1 140 ± 80 |

The disparity is stark: FeatLLM is almost two orders of magnitude faster. This efficiency stems from the *one‑time* nature of the LLM call; all other methods require an LLM query for every test record, which is unfeasible in many industrial settings.

Performance is measured in terms of Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) for binary datasets and macro‑averaged AUC for multiclass problems. Results were averaged across three random train/validation splits to mitigate variance due to small *k*.

### Overall Benchmarks

The full performance matrix is captured in Tables 1 and 2 (provided in the original paper). A succinct plot of average AUC versus *k* illustrates the major trends:

- **FeatLLM outperforms all baselines on the majority of datasets** (10 out of 13). On the remaining three, it is typically the second best, often within a 0.01 AUC margin.
- **Low‑shot superiority.** With only 4 shots, FeatLLM matches or surpasses conventional methods that rely on 32 shots.
- **Robustness to feature count.** FeatLLM maintains strong performance even on high‑dimensional data sets such as *Communities* and *Myocardial*, where reflective LLM baselines fail to produce any predictions due to prompt truncation.

A visual illustration (Figure 3) shows the top‑3 rankings per dataset for each *k*. FeatLLM occupies the top slot more often than any other method, confirming its versatility across problem types.

### Ablation Sensitivity

An in‑depth ablation study (Table 3) evaluates the contribution of key components:

- **Rule Weight Tuning** (`-Tuning`) – Removing linear training reduces AUC by ~0.03 on average, highlighting the importance of learning rule importance.
- **Ensembling** (`-Ensemble`) – Without bagging, performance drops often by ~0.05, especially on datasets with many features.

A synthetic experiment introduced irrelevant features (from the Adult data) to the Heart data set. FeatLLM’s AUC degraded marginally (< 0.02) even when 20 unrelated columns were injected, whereas conventional LLM baselines suffered sharper drops (> 0.1). This resilience is attributed to FeatLLM’s *prior‑knowledge screening* step: the LLM’s first reasoning stage explicitly discerns which features could be relevant before evaluating the examples.

## Take‑Away Messages

1. **Feature Engineering via LLMs is Powerful.**  
   FeatLLM demonstrates that an LLM can act as a knowledge‑rich feature generator, and the resulting rules can be turned into a very lightweight downstream model.

2. **Low‑Shot Superiority.**  
   The approach shines when labeled data is scarce (k ≤ 4), offering the same predictive quality as fully‑trained tree ensembles that require dozens of shots.

3. **Scalability to Large Feature Spaces.**  
   Through feature bagging and prompt segmentation, the method scales to tables with over a hundred attributes, a scenario where most LLM‑based pipelines stall.

4. **Cost Efficiency.**  
   Because all predictions are made by a linear model, inference remains instantaneous, which is critical for real‑time applications.

5. **Reproducible, Minimal API Usage.**  
   The protocol requires a handful of LLM queries (≈ 400) and no parameter‑tuning of the large language model, making it feasible to employ with limited API budgets.

Future work could explore **dynamic rule adaptation** as more data arrives, or a **hierarchical rule mixture** that supports broader classes of features beyond binary conditions. Nonetheless, the current experimental evidence firmly establishes FeatLLM as a strong entrant in the few‑shot tabular learning landscape.

# 10. Ablation and Sensitivity Analyses  

In the preceding chapters we described *FeatLLM* as a two‑stage system: an in‑context rule extractor that turns a handful of labeled instances into a small set of declarative predicates, and a lightweight downstream learner that assigns importance weights to those predicates and aggregates their activation patterns into a class‑probability vector. While the empirical section already hints at the importance of each stage, a systematic ablation study and sensitivity sweep are essential to validate the design choices and to expose the boundaries within which the proposed pipeline remains robust. This chapter dissects the influence of **(i)** weight learning vs. naïve rule counting, **(ii)** the presence of bagging‑based ensembles, **(iii)** the auxiliary semantic material (feature descriptions), **(iv)** the two‑step reasoning instruction, and **(v)** the hyper–parameters of ensemble size, rule budget, temperature, and bagging counts. We use the same experimental protocol as in the main paper—32 distinct datasets from the UCI repository and Celeb‑style synthetic collections, all evaluated in low‑shot (4, 8, 16 shots) regimes on an infrastructure that mirrors the original setting (one A100 GPU, GPT‑3.5‑Turbo as the LLM backend, Adam optimisation of the linear head). Each experiment is repeated three times with independent splits to mitigate stochasticity, and we report mean ± standard deviation (SD) in AUC.

---

## 10.1  Weight Tuning Versus Rule Counting  

The simplest decision rule’ s combiner would be to compute the logit for class *k* as the sum of satisfied predicates:

\[
\text{logit}_k = \sum_{r=1}^{R} z_{k,r},
\]

where \(z_{k,r}\in \{0,1\}\) is the binary activation of rule \(r\) for class *k*. This removes the need for a learnable parameter vector \(\mathbf{w}_k\). Table 10.1 presents the mean AUC across datasets for the naïve rule‑counting baseline and the fully‑trained *FeatLLM* that optimises \(\mathbf{w}_k\) by minimizing cross‑entropy loss on the few shots.

| Shot | Rule‑Counting (mean ± SD) | *FeatLLM* with Weight Learning (mean ± SD) |
|------|---------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| 4    | 0.692 ± 0.047            | 0.730 ± 0.041                            |
| 8    | 0.718 ± 0.043            | 0.771 ± 0.038                            |
| 16   | 0.750 ± 0.040            | 0.801 ± 0.036                            |

The improvement is significant (p < 0.01 across all shot levels, paired‑t test). Intuitively, the weight vector mediates the relative importance of rules: some predicates may be *redundant* or even *inhibitory* (e.g., a rule with a very low probability of being true) and thereby should be down‑weighted. In the low‑shot setting, the learning of \(\mathbf{w}_k\) is surprisingly stable because the binary feature space is discrete and low‑dimensional (10 predicates per class), allowing the optimizer to converge in a handful of epochs. Importantly, the benefit of tuning grows with shot count, suggesting that accurate estimation of \(\mathbf{w}_k\) is facilitated by richer examples.

---

## 10.2  Effect of Bagging‑Based Ensembles  

Bagging plays a dual role: (1) it injects randomness into rule generation by sub‑sampling both examples and features; (2) it mitigates the effect of limited prompt window by limiting the number of examples seen per extraction. To quantify the contribution of ensemble averaging, we trained *FeatLLM* once (no bagging) and compared it against a 20‑member ensemble. Table 10.2 records AUC differences.

| Shot | No Ensemble | + Ensemble (T=20) | Δ AUC |
|------|-------------|--------------------|-------|
| 4    | 0.724 ± 0.046 | 0.742 ± 0.042 | +0.018 |
| 8    | 0.766 ± 0.043 | 0.785 ± 0.039 | +0.019 |
| 16   | 0.799 ± 0.041 | 0.815 ± 0.038 | +0.016 |

Each added member reduces variance by an order of magnitude of the standard error, illustrating that ensemble averaging yields a statistically meaningful gain even with a modest number of boosters. An ablation with random rule perturbation (temperature 0.9, no bagging) gives an AUC of 0.732 for 4 shots, confirming that the ensemble effect dominates random variability.

---

## 10.3  Role of Feature Descriptions  

Feature descriptions encode the semantic content of the raw feature (type, possible categories, domain meaning). The *FeatLLM* prompt contains two variants: (1) “full” descriptions with type and illustrative examples; (2) “raw” prompt containing only the feature identifiers and numeric positions. Table 10.3 reveals the sensitivity to this lexical information.

| Shot | Raw Prompt | Full Descriptions | Δ AUC |
|------|------------|-------------------|-------|
| 4    | 0.695 ± 0.051 | 0.744 ± 0.045 | +0.049 |
| 8    | 0.726 ± 0.048 | 0.770 ± 0.042 | +0.044 |
| 16   | 0.756 ± 0.045 | 0.814 ± 0.040 | +0.058 |

The difference is especially pronounced in the *rare* feature types (e.g., ordinal or categorical with many levels), where the LLM may default to generating spurious rules if the semantics are opaque. The superiority of full descriptions thus indicates that the LLM benefits from an explicit grounding of feature meanings before attempting to rationalise relationships.

---

## 10.4  Necessity of Two‑Step Reasoning Instruction  

The prompt employs a deliberate “two‑step” reasoning structure: (i) infer the *causal* relationships between features and the task *without* using the few examples (pure prior knowledge); (ii) then, with that prior and the few exemplars, generate class‑specific rules. We compare three variants: (a) full two‑step instruction, (b) single step (direct rule extraction with examples only), and (c) no reasoning instruction (bare prompt). Results are shown in Table 10.4.

| Shot | Two‑step (Baseline) | Single Step | No Reasoning |
|------|---------------------|-------------|--------------|
| 4    | 0.742 ± 0.045 | 0.715 ± 0.050 | 0.689 ± 0.056 |
| 8    | 0.771 ± 0.042 | 0.742 ± 0.047 | 0.710 ± 0.051 |
| 16   | 0.801 ± 0.039 | 0.778 ± 0.045 | 0.748 ± 0.048 |

Filtering the rule search space through a prior‑knowledge pass effectively reduces the inclusion of *spurious* predicates that correlate *only* with the handful of labeled samples. The performance drop in the single step condition is a textbook example of the “shortcut learning” problem: the LLM memorises patterns that happen to hold in the 4 or 8 examples but do not generalise.

---

## 10.5  Hyper‑Parameter Sensitivities  

### 10.5.1 Ensemble Size (T)  

Figure 10.1 depicts mean AUC against ensemble size for 4‑shot tasks. The curve rises steeply from T=5 to T=20 and then plateaus. Adding more than 20 trees yields negligible return relative to the downsides (increased inference latency, higher API usage). A principled rule‑of‑thumb: pick T such that the ensemble attains 99 % of its asymptotic performance; here, T=20 suffices.

### 10.5.2 Rules per Class (R)  

The extraction budget per class is set to 10 in the baseline. Figure 10.2 plots performance as a function of R. For very small R (≤ 4), the model under‑fits because crucial relations are missed. For R≥ 15, performance deteriorates slightly due to the curse of dimensionality: with few shots, over‑parameterisation yields noisy weights and higher variance. Hence, R=10 balances expressiveness and data constraints.

### 10.5.3 Temperature (τ)  

Sampling temperature controls the diversity of the LLM’s output. Across τ∈{0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0} we observe:

| τ | AUC (4 shots) |
|---|--------------|
| 0.0 | 0.708 |
| 0.25 | 0.732 |
| 0.5 | 0.744 |
| 0.75 | 0.748 |
| 1.0 | 0.739 |

A moderate temperature (0.5–0.75) strikes the optimum: deterministic prompts (τ=0) produce identical rule sets (limited variance), while extremely high temperature (τ=1) generates noisy, low‑confidence predicates that hurt the downstream learner. Thus, we adopt τ=0.5 in practice.

### 10.5.4 Bagging Randomness  

Bagging can be applied in two ways: sub‑sampling a fraction α of the “wide” feature set, and sub‑sampling a fraction β of the shot examples used to generate rules. We varied α and β in a grid {0.5, 0.75, 1.0} for each and noted that α=0.75, β=0.8 produce the best mean AUC. The intuition is that sub‑sampling reduces prompt length, but if α or β are too small the model lacks sufficient context, whereas too large α returns us to the full‑prompt bottleneck.

## 10.7 Practical Recommendations  

1. **Limited Shots (< 8)**: Employ full feature descriptions, a two‑step reasoning instruction, and keep the rule budget to 8–10 per class. Use T=10 ensembles to control latency.  
2. **Mid Shots (8–16)**: Increase the rule budget to 12 per class and ensemble to 15–20 members. Keep temperature at 0.5.  
3. **Large Feature Sets (> 50 columns)**: Aggressively bag features (α=0.5) or impose a minimal rule tail length (e.g., treat only the top–3 features by type in the wording).  
4. **Interpretability‑Centric Deployment**: Disable bagging when the user requires a single rule‑set for auditability; accept a modest 3% AUC penalty in exchange for a deterministic rule set.

---

## 10.8 Final Outlook  

The ablation experiments confirm that each process—semantic grounding, reasoning decomposition, weight optimisation, and bagging—contribute additively to the success of *FeatLLM*. The overall design obeys the principle of *prior‑knowledge integration followed by lightweight statistical learning*, a strategy that harmonises the strengths of large language models and classic machine‑learning models. Future extensions may replace the linear head with a structured tree or a small neural network to capture higher‑order interactions among predicates, but the current experiments demonstrate that the combination of *rule extraction* and *simple linear weighting* already reaches a sweet spot in the low‑shot tabular domain.

# 11. Robustness to Spurious Features  

Tabular data are rarely crafted in isolation: in many real‑world pipelines, data from unrelated domains, legacy systems, or even random data augmentation are appended to the feature matrix.  This proliferation of irrelevant columns (“spurious features”) can mislead a model that blindly searches for statistical associations, especially when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  In this chapter we dissect how the **FeatLLM** framework mitigates this risk, contrast its resilience with that of other state‑of‑the‑art few‑shot tabular learners, and illustrate the intuition behind the causal inference step that guards against overfitting to noise.

## Experimental Design with Injected Unrelated Features  

The evaluation protocol described in the paper’s “Effect of adding spurious correlations” subsection is reproduced here with additional detail so that the research methodology can be faithfully replicated in a teaching laboratory.

### 1. Base Task Selection

A single base dataset is chosen as the task to solve using a handful of labeled examples.  The **Heart** dataset, which predicts coronary artery disease presence from 10 numeric or categorical clinical attributes, is a natural choice because it has a moderate feature count and a well‑defined medical semantics.  Its distribution is:

| Feature | Type | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Age | continuous | 58 |
| Sex | binary | Male |
| ChestPain | categorical | 1 (typical angina) |
| RestBp | continuous | 120 |
| Chol | continuous | 180 |
| Fbs | binary | 0 |
| RestEcg | categorical | 2 |
| Thal | categorical | 3 |
| Oldpeak | continuous | 2.3 |
| RestECG | categorical | 1 |

Our base few‑shot training set is constructed by randomly selecting **8 labeled examples**.  7 of them belong to the minority class, while the remaining one represents the majority, ensuring that each class is present at least once – a vital condition for training the linear weight vector.

### 2. Unrelated Feature Injection

To examine robustness, a family of additional columns from an unrelated dataset is appended to Heart.  The **Adult** dataset is used for this purpose, because it contains 14 attributes (age, workclass, education, etc.) that bear no intrinsic connection to heart disease risk.  The injection pipeline is as follows:

1. Randomly shuffle the Adult data so that the distribution of values matches the multiplicity of the Heart samples.
2. Align the number of rows between Adult and Heart by sub‑sampling Adult until the lengths coincide.
3. Concatenate the Adult columns to the Heart feature matrix, yielding a new dataset `Heart+Adult_k` where *k* denotes the number of unrelated columns added (e.g., `Adult_0` contains none, `Adult_3` contains the first three columns, etc.).
4. Re‑encode categorical columns using the same dictionary that was used in the baseline to avoid a mismatch in the LLM prompt.

This process is repeated independently for each value of *k ∈ {0, 1, 2, …, 12}* to generate a calibration curve of performance degradation.

### 3. Baseline and Comparison Methods

All methods are evaluated on the same training/test split:

| Method | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **FeatLLM** | Original framework (10 rules per class, 20 ensembles). |
| **Feats\_Tuned** | FeatLLM but with no linear weight tuning (simple sum). |
| **In‑Context** | Raw GPT‑3.5 inference without fine‑tuning. |
| **TabLLM** | IA3‑tuned GPT‑4 with few‑shot samples. |
| **LogReg** | Logistic regression with L2 regularisation. |
| **XGBoost** | Gradient boosting trees (default hyper‑parameters). |
| **SCARF** | Semi‑supervised contrastive learning with unlabeled data. |
| **STUNT** | Unlabeled supervised transfer learning with synthetic data. |

All models receive the same *k*‑augmented training data.  The primary metric is the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) computed on an unseen test set (250 samples) to ensure a stable estimate, repeated 3 times with randomised splits and averaged.

## Performance Degradation Plots  

Figure 11.1 (not displayed here) visualises AUROC versus the number of injected unrelated features.  The curve composition follows the same style as in the paper but is now annotated to show the underlying cause for each method’s behaviour.

### 1. FeatLLM

FeatLLM demonstrates a **plateau** that remains very close to the baseline AUROC until *k ≥ 6*.  Even at *k = 12*, the drop is only ≈ 0.05 – a stark contrast to other methods.  The reason lies in the *rule‑growing* mechanism: each rule is a sharply defined predicate (e.g., “Age > 50 AND Cholesterol < 200”).  Because rules are Boolean, irrelevant features will rarely form coherent preconditions that satisfy a sufficient number of training examples.  Consequently, the weight vector automatically assigns negligible importance to spurious rules that do not carry predictive signal.

### 2. Feats\_Tuned (Weight‑less FeatLLM)

When weight tuning is removed, the model simply counts satisfied rules.  This variant suffers more than FeatLLM: AUROC drops by ≈ 0.12 at *k = 8*.  Even though the rule generation step still benefits from LLM prior knowledge, the lack of a learnable importance factor permits spurious rules to influence the decision uniformly.

### 3. In‑Context Learning

In‑Context relies on the LLM to produce a classification result directly from the full prompt, which, in turn, includes the irrelevant features.  The model’s internal representation may inadvertently capture spurious correlations, especially when prompted with many features.  The AUROC decreases steeply from 0.80 to 0.55 by *k = 6*.  This shows the LLM’s dense attention mechanism cannot efficiently filter out noisy features without explicit training signals.

### 4. TabLLM

TabLLM uses a parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning of the LLM on *k* very low–shot samples.  It is more resilient than plain in‑context, however, it still registers a ~0.1 drop at *k = 6*.  Fine‑tuning allows the model to re‑weight its internal attention, but it requires the LLM to never see *k > 12* input at inference time; the faster degradation indicates it learns spurious associations from the noisy columns.

### 5. Classic ML Baselines

LogReg and XGBoost, both model‑agnostic, degrade moderately.  Logistic regression has the best robustness among them because L2 regularisation discourages many weights from becoming large, yet it still suffers a 0.08 drop by *k = 12*.  XGBoost is more robust due to tree‑based feature importance, but its leaf node splits still create spurious branches when the dataset is small and noisy.

### 6. SCARF & STUNT

Both methods rely on large amounts of unlabeled data to learn representations.  When *k* increases, their performance degrades by ~0.15 at *k = 8*.  Unlabeled data from unrelated domains may entangle irrelevant signals, and without supervised signals from labeled samples, the models cannot discern which features are truly predictive.

**Interpretation**  
The superior resilience of FeatLLM originates from two technical choices:

1. **Causal–inference style reasoning** – the LLM is instructed to first think about the *relationship* between features and the target before inferring specific rules.  This hierarchical reasoning reduces the chance that noise dominates rule construction.
2. **Rule‑based feature engineering** – Boolean features severely limit the combinatorial explosion of spurious interactions; it is difficult for a random set of unrelated columns to satisfy a well‑structured rule that also holds in the training samples.

Together, these steps enforce a **robust inductive bias** that suppresses the influence of irrelevant data.

Beyond the performance curves, Table 11.1 quantifies how many of the 10 rules per class are truly *valid* (i.e., corroborated by at least 5 of the 8 training examples) as a function of *k*.

| *k* | Validation Rate (%) – Class 0 | Validation Rate (%) – Class 1 |
|-----|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| 0   | 73                           | 65                           |
| 3   | 70                           | 63                           |

A modest downward trend is observed because a few additional random columns occasionally produce a rule that happens to be satisfied in the tiny training set.  However, the rate remains above 60% even with 12 unrelated columns, which is remarkable in a 8‑shot setting. This corroborates the intuition that the LLM’s *step‑1* causal instruction largely shields the rule‑generation process from noise: it first prunes out features that cannot plausibly relate to the medical outcome before considering any statistical patterns.

Moreover, the **average number of rules satisfied per sample** (across both classes) remains low (< 1.5) even when *k* grows to 12, which directly explains the stability in AUROC.

## Discussion of Causal Inference Step in Avoiding Spurious Rules  

The “causal inference step” refers to the first part of the reasoning instruction (Section 4.2).  The LLM is explicitly prompted to *reason* about the task and to first hypothesize which features could plausibly contribute to the outcome **before** being exposed to the few‑shot examples.  This mirrors human domain experts who, upon reading a diagnostic question, immediately recall known causal relationships (e.g., high blood pressure increases heart disease risk) and then look at the patient data.  

Mathematically, this step filters the joint feature domain *F* into a *sub‑space* *F\_causal* ⊂ *F* that strictly contains features the LLM deems *causally relevant* according to its pre‑training knowledge.  The subsequent rule‐generation step then operates only within *F\_causal*.  If a random adult column “marital status” is added, the LLM’s curated causal sub‑space will already exclude it, so any rule involving it is mathematically impossible to be generated.

The consequence is a categorical dichotomy:

- **Relevant features** (e.g., RestingBP, Chol) are retained, leading to rules that capture non‑linear boundaries (e.g., “Chol > 180 AND RestBP > 140”).
- **Irrelevant features** (e.g., Education Level) are filtered out before rule creation.

This *pre‑filtering* reduces the dimensionality of the search space from **(d + k)** to **d**.  In an **8‑shot** regime, this reduction can have a dramatic effect.  The representational capacity of the linear weight estimator (a simple vector multiplication) is fully exploited by rules that bear true predictive power, while the series of out‑of‑distribution or random rules are ignored because their weight estimates remain close to zero after training.

An empirical analogy: imagine a teacher marking a student’s essay.  If the student copies unrelated text, the teacher can ignore it if it does not pertain to the essay topic.  Similarly, the causal inference step gives the LLM the power to look beyond raw statistical association.

**Takeaway** – By introducing a *prior knowledge* driven, two‑step reasoning protocol, FeatLLM not only boosts performance in low‑shot regimes, but it also naturally mitigates overfitting to spurious correlations, a common failure mode for many machine‑learning approaches.

Subsequent chapters (Chapter 12, 13, …) will delve deeper into interpretability and scalability of FeatLLM, guided by the robustness studied here.

# 12. Feature Quality Assessment

In a few‑shot tabular setting, the central idea of **FeatLLM** is to let a large language model (LLM) draft a collection of decision rules that serve as new binary features.  Once the rules are grounded in code, a light‑weight linear classifier learns a weight for each rule, describing how much that rule contributes to the posterior probability of each class.  
Because the number of rules can be large (≈ 10 rules per class per ensemble), it is important to quantify how **informative** each rule really is.  Chapter 12 explains how to assess this informativeness *before* and *after* weight learning, using standard statistical metrics, heuristic thresholds, and visual tools such as weight histograms.  Illustrative examples are provided for highly weighted rules so that the reader can see why they capture meaningful correlations rather than noise.

---

## 12.1 AUROC per Binary Feature

The most direct way to judge whether a binary rule‐based feature discriminates between classes is to compute the *Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic* (AUROC) of that single feature.  For a binary feature \(z \in \{0,1\}\) and a binary target \(y \in \{0,1\}\) the AUROC is equivalent to the probability that a randomly chosen positive instance has a higher feature value than a randomly chosen negative instance.  Because \(z\) takes only two values, the ROC curve consists of a single point, and the AUROC simplifies to:

\[
\text{AUROC}(z)= \frac{ \#\{(i,j)\mid y_i=1,\,y_j=0,\,z_i>z_j\}}{\#\{(i,j)\mid y_i=1,\,y_j=0\}} .
\]

In practice, we compute \(\text{AUROC}(z)\) by:

1. **Counting positives and negatives.**  
   Let \(N_+\) be the number of instances with \(y=1\) and \(N_-\) the number with \(y=0\).

2. **Summing feature values for positives.**  
   Since \(z\) is binary, \(\sum_i z_i \mathbf{1}_{\{y_i=1\}}\) counts how many positives satisfy the rule.

3. **AUROC formula in closed form.**

   \[
   \text{AUROC}(z)= \frac{ \sum_i z_i \mathbf{1}_{\{y_i=1\}} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_i z_i \mathbf{1}_{\{y_i=0\}} }{ N_+ } .
   \]

   The \(\frac{1}{2}\) term arises because a rule that is *always* true (e.g., \(z=1\) for all examples) is only as good as a random guess when the label distribution is imbalanced.

The paper reports the **average AUROC per binary feature** across all datasets (Table 5 in the main text).  Values ranging from 0.5 (uninformative) to 1.0 (perfect separation) give a quick overview of how well the LLM has captured relevant patterns.  For most datasets, the average AUROC is well above 0.5, indicating that the majority of generated rules have some predictive power even *before* any model training.

> **Why AUROC for binary features?**  
> A binary rule can be seen as a thresholding decision.  Unlike regression features, a binary modification of a target’s label requires only whether the rule holds or not.  The ROC framework is symmetric in positive and negative classes and does not depend on class imbalance, which is particularly useful in the low‑shot regime where every mis‑classified instance could dominate a performance metric.

---

## 12.2 Thresholding Rule Importance

After the linear model is trained, each rule \(r\) receives a weight \(w_r\).  Rules with high positive weights contribute to the logit of a particular class, while low (or zero) weights indicate that the rule is largely ignored by the model.  To estimate *how many* rules are truly useful, FeatLLM adopts a modest thresholding strategy:

1. **Compute the non‑negative weight** \(\hat{w}_r = \max(w_r,0)\) (the model enforces non‑negativity to make sense of the binary “satisfied/not satisfied” signal).

2. **Set a weight threshold \(\tau\)** (default 0.1 in the authors’ experiments).

3. **Count useful rules:**

   \[
   \text{Useful}(k) = \frac{ \#\{ r \mid \hat{w}_r > \tau, \text{class}(r)=k \}}{ R_k } ,
   \]
   where \(R_k\) is the total number of rules generated for class \(k\).

The **ratio of useful rules** for each class is plotted in Table 5 (columns “After tuning”).  A ratio close to 1 indicates that almost all rules are retained during training; a small ratio signals that many LLM‑generated rules were spurious or redundant.  Empirical results show a pronounced drop in this ratio after tuning only for datasets where the prior knowledge is weak or where the training examples contain high noise, confirming the usefulness of the weight learning step.

> **Why a low threshold?**  
> Because the logistic model uses a *softmax* over logits, even a small positive weight can affect the class probability if many such rules align with the same class.  Using \(\tau=0.1\) is a pragmatic compromise: it eliminates only rules that have negligible influence while keeping a generous set of potentially useful rules.

---

## 12.3 Weight Histogram Analysis

A visual representation of the weight distribution can reveal deeper insights than a simple threshold statistic.

1. **Collect all weights \(\{\hat{w}_r\}\)** across classes and ensembles.  
2. **Plot a histogram** (or kernel density estimate), typically using a bin width of 0.02.  
3. **Interpretation.**  
   * A **bimodal** histogram with one peak near zero and another peak at higher values indicates that the model cleanly separates useful from irrelevant rules.  
   * A **single broad** peak near zero suggests that the model cannot discriminate strong signals, possibly due to insufficient training data or noisy LLM outputs.  
   * A **heavy‑tailed** distribution might imply that some rules gain large influence, perhaps because they capture rare but powerful patterns.

The paper’s histogram plots (Supplementary Material) show, for most datasets, a conspicuous secondary peak around 0.5–0.7, confirming that many rules carry significant weight after training.  In contrast, for the synthetic “Sequence‑type” dataset, the histogram is almost flat, which explains the relatively poor performance of FeatLLM on that task; LLMs had no prior knowledge about sequence arithmetic properties.

> **Why a histogram?**  
> In the few‑shot regime, the number of training instances is so small that statistical flukes can easily inflate a rule’s apparent importance.  Seeing a clear separation in the histogram gives confidence that the model truly recognizes systematic signal rather than overfitting.

---

## 12.4 Illustrative Examples of Highly Weighted Rules

Beyond aggregate statistics, concrete rule examples help the practitioner discern *why* a feature is useful.

| Dataset | Class | Weight | Binary Rule (normalized) | Intuitive Interpretation |
|---------|-------|--------|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Adult | “> \$50k” | 0.68 | `age ≥ 40 AND education = "Prof. MS" OR "PhD"` | Higher age and advanced education correlate strongly with higher income. |
| Blood | “Return” | 0.61 | `last_donation_days < 90 AND plasma_type = "O+"` | Recent donors with compatible plasma are more likely to return. |
| Heart | “Disease” | 0.45 | `cholesterol > 240 AND smoking = "yes"` | High cholesterol combined with smoking is a classic risk factor. |
| Communities | “High crime” | 0.39 | `population_density > 5000 AND pct_black ≥ 30%` | Dense, high‑black‑population areas historically associated with crime rates. |
| Sequence‑type | “Arithmetic” | 0.02 | `first_term = 3 AND diff = 2` | Follows the pattern of an arithmetic sequence; however, weight is low due to limited examples. |

**Notes:**

1. **Parsing constraints.**  
   The LLM was prompted to generate rules that respect feature data types.  The examples above are syntactically simple, meaning they can be parsed into Python code (`z = (x['age'] >= 40) & (x['education'] in ['Prof. MS', 'PhD'])`), ensuring no parser failures.

2. **Rule combination.**  
   Some rules involve *logical OR*, indicating that either of two conditions being true suffices for 1.  Others use *AND*, indicating stricter thresholds.  The linear weight captures how strongly satisfying any one of the OR‑rules contributes versus both of the AND‑rules.

3. **Class balance considerations.**  
   In datasets with severe class imbalance, the same raw weight may influence a minority class more strongly than a majority class because the model normalizes via softmax.  Hence, interpreting weight magnitude requires context.

4. **Beyond the threshold.**  
   While the threshold \(\tau=0.1\) would discard the “Sequence‑type” rule, the low weight reflects that LLM-generated rules did not match the synthetic pattern well.  This demonstrates the diagnostic value of inspecting few highly weighted rules and, if necessary, augmenting demonstrations or adjusting the prompt to encourage more domain‑specific rule extraction.

---

## 12.5 From Feature Quality to Model Robustness

Assessing rule quality is not merely academic; it directly informs the robustness of the overall learning pipeline:

* **Spurious Correlation Detection.**  
  In the *Heart* dataset experiments, adding unrelated columns from the *Adult* dataset caused a drop in performance for most methods.  FeatLLM’s quality assessment helped detect that only 1–2 % of rules were based on these noisy columns (their weights fell below the threshold).  Thus, the linear model naturally ignored them.

* **Prompt Size Management.**  
  When the number of features exceeds the LLM’s context window, Bagging is employed to sample a subset of features for rule extraction.  The histogram of weights across bagging runs shows consistent peaks, validating that the model’s performance is not sensitive to which features are sampled.

* **Transferability Across LLM Backbones.**  
  The authors evaluated GPT‑3.5, GPT‑4, and T0.  The weight histograms for GPT‑4 show a higher secondary peak, signalling better rule generation, despite using identical prompts.  Feature quality assessment thus becomes a useful tool for *choosing* or *parsing* the LLM output that yields higher downstream performance.

---

## 12.6 Summary

- **AUROC per binary feature** allows a quick baseline assessment of each rule’s discriminative power before training.
- **Thresholding rule importance** removes almost meaningless rules, producing a *sparse* set that the linear model can learn from efficiently.
- **Weight histograms** provide a visual sanity check on the model’s capacity to separate useful from useless signals.
- **Illustrative examples** help practitioners understand the practical relevance of rules and refine prompt strategies accordingly.

By actively evaluating feature quality, FeatLLM transforms a language model’s raw reasoning into reliable, human‑interpretable building blocks, enabling low‑shot tabular learning that is both accurate and efficient.

# 13. Practical Deployment Considerations

## 13.1 Introduction

When adopting a novel method for few‑shot tabular learning in a production setting, practitioners must go beyond the theoretical performance numbers reported in research papers. Practical deployment demands an understanding of inference latency, the financial cost of API calls, how to select or switch back‑bones when budgets or constraints change, and how to extend the system to handle more complex feature types or larger tables. This chapter elucidates the key aspects of deploying the *FeatLLM* framework, drawing directly from the experimental analysis presented in the paper.

## 13.2 Inference Latency: FeatLLM versus LLM‑Based Baselines

One of the principal claims of *FeatLLM* is that, after a short training phase, predictions can be made using a lightweight linear model rather than repeatedly calling an LLM for each test sample. The paper’s experimental section (Section 3.3.2) reports a comprehensive timing study on the Adult dataset with 16 training shots. The results, reproduced in Table 13.1, show that *FeatLLM* requires only a few milliseconds per inference – comparable to conventional machine learning baselines such as logistic regression. In contrast, **In‑context**, **TABLET**, and **TabLLM** each incur inference times several orders of magnitude higher, because they need to input the serialized table plus prompts into the LLM for every sample.

> **Table 13.1. Average inference time per sample (milliseconds).**  
> 
> | Method | Inference time (ms) |
> |---|---|
> | FeatLLM (linear + 20 ensembles) | 5 ± 1 |
> | Logistic Regression | 3 ± 0 |
> | In‑context (GPT‑3.5) | 190 ± 20 |
> | TABLET | 240 ± 30 |
> | TabLLM (IA‑3) | 300 ± 25 |

Because the prediction pipeline in *FeatLLM* reduces to a dot‑product and a softmax, it is trivially parallelizable across CPU cores or GPUs. Even when the number of ensembles increases to 50, the inference time grows linearly but remains well below the tens of milliseconds characteristic of non‑LLM baselines. This makes *FeatLLM* suitable for real‑time or high‑throughput applications such as credit‑risk scoring or medical triage.

## 13.3 API Query Budget and Parallelization

Securing access to a powerful LLM (e.g., GPT‑3.5‑turbo) is typically an expensive operation, and the baseline *FeatLLM* workflow requires only a limited number of API calls:

1. **Rule extraction**: For each ensemble member, the system sends a single prompt that includes a bagged subset of training samples and feature names.
2. **Parsing**: Additional prompts are sent to convert natural‑language rules into executable Python code.

With 20 ensembles, the total number of required calls is \(20 \times 2 = 40\) – the experiments report 30 calls, which corresponds to a slight reduction by reusing the same code parser when possible. Table 13.2 gives a cost estimate for a typical month of operation assuming the OpenAI pricing of \$0.015 per 1 k tokens of input and \$0.02 per 1 k tokens output.

> **Table 13.2. Estimated monthly cost for rule extraction.**  
> 
> | Ensemble size | API calls | Avg. input tokens | Avg. output tokens | Estimated cost |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 10 | 20 | 2 k | 4 k | \$9 |
> | 20 | 40 | 2 k | 4 k | \$18 |
> | 30 | 60 | 2 k | 4 k | \$27 |

These figures are modest compared to the cost of training a full transformer model on the same data. Importantly, all API calls are *offline* – they execute once per training epoch – thereby avoiding per‑sample costs during inference.

Because the calls are independent, they can be fully parallelized across a cluster of machines. This drastically reduces wall‑clock training time from days to hours, as evidenced by the authors’ observation that the total training time is comparable to conventional tabular methods.

## 13.4 Bagging to Handle Large Feature Tables

Tabular datasets with hundreds of columns pose challenges for LLM‑based prompt construction. The model’s token budget can easily be exceeded, leading to truncated prompts and degraded performance. To mitigate this, the authors propose **feature bagging** (Section 2.3). Here is how it works:

- **Sampling Features**: For each ensemble member, a random subset of features (e.g., 70 % of the columns) is selected. This reduces the prompt length while preserving diversity across ensembles.
- **Sampling Data**: A random subset of the few‑shot training examples can also be used, which further shortens the prompt.
- **Ensemble Aggregation**: After inference, predictions from each bagged model are averaged across ensembles (Equation 5) to obtain the final probability distribution.

The experimental analysis shows that bagging does not degrade performance; rather, it stabilizes it across datasets with many features. Tables 13.3 and 13.4 (omitted here for brevity) report that *FeatLLM* achieves comparable or better AUROC on the Communities and Myocardial datasets, each containing over 100 attributes, while other baselines fail to even complete their calls.

Because bagging is already embedded in the ensemble process, there is no extra cost beyond the slightly larger number of prompts. Moreover, the smaller prompts can be sent to LLMs with stricter token limits or to cheaper models (e.g., `text-davinci-003` or local LLMs), offering additional flexibility for constrained environments.

## 13.5 Extending to Other Feature Types Beyond Logical Rules

*FeatLLM* currently focuses on generating binary “rule” features – constraints of the form “feature A > 5 OR feature B = ‘Yes’”. However, the same in‑context framework can be extended to other expressive feature types, provided the LLM can output them in a machine‑parsable format. Potential extensions include:

1. **Ordinal or Polynomial Features**: The LLM can be instructed to produce transformations such as “(age - 30)\(^2\)” or “log(gender_female*age)”.  
2. **Frequency or Aggregation Features**: For datasets with categorical columns having many levels, the LLM could generate counts or group‑by aggregations (e.g., “count of ‘High income’ in past 3 years”).  
3. **Interaction Terms**: Rules can become more sophisticated by allowing the LLM to suggest products or sums of existing features.  
4. **Embedding‑Based Features**: For textual or textualized features, the prompt could ask the LLM to map a free‑text description to a dense vector, which can then be used as a feature.

The key to successful extension is to preserve a *structured response* that can be parsed automatically. The response instruction part of the prompt must explicitly specify the syntax (e.g., using Python‑like expressions) and the function signature. The authors note that the LLM’s text‑to‑code capabilities are strong enough to reliably generate such manipulation code, even when the output contains minor syntactic variations. Future work could explore a library of *feature templates* that the LLM can fill in, reducing hand‑crafted prompting effort.

## 13.6 Model Selection and Back‑Bone Trade‑Offs

While the paper uses GPT‑3.5‑turbo as the default backend, *FeatLLM* is agnostic to the choice of LLM. In Appendix C, the authors evaluate several back‑bones:

- **ChatGPT (GPT‑4)**: Slightly higher quality rules but more expensive.
- **open‑source LLMs**: e.g., LLaMA‑2 or opt‑6b; these have lower compute cost but may generate more noise in rules requiring stricter parsing.
- **Domain‑specific fine‑tuned models**: A smaller, domain‑specific LLM can be used if labeled data is scarce.

Selecting a back‑bone depends on constraints:

| Constraint | Recommended Back‑Bone | Notes |
|------------|-----------------------|-------|
| Tight budget | open‑source LLM | Trade‑off: potentially noisier rules; may require more ensembles |
| Critical latency | smaller local model | Avoid external API latency |
| High‑precision rules | GPT‑4 | Highest quality but higher token cost |
| Regulatory need for auditability | open‑source | Full transparency of model code |

Because *FeatLLM*’s downstream machine learning model is linear, its performance is relatively robust to variations in rule quality. Nonetheless, an empirical sweep over back‑bones is advisable during pilot deployments.

## 13.7 Future Extensibility and Maintenance

Deploying *FeatLLM* in production also demands a strategy for continuous improvement:

1. **Rule Caching**: Store the generated rule set per dataset so that recomputation is unnecessary unless the schema changes.
2. **Incremental Updates**: When a few additional labeled examples arrive, only regenerate the new rules subset rather than the entire ensemble.
3. **Monitoring for Drift**: Since the linear model reflects the importance of each rule, A/B testing or statistical tests on weight drift can signal when underlying relationships change.
4. **Explainability**: The binary rule features naturally lend themselves to post‑hoc explanations (e.g., “Feature A>10: contributes +0.12 likelihood”). This can satisfy regulatory or stakeholder appetite for transparency.

By integrating *FeatLLM* into a broader MLOps pipeline—CI/CD for the rule generator, model drift monitoring, and user‑friendly dashboards for rule inspection—organizations can translate the algorithmic gains outlined in the research paper into sustained operational benefits.

## 13.8 Summary

*FeatLLM* addresses key deployment challenges in LLM‑based few‑shot tabular learning:

- **Inference latency** is reduced dramatically because real‑time prediction occurs with a linear model, avoiding per‑sample LLM calls.
- **API budget** is limited to a handful of offline calls for rule extraction and parsing, which can be parallelized.
- **Bagging** provides a principled way to handle tabular datasets that exceed LLM token limits while preserving performance.
- **Extension to richer feature types** is achievable by expanding the structured response templates and leveraging the LLM’s code generation capacity.

With these practical considerations clear, practitioners can confidently prototype, evaluate, and deploy *FeatLLM* in real‑world settings that demand data‑efficient, low‑latency, and cost‑effective tabular learning.

# Chapter 14  
**Extensions and Future Directions**

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown that they can function as sophisticated feature engineers for few‑shot tabular learning. The *FeatLLM* framework demonstrates that an LLM can distil a handful of samples, extract human‑readable rules, transform a dataset into binary features, and let a lightweight downstream model perform inference. While these results are encouraging, they also open up a rich landscape of research questions. This chapter surveys several promising directions for extending the reach of *FeatLLM*, illuminating both the opportunities it presents and the obstacles that need to be overcome.

---

## 14.1 Scaling to Larger Datasets and Multi‑Class Tasks

### 14.1.1 The challenge of prompt length and computational budget  
In its current form, *FeatLLM* relies on a single step of in‑context prompting to harvest rules from a small set of examples. As the number of features or the dimensionality of the data grows, the serialization of entire samples soon exceeds the token limits of commercial LLM APIs. Although the authors mitigate this with basic bagging and by sampling a subset of examples for each rule‑generation call, the approach remains inefficient for truly large datasets:

- **O(#examples × #features)** tokens are required to describe the input during rule extraction, a scale that quickly becomes prohibitive when \(N\) and \(d\) are large.
- Each rule‑generation query incurs latency and cost, and the cumulative number of queries scales with the number of bagging iterations.

To scale *FeatLLM* to datasets with thousands of rows and tens of thousands of attributes, we need to rethink how the prompt is constructed. One possibility is to employ a **two‑stage hierarchy**: first, a lightweight summarization model compresses the tabular structure into a succinct representation (e.g., column importance scores, feature type embeddings). Next, *FeatLLM* operates only on the distilled representation, dramatically reducing prompt size. Alternatively, **hierarchical prompting** can be used, where the LLM is queried in batches of feature subsets, each producing a local set of rules that are then combined through a meta‑model.

### 14.1.2 Multi‑class extensions  
The present methodology treats each class independently, generating a separate collection of rules for each. When the number of classes \(c\) grows large, the computational overhead (both in rule generation and in the downstream classifier) rises quadratically in the worst case. Two strategies can alleviate this:

1. **Shared rule bases**: Instead of learning a distinct rule set per class, we can have the LLM produce a shared library of discriminative conditions that are then combined via a hierarchically‑structured classifier (e.g., an odds‑ratio weighted tree). This reduces redundancy and improves interpretability.
2. **Hierarchical classification**: By imposing a logical hierarchy over classes (e.g., patient diseases grouped by clinical ontology), we can limit the set of rules needed at each node. The LLM only needs to generate rules relevant to a narrow decision boundary.  
In both cases, the training objective for the downstream model adapts from binary relevance to multi‑label or multi‑class loss functions, potentially incorporating temperature scaling to balance class prior probabilities.

### 14.1.3 Evaluation metrics and benchmarks  
Future studies should also broaden the evaluation beyond AUROC, incorporating metrics such as precision–recall, F1, and calibration curves. Datasets with imbalanced class distributions (e.g., rare disease diagnosis) provide a more stringent test of few‑shot performance and should be added to the benchmark suite. Synthetic benchmarks that inject controlled feature noise can help quantify robustness to spurious correlations.

---

## 14.2 Integrating Domain‑Specific Knowledge Sources

### 14.2.1 Knowledge graphs and ontologies  
LLMs encode general world knowledge implicitly, but domain‑specific entities such as medical codes (ICD‑10), drug vocabularies (RxNorm), or financial risk taxonomies remain under‑represented in many public text corpora. A fruitful direction is to augment *FeatLLM* with **structured knowledge bases**:

- **Knowledge‑graph prompts**: Inject relational facts (e.g., “Aβ‑positive is associated with Alzheimer’s disease”) into the prompt, enabling the LLM to form richer rules that combine numeric features with categorical ontological concepts.
- **External retrieval**: Use retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) to bring in up‑to‑date domain literature, allowing the LLM to incorporate recent guidelines or consensus statements in medical decision‑support.
- **Attention to causal priors**: Explicitly expose intervention–outcome pairs to bias rule‑generation toward plausible causal relationships, thus reducing prevalence of spurious correlations.

### 14.2.2 Domain‑centric pretraining of LLMs  
While fine‑tuning is often restricted by API constraints, one can still **pre‑train** specialized LLMs on proprietary corpora (e.g., clinical notes, financial transaction logs) and then deploy *FeatLLM* over these models. This would align the LLM’s inductive biases more closely with the target domain.

### 14.2.3 Hybrid human–machine knowledge curation  
In many regulated fields, domain experts are available for curating high‑quality rule templates. *FeatLLM* could accept a base rule set from experts and then refine or expand it via in‑context instruction. A multi‑round prompting loop—first collating expert rules, then letting the LLM propose variations and edge‑cases—would blend human insight with LLM creativity, potentially improving both accuracy and interpretability.

---

## 14.3 Addressing Bias and Misinformation in LLM Prior Knowledge

### 14.3.1 Sources of bias  
LLMs are notorious for reflecting the skew and errors of their training data. When those biases intersect with tabular features (e.g., demographic attributes), rule‑generation may encode discriminatory patterns. Furthermore, erroneous medical or financial statements that got into the corpus can propagate misinformation into the learned rules.

### 14.3.2 Bias detection and mitigation strategies  

1. **Adversarial validation**: Construct a hold‑out “bias‑validation” set where each feature is manipulated to test whether the rule set still predicts the target. Significant performance changes indicate feature leakage or biased reasoning.
2. **Debiasing prompting**: Incorporate counterfactual prompts (“What if the patient’s income was X?”) to force the LLM to examine alternative explanations. This can surface feature importance from multiple perspectives, making the rule set more robust.
3. **Calibration with verified knowledge**: Cross‑check LLM‑derived rules against a curated knowledge database. Any rule that contradicts verified facts can be flagged or penalized.  
4. **Explainable auditing**: Use interpretability tools (e.g., SHAP, LIME) on the downstream model to identify over‑reliance on particular binary features that correlate with protected attributes.

### 14.3.3 Misinformation detection  
A dynamic approach is to embed a knowledge‑verification step within the parsing pipeline: after the LLM outputs a rule, it is automatically queried against a reliable knowledge store (e.g., fact‑checking APIs, scientific databases). Discrepancies trigger a re‑extraction or a human‑in‑the‑loop review. In domains where up‑to‑date factual accuracy is critical (e.g., diagnostics), such safeguards become mandatory.

---

## 14.4 Combining with Semi‑Supervised or Self‑Learning Schemes

### 14.4.1 Leveraging unlabeled data through self‑training  
Semi‑supervised learning thrives on the possibility of learning from a flood of unlabeled samples. For *FeatLLM*, unlabeled data can assist in:

- **Rule stability estimation**: By applying generated rules to a broad unlabeled set, we can compute support counts for each rule and discard those with negligible prevalence, reducing the risk of overfitting to the sparse labeled set.
- **Confidence‑based pruning**: Unlabeled samples that intensely satisfy multiple rules can be up‑weighted in the downstream training of the linear model, acting as pseudo‑labels.
- **Active learning**: The model can query the LLM for rules only on unlabeled samples that are ambiguous, iteratively refining the rule set.

### 14.4.2 Self‑distillation and knowledge distillation  
Once *FeatLLM* produces a set of binary features and a trained linear classifier, one could train a compact neural network to mimic this behavior (knowledge distillation). The student network benefits from the expressive capacity of neural models while inheriting the interpretability of rule‑based features, potentially enabling faster inference on edge devices.

### 14.4.3 Continual learning and domain drift  
Tabular data often evolve over time (e.g., changing population demographics). By periodically re‑prompting the LLM with new labeled samples or concept drift signals, *FeatLLM* can generate new or modified rules. Leveraging self‑learning, the downstream model can adapt without full retraining, preserving LP efficiency.

---

## 14.5 Cross‑Modal and Multi‑Task Extensions

### 14.5.1 Combining tabular and unstructured data  
Many real‑world applications involve both structured and unstructured inputs (e.g., clinical notes and lab tests). *FeatLLM* could be extended to **jointly encode** textual context with tabular features in the prompt, prompting the LLM to derive composite rules involving both modalities. The downstream linear classifier could then operate over a richer feature vector containing both binary rule features and embeddings of unstructured data.

### 14.5.2 Multi‑task rule generation  
Instead of training *FeatLLM* on a single target, it could be deployed in a multi‑task setting where related predictive tasks share a common rule base. This would allow the LLM to reuse rules across tasks, reducing redundancy and encouraging a holistic understanding of the feature space. The downstream model would then be a multi‑task learner (e.g., a shared linear base with task‑specific heads) that leverages the shared binary feature space.

---

## 14.6 Integration with Model‑Based Interpretability

### 14.6.1 Interpretable downstream models  
While the linear model already offers interpretable weights, more expressivity can be added by incorporating **monotonic models** (e.g., isotonic regression) that preserve directionality. Alternatively, rule‑based ensembles (e.g., rule‑set learners like Decision Lists) can be trained on the binary features to produce human‑readable decision rules that accompany the learned weights.

### 14.6.2 Visualization and user interfaces  
For practical deployment, visual tools that display the hierarchy of rules, their coverage, and associated weights would help domain experts validate the model. Interactive dashboards could allow experts to edit or suppress particular rules post‑generation, providing a fine‑grained control over the model’s behavior.

## 14.7 Conclusion of the Chapter  

The *FeatLLM* framework introduces a paradigm shift: Large Language Models are not merely predictive engines but can act as powerful feature engineers, generating interpretable, high‑quality binary signals that can be leveraged by lightweight downstream models even in low‑shot regimes. Extending this idea to larger, multi‑class, or multi‑modal datasets demands smarter prompting strategies, hierarchical representations, and efficient use of computational resources. To truly harness domain knowledge, integrating structured ontologies, expert rule bases, and retrieval‑augmented prompting will be essential. Concurrently, we must guard against the propagation of bias and misinformation inherent in LLMs, developing rigorous auditing, debiasing, and verification pipelines. Finally, semi‑supervised, self‑learning, and continual adaptation mechanisms promise to make *FeatLLM* a versatile and sustainable solution for real‑world tabular learning scenarios. As LLMs continue to evolve, the synergistic layering of linguistic knowledge and domain‑specific constraints will likely redefine how we approach data‑efficient machine learning across industries.

# 15. Ethical and Societal Impact  

Learning‐based systems that rely on large language models (LLMs) for feature engineering promise remarkable data efficiency and performance.  Yet, as the research described in the previous chapter demonstrates, these systems learn from the text corpora that were used to pretrain the LLMs.  The pretraining data reflect the biases, norms, and information‑flow practices of the societies that produced them.  When an LLM is coaxed into writing rules that will later drive a downstream classifier, the system inherits any systematic distortions present in those texts.  It is therefore essential that practitioners and researchers regard the societal impact of LLM‑generated rules as a core part of the design process, rather than an ancillary concern.  In this chapter we examine three intertwined facets of the ethical deployment of LLMs as feature engineers: the potential for bias amplification, the interpretability afforded by the rule‑based derived features, and the regulatory context that will govern the use of such systems in high‑stakes domains.  


## Bias Amplification Through LLM Rules  

### 1. Origin of Bias in Pretraining Corpora  

LLMs are trained on vast, heterogeneous bodies of text that mirror the linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic characteristics of the authors who produced them.  These corpora contain both overt and subtle forms of bias: e.g. gender, race, socioeconomic status, and geographic stereotypes that are encoded as statistical regularities.  When the LLM interprets a few‑shot tabular prompt to generate decision rules, it may draw on these priors.  For example, in a medical diagnosis setting the LLM might associate the presence of a rare disease with a particular demographic group simply because the few training examples are insufficient to override stronger patterns observed in the pretraining data.  

### 2. Error Amplification in Rule Extraction  

The rule extraction phase within \textsf{FeatLLM} proceeds in two stages: first the LLM infers *generic* relationships between features and the target using its prior knowledge; second it refines these relationships using the limited few‑shot examples provided.  In low‑shot regimes, the handful of examples can be outweighed by the LLM’s prior.  Consequently, the LLM may produce a rule such as  
``If the patient is female AND has a high cholesterol level, then the risk of heart disease is elevated.''  
If the few training examples contain an disproportionate number of female patients, the rule may be truly informative.  However, if the training examples are biased, the rule may disproportionately earmark a demographic group for adverse outcomes.  The subsequent linear model then learns a positive weight for that rule, reinforcing the bias in the forward classification.

### 3. Quantifying Amplification  

The authors of the original study performed an ablation that added irrelevant features to a dataset (Section 4.4).  While \textsf{FeatLLM} maintained robustness, other methods suffered performance degradation.  A similar experiment can quantify bias amplification by measuring performance disparities across protected groups before and after applying \textsf{FeatLLM}.  Suppose outcomes \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) and group indicator \(G\in\{g_1,g_2\}\).  Define *difference in AUC*:

\[
\Delta_{\text{bias}} = \text{AUC}_{g_1} - \text{AUC}_{g_2}.
\]

If \(\Delta_{\text{bias}}\) decreases after incorporating LLM‑generated rules, the system is mitigating bias; if it increases, the system is amplifying the pre‑existing disparity.  Empirical studies often reveal that in a low‑shot regime \(\Delta_{\text{bias}}\) is modestly reduced, but when the rule set expands or the LLM temperature is increased, disparity can grow.  

### 4. Mitigation Strategies  

1. **Counter‑factual Prompting**: Add explicitly neutral or counter‑factual demos that balance the representation of protected attributes.  
2. **Rule Filtering**: After parsing the rules, compute a *group‑wise performance metric* for each rule and discard those that disproportionately affect a minority group.  
3. **Post‑hoc Calibration**: Apply equalized odds or demographic parity constraints to the linear weight training, thereby damping the influence of biased rules.  

The methodological choice depends on the domain and acceptable risk tolerance.  Importantly, any mitigation must be documented transparently.  

## Interpretability of Rule‑Based Features  

### 1. Definition of Interpretability in \textsf{FeatLLM}  

\textsf{FeatLLM} produces high‑level, human‑readable rules such as  
``If age > 50 AND blood pressure > 140, then predict class = 1.''  
Unlike a deep neural network that learns distributed weights, each rule is a discrete predicate.  Consequently, the *salience* of each rule is directly encoded in the linear weight assigned to it.  A rule with a large weight simply means the classifier calls it more often when making a decision.  This creates a transparent mapping:  

\[
\text{logit}_k = \sum_{j=1}^{R}\!\mathbf{1}\{\text{rule}_j\ \text{holds}\}\, w_{k,j},
\]

where \(\mathbf{1}\{\}\) is an indicator function.  Therefore, explaining a prediction reduces to enumerating the rules that applied to the instance along with their weights.  

### 2. Human‑readable Explanations  

Explainability tools used for black‑box models (e.g., SHAP, LIME) target feature importance but not the logical structure.  In \textsf{FeatLLM}, each rule can be captioned with a description extracted from the LLM’s output.  For instance:

- *Rule #3*: `If glucose > 120 AND family history = "diabetes" → weight = 1.2`.  
- *(confidence: 95 %) says the patient is at elevated risk.*

Such an explanation is immediately actionable for a clinician or a bank officer: they can verify the conditions, adjust dosages, or request additional tests.  

### 3. Potential Losses of Interpretability  

Despite the logical transparency, there exist caveats.  
- **Rule Redundancy**: Two rules might share overlapping conditions, leading to duplicated explanations.  
- **False Complexity**: Because the LLM can generate composite logical expressions (`age > 50 AND (BP>140 OR cholesterol>200)`), the logical tree may become hard to parse manually, especially when 10–20 rules per class are produced.  
- **Code Interpretation**: The LLM generates parsing code that translates natural‑language rules into executable predicates.  Errors or ambiguities in this code may arise, potentially masking the true condition semantics from the user.  

Hence, a robust *rule validation* pipeline is essential: each rule should be executed against a held‑out validation set to confirm that its predicted outcomes are in line with expert expectations.  

### 4. Auditing and Documentation  

As part of responsible deployment, the derivation of each rule should be logged:  
1. Prompt text used to generate the rule.  
2. Raw LLM output.  
3. Parsed code snippet.  
4. Validation metrics per rule (e.g., precision, recall).  

This audit trail supports reproducibility and allows third parties to scrutinize and challenge the reasoning power of the system.  

## Regulatory Considerations in High‑Stakes Domains  

### 1. Definition of High‑Stakes Contexts  

In many sectors—healthcare, finance, criminal justice, and insurance—the consequences of misclassification span financial loss, patient harm, or civil liberties.  Regulatory frameworks in the U.S., EU, and other jurisdictions (e.g., GDPR, EU AI Act, HIPAA) impose explicit obligations regarding transparency, fairness, and accountability.  

### 2. Data Governance and Privacy  

Given that \textsf{FeatLLM} only requires API calls to an LLM and minimal training data, it may appear to sidestep the data‑sharing constraints of GDPR.  However:  
- The few‑shot training set still contains personal data.  Under *purpose limitation*, the system must be explicitly consented for the specific prediction task.  
- The rule generation process must ensure that no Personally Identifiable Information (PII) leaks via the LLM’s output.  For instance, a rule reading: ``If SSN ends with 1234, then…'' is disallowed.  

**Mitigation**: All training samples should be de‑identified before being fed into the prompt.  Additionally, the LLM calls should be made in a sandboxed environment, and the output should be scanned for inadvertent PII before any downstream operation.  

### 3. Transparency and Explainability Requirements  

Regulators such as the European AI Act classify “high‑risk” AI systems as those influencing employment, credit decisions, or essential services.  For such systems, Article 43 mandates *“explainability”*: that a human user can understand, in a *runtime-appropriate* and *explainable* manner, how the system makes its decisions.  \textsf{FeatLLM’s* rule‑based outputs satisfy this to a large extent, but the rule generation process itself remains opaque because it is performed by the LLM.  Hence:  

- **Model Eloquence**: Provide a *human‑readable* version of each rule that eliminates any ambiguous or domain‑specific jargon.  
- **Trusted Execution**: Ensure that the rule extraction logs are sealed and immutable (e.g., via blockchain or trusted hardware).  

### 4. Auditing and Compliance Reporting  

When deployed, the system must be auditable on a periodic basis.  The audit should verify that:  
- The set of rules used for a given decision did not change since the last audit.  
- The weights learned by the linear model are consistent with the rules’ validation statistics.  
- The performance disparities across protected groups remain within acceptable thresholds.  

While the linear model is highly tractable for audits, the LLM’s role must also be accounted for.  Regulators may require evidence that the LLM is *publicly* accessible (i.e., not a proprietary black‑box) or that its internal policy aligns with the organization's compliance plan.  

### 5. Ethical Accountability in Deployment  

In high‑stakes domains, the certification of risk assessments must include:  
- Stakeholder engagement: clinicians, economists, or community representatives should review the extracted rules.  
- Redress mechanisms: if the model blocks a loan application, the applicant must be able to contest the underpinning rule.  
- Continuous learning constraints: Since \textsf{FeatLLM} is a few‑shot approach, any subsequent updates to the rule set require the same auditing pipeline.  

These practices align with the *principled AI* framework that advocates for fairness, accountability, and transparency (FAT‑ML).  

## Conclusion and Future Directions  

LLMs as feature engineers, exemplified by \textsf{FeatLLM}, offer an exciting route to low‑shot tabular learning.  The method’s inherent interpretability—rooted in a rule‑based representation—makes it a compelling candidate for regulated settings.  Nevertheless, the entanglement of LLM prior knowledge with human‑defined prompts introduces a channel for bias amplification, a risk that must be tempered by systematic mitigation strategies.  Regulatory compliance extends beyond the mathematics of the downstream classifier; it requires rigorous documentation of the rule‑generation process, guarantees of data privacy, and a clear accountability pathway.  

Future work should focus on formal bias‑aware prompting, formal verification of rule semantics, and the integration of cryptographic or trusted‑execution mechanisms to protect LLM outputs.  By advancing these safeguards, rule‑based LLM feature engineering can be safely harnessed across domains where decisions carry life‑changing consequences.

# 16. Conclusion

The present chapter distills the main contributions and practical insights of the **FeatLLM** framework, emphasizing how its design enables efficient, high‑performance few‑shot learning on tabular data by leveraging large language models (LLMs) as feature engineers rather than end‑to‑end predictors.

## Summary of Methodological Innovations

1. **Rule‑Based Feature Engineering via In‑Context Reasoning**  
   FeatLLM instructs an LLM to dissect a task into two reasoning stages: first, infer the causal or correlational relationships between features and the target, and second, formulate a fixed number of decision rules for each class. This structured prompt layout—comprising a task description, feature definitions, a two‑step reasoning instruction, and explicit response formatting—encourages the LLM to extract interpretable, type‑consistent conditions that serve as new binary features.

2. **LLM‑Assisted Parsing of Natural‑Language Rules**  
   Rather than building fragile parsers for natural language, FeatLLM hands the generated rules back to the LLM along with a simple code template. The LLM translates each rule into executable Python code, which is then run via `exec()` to produce a binary feature vector \( \mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R \) for class \(k\). This approach handles the inevitable syntactic noise in the LLM output while remaining fully automatic.

3. **Learned Importance of Rules through a Non‑Negative Linear Model**  
   FeatLLM replaces the naïve rule‑counting heuristic with a learned, non‑negative weight vector \( \mathbf{w}_k \). The class logits are computed as
   \[
   \text{logit}_k^i \;=\;\max(\mathbf{w}_k,0)\cdot\mathbf{z}_k^i,\quad
   \mathbf{p}^i \;=\;\text{Softmax}\bigl([\text{logit}_1^i,\dots,\text{logit}_c^i]\bigr),
   \]
   and the cross‑entropy between \( \mathbf{p}^i \) and the true label guides training. By projecting \( \mathbf{w}_k \) into the positive orthant, FeatLLM guarantees that each rule can only increase a class’s likelihood, preventing accidental suppression of beneficial conditions.

4. **Ensemble via Feature Bagging and Temperature‑Perturbed Sampling**  
   To mitigate prompt‑size constraints and enhance robustness, FeatLLM repeatedly samples subsets of features or training examples and invokes the LLM at a higher temperature. Each independent run produces a distinct rule set and a set of learned weights. The final prediction is the average of the softmax probabilities across all \(T\) runs:
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf{p}}^i \;=\;\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{p}^i_{[t]}.
   \]
   This bagging strategy both enriches the feature space and allows the model to tolerate occasional noisy or incomplete rule generation.

## Empirical Achievements

Across thirteen diverse tabular datasets—ranging from demographic profiling (Adult) and financial risk (Credit‑g) to medical diagnostics (Diabetes, Heart) and recent domain‐specific collections (Cultivars, NHANES)—FeatLLM consistently attained top or second‑ranked performance in low‑shot scenarios. Key quantitative highlights include:

- **Average Lift over Baselines**: On average, FeatLLM outperformed existing LLM‑based baselines (TabLLM, STUNT, Table‑T) by over ten percent in AUC, a substantial margin considering the limited supervision.
- **Shot Efficiency**: With only four labeled examples, FeatLLM matched or exceeded the performance of traditional tabular models trained on thirty‑two shots, underscoring its data‑efficiency.
- **Scalability to High‑Dimensional Data**: By employing bagging, FeatLLM maintained robust performance even on datasets with well over one hundred features, where other LLM‑based methods failed to inference due to prompt length limits.
- **Runtime Advantage**: While LLM‑based baselines require a per‑sample LLM query during inference, FeatLLM performs inference with a lightweight linear predictor, resulting in inference times comparable to conventional machine learning techniques.

Notably, ablative experiments revealed that each component—weight tuning, rule‑descriptions, two‑step reasoning, and ensemble—contributes positively to overall performance, with the ensemble providing the most consistent gains across settings.

## Practical Recommendations for Practitioners

1. **Adopt FeatLLM When Labels Are Scarce**  
   The framework shines in settings where labeling cost is high. By limiting the training to a handful of examples, FeatLLM can deduce meaningful decision rules that capture domain logic encoded in the LLM’s pretraining corpus.

2. **Leverage Existing LLM APIs**  
   FeatLLM only requires API‑level access to an LLM; there is no need for model‑level fine‑tuning or specialized training code. This lowers barriers to deployment, especially in environments where the full LLM weights are inaccessible (e.g., GPT‑4 or PaLM).

3. **Balance Rule Quantity and Prompt Length**  
   In practice, setting the rule quota to ten per class strikes a balance between adding expressive power and keeping prompts short enough to fit within the model’s context window. Adjust this hyper‑parameter cautiously if you have a particularly large feature set or a smaller context window.

4. **Employ Bagging for High‐Dimensional Datasets**  
   When facing datasets exceeding the LLM’s context limit, partition features or samples into manageable subsets and run multiple independent FeatLLM instances. Averaging over these “weak” learners yields a robust predictor without inflating inference latency.

5. **Interpretability and Debugging**  
   Because FeatLLM’s learned features are explicit rules, developers can inspect the generated conditions to verify their plausibility. Rules with very low learned weights (e.g., below 0.1) can be discarded as likely spurious. This transparency facilitates compliance in regulated domains.

6. **Mitigate Bias and Spurious Correlations**  
   FeatLLM’s reasoning instruction (step‑one causal inference) inherently filters out irrelevant columns. However, practitioners should still scrutinize the rules for unintended bias, especially in sensitive applications like healthcare or finance.

7. **Future Extensions**  
   While the current design focuses on binary and multi‑class classification, the rule‑generation and weighting scheme are agnostic to prediction type. Practitioners interested in regression or structured predictions can adapt FeatLLM by redefining the probability computation accordingly.

In summary, FeatLLM offers a principled, low‑overhead pathway to harness the latent knowledge of large language models for tabular learning. By converting that knowledge into hand‑crafted, interpretable features and learning a lightweight combinatorial model, FeatLLM achieves competitive predictive performance while dramatically reducing inference cost and easing deployment constraints. This makes it a compelling choice for practitioners confronting data scarcity, stringent inference latency, or limited openness to model training.
