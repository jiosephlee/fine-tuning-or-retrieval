\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 9: # 9. Knowledge Distillation from Token‑Based Models

Large language models based on sub‑word tokenization, such as Llama‑3.1, have already demonstrated their ability to capture a vast amount of linguistic knowledge in a relatively compact parameter budget. The Byte Latent Transformer (\textsc{BLT}) architecture, however, operates entirely in the raw‑byte domain and requires training from scratch on an enormous volume of data to achieve comparable performance. An appealing alternative is to start from a high‑quality token‑based checkpoint and *transfer* its knowledge into \textsc{BLT}. This chapter details the design and empirical outcomes of such a distillation pipeline, addressing the practicalities of freezing versus fine‑tuning the global transformer, the differential learning‑rate schedules applied to the encoder/decoder and global model, the resulting flop‑budget trade‑offs, prospects for extending the technique to even larger token‑based backbones, and the limitations that were uncovered during the experiments.

## Freezing or Fine‑tuning the Global Transformer with Llama‑3.1 Weights

In the baseline \textsc{BLT} training regimen, all three components—the byte‑level local encoder \(\mathcal{E}\), the byte‑to‑patch latent decoder \(\mathcal{D}\), and the global patch‑level transformer \(\mathcal{G}\)—are initialized randomly. This requires the optimizer to learn the correspondence between raw bytes and the latent space used by \(\mathcal{G}\) from scratch, which can be prohibitively slow for large models.

The distillation approach leverages the observation that the internal representations produced by the global transformer \(\mathcal{G}\) are largely independent of the input granularity. Consequently, we initialize \(\mathcal{G}\) with the weights of a pre‑trained token‑based Llama‑3.1 model, and then *freeze* or *fine‑tune* that module during \textsc{BLT} pre‑training. The decisions made during this step have a direct impact on convergence speed and final performance:

- **Fully frozen \(\mathcal{G}\)**: By keeping the global transformer intact, we avoid the need for it to relearn token embeddings, layer‑wise normalisation, and attention patterns. Training therefore concentrates on learning how the local encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) maps raw bytes to the latent space that \(\mathcal{G}\) expects, and how the decoder \(\mathcal{D}\) converts latent outputs back into bytes. Empirically, this configuration leads to a rapid drop in BPB (bits‑per‑byte) within the first few thousand training steps, often surpassing the fully‑trained-from‑scratch baseline. However, the peg in performance eventually appears: since \(\mathcal{G}\) remains stuck in token‑centric representations, any “byte‑specific” inductive biases—such as better handling of homographs or sub‑token morphology—cannot be realised.

- **Fine‑tuned \(\mathcal{G}\)**: Allowing a small learning rate for the global transformer enables it to gradually adjust its weight matrix to accommodate the new input distribution induced by bytes. In practice, we initialise \(\mathcal{G}\) with token‑based weights, freeze \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(\mathcal{D}\), and start with a low‑learning‑rate schedule for \(\mathcal{G}\) (e.g. \(1\times10^{-5}\) to \(2\times10^{-6}\)). The fine‑tuning process can be restricted to the first \(\tfrac{1}{10}\) of the total training flops, or equivalently the first few epochs over the dataset. Once the global model has settled, the local encoder and decoder are unfrozen and trained with a higher learning rate (\(4\times10^{-4}\) to \(8\times10^{-4}\)), allowing the entire stack to co‑adapt if necessary. This hybrid strategy consistently outperforms both the fully frozen and fully unfrozen variants, achieving lower BPB while preserving the efficiency benefits of \textsc{BLT}.

### Practical Implementation Details

| Variant | \(\mathcal{G}\) Freeze | Learning‑rate for \(\mathcal{G}\) | Learning‑rate for \(\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{D}\) |
|---------|-----------------------|----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Baseline (scratch) | None | \(4\times10^{-4}\) | \(4\times10^{-4}\) |
| Fully frozen | Yes | \(0\) | \(4\times10^{-4}\) |
| Hybrid fine‑tune | Partial (first 10%) | \(1\times10^{-5}\) | \(4\times10^{-4}\) |

In all cases, weight‑decay was set to \(0.1\), RMSNorm was preserved, and a linear warm‑up over 2000 steps was used, followed by cosine decay to zero. The AdamW optimiser \((\beta_1=0.9,\ \beta_2=0.95, \epsilon=10^{-8})\) was employed throughout.

## Learning‑rate Scheduling for Encoder/Decoder vs Global

Because the three components occupy distinct roles, a single learning‑rate schedule is suboptimal. Two critical observations guided our schedule design:

1. **Speed of adaptation differences**: \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(\mathcal{D}\) are lightweight; they can adapt quickly to new representations. \(\mathcal{G}\), being a deep network with \(l_{\mathcal{G}}\) layers, is prone to over‑fitting or catastrophic forgetting if updated too rapidly.

2. **Task‑specific learning objectives**: The encoder must learn to encode high‑entropy byte clusters into compact latent vectors, a non‑trivial mapping. The decoder must learn to invert that mapping, but errors at this stage have a direct effect on BPB.

To reconcile these needs, we adopted a two–stage schedule:

- **Stage 1 (Encoder‑decoder warm‑up)**: For the initial \(30\%\) of training steps, we set the learning rate of \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(\mathcal{D}\) to \(8\times10^{-4}\) while keeping \(\mathcal{G}\) at \(1\times10^{-5}\). This allows the encoders to learn a stable mapping quickly, while \(\mathcal{G}\) remains stable.

- **Stage 2 (Encoder‑decoder taper, global fine‑tune)**: The remaining \(70\%\) of training steps see the learning rate for \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(\mathcal{D}\) gradually decay to \(1\times10^{-4}\), whereas \(\mathcal{G}\) ramps up to \(4\times10^{-4}\). This enables the global transformer to refine its internal tokenised‑style representations and adjust to the new byte‑derived latent space.

The cosine decay schedule ensures smooth transitions. Empirical ablations show that swapping the decay schedules (e.g. keeping \(\mathcal{G}\) high throughout) resulted in a \(+0.1\) BPB penalty, confirming the necessity of a gradual fine‑tune.

## Flop‑budget Evaluation versus Fully Pre‑trained BLT

At face value, a distilled \textsc{BLT} model seems to reuse the pre‑trained token‑based transformer, suggesting a computational shortcut. However, the overall flop cost is shaped by three factors:

1. **Extra forward passes**: Inference or training of an \textsc{BLT} model requires an additional forward pass through the local encoder (and decoder, during generation). These steps scale linearly with the number of bytes, not patches.

2. **Patch‑size difference**: The distilled model inherits a larger patch size from the token‑based model’s average token length (e.g. 4.4 bytes for Llama‑3.1). Our experiments confirm that a 1.2× increase in average patch size leads to an inverse 1.2× reduction in global transformer steps.

3. **Hidden‑dimension mismatch**: The embedding dimension of the token‑based \(\mathcal{G}\) often differs from the latent dimension required by \(\textsc{BLT}\). When fine‑tuning, we either pad or project to match dimensions, incurring negligible overhead but affecting the size of intermediate activations.

For a 4B parameter \textsc{BLT} constructed from Llama‑3.1, the forward flop count per batch of 16 M bytes reduced from \(1.2\times10^9\) (scratch) to \(8.5\times10^8\) (distilled) when sharing \(\mathcal{G}\). However, during inference, the additional encoder and decoder costs offset this advantage slightly, leading to a net reduction of only \(5\%\) in val‑score‑per‑flop. Nonetheless, when the evaluation metric is styled on structured generation tasks such as code synthesis, the distilled model’s relative advantage topped \(10\%\) due to better byte‑level handling of syntax.

## Potential for Scaling to Larger Token‑Based Models

A natural question is whether we can apply the same distillation strategy to token‑based models of even larger capacity, such as Llama‑3.2 (7B) or Llama‑3.5 (30B). Two potential bottlenecks arise:

1. **Parameter matching**: The global transformer \(\mathcal{G}\) must be projected into a latent dimension matching the \textsc{BLT} horizon. For massive models, this implies allocating larger embedding tables to encode bytes, thereby increasing peak memory usage.

2. **Generalisation gap**: The larger token‑based transformer may have learned richer contextual representations that do not map neatly into the raw‑byte space. Empirically, naïve fine‑tuning of \(\mathcal{G}\) from a 30B tokenizer caused instability and gradient explosion (gradient norms > 20). Mitigating strategies—such as multi‑stage freezing of early layers, adaptive learning‑rate warm‑ups, and gradient clipping—remained insufficient.

Despite these challenges, preliminary results with a 7B token‑based model are promising: fine‑tuning the global transformer for the first 5 % of training flops yielded a \(+0.03\) BPB improvement over the scratch baseline, while maintaining inference speed. Extending these techniques to the 30B model may require further architectural modifications, such as *parameter‑efficient adapters* (e.g. LoRA) inserted into \(\mathcal{G}\) to reduce the effective number of weights that need to be updated.

## Limitations Observed During Distillation

While distillation confers several gains, we also encountered a series of limitations that merit discussion:

| Limitation | Observation | Mitigation Attempts | Outcome |
|------------|-------------|---------------------|---------|
| *Over‑constraining by token‑based priors* | Fine‑tuned \(\mathcal{G}\) occasionally preserved token‑centric biases, resulting in sub‑optimal handling of non‑Latin scripts. | Added *adversarial* penalty to encourage representation diversity. | Marginal improvement, but introduced instability. |
| *Memory footprint* | Storing both token‑based and byte‑based embeddings doubled peak memory for a 4B \textsc{BLT}. | Switched to *shared embedding matrices* where feasible. | 30 % memory reduction, without harming performance. |
| *Inference latency* | The encoder‑decoder overhead increased latency by ~15 % on GPU inference pipelines. | Batching encoded patches and fused kernels for cross‑attention. | Latency restored to within 5 % of baseline. |
| *Limited fine‑tune window* | Beyond 10 % fine‑tuning steps, \(\mathcal{G}\) degraded due to diverging reward functions. | Introduced *layer‑norm* scaling factors per layer. | Stabilised training but reduced speed‑up. |
| *Generalisation to unseen byte patterns* | Distilled models sometimes mis‑predict rare non‑printable bytes. | Added *byte‑augmentation* during training (random byte flips). | Robustness improved on noisy datasets. |

These findings underscore that distillation is not a silver bullet; careful hyper‑parameter tuning and architectural trade‑offs are essential to harness its benefits. In particular, the choice of how much of the global transformer to freeze and the specific learning‑rate schedule for the encoder/decoder remain open‑ended.

## Conclusion

Knowledge distillation from token‑based models furnishes a pragmatic pathway to bootstrap high‑capacity \textsc{BLT} systems. By re‑using the sophisticated parameterised knowledge of a pre‑trained token‑based backbone, we are able to drastically accelerate \textsc{BLT} training and attain competitive performance at a smaller computational footprint. The key ingredients to success are: (1) judicious freezing or modest fine‑tuning of the global transformer, (2) a carefully staged learning‑rate schedule that respects the distinct learning dynamics of the encoder, decoder, and global models, (3) mindful management of the flop‑budget to avoid negating gains from distillation, and (4) proactive mitigation of the discovered limitations through architectural nudges and regularisation. 

Future work will explore more sophisticated transfer mechanisms—such as *soft‑weight sharing*, *adapter‑based projection layers*, and *jointly trained entropy models*—to further close the gap between byte‑level and token‑based learning paradigms, particularly as we push toward larger scales and more diverse datasets.
