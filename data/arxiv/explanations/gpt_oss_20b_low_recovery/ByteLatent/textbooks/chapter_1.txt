## 1. Introduction to Byte‑Level Language Modeling

Large‑scale language models dominate the landscape of natural language understanding and generation.  Nearly all modern systems, from the early GPT‑2 to the latest Llama 3, are trained on token‑level representations: a preprocessing step groups one or more bytes into a fixed set of sub‑word symbols, producing a compact sequence that a transformer can consume.  This chapter unpacks the motivations behind departing from that convention, explains why raw‑byte training is often avoided, and then introduces the key innovations of the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** that make such a departure practical.  

---

### 1.1 Tokenization in Modern LLMs: Benefits and Constraints

Tokenization—typically performed with a byte‑pair‑encoding (BPE) or sentencepiece algorithm—offers several pragmatic advantages:

| Benefit | How it Helps |
|---------|--------------|
| **Sequence‑length compression** | By grouping multiple ASCII or UTF‑8 bytes into a single token, the input length shrinks dramatically (often 3–4 bytes per token), cutting the number of transformer steps and the cost of self‑attention. |
| **Vocabulary inductive bias** | Sub‑word units encode linguistic regularities (prefixes, suffixes, common words). Transformers can learn richer contextual dependencies when operating on these units. |
| **Variable‑length handling** | The tokenizer’s learnable dictionary can adapt to frequent patterns, yielding a more efficient representation for the target data distribution. |

Despite these benefits, tokenization is *heuristic*. The mapping from raw bytes to tokens is decided *before* training, thereby imposing an inductive bias that may not align with the true optimal representation.

---

### 1.2 Shortcomings of Fixed‑Vocabulary Tokenization

The literature and empirical experience reveal several systematic shortcomings that arise from a fixed tokenizer:

1. **Domain and Modality Sensitivity**  
   *Citation*: \citet{dagangetting} demonstrate that a tokenizer learned on one domain (e.g., news articles) performs poorly on another (e.g., code), because the sub‑word statistics shift dramatically.  

2. **Noise Susceptibility**  
   A malformed token (e.g., missing a byte) can corrupt an entire sub‑word, leading to cascading errors in perception or generation.  Token‑level models lack visibility into the actual byte values that produced the token, so subtle corruptions go unnoticed until they affect a downstream representation.  

3. **Orthographic Blindness**  
   Tokenization discards the fine‑grained character structure that underpins many language phenomena.  \citet{edman2024cute} show that token‑based LLMs struggle on tasks that require spelling manipulation or character‑wise transformations.  

4. **Multilingual Inequity**  
   Most tokenizers are trained primarily on Latin‑script languages; scripts such as Chinese ideographs, Arabic diacritics, or sub‑word markers in certain languages become underrepresented, creating uneven performance across linguistic families \citep{liang2023xlm, petrov2024language, limisiewicz2024myte}.  

These limitations motivate a shift toward *tokenizer‑free* modeling, where the network can access the raw byte sequence directly.

---

### 1.3 Why Byte‑Level Training is Computationally Expensive

Training a transformer directly on byte sequences appears straightforward but suffers from a severe *sequence‑length* problem:

- A typical English sentence contains ~20–30 bytes per token.  
- A transformer of dimension \(d\) processes a sequence of length \(n\) with compute scaling as \(O(n^2 d)\) for self‑attention and \(O(n d^2)\) for feed‑forward layers.  
- Because the number of tokens is proportional to the number of bytes (roughly 4.5 bytes per token in Llama 2), 4 billion byte‑tokens require 20–30 billion transformer steps, orders of magnitude higher than a token‑based sequence.

Historically, researchers circumvented this by introducing *efficient* self‑attention variants (e.g., sparse, linear) or by minimizing the length via *patching* or *down‑sampling*, but most of those efforts were limited to modestly sized models (< 400 M parameters).  The key question this chapter addresses is whether we can *scale* a byte‑level transformer to the same order of magnitude as token‑based LLMs (up to 8 B parameters and 1–4 T training bytes) while remaining computationally feasible.

---

### 1.4 High‑Level Ideas Behind BLT

BLT tackles the two core challenges of byte‑level training—sequence length and compute allocation—through a *three‑stage, hierarchical architecture* and a *dynamic, data‑driven patching strategy*.

1. **Dynamic Patching**  
   Instead of a static stride or a fixed dictionary, BLT segments the byte stream into *patches* whose size varies according to the *entropy* of the next‑byte prediction.  Bytes that are highly predictable (low entropy) are grouped into longer patches, effectively reducing the number of transformer steps for easy portions of the text.  Conversely, highly uncertain bytes (high entropy) trigger new patches, ensuring that the model dedicates more capacity to difficult decisions.

2. **Compute‑Aware Hierarchy**  
   BLT comprises three transformer modules:  
   - **Local Encoder**: a lightweight, byte‑level transformer that produces *patch representations* from raw bytes.  
   - **Latent Global Transformer**: a heavyweight, autoregressive transformer that operates on the sequence of patch embeddings.  
   - **Local Decoder**: another lightweight transformer that reconstructs byte sequences from patch embeddings.  
   The latent transformer runs *once per patch*, not once per byte, dramatically cutting its per‑token compute cost.  The local modules process bytes within a patch in parallel and are only invoked when a new patch is started, further economizing flops.

3. **Cross‑Attention Bridging Bytes and Patches**  
   To translate between byte spaces and patch spaces, BLT inserts cross‑attention layers that project byte representations into patch embeddings (encoder) and the reverse (decoder).  These layers are masked so that a patch can only attend to the bytes that belong to it, preserving context locality while enabling a compact global representation.

4. **Training from Scratch and Distillation**  
   Although BLT is designed as a *first‑principle* byte‑level model, it can also inherit knowledge from a pre‑trained token‑based LLM (e.g., Llama 3) by initializing the latent transformer with those weights and fine‑tuning the local modules.  This *byte‑ification* avenue yields superior performance while drastically reducing training compute.

---

### 1.5 Core Contributions of BLT

The design described above culminates in several empirical and theoretical advances:

- **Flop‑controlled Scaling**: BLT matches token‑based performance (e.g., Llama 3) at compute‑optimal regimes up to 8 B parameters and 4 T training bytes.  
- **Inference Efficiency**: For a fixed inference flop budget, BLT outperforms tokenization‑based models by up to 50 % savings, thanks to longer dynamic patches.  
- **Robustness**: BLT displays improved resistance to byte‑level noise, better orthographic reasoning, and stronger performance on low‑resource, long‑tail language pairs.  
- **Scaling Flexibility**: In contrast to token‑based approaches, BLT can grow both the global model and patch size simultaneously without altering the per‑token compute budget, enabling new scaling trajectories.

With this foundation laid, the subsequent sections of this textbook will systematically explore each component of BLT, derive intuition from the underlying mathematics, and connect the methods to both past research and practical application.
