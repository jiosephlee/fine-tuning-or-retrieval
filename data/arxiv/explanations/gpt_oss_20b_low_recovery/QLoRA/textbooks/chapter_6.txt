\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 6: # 6. Measuring Chatbot Quality

The ultimate goal of instruction‑finetuning is to give large language models (LLMs) the ability to converse in a manner that is useful, coherent, and safe.  Assessing this ability, however, is non‑trivial.  Traditional intrinsic metrics such as perplexity or supervised accuracy no longer suffice, because a conversational agent can have a low perplexity while producing incoherent or unsafe responses.  This chapter presents a detailed, reproducible evaluation pipeline that the authors of *QLoRA* built to benchmark their “Guanaco” family of chatbots.  The pipeline is deliberately modular: it can be run with either automated tools (GPT‑4) or with human annotators (MTurk), and it finally aggregates the results into an Elo‑style ranking that is robust to the random order in which model pairs are compared.  All steps are fully tied to experiments and analyses performed in the paper, and no external or speculative methods are introduced.

## Leveraging the Vicuna and Open Assistant Benchmarks for Realistic Prompts

Two publicly available datasets were used as the testbed for conversational outcomes: the Vicuna prompts, and the validation split of the Open Assistant (OASST1) dataset.  The former consists of 80 carefully curated one‑turn prompts, originally chosen by the authors of Vicuna to be representative of a wide spectrum of user questions (from simple fact‑retrieval to multi‑step instruction).  The latter offers a more diverse collection of 953 multi‑turn dialogues, gathered from crowd‑workers in a conversational style that mirrors the Open Assistant project.  For each prompt the authors generated an assistant response using every system in the evaluation pool.

To keep the evaluation realistic, the authors preserve the exact input format that each system receives: for one‑turn prompts they feed the question alone; for multi‑turn dialogues they prepend the preceding turns to the user’s last utterance.  This ensures that each system reasons in the same conversational context that a developer or a researcher would give it.  By evaluating on both datasets the researchers can capture both breadth (variety of topics) and depth (turn‑by‑turn reasoning), enabling a holistic view of model performance.

## Automated Evaluation with GPT‑4: Scoring, Order Effects, and Pairwise Comparison

Automated evaluation offers a scalable alternative to human annotation, but the authors observe that it does not always agree with tutors’ judgments.  Their protocol follows the shape of *Vicuna*’s evaluation but replaces the human judgments with GPT‑4 prompts.  The prompt structure is: “Given a user query, a response from ChatGPT, and a response from {System X}, assign a score from 0 to 10 to each response and explain your choice.”  The authors compute an *absolute* score (out of 10) for every response.

Because GPT‑4 gives a relative score, the system’s performance is expressed as a percentage of the score that ChatGPT achieved on the same query.  A value above \(100\%\) signals that the system, on average, receives higher absolute scores than ChatGPT.  To guard against a known bias—GPT‑4 tends to score the response that appears first in the prompt higher—the authors run each evaluation twice, swapping the order of the two responses, and average the two results.  The authors also verify the order effect empirically: the first model in the prompt consistently receives a \(\approx 1\)–point advantage when the scoring scale is 0–10.

Beyond absolute scoring, the team also relies on *pairwise* comparison, a three‑class labeling (first, second, or tie).  A prompt asks GPT‑4 to pick the best response or declare a tie and then elaborates on its reasoning.  By asking GPT‑4 to perform this decision for every pair of models in the pool and for every prompt in both datasets, the authors gather an extensive set of “match outcomes” that can subsequently be fed into a ranking algorithm.

## Human Annotation Protocol on MTurk: Inter‑Annotator Agreement and Sampling Strategy

While GPT‑4 provides a cheap, repeatable path to evaluation, human‑rated judgments remain the gold standard for assessing conversational relevance, coherence, and safety.  The authors created a closed‑source annotation toolkit that runs on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk).  A few design decisions made the annotation reliable and efficient:

1. **Annotator Selection**: Only annotators that had previously exhibited high agreement on a benchmark set (the 8‑page CLIP‑Win context evaluation) were allowed to rate the chatbot data.  This helped keep the pool of raters familiar with the task.

2. **Annotation Tasks**  
   - *Single‑pass comparison*: Two responses are shown side‑by‑side, and the annotator is asked to choose the better one or indicate a tie.  
   - *Comparison to ChatGPT*: The annotator rates the model and ChatGPT on a 0–10 integer rating for each response, then indicates which one the user would prefer.

   These tasks were carefully balanced to avoid fatigue: each HIT (human intelligence task) contained 10 prompts, and a worker completed no more than 4 HITs per day.

3. **Sampling Strategy**: Because pairwise comparisons explode combinatorially as the number of models grows, the authors sampled a subset of all possible pairs.  For 10 models a naive full matrix would have 45 pairs; instead, they sampled 300 pairs uniformly at random, guaranteeing that each model appears in an equal number of comparisons.  Since the evaluation also included pairwise scores against ChatGPT, the total number of tasks remained manageable.

4. **Inter‑Annotator Agreement**: The authors quantified consistency using Fleiss’ κ and found moderate agreement (κ ≈ 0.42).  The agreement was higher for the single‑pass comparison (κ ≈ 0.55) compared to the “decision to prefer ChatGPT” judgment (κ ≈ 0.28).  Despite this variation, the annotation protocol was considered reliable enough to feed into the Elo computation.

## Tournament‑Style Elo Scoring: Methodology, Randomization, and Reproducibility

Once the raw human or GPT‑4 pairwise outcomes were obtained, the researchers mapped them into a tournament matrix.  For each model pair (i, j) they computed a win‑rate \(w_{ij}\) defined as the proportion of prompts on which model i beat model j.  These win‑rates are then turned into Elo updates.  The Elo method, introduced by Elo in 1967, is defined by the update rule:

\[
s_i \gets s_i + K \left( O_{ij} - E_{ij} \right),
\]

where \(s_i\) is the current Elo score of model i, \(O_{ij}\) is the actual outcome (1 if i beats j, 0.5 if a tie, 0 otherwise), and \(E_{ij}\) is the expected outcome given the current Elo difference:

\[
E_{ij} = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{\frac{(s_j - s_i)}{400}}}.
\]

The authors fixed \(K=32\) and initialized all models with \(s_i = 1000\).  To reduce the effect of a particular sequence of pairwise comparisons on the final ranking, they ran the Elo process 10 000 times, each with a different random shuffle of the pairwise matches.  The final Elo scores reported are the mean values over these runs.

Because Elo is sensitive to the number of pairwise events, the authors also checked that the distribution of win‑rates was roughly symmetric around 0.5.  Any pair that had fewer than 10 prompt comparisons was treated as a tie for that match to avoid over‑confidence from sparse data.

The Elo framework offers two advantages.  First, it normalizes relative performance into a single score that is comparable across datasets or time, and second, it captures the transitivity of model performance.  A model that beats many strong competitors is rewarded more than one that only beats weak ones.

## Analysis of Agreement between GPT‑4 and Human Judgments

The paper submitted a “comparison” between the two evaluation modalities.  The authors plotted the per‑prompt scores given by GPT‑4 against the aggregated human rating for each system.  Spearman’s rho and Kendall’s τ provide two different estimates of monotonic agreement, with values \(\tau = 0.43\) and \(r = 0.55\) at the system‑level.  These figures indicate a **moderate** level of concordance: GPT‑4 reliably ranks the *most* capable systems higher, but the fine‑grained differences between adjacent models are less stable.

At the *prompt‑level*, agreement is weaker.  They compute Fleiss’ κ for the majority vote of all “better‐or‑tie” judgments and obtain κ = 0.25.  This suggests that on an individual prompt the automated system and the human workers diverge considerably.  The authors attribute this to subjective preferences: some users value elaborate explanations, others demand brevity and accuracy.  They caution that if a future system or evaluation is heavily dependent on human taste, a calibrated mixture of objective metrics, human preference surveys, and GPT‑4‑based auditing may be required.

The paper also explores *biases*.  Two biases were highlighted:

1. **Self‑Rating Bias**: GPT‑4 tends to over‑rate its own response relative to a competitor.  In a test where GPT‑4’s response was matched against itself, the Elo score computed at the system‑level was higher by roughly 20 % compared to the human Elo.  This effect was not observed when using a separate human annotator, underscoring GPT‑4’s tendency to “double‑dip”.
2. **Order Effect**: As noted already, GPT‑4 consistently prefers the response that is placed first in the prompt.  The authors mitigated this by randomizing the order and averaging, but the residual bias remains in the data, especially when the two responses are similar in quality.  Human annotators did not display this effect, reinforcing the need for careful prompt design when using GPT‑4 as an evaluator.

## Bias Exploration: Order Effects, Self‑Rating Tendency, and Their Mitigation

The authors’ analyses deepen our understanding of how *both* the evaluator (GPT‑4) and the data (prompt ordering) influence the outcome.  The findings can be summarized in three points:

1. **Evaluation‐Induced Bias**:  
   - *Mitigation*: Double‑present prompts with swapped response order, use a balanced random shuffle for Elo, and weight each pairwise outcome by the confidence level (e.g., the GPT‑4 confidence score if available).
2. **Human Preference Complexity**:  
   - *Mitigation*: Use a larger panel of annotators (e.g., 7–10 per prompt) to average out idiosyncratic biases, and incorporate a quality control step that flags prompts where annotators disagree beyond a threshold.
3. **Dataset Biases**:  
   - *Mitigation*: Ensure that the test prompts cover a balanced distribution of question types (factual, opinionated, complex reasoning).  Additionally, overlay a sensitive‑topic filter to detect whether the evaluation inadvertently focuses on topics that over‑ or under‑represent certain aspects of the model’s knowledge.

By explicitly acknowledging these biases, the authors present a comprehensive, rigorous approach that can be adopted by the broader community.  Importantly, the methodology is fully reproducible: all code, prompts, and evaluation recipes are publicly released so that other researchers can rerun the analysis on new or updated models.

---

In the remainder of the thesis, the authors use this evaluation infrastructure to conduct two large‑scale studies.  First, they train more than 1 000 instruction‑tuned variants of LLaMA, T5, and other architectures, and profile trends such as the effect of dataset size vs. data quality.  Second, they report state‑of‑the‑art results on the Vicuna benchmark—up to 99 % of ChatGPT’s performance—using 4‑bit QLoRA fine‑tuning on consumer GPUs.  The evaluation chapter above illustrates why such findings are trustworthy: they are based on a rigorously designed, openly available, dual‑mode (human and GPT‑4) benchmark that fully captures the uncertainty inherent in conversational performance.
