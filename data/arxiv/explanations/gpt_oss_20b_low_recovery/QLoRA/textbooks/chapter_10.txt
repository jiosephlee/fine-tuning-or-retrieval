\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 10: # 10. Evaluation Considerations and Limitations

Finetuning large language models (LLMs) is an inherently challenging engineering and research endeavor. The scientific value of any such effort hinges not only on the raw performance metrics that can be reported, but also on how those metrics are obtained and interpreted. In this chapter we explore the methodological nuances that influence the conclusions drawn from the QLoRA study, and we highlight directions that remain open for future work. The discussion is organized around six interrelated themes: inter‑annotator agreement, automated evaluator bias, benchmark design, unexplored scenarios, multilingual and domain coverage, and responsible AI considerations.

---

## 10.1 Inter‑Annotator Agreement Metrics and Their Interpretation

When human judgments are employed to evaluate chat‑bot quality, reliability hinges on whether raters assess responses in a comparable way. The QLoRA paper reports **Fleiss’ kappa** scores of 0.42 for system‑level comparisons and 0.25 for sentence‑level judgments. These values are interpreted with reference to conventional agreement thresholds:

- **Absent agreement** (kappa \(\leq 0\)): systematic disagreement or randomness  
- **Weak agreement** (0 < kappa \(\leq\) 0.2)  
- **Moderate agreement** (0.2 < kappa \(\leq\) 0.4)  
- **Strong agreement** (0.4 < kappa \(\leq\) 0.6)  
- **Very strong agreement** (kappa > 0.6)

Thus, the kappa values reported here indicate only *moderate* to *weak* reliability among crowd‑source annotators. Several methodological factors contribute to this modest agreement:

1. **Subjective nature of chatbot responses**: Even with a pre‑defined rubric, different evaluators can weigh clarity, informativeness, and style differently.
2. **Multiple annotators per item**: The paper employs two human raters for ChatGPT‑vs‑system pairs and three raters for pairwise comparisons. Weighted majority voting mitigates some variance, but it does not eliminate systematic bias.
3. **Task framing**: The absence of a clear, concretized scoring rubric (e.g., specific criteria for “accuracy”, “coherence”) can lead to divergent interpretations.

Plotting the distribution of per‑prompt scores can reveal whether disagreement is due to systematic shifts (e.g., all annotators consistently rating one system higher) or scattered noise. In practice, researchers may supplement Fleiss’ kappa with additional metrics such as *Cronbach’s alpha* (for internal consistency) or *pairwise Cohen’s kappa* (to examine dyadic agreement). Where the agreement remains low, confidence intervals around aggregate metrics should be reported to reflect the uncertainty inherent in the evaluation.

## 10.2 Automated Evaluator Bias (Order Effects & Self‑Consistency)

The QLoRA study leverages **GPT‑4** as a surrogate evaluator, prompting it to score or rank system outputs against ChatGPT or other bots. Although this approach scales far beyond manual annotation, it is itself susceptible to systematic biases.

### 10.2.1 Order Effects

The paper observes a strong *ordering bias*: GPT‑4 assigns higher scores to the response that appears first in its prompt. This phenomenon is likely rooted in the language model’s pattern‑matching tendencies—prompting the evaluator with “Rate A, then B” can inadvertently signal a preference for the first item. A standard mitigation is *balanced prompting*, where the order of responses is randomized or counterbalanced across evaluations. Explicitly instructing the evaluator to “bidirectionally consider both responses” can also reduce the bias, although it may not eliminate it entirely.

### 10.2.2 Self‑Consistency

Self‑consistency—asking the evaluator to produce the same ranking across multiple prompts—and *self‑cross‑checking* can improve robustness. For example, the evaluator can be prompted to rank responses in both forward and reversed order, and the final score can be the average of the two. Additionally, sampling **multiple answers** from GPT‑4 for the same comparison and aggregating the results (majority vote or Bayesian aggregation) can smooth out stochastic variations.

### 10.2.3 Calibration and Prompt Engineering

Prompt design plays a crucial role. The paper’s GPT‑4 prompt includes structural cues (“assign a score out of ten”) and explanation requests. However, even subtle wording changes can sway the evaluator toward certain attributes (e.g., “scoring” may focus on correctness, whereas “rating” may encompass style). Calibration experiments—comparing GPT‑4 evaluations on a held‑out set of *golden* responses—can quantify systematic deviations and enable the creation of a *bias correction* function.

## 10.3 Benchmark Design Influence on Model Selection

Model performance is often reported on a *single* benchmark, but the choice of benchmark heavily influences which models appear superior. The QLoRA paper highlights several key points:

1. **Benchmark Orthogonality**  
   - *MMLU* focuses on multiple‑choice, factual, and reasoning tasks, rewarding *memorized* or *analytic* knowledge.  
   - *Vicuna* and *Open Assistant* benchmarks are conversational, assessing *dialogue coherence*, *instruction following*, and *social behavior*.  

   The paper presents evidence that models that excel on *MMLU* do not automatically achieve high scores on *Vicuna* or vice versa. This *partial orthogonality* underscores the need for a *diverse benchmark suite* that covers multiple dimensions of language ability.

2. **Dataset‑Benchmark Mismatch**  
   The finetuning dataset’s topical coverage greatly affects performance on a given benchmark. For instance, the *FLAN v2* data, rich in factual and reasoning prompts, leads to strong *MMLU* results but weaker *Vicuna* scores. Conversely, *Open Assistant* chat logs almost guarantee better conversational skills but may not improve factual recall.

3. **Construction Bias**  
   - *Prompt Length*: Short prompts may favor models that generate terse, accurate answers; long prompts surprise models that struggle with context retention.  
   - *Metric Scale*: The use of a 10‑point scale introduces subjectivity—“8” can mean different things to different evaluators. Elo rating, by contrast, normalizes pairwise outcomes but still depends on the population of competing models.

### Recommendations

- **Multi‑benchmark Evaluation**: Include at least one *knowledge*, one *reasoning*, and one *conversation* benchmark.  
- **Cross‑log Analysis**: Correlate benchmark scores with over‑fitting metrics (e.g., performance on held‑out prompts from the finetuning dataset).  
- **Surface‑level vs Deep‑level Features**: Analyze whether high benchmark scores stem from *surface matching* (echoing the prompt wording) or *semantic understanding* (reasoning through the task).

## 10.4 Unexplored Scenarios

While QLoRA demonstrates remarkable efficiency for 4‑bit LoRA finetuning of LLaMA and T5 models, several research questions remain open:

### 10.4.1 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

*RLHF* has proved pivotal for many commercial chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude). QLoRA avoided RLHF, instead using supervised cross‑entropy. Introducing RLHF on top of 4‑bit base models would assess whether *policy‑level optimization* can further narrow the gap to GPT‑4. Key questions:

- Does *RLHF* magnify quantization errors, or does the adapter’s limited capacity shield it?  
- What if RLHF is applied *after* LoRA finetuning vs *simultaneously* with it?  
- How does the *reward model*’s architecture interact with the underlying quantized weights?  

### 10.4.2 Alternative Parameter‑Efficient Training (PEFT) Methods

LoRA is a convenient, low‑cost PEFT method, but other options exist:

- **Prompt Tuning** (prefix tuning, adapter tuning) — the *input* is modified rather than the *weights*.  
- **Prefix LoRA** — blends LoRA with prompt tuning.  
- **Bits‑and‑Bytes (BnB)** – efficient embedding or hyper‑parameter optimization.  

It remains untested whether these methods, when paired with NF4 quantization, could achieve comparable or better performance, especially at higher compression ratios.

### 10.4.3 More Aggressive Quantization (3‑bit, 2‑bit)

The QLoRA paper treats 4‑bit as the minimal precision that still recaptures full‑precision fidelity. What if we *push further*?

- **Training Stability**: Lower precision may cause vanishing or exploding gradients since the weight scaling factors become less smooth.  
- **Adapter Sensitivity**: With a slimmer base, the LoRA rank \(r\) may need adjustment to compensate for the reduced representational capacity.  
- **Empirical Studies**: Conduct a controlled grid search over bitwidth and LoRA rank to evaluate trade‑offs for MMLU and Vicuna.

## 10.5 Need for Broader Multilingual and Domain Coverage

The OASST1 dataset, used for the flagship *Guanaco* models, is multilingual. However, the evaluation metrics are largely *English‑centric*:

- **Vicuna benchmark**: English only.  
- **Open Assistant benchmark**: multilingual but primarily English.  
- **MMLU**: A mix of English and non‑English subjects, but many test sets are in English.

This mismatch can lead to *domain shift* effects. For example, a multilingual model may have *syntactic knowledge* for languages other than English, but the evaluation will not capture it. To fully assess cross‑lingual competence:

1. **Collect parallel test sets** for each language used in training.  
2. **Compute language‑specific Elo scores** and *cross‑language transfer* metrics.  
3. **Incorporate domain‑specific benchmarks** (e.g., medical, legal, scientific corpora) to examine adaptability outside of general conversation.

Furthermore, *localization*—the adaptation of language for a target culture—requires evaluating not only linguistic accuracy but also *social norms* and *safety* in each language. This is particularly vital for responsible AI deployment.

## 10.6 Responsible AI: Bias, Safety, and Fine‑tuning Liability

### 10.6.1 Bias Mitigation

QLoRA’s finetuned *Guanaco* models exhibit *lower bias scores* than their raw LLaMA counterparts on the *CROWDSCAPE* metric. Nonetheless, bias is multifaceted:

- **Demographic Bias**: Representation of protected attributes (gender, race, age).  
- **Content Bias**: Tendency to over‑produce stereotypical or harmful statements.  
- **Contextual Bias**: Divergence in behavior across prompts that mention sensitive topics.  

A systematic audit would involve:

- **Token‑level frequency analysis** across demographic lexicons.  
- **Prompt sampling** covering a spectrum of sensitive scenarios.  
- **Human review** of flagged outputs for sub‑token tokenizations that might mask bias.

### 10.6.2 Safety and Evasion

The qualitative section reveals that *Guanaco* can sometimes *refuse unclear or disallowed instructions* but fails when prompts are *adversarially phrased*. This raises safety concerns:

- **Prompt injection**: Subtle re‑wording to force the model to reveal disallowed content.  
- **Self‑harm**: Whether the model can refuse harmful self‑instructions.  
- **Evasion**: Whether the model can circumvent internal safeguards laid out in the finetuning data.

Future studies should implement *adversarial testing suites* that systematically probe for such weaknesses, following frameworks used in recent prompt‑injection literature.

### 10.6.3 Liability of Finetuning

The QLoRA paper makes the bold claim that *fine‑tuning at 4‑bit precision* democratizes LLM training. However, open‑source LLMs are often licensed under non‑commercial or *non‑model distribution* clauses. Even though the *trained adapters* can be considered *model weights*, their legal status depends on the license of the underlying base model and any derived datasets.

Responsible scholarship must:

- **Clarify license compatibility** for *fine‑tuned adapters*.  
- **Disclose any restricted data** used in finetuning (e.g., proprietary dialogue logs).  
- **Acknowledge implications** for *model ownership* and *attribution* in downstream applications.

## 10.7 Summary and Future Directions

The QLoRA paper demonstrates that *4‑bit* quantization, coupled with *Low‑rank Adapters* and clever engineering tricks (NF4, double quantization, paged optimizers), can bring *65B*‑parameter finetuning into the reach of a single high‑end GPU. Yet, the scientific rigor of the evaluation remains limited by several factors:

| Dimension | Status in QLoRA | Recommendation |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| Inter‑annotator agreement | Moderate (kappa 0.42) | Increase raters, provide finer rubrics |
| Automated evaluator bias | Order effect observed | Counterbalance prompt order, calibrate |
| Benchmark diversity | 3 benchmarks | Add *MMLU‑open* and *BigBench*, multilingual tests |
| Unexplored scenarios | RLHF, other PEFTs, 3‑bit | Systematic grid searches, RLHF trials |
| Multilingual coverage | Limited evaluation | Language‑specific Elo, domain benchmarks |
| Responsible AI | Preliminary bias tests | Full bias audits, adversarial safety tests |

Embarking on these extensions will not only strengthen the empirical claims of QLoRA but also shed light on the *robustness* and *generalizability* of low‑precision finetuning. Moreover, as the community progressively shifts toward *resource‑constrained* training paradigms, such scrutiny becomes essential to ensure that the benefits of democratization do not come at the cost of reliability, fairness, or safety.
