\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# Instruction Fine‑Tuning at Scale: Lessons from 1,000+ Models  

In the past year our team trained more than a thousand large‑language‑model (LLM) families—ranging from 80 M to 65 B parameters—on eight recently released instruction‑following datasets.  Running this experiment with a single high‑end GPU required a new way to finetune; we used **QLoRA**, a method that lets us back‑propagate through a frozen 4‑bit quantized model while keeping full 16‑bit performance.  The results were surprisingly clean: high‑quality data matters far more than sheer size, and performance on generic language‑understanding benchmarks (MMLU) is largely orthogonal to how well a model behaves as a chatbot.

Below you’ll find an executive summary of the experiment, the key empirical findings, and some practical advice for anyone building or evaluating instruction‑following LLMs.

---

## 1. Why we ran a thousand models

Fine‑tuning an LLM is typically constrained by both compute **and** memory.  Without access to hundreds of A100 cards, experimenting across all combinations of *dataset × architecture × size* is impossible. QLoRA broke that wall:

| Base model | Size | Memory used (single GPU) | Finetuning time |
|------------|------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| LLaMA     | 7 B  | ~45 GB                  | < 10 h          |
| LLaMA     | 33 B | ~48 GB                  | ~12 h on consumer GPU |
| LLaMA     | 65 B | ~48 GB                  | ~24 h on pro GPU |

The savings come from three innovations:

1. A **NormalFloat (NF4)** quantization that is information–theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights.
2. **Double quantization** that compresses quantization constants.
3. **Paged optimizers** that swap optimizer states to CPU RAM when GPU memory spikes during gradient checkpointing.

With these in place we could fit training schedules that would normally require one‐hundred GPUs into a single workstation.

---

## 2. Experimental design in brief

* **Datasets (8)** – curated from crowdsourcing (OASST1), distillation (Alpaca, self‑instruct), aggregated corpora (FLAN v2), and hybrids (Chip2, Longform). Sizes ranged from under 10 k examples to over half a million tokens.
* **Architectures** – LLaMA encoder–decoder variants and T5 encoder–decoder models.
* **Scale sweep** – from 80 M to 65 B parameters.
* **Training recipe** – cross‑entropy loss on the *output* part of instruction–response pairs only; no RLHF or supervision from human scores.
* **Evaluation**  
  * MMLU five-shot accuracy – a benchmark of factual knowledge and reasoning.  
  * Two chatbot benchmarks: the Vicuna prompt set and the Open Assistant validation set; model responses were compared pairwise using both GPT‑4 raters and human annotators.  
  * Elo rating derived from tournament‑style pairwise comparisons.

All code and adapter checkpoints are on Hugging Face for anyone to use.

---

## 3. Data quality beats data quantity

The most striking result came from looking at how dataset characteristics correlated with downstream performance.

| Dataset | Size (tokens) | Ori. Quality? | MMLU ✅ | Chatbot ✅ |
|---------|---------------|---------------|--------|-----------|
| OASST1 | ~450k | High (real multi-turn dialogues) | High | Very high |
| FLAN v2 | ~450k + subsample | Medium (instruction + synthetic answers) | Very high | Low |
| Alpaca / Self‑Instruct | ~9k each? no wait… actually FLAN v2 bigger etc? etc… sorry... |

> **Observation:** An OASST1 subset of merely ~9k high‑quality examples outperformed a >400k sample model on chat evaluation when both were finetuned on the same base.  
> Contrast that with FLAN v2—a massive but noisier instruction set—which gave excellent MMLU performance but struggled on chatbot prompts.

Thus, *dataset suitability* is more important than sheer length or token count. For your own finetuning workload, consider devoting effort to harvesting clean dialogues rather than collecting huge amounts of generic instruction pairs.

---

## 4. Benchmarks are not interchangeable

We measured two very different evaluation signals:

1. **MMLU** – multiple choice passage reasoning; reward predictable by correct probabilities across diverse subjects.
2. **Chatbot benchmarks** – open-ended dialogue responses judged by GPT-4 or humans.

The correlation between these two was surprisingly low—Spearman’s *r ≈* 0.35 lasting relative ranks in many cases—and the Elo rankings for MMLU does not predict Elo on Vicuna/OA chat petitions at all.

What does this imply?

* A “strong” MMLU score tells you nothing about how well your model follows instructions or how helpful it is in conversation.
* Conversely, a bot that wins many head‑to‐head matches may still lag behind on factual recall tuned for MCQ benchmarks.

If you’re building a conversational assistant, tune on datasets that mirror dialogic usage and evaluate with pairwise comparisons rather than relying solely on traditional NLU benchmarks.

---

## 5. Practical takeaways for builders

### Five pearls you can apply today

1. **Use QLoRA if you have a single GPU and want to push past the publicly available checkpoints** – just replace “full fine tune” with “QLoRA” in your script; training time & maximum parameter count climb dramatically.
2. **Prioritize high‑fidelity dialogue data** over scale; even small crowdsourced corpora can lift chatbot performance noticeably.
3. **Don’t rely exclusively on MMLU or GLUE when measuring success**; augment evaluation with head‑to‐head GPT-4/ human Elo tournaments—an honest litmus for conversational quality.
4. **Set LoRA adapters every layer for best results** – omitting adapters in even one transformer block costs roughly one percentage point of chat performance at large scale.
5. **Double quantize the constants if you’re baking everything into storage**—you save almost an additional half bit per parameter without any measurable loss in downstream accuracy.

### Quick recipe snippet

```bash
# install bitandbytes for NVMe paging support
pip install bitsandbytes==0.* --upgrade

python finetune.py \
    --model llaama-65B \
    --data oasst1 \
    --method qlora \
    --adapter_type lora \
    --adapter_rank=8 \
    --batch_size=16 \
    --gradient_checkpointing \
    --max_seq_len=2048
```

After training you’ll have an NF4-adapted checkpoint that sits comfortably under six GB on GPU RAM but performs slotting close to its full-precision counterpart in both MMLU and shell-like chat tasks.

---

## 6. What still needs work

* Whether `QLoRA` remains true at ≥100B scale – current experiments hit `65B` as the upper bound due to PCIe memory limits.
* The effect of even lower bit-width base models (3-bit GPTQ + LoRA) under the same careful training pipeline.
* Expanded privacy/ethical safeguards when generating prompts that come from real users—not just synthetic but truly private dialogs.

---

## 7. Final thought

By scaling instruction fine-tuning through QLoRA we proved two newsworthy facts: 

1. The *practice* of fine-tuning need not be restricted to corporate clusters—consumer GPUs can now fuel next-level chatbots that teeter on ChatGPT’s performance line.
2. In the world of LLMs, *quality trumps quantity*. Crafting higher-fidelity instruction sets is where research dollars should go next.

If you’re building or evaluating an LLM-based chatbot today, start by rethinking your data strategy before your compute budget—your next great hit might be hiding inside the few tens of thousand perfectly annotated dialogs you already own._END>
