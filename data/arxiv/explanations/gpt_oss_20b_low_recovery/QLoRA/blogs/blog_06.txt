\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# Quantization + LoRA = Near Full FP Performance – Why It Works

**Can a quantized base model ever match full precision when fine‐tuned?**  
The short answer is *yes*, and the paper **QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs** shows exactly how. By freezing a 4‑bit *NormalFloat (NF4)* version of a pre‑trained model and then back‑propagating gradients through it into a tiny set of Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA), the authors recover almost all the performance you would get from full‑precision fine‑tuning. The result is a method that can train 65 B‑parameter models on a single 48 GB GPU, with only a single epoch of data and without any quality loss.

Below we unpack the key ideas that make this possible, walk through the evidence presented in the paper, and explain why this has huge practical implications for anyone working on large language models.

---

## 1. The Challenge: Memory‑Hogging Fine‑Tuning

Fine‑tuning modern language models is two‑step expensive:

1. **Memory for weights:** 16‑bit (FP16) training of a 65 B LLaMA explodes from ~5 GB (inference) to >780 GB (training).  
2. **Memory for activations & gradients:** Even if you could fit the weights, gradients from every layer balloon more than the weights themselves, especially with long sequences and large batch sizes.

Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA help by only learning a tiny fraction of parameters – usually playing with a handful of adapters added to each projection matrix – while keeping the pretrained weights frozen. Still, paying for billions of floating‑point activations is the hard part.

---

## 2. The Core Insight: “Quantize Once, Train Later”

The key trick in **QLoRA** is to *freeze* the pre‑trained weights *after* aggressively quantizing them to **4-bit NF4**, then train only the adapters. The question is whether back‑propagating through these coarse weights can still produce useful gradients for the adapters.

### 2.1 NormalFloat (NF4) Quantization

- **NormalFloat** is an *information-theoretically optimal* data type for zero‑centered normally distributed data – precisely how pretrained transformer weights tend to behave.
- NF4 stores four binary digits per weight but chooses bin centers that split the weight distribution evenly (each bin gets roughly the same number of samples). This keeps quantization errors spread evenly over all weights, unlike naïve 4-bit integers or floats where rare outliers get massive error.
- The resulting format lets you pack any weight tensor into 1/8th its original size (e.g., 5 GB → ~625 MB) *without* adding significant error during inference or backwards passes.

### 2.2 Double Quantization

- A direct 4-bit quantization would still need an extra per‑block scaling factor (“quantization constant”) stored in FP32, adding ~0.5 bits/per param.
- **Double Quantization** compresses those constants themselves: first quantize them integer‑style into FP8 (block size 256), and then store residual scales in FP32 (block size 64). This cuts an additional ~0.37 bits/per weight on average – roughly another 3 GB saved on a 65 B model.

### 2.3 Paged Optimizers

Training very large seqs often spikes memory as check‑pointed activations are re‑computed during backward pass. QLoRA moves optimizer state into “paged” CPU memory via NVIDIA unified memory; when GPU memory is low, those states get swapped out automatically. It has negligible impact on speed but prevents out-of-memory errors that often plague large runs.

---

## 3. Why Backpropagating Through Frozen NF4 Works

Intuitively you might think: *If I froze my weights at such low precision, wouldn’t I lose fine-grained curvature information?* The authors show that this isn’t true because:

1. **Adapters are Linear Projections Additive to Pretrained Weights**  
   The LoRA update adds a rank-*r* factorisation \( s\, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2 \). During backward pass, you only need gradients with respect to \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\). These gradients are computed using dequantised versions of \(\mathbf{W}\), but because \(\mathbf{W}\) is fixed, any error in its representation is constant and doesn’t affect relative gradient directions that drive adapter learning.

2. **Quantization Error Is Additive Noise**  
   Prior studies have observed that adding lightweight noise to parameters rarely hurts performance if you’re fine-tuning very few parameters; sometimes it improves generalisation by acting like regularisation.

3. **Adapters On Every Layer Recapture Expressive Capacity**  
   Earlier LoRA work found that applying adapters only to queries/values often underperforms fully finetuned baselines when base models are very large (the paper’s Figure 4). QLoRA increases adapter density (adding one per linear block), giving adapters enough capacity to express small per-layer corrections that compensate for any lost precision.

Combined these factors explain why the gradients provided by frozen NF4 weights stay informative enough for LoRA to do a near-perfect job rewriting behaviour in just a few epochs.

## 4. What The Numbers Say

| Model / Task                           | Baseline                                  | QLoRA | Gap vs Full FP |
|---------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|-------|----------------|
| RoBERTa+GLUE (125 M→3 B)              | Full FP finetune                         | Full FP LoRA | ≤0 |
| LLaMA + FLAN v2 / Alpaca + MMLU       | Full FP finetune                         | NF4 + Double Quanta × LoRA | ≤0 |
| LLaMA up to **65 B**, Vicuna benchmark | Full FP finetune                         | NF4 LoRA     | ≈0               |
| Guanaco 65B (OASST1)                  | —                                         | ✓          | **99.3% relative to ChatGPT** |

Key takeaways:

- Across GLUE, MMLU, and many instruction datasets, *NF4+LoRA matches or exceeds* full‑precision LoRA and full finetune baselines.
- Even at **65 B** parameters—a model size previously limited to multi‐node GPU clusters—QLoRA succeeds on a single consumer GPU.
- Guanaco, trained with QLoRA on OASST1, reached almost all of ChatGPT’s Vicuna performance while occupying only half as much memory at inference time.

The paper even offers an ablation: using simple low‑bit integers or floats instead of NF4 causes measurable performance drops (~1–2 percentage points on MMLU), confirming NF4’s importance.

---

## 5. Practical Implications

### Lower Barrier To Entry
Anyone with a single 48 GB GPU can now:

- Fine-tune LLaMA‐33B or -65B models
- Train even larger models if they choose lower trade-offs on accuracy
- Deploy updated models easily due to half–memory hardware footprints

### “Rapid” Experimentation
A normal inference instance checks whether your dataset biases are hurting performance; then you can quickly celebrate results and iterate without accessing expensive clusters.

### Responsible AI Opportunities
Because QLoRA significantly reduces compute needs:

- Users can fine-tune purely local data sets to keep personal data private.
- Smaller teams can develop safer chatbots without heavy reliance on proprietary corpora.
- More researchers will experiment with different instruction sets / RLHF variants within reasonable budgets.

---

## 6. Caveats & Future Directions

While QLoRA already impresses:

- The work probes only standard GPT/NLP benchmarks; other domains might see slightly different behaviour.
- Coil hasn't yet proven equivalence at **≥ 80 B** or beyond; scaling up may introduce brittleness.
- Some biases/misbehaviours still surface post-finetuning; further fine practices (RLHF or guarding with steppers) may be necessary.

The authors encourage exploring other PEFT methods beyond LoRA under QLoRA’s framework—perhaps BitFit or Prefix Tuning could benefit even more from high-fidelity quantisation.

---

## 7. Bottom Line

Quantize once with NF4 → Freeze → Train tiny adapters everywhere → You’ve got almost full precision anywhere you'd normally need super-sized GPUs.

“Can a quantized base model ever match full precision when fine‐tuned?”  
**Yes – when you freeze it thoughtfully and let adapters consume the residual freedom.”**
