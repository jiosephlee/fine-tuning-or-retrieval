# Guanaco vs. ChatGPT: What We Learned from Head‑to‑Head Elo Tournaments

## The challenge of comparing chatbots

Open‑source chatbots are suddenly popping up everywhere, each claiming to “match” or “beat” industry‑rated models like ChatGPT.  The problem is that the metrics we usually see—accuracy on a fixed test set, log‑likelihood on a language modeling corpus—do not tell us how useful a system is when it’s actually talking to a human.  

The *QLoRA* paper tackles this head on.  The authors built a new family of chatbots called **Guanaco** by finetuning a 65 B parameter LLaMA model with their novel *QLoRA* method (4‑bit quantization + Low‑Rank Adapters).  They then set out to evaluate the models in a realistic setting: they ran **head‑to‑head Elo tournaments** where each pair of systems competes on the same prompt and does not seek the highest log‑p but the *best* response from an evaluator’s perspective.  

Below I explain how the evaluation works, what the numbers actually mean, and why Guanaco’s performance is so impressive even though it never saw GPT‑3.5 or GPT‑4 during training.

---

## 1. From Vicuna scores to Elo scores: why the metric matters

The Vicuna benchmark gives you a **relative score**: you collect a set of prompts, feed them into your model and into ChatGPT, and then let ChatGPT rate both responses on a ten‑point scale.  You compare your model’s average rating against ChatGPT’s score, and write something like *“Model X reaches 94 % of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark”*.

There are two serious pitfalls with this approach:

1. **Order bias** – GPT‑4 (or any evaluator) tends to give slightly higher scores to the response that appears earlier in its prompt.
2. **Scale ambiguity** – A “7 out of 10” can mean very different things for different prompts or for different people.

The QLoRA authors avoided these issues by moving from an absolute scale to a **pairwise comparison** system—exactly the way chess players, professional gamers, or even scientific methodologists determine the relative quality of two candidates.

### Pairwise comparison

For every prompt:

- Ask *two* independent evaluators (either GPT‑4 or humans) *which* response—Model A or Model B—is better.  
- Record the result as A wins, B wins, or TIE.
- Repeat across all pairs of systems for all prompts.

With pairwise results you can compute an **Elo rating** for each model.

### Getting an Elo rating

Elo starts everyone at 1000 points.
For any match between models \(X\) and \(Y\), let \(P_X\) be the expected win probability given their current ratings:
\[ P_X = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\bigl((R_Y-R_X)/400\bigr)} \]
When \(X\) actually wins we move its rating by
\[ \Delta R = K \times (1-P_X) \]
towards \(Y\)’s rating; if \(X\) loses it moves back by
\[ \Delta R = K \times (-P_X) \]
with \(K=32\).  

After thousands of random pairings and random prompt orders, each model ends up with an Elo score that reflects how often it wins against every other candidate *in head‑to‑head play*.  Importantly:
- An Elo difference of +100 reflects roughly a 64% win rate.
- An Elo difference of +400 would mean one model almost always beats the other.

Guanaco’s best variant reaches almost full parity with ChatGPT on Vicuna when evaluated by both humans and GPT‑4—a huge leap over all publicly released open‐source chatbots up to this point.

## 3. What does “99 % performance” really mean?

On Vicuna we measure relative quality by having GPT‑4 rate responses next to ChatGPT’s output.  
If Guanaco earns *99 %* then on average its response is rated only half a point lower than ChatGPT’s in terms of this custom accuracy metric.

However:

- The absolute values still depend on how GPT‑4 splits points between “good” vs “great.”  
- For many prompts GPT‑4 assigned both systems almost identical scores; in those cases MMR (matched follows?) is essentially 0—so they tie.

So while “99 %” looks like a near‐perfect match, it still means there are rare prompts where Guanaco’s answer is noticeably worse or even missing subtleties.

---

## 4. The role of GPT‑4 in evaluation

Using GPT‑4 as an evaluator has big advantages:

- **Speed:** It can simultaneously rate thousands of prompt pairs without human throughput bottlenecks.
- **Consistency:** Humans get tired; GPT‑4 provides stable judgment across thousands of samples.
- **Scalability:** We could add dozens more models or more granular sub‐routines without huge cost.

But we had to check that GPT‑4 isn’t just reinforcing its own biases.  
The authors compared GPT‑4 judgments against a panel of Amazon Mechanical Turk workers:

- Overall order effect: The first answer in GPT‑4’s prompt gets ~0.3 extra points on average.
- Correlation: Kendall τ around 0.43; Spearman r ≈ 0.55 between human vs.\ GPT‑4 rankings at system level.
- Sample‐level agreement (individual prompt compare): Fleiss κ≈ 0.25—moderate at best.

So while not perfect, GPT‑4 serves as a *cheap but surprisingly well calibrated* proxy for human evaluation for broad benchmarking purposes.

## 5. Where even state-of-the-art chatbots fall short

> “Even though Guanaco comes close to ChatGPT on standard benchmarks…”

The weaknesses surfaced in two main ways:

1. **Safety & Refusal Patterns**  
   - For some instructions requiring refusal (self-advertising or policy-bound queries), Guanaco sometimes refuses too strongly (“I cannot comply”) yet at other times fails to do so safely and side‐steps policy constraints.
   - Slightly adversarial prompts break its refusal logic in predictable ways.

2. **Reasoning & Math**  
   - Guanaco usually can explain arithmetic steps correctly if fed step-by-step reasoning but fails on simple factoring or complex multi-step algebraic derivations—a known weakness shared across many LLMs.
   - In ambiguous contexts it tends to produce overconfident but wrong statements rather than admitting uncertainty.

These shortcomings emphasise that passing standard benchmarks isn’t enough: real-world deployments need rigorous emergent behaviour analysis and safety hardening beyond what should be captured by any single scoring metric.

---

## 6. Practical takeaway: Can we build high-performing chatbots without huge GPUs?

Perhaps the biggest lesson is that *high accuracy does not require superglacial training resources*.  

Qua QLoRA methodology:

- Quantize pretrained weights down to **NF4 (NormalFloat–4 bits)** + store scaling constants as FP8 (*Double Quantization*) → drastically reduces memory footprint.
- Use **LoRA adapters on every layer**, still only adding a few megabytes per system in trainable parameters.
- Manage optimizer spikes with **Paged Optimizers**, relying on CUDA unified memory so training doesn’t crash even with long sequences.

Result? Finetuning a **65 B LLaMA** took under 24 hours on one NVIDIA RTX 6000/4090 (~48 GB GPU). A small local machine can finish training a **7–13 B** variant somewhere around an hour or two per epoch—something previously thought impossible without a multi‐node cluster.

This paves the way for smaller labs or even hobbyists to experiment with state-of-the-art instruction tuning and keep privacy intact (“train your own assistant in your kitchen”).

---

## Final thoughts

The Elo tournament framework offers an intuitive way for anyone publishing chatbots to benchmark against peer models without domain-specific test sets that may favor certain stylistic choices (“Llama style” vs “OpenAI style”).  

Even so:

- Human judgements add nuance confirming that automated metrics correlate reasonably well but are not perfect proxies.
- Never ignore emergent safety concerns—metrics look good yet hidden counterfactuals persist.
- Quantilized finetuning is already at hand; bringing it into daily research pipelines will shift progress dramatically away from hyperscale GPU silos toward democratised engineering practice.

If you’re building next-generation assistants—or just want to scrutinise whether your own lightweight chatbot truly stands its ground—start by running your own pairwise tournaments using this simple framework and watch Elo rise as you tune smarter rather than more expensive!
