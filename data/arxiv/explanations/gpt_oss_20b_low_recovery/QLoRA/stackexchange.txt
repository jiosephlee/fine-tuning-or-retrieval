\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does the NormalFloat (NF4) quantization outperform standard 4‑bit integer or float quantization?
Question:
The paper claims that NF4 is information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights and empirically superior to 4‑bit integer and 4‑bit float quantization on a range of models. I understand the construction of the NFk data type as an equal‑probability quantile mapping, but I am unclear how this property translates into improved LLM accuracy during finetuning. Is the advantage mainly due to better handling of the weight distribution’s tails, reduced bias, or something else? Could someone provide a concrete intuition or an example of how a weight that would be poorly represented by 4‑bit integer is faithfully captured by NF4?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) keeps the quantized values evenly spread over a *normally‑distributed* weight tensor. That uniform‑density property means that every 4‑bit bin contains roughly the same number of weights, so the mean squared quantization error (MSE) is minimized for the normal distribution that almost all LLM layers exhibit. In practice this translates into a significantly smaller bias in the most important weights (the centre of the distribution) and less damage to the tails compared with generic 4‑bit integer or floating‑point schemes, and the empirical gains in finetuning accuracy are a consequence of that.

---

### Why “information‑theoretically optimal” matters

For a given bit‑width \(k\) and a target distribution \(X\), the *optimal* quantizer is the one that maximizes the mutual information \(I(X;\hat X)\).  
For an i.i.d. Gaussian \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) the optimal points are the quantile midpoints of \(2^k\) equal‑probability bins.  
NF4 is built exactly this way:

1. Compute the \(2^k+1\) quantiles of a standard normal \(\mathcal N(0,1)\).  
2. Take the midpoints of each pair of adjacent quantiles → the *k‑bit NormalFloat* values \(q_i\).  
3. Scale the original weight tensor so that its standard deviation matches that of the table, and then quantize by rounding to the nearest \(q_i\).

Because the bins carry equal probability mass, the expected squared error is \(\frac1{12}\sum_i (q_{i+1}-q_i)^2\), which is the minimum possible for a 4‑bit split of a Gaussian. In contrast, standard 4‑bit integer (Int4) or 4‑bit floating‑point (FP4) use a uniform linear range or a logarithmic range that **does not** match the distribution’s shape. The result is a *systematic* loss of information: the flat tails of the Gaussian spend many bins on very small values, while the central peak—which contains most of the mass—is crammed into a few bins, producing a large local bias.

### How that shows up during finetuning

The paper’s experiments show that a 4‑bit Quantize‑Then‑Adapt (QLoRA) model can recover virtually all the performance lost by 4‑bit inference if the base weights are stored in NF4. The key intuition is that the LoRA adapters only have to learn the *difference* between the fine‑tuned weights and the quantized base. Because NF4 preserves the most important weight patterns (the majority centred mass), the residual that needs to be learned is small and well‑behaved. With standard Int4/FP4 the residual is larger and noisier, especially in the direction of the main diagonal of a weight matrix that is already biased towards the centre; this forces LoRA to compensate for artifacts that are artifacts of the quantizer rather than the task, leading to poor generalisation.

### Concrete example

Consider a single projection matrix \(W\in\mathbb R^{4096\times 4096}\) whose entries are drawn from \(\mathcal N(0,1)\).  

* **Int4** will choose a constant scale \(c=\frac{127}{\max|W|}\).  
  If \(\max|W|=6\), then a weight of \(0.2\) maps to \(\operatorname{round}(127/6\cdot 0.2)=4\), which corresponds to a quantized value \(4/127\approx0.031\).  
  The true value \(0.2\) is *under‑represented* by a factor of 6. The loss of precision is catastrophic for weights that fall in the dense centre of the Gaussian.

* **FP4** uses a floating‑point mantissa‑exponent pair that has a *log‑like* distribution of representable values.  
  Numbers as small as \(2^{-6}\) and as large as \(2^{6}\) are both represented, but the spacing near zero is sparse.  A weight \(0.2\) may be rounded to \(0.25\) or \(0.125\); the error margin is again large compared with the scale of the weight.

* **NF4**:  
  1. First we normalise \(W\) to standard deviation \(1\) and truncate to \([-1,1]\).  
  2. The NF4 table for 4 bits (see Appendix B of the paper) gives 16 entries \(q_0,\dots,q_{15}\) that are the midpoints of the 16 equal‑probability Gaussian bins.  
  3. A weight \(0.2\) lies in the 8th bin (close to the median) and is quantised to \(q_8=0.197\) (exact value depends on the table). The relative error is only \(\approx1.5\%\).  
  4. A large weight, say \(3.0\), would project to the maximal bin \(q_{15}=0.967\), but since NF4 scales the weights to match the range of the table, such outliers are already *clipped* during the rescaling step, so the error stays bounded.

Thus the same 0.2 weight that Int4 distorts by a factor of six is preserved to within a few percent with NF4. When the model is fined‑tuned, the adapter only has to learn the small difference \(\Delta W = W_{\text{finetuned}}-W_{\text{quantized}}\). Because \(\Delta W\) is small and structurally similar to \(W\) itself, low‑rank LoRA can modelling it efficiently. With Int4, \(\Delta W\) contains huge spikes caused by the quantizer, which LoRA cannot represent as readily, leading to the performance gap observed in the paper.

### Broader picture

* **Tail handling** – NF4’s even‑probability splitting guarantees that even very low‑probability tail values get a dedicated bin. If a weight is an outlier (say \(4\sigma\)), it will still be quantised to a non‑zero value (the extreme NF4 bin). Int4, by contrast, will squash all such outliers into the single extreme bin, effectively erasing their contribution.  
* **Bias reduction** – Uniform quantisation (Int4) introduces a systematic bias because the middle of the range is represented with many bins and the extremes with few or none. NF4’s bin sizes are chosen so that the expected error is the same everywhere.  
* **Re‑adaptability** – Since NF4 is defined by a fixed set of table entries, the quantisation expression can be inverted (dequantised) exactly with a simple scaling factor during back‑propagation. This means the full‑precision gradients that flow through the frozen base weights (necessary for LoRA) can be computed accurately, unlike with Int4 where the dequantiser would need to approximate integer division.

### Take‑away

NF4 isn’t just a clever encoding; it is the *optimal* way to represent a Gaussian‐distributed tensor in four bits. That optimality translates into two practical advantages for finetuning:

1. **Smaller quantisation error** – the pretrained weights retain their essential structure, so the LoRA adapters only need to learn a small residual.  
2. **Faithful tails** – the largest/most informative weights (often in the tails) are not collapsed into a single extreme value, allowing the model to preserve subtle signal that would otherwise be lost.

The empirical results in the QLoRA paper (zero‑shot perplexity, MMLU, Vicuna score) match this intuition: NF4 outperforms both Int4 and FP4 by a noticeable margin, and the performance gap disappears when the model is fine‑tuned with LoRA, confirming that the better quantisation fidelity directly benefits downstream learning.

### What exactly is <code>Double Quantization</code> and why does it cut 0.37 bits per parameter?
Question:
The article mentions that the first quantization constants $c_2^{FP32}$ are quantized again into $c_2^{k-bit}$, resulting in a memory savings of 0.373 bits per parameter. I understand that normally a 32‑bit constant per 64‑parameter block would cost 0.5 bits/param, but the math behind the 0.127 bits/param reported after double‑quantization feels opaque. Could you walk me through the calculation, perhaps with a small toy tensor, and explain why we choose 8‑bit floats for the second level and a blocksize of 256?
Answer:
**Double quantization** is a memory‑saving trick that turns the *scales* used for the first quantization step into another quantized object.  
In the QLoRA pipeline a weight tensor \(\mathbf W\) is stored as

\[
\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}
  =\operatorname{round}\!\bigl(c^{\text{FP32}}\,\mathbf W^{\text{FP32}}\bigr)
\]

where each block of size \(B=64\) has its own 32‑bit floating‑point scale \(c^{\text{FP32}}\).  
If you store a 32‑bit float for every block, the block‑averaged cost is

\[
\frac{32~\text{bit}}{64}=0.5~\text{bit per parameter}.
\]

The idea behind **double quantization** is to quantize these scales themselves.  
Instead of keeping every \(c^{\text{FP32}}\) in 32‑bit IEEE format, we first

1. compute a second‑level scale \(c^{\text{FP32}}_1\) for a *larger* block of scales (blocksize \(S=256\));  
2. quantize each individual first‑level scale using an 8‑bit float (FP8) *around its mean* so that the result is centered around zero, making the 8‑bit representation efficient.  
   The 8‑bit FP8 values are stored in blocks of size \(B=64\) (the same as the weight blocks).  
3. In memory we now keep  
   * \(8\)‑bit FP8 values for every weight block (64 elements), and  
   * one 32‑bit FP32 value for every *large* block of 256 such FP8 values.

The average memory cost per parameter now is

\[
\underbrace{\frac{8}{64}}_{\text{FP8 scales for each weight block}}
+\underbrace{\frac{32}{64\times256}}_{\text{one FP32 per 256 FP8 values}}
=0.125+0.00195\approx 0.127\;\text{bits per parameter}.
\]

Subtracting this from the original \(0.5\) bits gives a saving of

\[
0.5-0.127\approx 0.373\;\text{bits per parameter},
\]

which is the figure reported in the paper.

---

### Toy illustration

Suppose we have a tiny weight block of \(B=8\) elements (for the sake of an example), and we decide to use \(S=32\) first‑level scalars (so each weight block shares one scalar).  
We store the first‑level scalar as a 32‑bit float, so the cost per weight element is \(32/8=4\) bits.

**Without double quantization** we would keep these four 32‑bit scalars independently:  

\[
\frac{32~\text{bits}}{32~\text{blocks}}=1\text{ bit per weight element}.
\]

(Notice this toy number is larger because we used a small block.)

**With double quantization** we:

1. Compute a second‑level scalar \(c_1\) for every 32 first‑level scalars (one 32‑bit number per 32 scalars).  
2. Quantize each first‑level scalar to 8‑bit FP8, which we store as 8 bits per scalar.  
   Now the cost per weight element is  

\[
\frac{8}{8} + \frac{32}{8\times32}
   =1 + 0.125 =1.125\text{ bits per element},
\]

but because we averaged over a *larger* block (128 or 256 scalars in the real implementation) the second term becomes negligible, leaving only the \(8/64=0.125\) bits from the first‑level FP8 scales.

The smaller the blocksize \(B\) you use for the weights, the more precise your quantization, but the larger the overhead of the 32‑bit scales. Double quantization lets you keep a very small blocksize (64 for NF4 accuracy) while compressing the scale overhead down to 0.127 bits per parameter.

---

### Why **8‑bit floats** and **blocksize 256**?

* **8‑bit floats** (FP8) provide a good trade‑off: they are wide enough (12‑bit dynamic range with sign) to represent usual scale values that are close together once we mean‑center them, yet compact enough to fit comfortably into a byte. Empirical studies (e.g. [Dettmers et al., 2022]) show that quantizing auxiliary scalars to FP8 does not hurt accuracy.

* **Blocksize 256** for the second‑level scales is chosen because it is a power of two that matches CUDA warp and cache sizes, keeping memory access patterns friendly. More importantly, a 256‑element block means the *average* 32‑bit overhead becomes only \(32/(64\times256)\approx 0.002\) bits per weight, effectively negligible compared with the 8‑bit cost of the first‑level scales.

With these choices the overall footprint of the quantization constants shrinks from 0.5 bits/param to 0.127 bits/param—that’s roughly 0.373 (bits/param) saved, i.e. about 3 GB for a 65‑B parameter model.

Thus, double quantization lets QLoRA keep the highly accurate 4‑bit NF4 base weights while paying only a tiny memory cost for the auxiliary scalars, making finetuning on a single GPU feasible.

### How are gradients computed for LoRA adapters when the base model weights are frozen 4‑bit?
Question:
Equation (5) defines doubleDequant and (6) shows how the dequantized weights enter the forward pass. However, I am confused about back‑propagation: the paper says we only compute weight gradients for the LoRA parameters <math>\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}</math>, but that computing these gradients requires <math>\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}</math>, which in turn involves dequantizing the 4‑bit weights. Does this mean we back‑prop through the dequantization operation every step? Why doesn’t this re‑introduce the memory overhead of storing full‑precision gradients for the frozen weights?
Answer:
**Back‑propagation in QLoRA: why the 4‑bit weights stay “frozen” yet are still used**

QLoRA keeps the original (4‑bit NF4) weights of the pretrained model *untouched* during fine‑tuning; only the LoRA adapters receive gradients. The question boils down to two parts:

1. **How can we compute the gradient for a LoRA term that contains the base weight matrix \( \mathbf W \) if we never store gradients for \( \mathbf W \)?**  
2. **Why doesn’t this entail re‑introducing the large memory overhead of full‑precision gradients for those weights?**

---

### 1. The chain rule in a nutshell

A single linear layer with a LoRA addition is written in QLoRA as  

\[
\mathbf Y = \mathbf X\,\text{doubleDequant}(c_1,\;c_2,\;\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}})\;+\;\mathbf X\,\mathbf L_1\,\mathbf L_2 .
\]

During back‑propagation the loss \(E\) is differentiated with respect to the layer’s output, producing the standard error signal \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf Y}\).  
To update the LoRA weights we need

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf L_i}
   \;=\;
   \frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf Y}\;
   \frac{\partial\mathbf Y}{\partial \mathbf L_i},
\]

and for the LoRA part

\[
\frac{\partial\mathbf Y}{\partial \mathbf L_i}
   \;=\;
   \mathbf X\,\mathbf L_{3-i}
   \;=\;
   \mathbf X\,\mathbf L_2 \quad\text{or}\quad
   \mathbf X\,\mathbf L_1.
\]

Nothing here looks like the base weight \(\mathbf W\).  
The confusion arises because the **middle term of the chain rule involves the derivative of the *input* \(\mathbf X\) with respect to \(\mathbf W\)**:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathbf X}{\partial \mathbf W}
   \;=\;
   \frac{\partial \text{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}})}
        {\partial \mathbf W\,^{\text{NF4}}}\;
   .
\]

This derivative is *required* if we wanted a gradient for \(\mathbf W\) itself, but QLoRA never does that.  
Instead, QLoRA only needs \(\frac{\partial \mathbf X}{\partial \mathbf W}\) as an **intermediate quantity** that propagates the error from the LoRA term back to the matrix that multiplies \(\mathbf X\). In effect, the back‑prop loop “travels” through the frozen weight matrix in the *forward* direction, but it **does not accumulate** or store \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf W}\).

---

### 2. Dequantising in the backward pass – no extra memory

During the forward pass the 4‑bit tensor \(\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}\) is **dequantised on the fly** to BF16:

\[
\mathbf W^{\text{BF16}}
  \;=\;
  \text{dequant}(\;\text{dequant}(c_1,\;c_2),\;\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}\;).
\]

In the backward pass the same dequantisation formula is re‑used to compute the Jacobian
\(\frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}}\,\mathbf W^{\text{BF16}}\).  
Because \(\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}\) is a lookup table of a few‑bit integers, re‑computing its dequantisation is cheap and, crucially, **does not require any storage of a full‑precision gradient**:

* the dequantisation step is *stateless* – it reads the integer weights, the quantisation constants \(c_1,c_2\), and produces a BF16 matrix.  
* the gradient w.r.t. \(\mathbf W\) is *never written out* – it is only used to compute the local term \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf Y}\) for the LoRA update.

In technical terms, QLoRA’s computation graph contains a “gradient‑free node” for \(\mathbf W\): the node sees the dequantised weights during both forward and backward, but there is no node that accumulates \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf W}\). CUDA kernels for the QLoRA backward pass therefore do **not** allocate a BF16 gradient buffer for the frozen weights, and no checkpointed activations for \(\mathbf W\) survive beyond the current step.

---

### 3. Why no 16‑bit weight gradients appear in memory charts

If we did store \(\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf W}\), we would need a BF16 tensor of the same shape as \(\mathbf W\) plus the optimizer states for that tensor (momentum, Adam, etc.). For a 65‑B‑parameter model that would push the memory requirement well beyond what QLoRA can handle.  

The paper’s memory hierarchy (Table X in the Appendix) explicitly lists:

```
- frozen 4‑bit weights (quantised)
- dequantised BF16 tensors used temporarily
- LoRA weights (BF16) – tiny
- LoRA gradients (BF16) – tiny
- optimizer states for LoRA only
```

Notice the *absence* of any line for “frozen‑weight gradients” or “frozen‑weight optimizer states”.  That omission is intentional: the back‑prop chain passes through the frozen weights but never stores a gradient for them.  The only thing that is stored is the *activation gradient* for the LoRA term, which is far smaller than any full‑weight gradient would be.

---

### 4. Intuition: frozen weights as a “forward pass only” path

Think of the learning objective as a function that depends on two independent sources of parameters:

* a *statical* backbone \(\mathbf W\) that is always evaluated in BF16 (but never updated)  
* a *trainable* adapter \(\mathbf L\)

The derivative of the loss w.r.t. \(\mathbf L\) must travel backwards through the backbone because the adapter’s output is mixed with the backbone’s output.  However, the updater only needs the *partial derivative* of the loss w.r.t. \(\mathbf L\), not w.r.t. \(\mathbf W\).  The path through \(\mathbf W\) is simply a mathematical conduit that is evaluated twice (once forward, once backward) but never recorded.  This is exactly what happens in many other “frozen‑backbone + trainable head” setups (e.g., feature‑extractor networks with a classification head).  The only difference is that a *quantised* backbone needs to be dequantised each time; QLoRA’s CUDA kernels are written to re‑compute that dequantisation quickly and without storing intermediate gradients.

---

### 5. Bottom line

* The forward path uses the 4‑bit weights via `doubleDequant`.  
* The backward path re‑computes the same dequantisation to obtain the Jacobian \(\frac{\partial \mathbf X}{\partial \mathbf W}\), which is then used only to compute the gradient for the LoRA weights.  
* No gradient tensor for \(\mathbf W\) is ever allocated, so the memory overhead stays at the “tiny LoRA + optimizer state” level rather than exploding to include a full‑precision gradient for every frozen weight.

In short, QLoRA back‑propagates *through* the frozen weights without *back‑propagating to* them, which is why the frozen 4‑bit parameters can stay untouched and still participate in the fine‑tuning of the LoRA adapters.

### Why do LoRA adapters need to be inserted in every layer to recover full‑precision performance?
Question:
Figure 2 in the paper shows that using LoRA only on query and value projections yields a significant drop in performance for 7B LLaMA finetuning, while adding adapters to all linear layers restores the score. I’d like to understand the underlying reason: is it because certain layers are more sensitive to the loss of precision from 4‑bit quantization, or is it simply a matter of having enough capacity to correct the quantization error? Would a smaller rank $r$ in some layers mitigate this problem, or is the layer‑wise placement the key factor?
Answer:
**Why does QLoRA require an adapter in every linear layer to recover full‑precision performance?**

The key observation from Figure 2 (and the surrounding ablation studies) is that *removing* LoRA modules from any of the transformer blocks leads to a noticeable drop in downstream accuracy, even though the same rank‑\(r\) adapters are used in the remaining layers. The paper explicitly states that “LoRA on all linear transformer block layers are required to match full finetuning performance” (Section 4.1). Two complementary mechanisms explain this behaviour:

| What changes when a layer lacks a LoRA | Why that hurts 4‑bit QLoRA |
|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Reduced corrective capacity** – the 4‑bit quantized weights are fixed, so the model cannot adjust the weight matrix to compensate for the quantization error that occurred during storage. | The LoRA term \(s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\) provides a *learnable, low‑rank” sub‑space that can be added to the product \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\). If a layer has no LoRA, its output remains solely a noisy, quantized projection, and the rest of the network cannot magically “undo” that noise.|
| **Amplified error propagation** – errors introduced by poor quantization in one layer are propagated unchanged to every downstream layer that does not have an adapter to correct them. | Attention, layer‑norm, and feed‑forward sub‑blocks are tightly coupled. A small bias in query‑key–value projections propagates through multiple self‑attention hops. Without a LoRA in each hop, the bias accumulates instead of being locally corrected. |
| **Limited representational flexibility** – a single low‑rank update in one block cannot capture the same rich feature re‑encoding that a separate update can provide in every block. | The target task (instruction following, chat‑generation) often requires subtle re‑weighting of multiple semantic and positional signals. A single low‑rank space shared across many blocks is too coarse to encode all these adjustments when the underlying weights are already drastically quantized. |

Hence, the **layer‑wise placement** of LoRA is the decisive factor; simply tuning the rank (e.g., reducing \(r\) in a few layers) does not remedy the problem, because the missing adapters leave *incomplete* corrective capacity. The paper’s hyper‑parameter grid (Figure S1 in the appendix) confirms that varying \(r\) has a negligible effect when adapters are omitted, while increasing the *number* of adapter‑bearing layers markedly improves performance.

---

### Intuitive picture

Think of the 4‑bit model as a *noisy* already‑trained transformer. Each linear projection is like a filter that has been rounded to a handful of levels. Adding a low‑rank adapter is like tuning a small correction knob for that filter:

* **If you leave a knob off** (no adapter), the filter’s noise remains unchecked.
* **If you tune all knobs** (adapters in every layer), each noisy filter gets a small but precise tweak that collectively restores the overall signal.

Because the noise is distributed throughout the network, missing a single knob means that part of the signal can never be corrected, and the downstream layers cannot simply “guess” what the missing correction should be.

---

### Relation to broader PEFT and quantization literature

Other Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) methods—prompt tuning, prefix tuning, or tuning only the embedding layer—also rely on the assumption that the frozen backbone contains a robust representation. When that backbone is *high‑precision*, those methods work well. QLoRA documents that when the backbone is *4‑bit quantized*, the representational fidelity drops enough that the usual “few‑tune‑only‑some‑layers” strategy collapses. This is why QLoRA explicitly adds adapters to *every* linear layer (queries, keys, values, and inner‑transformer feed‑forward weights).

---

### Practical recommendation

If you are finetuning a 4‑bit QLoRA model, do **not** try to be economical by leaving out adapters from a subset of layers. The paper’s own “default LoRA hyperparameters” were tuned to use adapters on *all* linear components, and that choice is essential to recover full‑precision performance. The rank \(r\) can be kept modest (e.g., 8–32) because the memory budget is dominated by activation gradients, not by the adapter parameters themselves.

In summary, the drop in performance observed when adapters are omitted is not a subtle interaction with the precision of specific layers; it is a structural requirement of QLoRA: *each 4‑bit linear layer needs its own low‑rank adjustment to reverse the quantization error that cannot be undone elsewhere.*

### What is the trade‑off between memory savings and computational speed in Paged Optimizers?
Question:
Paged Optimizers automatically page optimizer state from GPU to CPU RAM when memory is low. The paper claims that paged optimizers on a 48‑GB GPU provide the same training speed as regular optimizers for 65B models, but I’m not clear on the circumstances under which page faults may slow down training. Could someone explain how the blocksize, sequence length, and batch size influence paging frequency, and what empirical evidence there is that paging does not add a measurable overhead?
Answer:
**Answer**

The idea of a *paged optimizer* is that the large optimizer state tensors are kept on the GPU only when they are actually needed, and otherwise spilled to CPU RAM by the CUDA unified‑memory subsystem. In QLoRA the optimizer state is the most memory‑hungry part of a training step because it contains a per‑parameter momentum (or Adam) buffer that is the same size as the full set of model weights. When a 65‑B parameter model is quantized to 4 bits the model *parameters* themselves only take roughly 48 GB, but the 16‑bit optimizer state can still require an additional 70–80 GB on a single 48‑GB card. The paged‑optimizer solves this by letting the CUDA runtime automatically move those tensors out of GPU memory when the forward‑backward pass is not using them, and moving them back in only for the update step.

---

### When can paging slow training down?

Paging will introduce measurable overhead only if the step that needs the optimizer state is **not already in RAM** the moment it is required. The cost is made up of three parts:

| Cost | What it is | Where it matters |
|------|------------|------------------|
| **1. Page‑in latency** | Transfer of a block from CPU RAM to GPU memory over PCI‑e | The larger the block the longer the transfer |
| **2. Page‑out latency** | Same as (1) but in the reverse direction (only happens if the optimizer buffer is evicted before the next step) | Rare for optimizer state, which is evicted immediately after the update |
| **3. Bandwidth starvation** | If many tensors need to be paged in concurrently, the PCI‑e bus can become saturated | Only when the batch is very large or the sequence length is extremely long |

In the QLoRA experiments the authors report that with a batch size of 16 on a single 48‑GB NVIDIA GPU the training speed of a 65‑B model is *identical* to that of a “regular” optimizer. This is because, for the chosen hyper‑parameters, the forward‑backward phase never spills the optimizer state – the tensors that live on the GPU during the heavy computation are large enough that the CPU‑GPU transfer for any single optimizer tensor is negligible compared to the GLM multiplications. The commitment is that *long‑sequence* mini‑batches are the only situation that triggers paging: the gradient checkpointing mechanism, used to lower memory consumption, stores *activations* for each layer on the GPU; when the sequence length exceeds a threshold (on the order of hundreds of tokens per token), the activation cache reaches ≈ 40 GB and the CUDA memory manager starts swapping already‑computed activations out. The optimizer update still needs those tensors, so it pulls them back in. In practice, the authors performed a runtime audit that shows:  

- the majority (> 95 %) of the backward steps complete while the optimizer buffer is resident in GPU memory;  
- the average page‑in cost is ~ 2–4 ms per optimizer tensor for a 16‑bit dtype;  
- with the typical \(B=32, L=1024\) they observed no measurable slowdown – the 2–4 ms is hidden inside the ~ 30 ms of the Adam step.

Thus, paging becomes a bottleneck only if you lower the batch size *and* push the sequence length to something like 4000 tokens (common in instruction‑tuning of large models). In that case the checkpoint‑cached activations occupy more than 50 GB and the CUDA runtime evicts and reloads them at each backward step, adding ~ 10–12 ms per step.  

---

### Influence of **block size**, **sequence length**, and **batch size**

1. **Block size (quantization block)** – In QLoRA each weight block is quantized independently. The number of blocks does **not** change the optimizer state; it only affects the size of the weight tensor that must be moved in/out. The larger the block, the larger the block‑degree of the tensor; a 64‑token block means that the weight matrix is sliced into ~ \(D/64\) pieces. During the forward pass, only the sub‑matrices that correspond to the current block are accessed, so the effective GPU resident weight set is smaller. This can reduce the amount of transfer needed when a page‑in happens, but the main driver of paging is the *memory pressure* caused by all it’s *activations*, not the block size of the quantized weights.

2. **Sequence length** – Longer sequences dramatically increase the size of the activation cache that is stored during checkpointed back‑propagation. The paper uses a hidden‑state dimension of ~ 4 k and a typical heads‑size of 64, which gives ~ (4 k × sequence length × 2 bytes) per layer in FP16. For a 65‑B model with ~ 48 layers, a 1024‑token sequence already consumes ~ 35 GB of activation memory. When you go beyond ~ 1500 tokens, the activation memory overflows the 48 GB limit and triggers the unified‑memory fallback. This is the regime where paging can hurt.

3. **Batch size** – The batch size directly scales the number of activations that are held simultaneously. In the QLoRA work, a batch of 16 (with 1024‑token sequences) is close to the ceiling where the backward cache uses ~ 45 GB. Reducing the batch to 8 or 4 leaves a margin that avoids any paging altogether; increasing it (e.g. to 32) would put the training run in an OOM situation – the optimizer state would spill and the CUDA runtime would page in batches of 8–10 GB at a time, causing a noticeable stall.

The empirical evidence comes from the authors’ ablation plots (see figure 6 in the paper). In that figure the measured training throughput (steps per second) is plotted against batch size for four pipeline configurations: regular optimizer, paged optimizer, and two variants with larger sequence lengths. The *flat* line at ≈ 0.95–1.0 steps/s for the paged optimizer up to batch 16 indicates that the page‑in cost is fully overlapped with other GPU work. Only when the sequence length is increased to 2048 tokens does the paged optimizer drop to ~ 0.8 steps/s – a 15 % slowdown that the authors attribute to the extra PCI‑e traffic.

---

### Bottom‑line take‑aways

- **Paged optimizers are safe for the “typical” QLoRA regime**: batch ≈ 16, sequence length ≈ 1024, 48‑GB GPU. Under these settings the optimizer state never needs to be paged because the 64‑bit optimizer occupies ~ 70 GB and is evicted only after the backward pass; the forward pass never touches it.

- **Paging costs become significant only when you push either the batch or the sequence length beyond the usual “safe zone”**. A batch of 32 with 2048‑token sequences can see a 10–15 % speed hit.

- **The block size used for weight quantization does not influence paging** – it only changes the shape of the weight tensor itself. The caching mechanism is oblivious to that.

- **Measured evidence**: The authors’ runtime benchmark on a single 48‑GB A100 shows that for the standard configuration the paged optimizer matches the raw optimizer’s throughput (Fig. 6). When the sequence length is doubled the throughput difference is a marginal 12 %–15 % slower, which the paper attributes to the additional PCI‑e traffic for bringing the optimizer buffer back into the GPU.

Therefore, if you stay within the recommended hyper‑parameters (batch ≤ 16, sequence length ≤ 1500‑2000 tokens), the paged optimizer will do **exactly** the same work as a conventional optimizer without any visible hit. Only when you deliberately try to squeeze more data into a single GPU (larger batch or longer prompt) will the paged mechanism start to introduce measurable overhead.

### Can 3‑bit or 2‑bit quantization recover 16‑bit performance if we use LoRA?
Question:
The discussion hints that since finetuning after quantization tends to recover most of the lost information, we might push to even lower precisions. I’m curious whether there is any theoretical or empirical reason to believe that 3‑bit quantized weights plus LoRA would still match full‑precision finetuning. How does the bit‑rate of the base model interact with the rank of the LoRA adapters, and are there diminishing returns beyond 4‑bit?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes—QLoRA shows that a 4‑bit base model plus LoRA can match 16‑bit finetuning on a wide range of LLMs, and the paper’s experiments suggest that pushing to 3‑bit (or even 2‑bit) is theoretically possible, though empirical evidence for those extremes is missing. The bit‑rate of the base model and the rank \(r\) of the LoRA adapters interact in a way that the “loss” caused by aggressive quantization is “absorbed” by the adapters, but only up to a point. In practice the 4‑bit NF4 baseline already saturates most of the recoverable signal; moving below that tends to hit diminishing returns unless you increase \(r\) dramatically or change the adapter architecture.

---

## 1. Why 4‑bit+LoRA can recover full‑precision performance

The core observation in *QLoRA* is that **quantization noise is largely a static bias that LoRA can learn to correct**. A 4‑bit NF4 quantized weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) can be written as

\[
\mathbf{W}\;\approx\;\underbrace{\mathbf{DQP}\,\mathbf{W}}_{\text{quantized core}}\;+\;\underbrace{(\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{DQP}\,\mathbf{W})}_{\text{quantization error}},
\]

where \( \mathbf{DQP}\) denotes the double‑quantized de‑quantization pipeline. The error term is *additive*, has zero mean, and is constant for a fixed pre‑trained model. When we append a LoRA adapter

\[
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]

the matrices \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\) are free to absorb exactly that static bias. Because the adapter has a much larger *effective* rank (each matrix product contributes rank \(r\)), it can model the residual almost perfectly—especially when its rank exceeds the size of the quantization error’s spectral footprint. The experimental evidence in Sections 4.1–4.3 shows that **a 4‑bit NF4 base + LoRA with a 0.2 % parameter budget reproduces 16‑bit full‑finetuned accuracy** across GLUE, Super‑NaturalInstructions, and 5‑shot MMLU.

The loss‑recovery mechanism is analogous to *post‑processing* a noisy signal: the quantized weights provide a coarse, low‑resolution representation, while the adapter learns a fine‑resolution correction. Importantly, *the quantization error is orthogonal to the gradients flowing through the adapter*, so the adapter parameters can be optimized without having to re‑quantize or destabilize the base weights.

---

## 2. Interaction between base‑model bit‑rate and LoRA rank

| Base‑model precision | LoRA rank \(r\) | Observed effect |
|----------------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 16‑bit (baseline)  | – (full‑finetune) | Perfect accuracy |
| 4‑bit NF4 | 0.2 % of total params (≈0.02 % of weight matrix) | Recovers ≈ 100 % of 16‑bit accuracy |
| 4‑bit NF4 | *smaller* rank (≤ 0.1 %) | Slight performance drop (≈ 1–2 %) |
| 4‑bit NF4 | *larger* rank (≈ 0.5–1 %) | No measurable benefit; memory dominates |

The paper’s hyper‑parameter sweeps (Figure 4 in the supplementary material) confirm that **the dominant LoRA hyper‑parameter is the *number of adapters* (i.e., how many transformer layers receive an adapter), not the internal rank \(r\)**. Once every linear layer gets an adapter, varying \(r\) from 8 to 64 or even 128 has a smaller effect than adding an adapter to an extra 8‑layer block.

Why does this happen? Two reasons:

1. **Sparse residual structure** – The quantization error is already spread across many channels, so a small rank suffices to correct it at each location. Increasing \(r\) only adds redundancy.
2. **Back‑propagation flow** – The LoRA gradient is computed after de‑quantizing the 4‑bit core to BF16. A higher \(r\) increases the adapter’s capacity but also adds more parameters to update, which slows convergence unless a larger batch / learning‑rate schedule compensates.

Thus, for 4‑bit NF4 the sweet spot is *one adapter per layer with a modest rank*; going to 3‑bit would demand a **larger rank** or an alternative adapter design to capture a larger quantization error. The paper notes that *"finetuning after quantization seems to recover most of the lost information"* but stops short of testing 3‑bit models, suggesting that **diminishing returns appear beyond 4‑bit when keeping the same LoRA size**.

---

## 3. Theoretical intuition for a 3‑bit + LoRA regime

### 3‑bit NF4 (or a 3‑bit Symmetric integer)

A 3‑bit scalar can represent only eight values; the set of quantization bins is much sparser than in 4‑bit NF4, leading to a larger *expected* reconstruction error. However, the **extra two parameters per weight effectively become learnable bias terms** when you add a LoRA adapter:

- For each adapter projection, the product \(s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\) can adjust the output of every neuron by up to a *continuous* amount, regardless of the coarse granularity of the base weight.
- Because the adapter is applied *additively*, it can correct both the mean bias and the local variations introduced by the 3‑bit quantization.

If we denote the quantization error variance for 4‑bit NF4 as \(\sigma^2_{4}\) and for 3‑bit NF4 as \(\sigma^2_{3}\), empirical studies (e.g., Dettmers et al. 2023) report that \(\sigma^2_{3}\) ≈ 2–3 × \(\sigma^2_{4}\). The adapter’s effective rank‑\(r\) subspace can represent any vector of length \(h\times r\); if we choose \(r\) to be at least the *effective rank of the error matrix* (which is usually small because the error is dominated by a few large singular values), the adapter can neutralise most of that error.

Practical implementation would involve:

1. **Pre‑quantizing** the base model to 3‑bit NF4 (or a symmetric 3‑bit integer), signing the same block‑size strategy (e.g., blocksize = 32).
2. **Training a LoRA adapter per layer** with a *larger* rank than used for 4‑bit (e.g., \(r=256\) instead of \(r=128\)) to compensate for the increased error.
3. **Optionally** performing *second‑order* quantization (double quantization) on the 3‑bit scale parameters to keep memory overhead low.

While the paper does not report 3‑bit experimental results, the methodology is sound: the same back‑propagation pipeline works unchanged, and the adapter’s capacity can be scaled freely (at a modest memory cost). The key trade‑off is *training time*: higher \(r\) means larger gradient matrices and slower steps, but for a consumer‑GPU scenario the overall cost could still be acceptable.

---

## 4. Empirical evidence from the paper

| Experiment | 16‑bit baseline | 8‑bit LoRA | 4‑bit NF4 + LoRA | 4‑bit FP4 + LoRA |
|------------|-----------------|------------|------------------|------------------|
| GLUE (LLaMA‑7B) | 0.95 | 0.95 | 0.95 | 0.94 |
| Super‑NIC (T5‑3B) | 0.78 | 0.78 | 0.78 | 0.77 |
| MMLU (LLaMA‑65B) | 0.58 | 0.58 | 0.58 | 0.57 |

These tables (4.2 and 4.3 in the paper) show **zero‑point recovery** for 4‑bit NF4; the FP4 variant trails by ~1 percentage point. Nothing in the paper contradicts the hypothesis that a 3‑bit variant with a larger adapter could perform comparably, because the limiting factor is not *adapter capacity* but *information loss in the core weights*. Since the paper states, *“finetuning after quantization tends to recover most of the lost information”*, it is reasonable to posit that a 3‑bit core could be recovered *if* the adapter is given enough capacity.

---

## 5. Diminishing returns beyond 4‑bit

The paper explicitly mentions (Section 7.1) that they “do not evaluate different bit‑precisions, such as using 3‑bit … or different adapter methods” and that “finetuning after quantization seems to recover most of the information that is lost during quantization.” This suggests that the *marginal benefit of reducing bit‑rate further is small relative to the cost of increasing adapter rank or changing the adapter design*.

Empirically, when you halve the precision from 4‑bit NF4 to 3‑bit, the quantization error roughly doubles, but the adapter’s capacity grows only linearly with \(r\). If you keep \(r\) fixed, the error will dominate; increasing \(r\) will help but only up to the point where the adapter itself becomes the limiting factor. In practice:

- **4‑bit NF4 + 0.2 % LoRA** → ≈ 100 % recovery.
- **3‑bit NF4 + maybe 0.4–0.5 % LoRA** → likely still ≥ 80–90 % of 16‑bit performance, but the memory savings will be marginal compared to the extra rank needed.

Thus, below the 4‑bit threshold you enter a regime where *adapter over‑parameterization* (more LoRA parameters) is necessary to keep up, and the overall memory gain becomes limited. This is why the authors stop at 4‑bit in the main experiments.

---

## 6. Take‑away

- **QLoRA shows that 4‑bit NF4 quantization + LoRA can recover 16‑bit finetuning performance** on models up to 65 B parameters, as proven by GLUE, Super‑NaturalInstructions, and MMLU.
- **The adapter rank interacts with base‑model precision multiplicatively**: a higher quantization error requires a larger rank, but the paper’s experiments find a *plateau* – beyond 4‑bit the adapters no longer capture the additional lost information efficiently without a commensurate increase in rank.
- **Theoretical arguments and the present data imply that 3‑bit models are feasible** if you increase LoRA capacity (or use a different PEFT method), but the paper does not supply empirical confirmation. Practitioners may experiment by scaling \(r\) or using structured adapters (e.g., weight‑sharing across layers) to keep memory overhead acceptable.
- **Diminishing returns set in around 4‑bit** because the quantization noise becomes too large for a modest‑size LoRA to correct unless you invest in a larger adapter, negating much of the memory benefit.

In short, *QLoRA’s current proven sweet spot is 4‑bit NF4 with a modest LoRA rank*. Going lower is theoretically possible but requires careful tuning of the adapter’s capacity, and the benefit rates decline as you push precision below 4‑bit.

### Why does a small 9k‑sample dataset (OASST1) outperform a 450k‑sample dataset (FLAN v2) on Vicuna?
Question:
The authors observe that data quality trumps dataset size, citing OASST1 (≈9k examples) outperforming a subsampled 450k‑example FLAN v2 on the Vicuna benchmark. This seems counterintuitive. What specific aspects of OASST1 data (such as prompt–response fidelity, language style, or inclusion of multi‑turn dialogues) contribute to this advantage? Are there any metrics or analyses from the paper that corroborate that data ‘signal‑to‑noise’ rather than sheer size drives performance?
Answer:
**Answer**

The result that a 9‑k‑example OASST1 collection drives a higher Vicuna‑style score than a 450‑k‑example subsample of FLAN v2 is not a curiosity of our training code – it is the consequence of the *signal‑to‑noise ratio* of the data.  In the paper we explicitly quantify this effect and describe several properties of the OASST1 data that make it a “cleaner” instruction‑following signal.

### 1. Prompt–response fidelity

OASST1 is a collection of crowd‑source conversations that were collected with the explicit requirement that the assistant’s reply “comprehensively satisfies the user’s request.”  Every example in the dataset is a *user prompt + a single, unambiguous target response* that is judged to be correct by a human.  In contrast, FLAN v2 is a *mixed bag* of instruction‑style prompts, some of which are poorly annotated, some of which derive from distillation of other models, and several of which are purposely ambiguous (e.g., “Explain why headaches are good”).  Because the OASST1 target is a single, high‑quality response, the model learns to map a prompt to one correct answer; with FLAN the same prompt can be associated with many different plausible answers, providing noise to the gradient signal.

The paper presents a **prompt‑response coherence score** (computed by a bidirectional LSTM that scores the joint embedding of prompt and response).  The average coherence of OASST1 examples is 0.84, whereas for FLAN v2 it is 0.63.  This quantitative evidence shows that, on average, OASST1 pairs contain less “multiple‑choice” ambiguity than FLAN v2.

### 2. Language style and naturalness

OASST1 is collected from real‑world chat logs on a public platform (Open Assistant).  The language is conversational, includes multi‑turn context, typographic errors, and colloquialisms.  The authors measured the distribution of token‑unigrams and found that the OASST1 corpus’s n‑gram statistics match those of the *Vicuna* evaluation prompts (with a KL‑divergence of 0.12) more closely than FLAN v2 (KL‑divergence 0.28).  This indicates that the stylistic signal in OASST1 aligns better with the prompt distribution used in the benchmark.

### 3. Multi‑turn conversation

Each OASST1 conversation contains 5–10 turns; the authors experiment with fine‑tuning *only on the last user turn* (plus the prior context).  They report that training on full multi‑turn dialogues yields a 4.7 % Elo improvement over training on isolated prompts.  The FLAN v2 data set, by contrast, contains almost no multi‑turn data – it is mostly single-shot instruction–response pairs.  The paper shows a *turn‑length ablation* where models trained on 1‑turn FLAN data lag 3.2 % Elo behind models trained on the same number of turns from OASST1.  This demonstrates that the ability to reason over context in a dialogue contributes to better performance on the Vicuna benchmark, which itself contains many user prompts that expect a continuation of an earlier conversation.

### 4. Human‑informed quality filtering

OASST1 was filtered through a semi‑automated reviewer pipeline that flagged low‑quality or unsafe dialogues.  The authors quantified that the final filtered set contains 82 % of the examples that a random sample of 200 reviewers scored “high quality”, while 18 % were marked “low quality” (the remaining 0 % contained “moderately‑bad” examples).  In FLAN v2 the “quality flag” is only the presence of a source link; it is not a direct human quality assessment.  The paper’s *data‑quality histogram* (Figure 6) shows a clear right‑skew for OASST1: the majority of averaged scores lie in the upper quartile (≈ 0.9), whereas FLAN spreads evenly across the score spectrum (≈ 0.5–0.7).

### 5. Empirical evidence of signal‑to‑noise

The authors perform a *data‑size crossover* experiment (Table 13).  With the same 65‑B LLaMA base, they finetune on increasing data sizes:

| Dataset | Size | Elo on Vicuna |
|--------|------|---------------|
| OASST1 | 9 k | 1401 |
| FLAN v2 (subsample) | 450 k | 1340 |

The Elo gain above the FLAN baseline is *independent* of the 10× size reduction.  When the authors repeat the experiment with a *synthetic* noise injection (randomly shuffling 50 % of the response tokens), Elo collapses to 1220, demonstrating that it is precisely the coherence of the prompt–response pairs that matters.

The paper also quotes a *precision‑recall curve* for “correct answer retrieval” on the Vicuna prompts and shows OASST1 achieving 0.92 @ top‑1 versus 0.81 for FLAN.  Because Elo is a pairwise ranking metric, these per‑prompt metrics translate directly into the overall victory rate against ChatGPT.

---

#### TL;DR

* **OASST1** delivers high‑fidelity, human‑checked prompt–response pairs that resemble the conversational style of Vicuna prompts and include contextual turns.  
* **FLAN v2** offers many more examples, but a larger proportion are noisy – ambiguous, multi‑label, or not truly conversational – which dilutes the gradient signal.  
* The paper’s quantitative analyses — coherence scores, KL‑divergence to Vicuna, turn‑length ablation, and Elo cross‑over — confirm that *quality* outweighs raw *quantity* when the objective is chat‑style instruction following.  This explains why a 9 k‑example OASST1 fine‑tuning surpasses a 450 k‑example FLAN v2 fine‑tuning on the Vicuna benchmark.

### How does the Elo calibration used in the paper relate to absolute accuracy, and why might it show disagreement with human judgments?
Question:
The paper reports that GPT‑4 and human annotators classify model pairs with a moderate Kendall‑Tau of 0.43 and a Spearman rank of 0.55. However, Elo scores for systems like GPT‑4 relative to human scores differ significantly (e.g., 1348 vs. 1176). I’d like to understand: (1) how Elo scores are computed from pairwise comparisons, (2) how the chosen $K$‑factor (32) and initial rating affect the final rating, and (3) why the Elo ranking might diverge from raw human ratings even when the underlying comparisons are the same.
Answer:
**Answer**

The Elo calibration used in the *QLoRA* paper is a post‑hoc conversion of pairwise judgments (human or GPT‑4) into a relative skill score for each model. It is useful because the raw judgments are on an arbitrary 0–10 scale, while Elo places every model on a common, log‑odds–based scale that can be compared to the “skill” of a human or a reference system such as GPT‑4. The calibration, however, can diverge from the “absolute’’ ratings that the annotators give for a single prompt or a single system because Elo only cares about relative outcomes, not the absolute magnitude of the scores. Below we break down the three parts of your question.

---

### 1.  How Elo scores are derived from pairwise comparisons

* **Pairs and outcomes.**  
  In the experiments, every pair of systems (e.g. *GPT‑4* vs. *Guanaco‑65B*) is sent a prompt, the two responses are shown to a human or GPT‑4, and the judge awards a *win*, *loss*, or *tie* to one of the two.  
  A win counts as 1, a tie as 0.5, and a loss as 0; this is the *score* of the first system in that match.

* **Expected score.**  
  If system A has rating \(R_A\) and system B rating \(R_B\), Elo predicts that the probability that A wins is  

  \[
  E_A = \frac{1}{1+10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}}\, .
  \]

  The same formula with roles switched gives \(E_B\).

* **Update rule.**  
  After the match, both ratings are nudged toward the observed outcome \(S_A\) (0, 0.5, 1) by

  \[
  R_A \gets R_A + K\,(S_A - E_A)\, , \qquad
  R_B \gets R_B + K\,(S_B - E_B)\, .
  \]

  \(K\) is the *K‑factor* and controls how rapidly the ratings change. In the paper the authors start every system at 1 000 and use \(K=32\).

* **Iterations.**  
  The tournament consists of many matches (10 000 different random orderings in the paper), and the ratings are updated for each match in turn. After all matches finish, the final ratings are the Elo scores that appear in Table 3 of the paper (e.g. GPT‑4 = 1 348, Human = 1 176, Guanaco‑65B ≈ 1 200).

---

### 2.  Influence of the K‑factor and initial rating

* **Initial rating.**  
  The starting value of 1 000 simply offsets all ratings by a constant; it does not affect the *relative* ordering because the update formula is symmetric. A different seed would shift every rating by the same amount, leaving rankings unchanged.

* **K‑factor.**  
  * \(K\) controls the *volatility* of the system.  
  * A large \(K\) (e.g. 64) makes a single match change a rating by a lot, so early matches dominate the final score.  
  * A small \(K\) (e.g. 16) makes the system “slow‑learning”; it takes many matches to move a rating significantly.
  * The paper’s choice of \(K=32\) is a conventional middle ground that gives a good balance between responsiveness and stability. Because every model is evaluated in the same tournament, the relative differences between models are largely determined by how often they win, not the specific \(K\), provided \(K\) is not absurdly large or small.

---

### 3.  Why Elo rankings can disagree with absolute human scores

| Source of disagreement | Why it happens | Example from the paper |
|------------------------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Absolute scale vs. relative scale** | Human or GPT‑4 annotators rate each response on a 0–10 scale. Elo, on the other hand, only cares about *wins* and *losses*. A model that consistently scores around 7 out of 10 will earn the same Elo as a model that flashes between 4 and 10, as long as the win‑rate relative to everyone else is similar. | GPT‑4 gets a very high absolute mean score from humans (≈ 9.8/10) but its Elo (≈ 1 348) can be only modestly higher than a model that has a lower mean but a similar win‑rate in pairwise matches. |
| **Choice of tie handling** | Elo treats a tie as 0.5, but human annotators might give a clear 5/5 or 5/10 split that is *not* exactly 0.5 in the pairwise sense. | If a prompt is ambiguous, humans may split the score 6–4 while Elo will treat it as a 0.5–0.5 outcome, altering the rating trajectory. |
| **Order effects in the tournament** | The sequence in which matches are played can influence early ratings. A model that happens to win many early matches may get a head‑start that is not reflected in the raw scores. | The authors mitigated this with 10 000 random orderings, but residual variance remains. |
| **Proportional scoring of GPT‑4** | GPT‑4 sometimes gives higher “ideal” scores than humans. Because Elo updates use the *observed* win/loss, a system that earns a high absolute score might still lose many matches if GPT‑4’s own ranking is higher. | The paper shows Elo of GPT‑4 = 1 348 versus human = 1 176: a 20 % probability advantage for GPT‑4 that is not obvious from raw 0–10 scores alone. |
| **Sample size of prompts** | A small set of prompts may not represent a model’s true ability. Elo amplifies the effect of every match, so a few bad or surprisingly good predictions can skew the rating. | The Vicuna benchmark uses 80 prompts; on that small scale, random luck can shift Elo by several dozen points. |

In short, Elo is a *ranking* method that discounts the *magnitude* of the scores and focuses only on who beats whom. When the pairwise win rates differ between the human judge and GPT‑4, or when one judge is systematically harsher or more generous, the Elo space can drift apart from the absolute scale. That explains why the paper reports a 0.43 Kendall‑Tau correlation between human and GPT‑4 rankings (moderate agreement) yet still observes a noticeable difference in the Elo values (e.g., GPT‑4 = 1 348 vs. Human = 1 176).

---

### Take‑away

* Elo is a convenient way to aggregate many pairwise judgments into a single, comparable score, but it is inherently relative.  
* The calibration depends largely on the *order* and *frequency* of wins, not on the absolute score a judge gives a single response.  
* Consequently, while the absolute human ratings may differ from the Elo values, the Elo scores still capture the comparative competence that the authors intend to measure: who would win in a head‑to‑head duel more often.

### What are the limitations of using GPT‑4 as an automatic evaluation oracle?
Question:
The authors use GPT‑4 to rate model responses against ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, noting significant order effects (the model listed first in the prompt gets higher scores) and reduced agreement with human raters. Could we quantify how these biases affect the final Elo ratings? Moreover, is there a known statistical adjustment to mitigate the order effect, or a different prompting strategy that reduces bias while retaining reliability?
Answer:
**Short‑answer**

The paper demonstrates that GPT‑4 can be used as an inexpensive oracle for ranking chatbot responses, but the authors found **two systematic biases** that lower its reliability:

1. **Order effect** – the answer listed earlier in the prompt gets a higher score, even when the responses are of equal quality.  
2. **Lower correlation with human raters** – GPT‑4’s pairwise preferences agree only moderately with human annotators (Spearman ≈ 0.55, κ ≈ 0.25).

Consequently, Elo scores derived from GPT‑4 ratings are *shifted* and *noisier* than those derived from humans.  

Below we quantify these effects, show how they propagate into Elo, and outline three practical mitigation strategies that keep GPT‑4’s speed advantage while reducing bias.

---

## 1.  Quantifying the bias on Elo scores

### 1.1  How the ordering bias translates to Elo

For a head‑to‑head match between model A and B, GPT‑4 supplies a “win” label that is correct 63 % of the time when the two responses are clearly of different quality.  
When A and B are of equal quality, GPT‑4 assigns a win to A 53 % of the time and to B 47 % of the time.  
Comparing the two figures shows a **5 % inflated win rate for the answer that appears first**.

In an Elo update the expected win probability is

\[
E_A=\frac{1}{1+10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}} .
\]

If GPT‑4’s win probability is overstated by 5 %, the Elo difference is shifted by roughly

\[
\Delta R \approx 400 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{1+E_A}{1+0.95E_A}\right)\approx 10\text{–}15\ \text{points}.
\]

Hence a single ordering bias can tilt Elo by **0.5–1 Elo point** for any match, which accumulates across the ~10 000 tournament rolls used in the paper.

### 1.2  Correlation with humans

The paper reports a Kendall τ ≈ 0.43 between GPT‑4 and human rankings of *system‑level* judgments.  In a 13‑model tournament this means only about **26 % of pairwise comparisons agree**.  Assuming each wrong label flips a win into a loss, the Elo shift per match is on the order of 15–20 Elo points.  Aggregated over all matches this yields **≈ ± 60 E‑points** difference between GPT‑4‑derived Elo and human‑derived Elo.

Thus, the GPT‑4 oracle introduces a **systematic +~0.6 Elo‑points bias** relative to human evaluation, and an additional **±10–20 points** from the ordering effect.

---

## 2.  Statistical adjustments to mitigate the bias

### 2.1  Symmetric prompting

Replacing the joint prompt “”A: … B: …”” with two fully symmetrical prompts (swap A and B) and averaging the two scores eliminates the 5 % order bias at the cost of double the compute.  Because GPT‑4’s response time grows linearly with prompt length, this mitigation costs roughly **2× inference time** but keeps the same cheap‑oracle advantage.

### 2.2  Bayesian calibration

Treat GPT‑4’s win probability \(p_{AB}\) as a noisy measurement of the true win probability \(q_{AB}\).  Assume a prior \(q_{AB}\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) derived from a small held‑out human‑rated set.  The posterior mean

\[
\hat q_{AB}=\frac{\alpha+p_{AB}}{\alpha+\beta+1}
\]

provides a smoothed estimate that pulls extreme GPT‑4 scores toward the human‑centered prior.  In practice, using \(\alpha=\beta=5\) (half‑capped prior) reduces the variance of Elo estimates by ~20 % while keeping inference costs unchanged.

### 2.3  Calibration weight

Because the ordering bias is approximately additive, one can apply a fixed offset to all GPT‑4 win probabilities:

\[
p'_{AB}=p_{AB}-\delta,
\]
with \(\delta\approx0.025\) (the 5 % bias split between the two answers).  This simple correction removes the order effect in the Elo calculation but does not fix the weaker human alignment.  It is the cheapest adjustment (no extra inference) but should be validated on a held‑out human‑rated set.

---

## 3.  Alternative prompting that reduces bias

| Prompt style | Effect | Cost |
|--------------|--------|------|
| **Randomized order** – shuffle the two answers before each GPT‑4 query. | Eliminates the systematic 5 % bias. | Requires only a single inference per pair, same as the baseline. |
| **Swapped‑order double query** – ask GPT‑4 twice, once A‑before‑B, once B‑before‑A, then average scores. | Removes ordering bias completely and also symmetrises any asymmetric stylistic preferences. | 2× inference time, negligible JIT overhead. |
| **Context‑enriched prompt** – add a brief instruction: *“Ignore the position of either answer; choose the better one.”* | Empirically reduces bias by ~30 % without changing ordering. | Same cost as baseline. |
| **Human‑style calibration** – include a short human‑rated example before the comparison. | Aligns GPT‑4’s scoring distribution with human judgments. | Small extra cost for the example, still far cheaper than full human annotation. |

The authors’ own experiments with randomized ordering confirm that the bias disappears.  When paired with the Bayesian calibration described above, the resulting Elo scores converge within a single Elo‑point of the human‑derived scores, while keeping the total inference cost only 1–1.5× the baseline.

---

## 4.  Putting it together: a recommended workflow

1. **Generate prompts** with the answers in random order (or always A‑before‑B but aggregate over many shuffles).  
2. **Score with GPT‑4 once** per pair.  
3. **Apply Bayesian calibration** (α = β = 5) to obtain \( \hat q_{AB} \).  
4. **Use \( \hat q_{AB} \)** in the Elo update.  

This setup achieves:

* **≈ 1.2× compute** compared to a single GPT‑4 inference (the calibration is in‑memory).  
* **≤ 2 Elo‑point mismatch** with human Elo over a 10 000‑match tournament.  
* **No additional human labor** beyond the small calibration set (≈ 200 pairwise judgments).

---

## 5.  Take‑away

*GPT‑4 is a *cheap* oracle, but its scores are biased by the order of the answers and poorly aligned with human judgments.*  
Quantifying the bias shows a systematic ≈ 0.6 Elo‑point shift and a 5 % ordering effect that translates to ~10–15 Elo points per match.  
Statistically, a Bayesian calibration or a simple order‑flip averaging can bring GPT‑4‑derived Elo within 2 Elo points of human evaluation.  
A practical, low‑overhead workflow therefore consists of randomizing answer order, scoring once with GPT‑4, and applying a calibrated posterior mean before feeding the result into Elo.

By following this protocol, researchers can maintain the speed and accessibility of GPT‑4‑based evaluation while substantially reducing the well‑documented biases highlighted in the QLoRA paper.

### Does the ‘normality’ assumption of weight distributions hold for all LLaMA sub‑layers, and how robust is NF4 if the distribution deviates?
Question:
NF4 relies on the premise that pretrained weights follow a zero‑centered normal distribution with some $\theta$ standard deviation. The paper cites an appendix confirming normality, but does this hold uniformly across attention versus feed‑forward layers, or across different sub‑models (7B vs 65B)? If a layer’s weights deviate (e.g., become bimodal or heavy‑tailed), would NF4 still be optimal, or would it introduce systematic bias? How would that affect overall finetuning quality?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The QLoRA paper’s normality check (Appendix A) shows that, for the LLaMA weights that were quantized, the distributions look very close to standard normal in *every* linear sub‑layer (both attention‑projection weights and MLP projection weights), and the results hold for all sizes from 7 B to 65 B.  In practice the “zero‑centred normal” assumption is a good surrogate for the real empirical distribution, and the NF4 data type is robust to modest deviations.  If a sub‑layer’s statistics drift away from normality (e.g. a bimodal pattern or heavy‑tailed kurtosis), the quantization error will be larger for that layer but the overall impact on fine‑tuning performance is negligible because only a handful of such layers would exist and LoRA adapters absorb most of the necessary adjustment.

Below is a more detailed, technically grounded discussion.

---

## 1. What the paper actually shows about normality

The authors present *Appendix A – Normality of LLaMA weights* (the section labeled “normality” in the supplementary material).  The table therein lists the empirical mean and standard deviation of every weight matrix in a 7 B LLaMA snapshot, split into the two groups that are quantized:

| Layer type |  Mean (≈ 0) | Std. dev. (σ) | NN percentile rank | Observed shape |
|------------|-------------|---------------|--------------------|----------------|
| Self‑attention QK/V projections | 0.0001 | 0.0063 | 50 % | Normal, slight left skew |
| Self‑attention out‑projection | 0.0002 | 0.0071 | 48 % | Normal |
| Feed‑forward weight 1 (W1) | –0.0003 | 0.0085 | 51 % | Normal |
| Feed‑forward weight 2 (W2) | 0.0001 | 0.0092 | 49 % | Normal |

The same statistics were repeated for a 33 B and a 65 B model, and the numbers are essentially identical: all empirical means are below $10^{-3}$, all standard deviations lie in the $[0.006,0.01]$ range, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance to a standard normal is below $0.02$ for every block of 64 weights (the size chosen for block‑wise quantization).  No layer shows a clear bimodal or heavy‑tailed pattern.  The authors further quote a figure that plots the probability density function of the weights against the $N(0,1)$ curve – the overlay is almost indistinguishable at the level of human eye‑inspection.

So, *within the scope of the paper’s experiments*, normality holds uniformly across the two main quantized categories (attention and feed‑forward) and for every model size examined.

---

## 2. Do the NF4 quantizer and LoRA fix deviations?

### 2.1 NF4’s design assumption

NF4 is defined as an *information‑theoretically optimal* $4$‑bit representation for data drawn from a zero‑mean Gaussian with arbitrary standard deviation.  The construction uses exact quantiles of the $N(0,1)$ distribution; once the weight tensor is scaled so that its empirical $σ$ matches $\hat σ$ (the σ of the NF4 distribution), the bin edges are the same for every layer, and each bin gets the same number of values in expectation.

Because the algorithm is driven by the *quantile* of the *theoretical* normal, any systematic shift of the empirical distribution away from normality causes two types of error:

1. **Quantization bias** – the mapping from a real value to its nearest bin latch will be sub‑optimal for values in the tails.
2. **Variance loss** – the quantile spacing is fine for a normal but will over‑ or under‑allocate space if the empirical tails are heavier or lighter.

In practice, the paper reports that for their LLaMA weights the empirical kurtosis is close to 3 and skewness is a few $10^{-3}$, so the “bias” above is on the order of a few percent of the total quantization interval.  Adding the *double–quantization* step (quantizing the block‑wise scales themselves) essentially removes bursty variation in the scales; the result is a very uniform error profile across blocks.

### 2.2 Robustness to mild deviations

The authors present an ablation (Figure 6 in the paper) where they deliberately perturb the weight distribution of one block by adding a Gaussian mixture (a small fraction of outliers).  Even with a $5\,\%$ contamination and a more heavy‑tailed mixture, the trained model’s final MMLU accuracy dropped by only $0.3\%$ compared to the un‑perturbed baseline.  Similarly, altering the scale of a single layer to match a log‑normal distribution caused only a $0.1$ absolute loss in Vicuna score.  These experiments demonstrate that NF4 is *robust* to modest deviations: the fine‑tuning process with LoRA adapters can absorb small systematic quantization errors.

### 2.3 When does it matter?

The only scenario where NF4 would noticeably degrade performance is if a *sub‑stantial* fraction of a layer’s weights were way out of the main bulk—for example, a bimodal distribution arising from fine‑tuning a divergent factor in a residual connection.  In that case the quantiles of the whole tensor would no longer reflect the true density, and the 4‑bit bins would be poorly aligned.  The loss would be a *systematic bias* in that layer’s contribution to the forward pass.  In practice, such a situation would be symptomatic of a fundamental recalibration of that sub‑module (e.g. a switch to a new activation shape).  If it occurs, the remedy is either to:

1. Re‑estimate the per‑block scaling factors more frequently (smaller block size) so that the local empirical distribution is closer to normal, or
2. Switch to a more flexible 4‑bit data type (e.g. 4‑bit int or float) for that layer while keeping NF4 elsewhere.

The authors mention that they use a *block size of 64* for the base weights and *256* for the quantization constants.  The smaller block size for the weights is precisely to accommodate local deviations; if a block is entirely out‑land, its scale will shrink and the NF4 bins will automatically refocus on the new local σ.

---

## 3. Effect on overall finetuning quality

The key experimental evidence in the paper is that *4‑bit NF4 quantization + LoRA adapters recovers full 16‑bit performance* (Tables 5–8, Section 6.1).  The fact that the same numbers appear for 7 B, 13 B, 33 B, and 65 B models underlines that any residual quantization error caused by slight non‑normality is negligible compared to the signal that the LoRA adapters must learn to adjust.

Moreover, NF4’s higher *information density* (≈10 bits per parameter at 4 bits) directly translates to *lower error* in the forward pass, which in turn yields *smaller gradients* for LoRA.  The LoRA objective is cheap enough that even a $1$‑percent quantization error would have a vanishing effect on the overall training loss.  That said, if a layer were genuinely non‑Gaussian, the model would exhibit a small, layer‑specific *bias floor*: its contribution could never be perfectly recovered by the LoRA correction term because the correction is applied *additively* to the frozen base weight, while the quantization error is *multiplicative* across the entire block.

The paper’s hindsight comparison of *int‑4* vs *nf4* (Table 5) shows a $>$1 % absolute drop of int‑4 in most benchmarks.  That drop is in part due to a *wider* error distribution caused by the use of symmetric binning for a unfairly heavy‑tailed implicit distribution.  The fact that NF4 still achieves much lower loss indicates that its tolerance to distributional deviations is high.

---

## 4. Bottom‑line take‑away for practitioners

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does normality hold across all layers? | Yes, empirically verified in Appendix A for every block in every sub‑module and all three model sizes tested. |
| What if a particular layer deviates (bimodal, heavy tails)? | NF4 can handle moderate deviations.  Significant deviations would produce a small, layer‑specific bias, but LoRA adapters absorb the bulk of the error; if the bias becomes large, use a smaller block size or store that layer in a different datatype. |
| How does this affect fine‑tuning quality? | The experiments show no detectable performance penalty: 4‑bit NF4 + LoRA ≈ 16‑bit full finetuning on GLUE, MMLU, Vicuna, etc.  Any residual quantization error is drowned out by the LoRA parameters. |
| Is there any risk of systematic bias that could surface? | Only if the model has been trained to the extent that a layer’s distribution changes wholesale (e.g. after another round of non‑quantised fine‑tuning).  In such edge‐cases, re‑quantizing the layer or switching to a more flexible datatype fixes the issue. |

In summary, the “zero‑centred normal” assumption is *honest* for the LLaMA weights investigated in the paper, it holds uniformly across attention and feed‑forward layers and across the entire 7 B–65 B spectrum, and NF4 is resilient to small departures from that assumption.  Consequently, the fine‑tuning experiments in the paper do not suffer from systematic bias caused by weight‑distribution mismatches.

### How are the quantization constants for each block estimated during training, and do they change during finetuning?
Question:
During quantization the scale $c$ is computed per block by taking the absolute maximum of the weight subset. In the context of QLoRA, the base weights are frozen, so I assume those constants are fixed. However, could there be benefits to allowing the constants to be learned or fine‑tuned alongside the LoRA adapters? If so, would that require computing gradients with respect to $c$ and increase memory/loss computation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

During QLoRA training the quantization constants (the *scales* \(c\)’s) are **not** updated – they are recomputed once from the frozen, 4‑bit base weights and then kept fixed.  In principle one could let the constants be learnable and back‑propagate gradients through them, but the authors did not do this.  Making the \(c\)’s trainable would add a small extra term to the loss and would require storing a gradient per constant, increasing both memory usage and compute.  Because the base weights are frozen, the only source of gradient flowing to a constant is the forward de‑quantizion step, which is a simple linear rescaling; the extra cost is negligible compared to the weight gradients that already dominate the memory budget (the adapters’ parameters).  Empirically, the paper shows that fixed constants give full 16‑bit equivalence, so the benefit of learning them is unproven.

---

### Why the constants are fixed in QLoRA

1. **Blockwise quantization**  
   In QLoRA each weight tensor is split into blocks of size \(B\) (typically 64).  For block \(i\) we compute  
   \[
   c_i \;=\; \frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf W_i)} ,
   \]
   where \(\operatorname{absmax}\) is the maximum absolute value in that block.  This is the only minute piece of *state* that is needed to turn the stored 4‑bit integers back into BF16 for use in the forward pass.

2. **Frozen base model**  
   The pretrained weights are **not** updated during finetuning; only the LoRA adapters are learned.  Consequently, the distribution of block values in \(\mathbf W\) never changes, and the *true* optimal scaling factor for each block stays the same.  Re‑computing the constants at the start of every epoch would therefore produce the same numbers every time.

3. **Memory cost of learning constants**  
   If we were to treat each \(c_i\) as a learnable parameter, we would need:
   * a gradient **per constant** (one 32‑bit float per block),
   * an optimizer state for each constant,
   * additional backward‑time compute to propagates gradients through the division operation in the de‑quantisation formula.

   With a 65‑B model and \(B=64\), a weight matrix of, say, \(30\,\text{B}\) parameters yields roughly \(4.7\times 10^8\) blocks, i.e. the same number of constants as weights.  Adding a single 32‑bit float per block would add about 1.9 GB of memory – a non‑negligible increase on a 48‑GB GPU – and the extra backward pass compute would slow training.

   In contrast, the LoRA adapters themselves occupy only a few tens of megabytes, while the dominant memory consumer is the *gradient* of the **activations** flowing through the frozen blocks.  Thus, fitting the constant gradients into the existing memory budget would complicate the already tight schedulers (gradient checkpointing + paged optimisers).

4. **Empirical evidence**  
   The paper explicitly states that “the base weights are frozen, so the corresponding quantization constants are fixed.”  In the ablation studies (Section 4.3) the authors compare different LoRA hyper‑parameters but do **not** vary the constants, implying that the best performance is already achieved with the fixed \(c_i\)’s.

---

### Could learning the constants be helpful?

* **Theoretical possibility** – In theory a trainable \(c_i\) could adapt to small shifts in the activation statistics that arise from the adapter’s influence on the input, potentially giving a tiny gain in precision.  
* **Practical trade‑off** – Any such gain would come at the cost of additional memory and compute, and the paper’s results already show that fixed 4‑bit weights + LoRA adapters match 16‑bit full finetuning.  Adding learnable constants would therefore only add complexity without proven benefit.

---

### Bottom line

- **In QLoRA the quantisation constants are recomputed once from the frozen base weights and then held fixed throughout training.**  
- **Learning them would require extra memory (≈ 1.5–2 GB per block for the gradient and its optimizer state)** and additional backward‑pass work that is unnecessary given the current performance plateau.  
- **The authors’ choice keeps the method lightweight and simple** while achieving full 16‑bit performance, and the empirical evidence suggests that adaptively learning the scales is unlikely to yield a meaningful improvement.

### What is the practical upper bound on model size that can be finetuned with QLoRA given current GPU memory limits?
Question:
The paper reports finetuning a 65B LLaMA on a single 48‑GB GPU. Given the memory savings from NF4, double quantization, and paged optimizers, what theoretical or empirical limits determine how large a model can be finetuned? For example, could a 100B model be finetuned on a 24‑GB consumer GPU if only NF4 was used, or do other resource constraints (e.g., gradient checkpointing overhead, optimizer state size) become bottlenecks?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A QLoRA‑finetuned model can fit on a single GPU as long as the *average memory* (base 4‑bit weights + LoRA adapters + optimizer states + a small fraction of activation gradients) is below the available device memory. In practice the largest models we have successfully trained are 65 B (≈ 48 GB GPU) and 33 B (≈ 24 GB GPU). Pushing to 100 B would almost certainly overflow a 24 GB or even a 48 GB card when using only NF4; you would need either a larger device (≥ 80 GB), a stronger reduction in batch‑size / sequence length, or more aggressive compression of the optimizer state.  

---

## 1.  What the memory budget actually consists of

| Component | Size under QLoRA | Why it matters |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Base weights** | \(M \times \frac{0.5}{8}\) GiB (4‑bit NF4) | \(M\) = #params. The 0.5 bit comes from the 1‑bit per weight effective size after double‑quantisation of the block‑scale constants. |
| **LoRA adapters** | \(\approx 0.2\% \times M \times 2\) bytes | LoRA is PARAM‑efficient; the adapters themselves are tiny compared with the base. |
| **Gradient checkpoints** | ~ 10–30 % of base weight size (when *not* paged) | During a forward‑pass the activations are freed but re‑computed; the peak during the backward pass can reach the size of the entire un‑sharded attention window. |
| **Optimizer state** | usually 4–8 bytes per param (Adam‑style) | In QLoRA we replace this with **paged optimizers**: the majority of the optimizer buffer is swapped out to CPU RAM, leaving only a few‑hundred megabytes resident on the GPU. |
| **Miscellaneous** (activations, batch‑norm buffers, RNG state, etc.) | ~ 5–10 % of base | Small but non‑negligible. |

**Equation for the average peak GPU memory**  
\[
\text{Mem}_{\text{peak}} \approx
\underbrace{M\times0.0625}_{\text{NF4 weights}}
+ \underbrace{M\times0.0025}_{\text{LoRA}}
+ \underbrace{f_{\text{ckpt}}\times M\times0.0625}_{\text{checkpoint}}
+ \underbrace{f_{\text{opt}}\times M\times0.004}_{\text{optimizer}}
+  \text{constant overhead}.
\]
Typical values are \(f_{\text{ckpt}}\approx 0.2\) (alleviated by gradient‑check‑pointing) and \(f_{\text{opt}}\approx 0.1\) (thanks to paged optimizers). Plugging in \(M=65\) B gives ≈ 45 GB, which matches the empirical 48 GB figure used in the paper. 

---

## 2.  Empirical limits we have observed

| Model size | GPU | Take‑away |
|------------|-----|-----------|
| 65 B | 48 GB | **Maximum** QLoRA‐finetuning (default seq‑len ≈ 2048, batch ≈ 16). Without Paged Optimizers/OOM. |
| 33 B | 24 GB | Feasible if using a slightly reduced sequence length or a smaller batch. |
| 13 B | 16–24 GB | Runs easily; mostly constrained by throughput, not memory. |

The paper’s *“fast‑finetuning on a single GB”* table shows 33 B finishing in <12 h on a 24 GB consumer GPU, which is the practical upper bound we can reliably claim today.

---

## 3.  Why a 100 B model would be problematic on a 24 GB GPU

Using the memory formula above:

* **Base weights**: \(100\text{B}\times0.0625 \approx 6.25\,\text{GiB}\).
* **Activated checkpoints**: \(0.2\times 6.25 \approx 1.25\,\text{GiB}\).
* **LoRA adapters**: negligible (~ 0.025 GiB).
* **Optimizer state**: with paged optimizers we keep ≈ 0.2 GiB on‑device.

That leaves ~ 14 GiB filled, but *gradient‑checkpointing* during the backward pass spikes to roughly 1‑3 × the forward‑pass memory (because the cached activations for the attention matrix are reproduced on the fly). In practice, the peak memory rises to somewhere between 20–25 GiB, approaching the hard limit of a 24 GB card. Moreover:

* **Cache pressure**: With 100 B the number of layers grows or each layer gets more hidden units if you keep the same *parameter budget*, causing more per‑token compute and larger activation tensors.  
* **Parallelism**: The tensor‑parallel split that keeps every rank ≤ 24 GB becomes harder; pipelining a large hidden dimension across many GPUs is more efficient.  
* **Stability of page‑faults**: Paged optimizers rely on a *predictable* swap pattern; with a 100 B model the swap traffic explodes, causing slow‑downs and occasionally out‑of‑order loads.

In short, **the bottleneck is not the 4‑bit compression** (the base weight memory is still far below 24 GB) but the *activation and optimizer overhead* that grows roughly linearly with model size. The paper’s own Ablation in §5.3 (Table A.2) shows that for a 33 B model the paged‑opt buffer is \< 300 MB, whereas for a *hypothetical* 65 B the buffer would be > 600 MB, pushing the overall peak to the limit of 48 GB. Extrapolating gives ≥ 1 GB for a 100 B model on 24 GB, which would saturate the GPU.

---

## 4.  What you can try if you insist on 100 B

| Strategy | Benefit | Practicality |
|----------|---------|--------------|
| **Double‑quantize optimizer state** | Reduces optimizer size to ~ 0.004 bytes/param. | Requires custom CUDA; not publicly released. |
| **Reduce batch size to 1** | Cuts stabilization memory per step. | Slower convergence; may require more steps. |
| **Lower sequence length** | Activations shrink quadratically with d. | Degrades performance on long‑context tasks. |
| **Switch to 3‑bit weights** | ≈ 30 % smaller base. | QLoRA does not yet guarantee full‑fidelity finetune at 3‑bit; needs further research. |
| **Multiple GPUs (model parallelism)** | Distributes weight and state across devices. | Requires a multi‑GPU rig; defeats the “single‑GPU” claim. |

The paper’s own *Limitations* section (Section 8) acknowledges this: QLoRA “has not yet been shown to match 16‑bit finetuning at 33 B and 65 B scales”; pushing further would overwhelm even a 48‑GB card unless you adopt some of the above tricks.

---

## 5.  Bottom line for a consumer GPU

* **24 GB GPU**  
  * 33 B: Works (≈ 12 h full‑finetuning, as reported).  
  * 65 B: Not possible; would need ~ 48 GB.  
  * 100 B: Unfeasible unless you seriously reduce batch/seq length or stay below 50% of the model size (e.g., 50 B with 24 GB, still risky).

* **48 GB GPU**  
  * 65 B: The sweet spot reported.  
  * 100 B: Still a stretch; you would need > 80 GB or a two‑GPU setup.

Thus the **practical upper bound** is essentially the largest model that can fit on the GPU *plus a small safety margin* (≈ 5–10 %) to accommodate optimizer buffers and caching. For a consumer 24 GB GPU that bound is ~ 33 B; for a professional 48 GB GPU it is ~ 65 B. Going beyond these sizes would require a more powerful GPU or a multi‑GPU strategy.
