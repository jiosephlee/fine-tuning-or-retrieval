# *Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM): Foundations of the New Era in Sequence Modeling*

---

## 1. Introduction to Sequence Modeling and LSTMs

The study of sequential data—speech signals, handwritten strokes, textual corpora, or trajectories of an autonomous agent—has long driven the development of recurrent neural networks (RNNs). These models, conceived in the 1980s, are designed to process a stream of inputs \(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T\) and maintain a hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_t\) that captures the influence of past tokens. In its simplest form, an RNN updates its hidden state by applying a fixed, element‑wise non‑linearity to a weighted sum of the current input and the previous hidden state. Although conceptually elegant, vanilla RNNs succumb to two crippling issues when trained on real‑world sequences: the problem of *vanishing gradients* and the problem of *exploding gradients*.  
  
### 1.1 From vanilla RNNs to LSTMs

The Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM), introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997, revolutionized the training of RNNs by embedding a *cell state* \(c_t\) that evolves through an addition of gated contributions.  The key insight is the *constant error carousel*—an additive recurrence that preserves the flow of error signals backward through time, thereby mitigating gradient decay. Concretely, the LSTM cell maintains a scalar or vector memory \(c_t\) and modulates its evolution through three sigmoid gates:
* an **input gate** \(i_t\) that governs how much new information \(\tilde{z}_t\) should be injected;
* a **forget gate** \(f_t\) that determines the proportion of the previous memory to retain;
* an **output gate** \(o_t\) that dictates how strongly the updated memory is exposed to the rest of the network.

Mathematically, the update rules are
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t\, c_{t-1} \,+\, i_t\, z_t, \\
h_t &= o_t\, \psi(c_t),
\end{aligned}
\]
with \(\psi\) typically a hyperbolic tangent or a more recent activation. The *cell input* \(z_t\) and gates \(i_t, f_t, o_t\) are affine transformations of the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and the past hidden state \(h_{t-1}\).

The LSTM’s architectural design—particularly the separate memory stream and the gating mechanism—prevents the vanishing gradient phenomenon that plagued vanilla RNNs, enabling learning of long‑range dependencies in diverse tasks.  

### 1.2 Gating and the Constant Error Carousel

Gating is the engine that keeps information flowing over many time steps. Unlike a plain RNN, which blends linearly the past hidden state with the new input, an LSTM can selectively *forget* or *remember* through \(f_t\) and *inject* or *ignore* newly computed candidate information through \(i_t\). The addition of \(c_{t-1}\) in the cell update is the "carousel" that keeps gradients stable because the derivatives of \(c_t\) with respect to earlier states are products of forget gate activations, which can be close to 1. When the forget gate is near 1, the cell state remains largely unchanged, and the gradient bypasses the squashing nonlinearities that often cause vanishing gradients.  

### 1.3 Historical Applications of LSTMs

Over the past three decades, LSTMs have powered many landmark breakthroughs:

| Domain | Representative Work | Contribution |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| **Natural Language Processing** | Graves (2013) language modeling; translation (Sutskever et al. 2014) | First deep LSTMs achieved competitive perplexity; pioneered seq2seq with attention. |
| **Computer Vision** | Graves & Schmidhuber (2013) handwriting synthesis | Demonstrated sequences of strokes for character generation. |
| **Reinforcement Learning** | Vinyals et al. (2017) AlphaStar; OpenAI Five | LSTMs modeled “the game state” to deliver expert play in real‑time strategy games. |
| **Time‑Series Forecasting** | RNNs for rainfall‑runoff modeling, hydrological forecasting | Captured long‑term dependencies in environmental data. |
| **Program Evaluation** | Zaremba (2014) evaluating computer programs | Treated code execution as a sequential decision process. |

These successes reinforced the belief that *memory* and *gated control flow* are indispensable for sequence learning.  

### 1.4 The Advent of Transformers

Despite LSTMs’ popularity, the 2017 introduction of the Transformer—an architecture based on scaled dot‑product self‑attention—shifted the field toward models that are *fully parallelizable* and *position‑agnostic*. Key innovations include:
* **Self‑Attention**: Each token queries all others, enabling instantaneous information flow across distant positions.
* **Layer Normalization & Residual Connections**: Stabilize training of very deep models.
* **Scalable Pre‑Training**: Massive parallelism allows the training of models with tens of billions of parameters, the hallmark of modern Large Language Models (LLMs).

Because self‑attention decouples computation from sequence length—apart from linear memory usage—Transformers dramatically improved efficiency at scale, achieving state‑of‑the‑art results on language modeling, question answering, and many other tasks. They also alleviated the sequential bottleneck inherent in RNNs, which forced each time step to wait for the completion of the previous one.

### 1.5 Three Key Limitations of LSTMs That Motivate xLSTM

With the rise of Transformers and other linear‑time models, several intrinsic shortcomings of the classical LSTM have become more pronounced:

1. **Inability to Revise Stored Information**  
   The LSTM’s cell update is purely additive: once a key‑value pair is stored, it is not straightforward to overwrite or refine it if later evidence suggests a better match. For example, in a nearest‑neighbor search task, the model must revise its memory when a closer vector is encountered, a property that LSTMs lack without additional mechanisms.

2. **Limited Storage Capacity**  
   Traditional LSTMs store information in a low‑dimensional scalar or vector \(c_t\). Compressing rich context into a single value heavily compresses the model’s representational power, leading to poorer performance on rare or complex tokens. A matrix‑structured memory can capture higher‑dimension interactions, much like a key‑value store in a Transformer.

3. **Sequential Processing and Sequential Dependency**  
   The hidden‑hidden connections (the recurrent term \(h_{t-1}\) in every gate) force a strictly sequential computation graph. This limits the ability to parallelize across time steps, undermining throughput, especially on modern hardware that thrives on SIMD or GPU acceleration.

These limitations directly motivated the design of *Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* (xLSTM), which augments the classical LSTM with two essential innovations:

* **Exponential Gating with Normalization and Stabilization**: Enables aggressive gating that can fully admit or entirely suppress content, while preserving numerical stability.
* **Modified Memory Structures**:  
  * **sLSTM** retains a scalar memory but introduces memory mixing across multiple cells (a form of recurrent mixing).  
  * **mLSTM** replaces the scalar memory with a matrix, allowing a covariance‑style update rule that is inherently parallelizable and increases storage capacity.

By integrating these variants into residual block backbones—forming *xLSTM blocks*—and stacking them into deep architectures—*xLSTM architectures*—the authors demonstrate a model family that rivals, and in certain regimes surpasses, Transformers and State‑Space Models on contemporary language modeling benchmarks. The balance struck between memory efficiency, parallelizability, and expressive power positions xLSTM as a compelling alternative in the era of large language models.

**Foundations of the Long Short‑Term Memory Cell**  

The long‑short‑term memory (LSTM) unit, introduced in the early 1990s, has become the bedrock of every recurrent sequence model that has survived the age of self‑attention.  The LSTM’s analytical simplicity and its powerful biological intuition – a memory cell that can be updated, forgotten, and read without suffering exponential decay – is why it continues to pop up in every textbook on deep learning.  In this chapter we do more than just copy the textbook equations.  We dissect each algebraic ingredient, we translate the gates into intuitive mental images, and we trace the flow of gradients through the constant error carousel (CEC).  We then generalise the scalar cell to a vectorised, recurrent‑matrix version, showing explicitly how the algebra switches from element‑wise to matrix multiplication.  Finally we test the limits of the design by ablating individual gates and demonstrating what each component truly delivers.  

---  

## 1.  Mathematical Formulation of the LSTM Cell  

An LSTM cell processes an input vector \(\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_x}\) at each discrete time step \(t\) and maintains two vectors: the **cell state** \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}\) and the **hidden state** \(\mathbf{h}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}\).  The hidden state is emitted to downstream layers, while the cell state is a slowly changing memory that is guarded by gates.  

The canonical LSTM equations are  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{W}_z^\top \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_z^\top \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_z  &&\text{pre‑activation of cell input} \\
\mathbf{z}_t &\;=\;\varphi(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t) &&\text{cell input (usually }\tanh{}) \\
\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{W}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_i^\top \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_i &&\text{pre‑activation of input gate} \\
\mathbf{i}_t &\;=\;\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t) &&\text{input gate (range }[0,1]) \\
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{W}_f^\top \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_f^\top \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_f &&\text{pre‑activation of forget gate} \\
\mathbf{f}_t &\;=\;\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t) &&\text{forget gate (range }[0,1]) \\
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{W}_o^\top \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{U}_o^\top \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_o &&\text{pre‑activation of output gate} \\
\mathbf{o}_t &\;=\;\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t) &&\text{output gate (range }[0,1]) \\
\mathbf{c}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t &&\text{CEC cell update} \\
\mathbf{h}_t &\;=\;\mathbf{o}_t \odot \psi(\mathbf{c}_t) &&\text{hidden state}
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

Here, \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication.  The matrices \(\mathbf{W}_{\{z,i,f,o\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_x\times d_h}\) couple the current input to each gate, \(\mathbf{U}_{\{z,i,f,o\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h\times d_h}\) couple the previous hidden state into the gates, and \(\mathbf{b}_{\{z,i,f,o\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_h}\) are biases.  The activation function \(\varphi\) is often \(\tanh\) and \(\psi\) is again \(\tanh\) or an equivalent squashing function to keep the cell state bounded.

### 1.1  Gate Pre‑activations and Biases  

The gate pre‑activations \(\tilde{\mathbf{i}}_t\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_t\), and \(\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t\) are simple affine combinations of the current input and the previous hidden state.  The linearity implies that each gate can decide, locally, whether to pass information through or to block it.  Because the sigmoid activation \(\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))\) squashes its argument to \([0,1]\), the gate outputs can be interpreted as soft switches: a value close to 0 means “do not let this piece of information through,” and a value close to 1 means “let it fully pass.”

The cell input pre‑activation \(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_t\) shares the same structure.  Its output \(\mathbf{z}_t\) is the new candidate content that could be mixed into the cell state, but the actual rate of mixing is controlled by the time‑varying input gate \(\mathbf{i}_t\).

### 1.2  Constant Error Carousel (CEC)  

The key to the LSTM’s ability to remember long‑range dependencies lies in the **CEC**:  

\[
\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t.
\tag{2}
\]

The forget gate \(\mathbf{f}_t\) directly multiplies the previous cell state \(\mathbf{c}_{t-1}\).  If a component of \(\mathbf{f}_t\) is equal to 1, that component of the cell state passes through unchanged; if it is 0, that component is completely erased.  By learning to set \(\mathbf{f}_t\) close to 1 for crucial information and to values near 0 for irrelevant information, the cell prevents gradients from vanishing: the derivative of \(L\) w.r.t.\ \(\mathbf{c}_{t-1}\) contains the same term \(\mathbf{f}_t\), ensuring that information can propagate backwards in time unmodulated.

The addition by \(\mathbf{i}_t \odot \mathbf{z}_t\) supplies fresh content.  The two gates together allow the cell to be **write‑controlled** (through \(\mathbf{i}_t\)) and **read‑controlled** (through \(\mathbf{f}_t\)).  In classical terms, we can view \(\mathbf{c}_t\) as memory that is updated by a **write** (scaled by \(\mathbf{i}_t\)) and a **retain** (scaled by \(\mathbf{f}_t\)).  

The hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \psi(\mathbf{c}_t)\) is the output you see at each time step.  It is derived from the cell content, damped (through \(\psi\)) to stay within a manageable range, and filtered again by the output gate \(\mathbf{o}_t\).

## 2.  Interpretation of Input, Forget, and Output Gates  

Each gate has a distinct semantic role.  Visualising the LSTM cell as a **memory palace** can help:  

* **Input gate \(\mathbf{i}_t\):** a lock on the door that allows new information into the palace. If the lock is opened (value close to 1), new input flows in; if it is closed (value close to 0), the palace ignores new visitors.  
* **Forget gate \(\mathbf{f}_t\):** a janitor who decides which old memories to prune. A value close to 1 preserves what was stored, a value close to 0 erases it.  
* **Output gate \(\mathbf{o}_t\):** a window: it decides how much of the internal state is shown to the outside world. If it is closed (0), the LSTM keeps its content private; if it is open (1), it reveals everything.  

Because each gate is differentiable, the network learns *when* to open, close, or temper each of these three switches.  The gates are **input‑dependent** (via \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_t\)) and **hidden‑state‑dependent** (via \(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\)), giving the model the ability to condition its memory dynamics on both the current observation and the past context.

---

## 3.  Cell State Update and the Importance of Squashing Activations  

An LSTM can, in principle, store unbounded numbers in \(\mathbf{c}_t\).  If the squashing function \(\psi\) were omitted, \(\mathbf{c}_t\) could grow arbitrarily large, causing numerical instability and vanishing or exploding gradients.  A squasher such as \(\tanh\) maps real values to the interval \([-1,1]\), preventing the cell from drifting too far away from zero.  

From a functional standpoint, the squashing operation also serves **non‑linear feature extraction**.  Substituting \(\psi\) with a larger‑range function such as \(\operatorname{relu}\) would massively change the dynamics: the cell could hold arbitrarily large positive values without being capped, which is rarely useful for sequence modelling.  Empirically, \(\tanh\) is the most common choice because it preserves the sign of the state while limiting its magnitude.

---

## 4.  Multiple Cell Variants and Recurrent Weight Matrices  

The vanilla scalar cell can be **vectorised** so that every time step updates a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\).  In this form, the gates are no longer scalars but vectors of the same dimension.  Thus equations (1) remain valid component‑wise, but the recurrent matrices \(\mathbf{U}_{\{z,i,f,o\}}\) become \(d\times d\) matrices and can **mix** information across different memory cells.  

This mixing is sometimes called *memory mixing* or *hidden‑hidden connections*.  Since the recurrent matrices appear in the pre‑activations of all gates, the hidden state at time \(t-1\) can influence how all gates are modulated in the next time step.  Researchers have observed that this mechanism gives RNNs higher expressive power: it allows them to solve more intricate state‑tracking tasks than models that rely purely on point‑wise gating.  

**Extending to multiple rectangular weight matrices** leads to the **SRU** or **GRU**-like simplifications, but the essential CEC remains.  In advanced LSTM families such as sLSTM and mLSTM, the memory mixing is explicitly designed or deliberately removed to trade expressive power for parallelism.

---

## 5.  Empirical Sanity Checks: What Happens When Each Gate is Ablated  

A simple but revealing experiment is to **disable** one gate, i.e. force it to a constant value, and observe the performance deterioration on a language modelling benchmark such as Penn‑Treebank or WikiText‑103.  The results consistently match the intuition outlined earlier.  

| Ablated Gate | Setup | Expected Behaviour | Empirical Observation |
|--------------|-------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| **Input gate** fixed to 0 | No new information can enter the cell | Model forgets to write, performance collapses rapidly | Test accuracy plummets to random, training loss explodes |
| **Forget gate** fixed to 1 | Memory never decays | Model accumulates noise, can overfit | Accuracy degrades slowly but steadily |
| **Output gate** fixed to 1 | Hidden state always shows full cell | Model can exhibit high variability, but often still solves tasks | Minor performance hit compared to fully gated case |
| **All gates disabled** | Cell reduces to a linear recurrent network | Loss of expressive gating | Worse than any single gate ablation |

These ablation studies highlight that the *forget gate* is the most critical to prevent runaway growth, the *input gate* is essential for learning new facts, and the *output gate* mainly modulates the information exposed but does not change the underlying store.  The synergy of all three gates is what makes LSTM a robust memory system.

---

## 6.  Gradient Flow Through the CEC  

To understand why the CEC solves gradient vanishing, consider the partial derivative of a loss \(L\) with respect to the cell state at an earlier time \(\mathbf{c}_k\).  Unrolling the recurrence, we find  

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{c}_k} = \left(\prod_{t=k+1}^{T} \mathbf{f}_t\right) \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{c}_T}.
\tag{3}
\]

Because each \(\mathbf{f}_t\) contains sigmoid outputs bounded between 0 and 1, the product can be close to zero or close to one, but never vanishes exponentially with the depth \(T-k\).  This is the primary technical difference between an LSTM and a vanilla RNN: in the former, gradients can survive through long sequences, whereas in the latter the multiplication by small recurrent weights forces gradients to decay to zero.

---

## 7.  Summary  

The LSTM cell is a carefully engineered algebraic device.  Its four gates are not arbitrary layers; they are functional remotes that control **when to write**, **when to forget**, and **when to reveal** information.  The constant error carousel ensures that backward‑time gradients stay intact.  Squashing functions keep the state bounded while allowing rich non‑linear transformations.  Vectorised cells add memory mixing via recurrent weight matrices, boosting expressive power.  Empirical ablation confirms that all gates contribute meaningfully.  Understanding these details equips a student not only to implement LSTMs from scratch but also to extend them – as later chapters will show with exponential gating, matrix memories, and the generalisation to “xLSTM” – while still respecting the core principles that made the LSTM a cornerstone of recurrent neural networks.

# 3. Exponential Gating and the sLSTM

The long‑term research heritage of the LSTM hinges on its gated state‑carrying structure, with the forgetting, inputting, and outputting behaviors governed by sigmoid activations. In *xLSTM* the first major departure from this LSTM canon is the replacement of the sigmoid with an exponential function in the input and forget gates. This seemingly modest change unlocks a host of new dynamical properties: the capacity to revise earlier storage decisions, the ability to keep critical signals from dissipating over long horizons, and the support for a novel form of memory mixing that directly encodes state‑tracking in the recurrent dynamics.

In what follows we dissect this idea in depth, beginning with an intuitive justification for the exponential choice, proceeding to the formal definition of the sLSTM, and ending with a discussion of its implications on the expressive power of recurrent models via synthetic benchmarks.

---

## Motivation for Replacing Sigmoid with Exp on Gates

The standard LSTM employs sigmoid activations, \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))\), for every gate, which bound gate values to the compact interval \([0,1]\). Although this boundedness is beneficial for numerical stability, it also induces an undesirable saturation effect: when a gate approaches either extreme, its gradient collapses, effectively freezing the corresponding signal. This property hampers the network’s ability to *revise* a memory location once it has been written. In a typical state‑tracking task, the network must replace a stored value with a newer, more relevant one; a saturated forget gate, by definition, cannot forget.

If instead we allow the gates to grow unboundedly through an exponential activation, \(\exp(\tilde{g})\), we obtain an unbounded non‑linear mapping that preserves sign and grows fast with its input. An exponential forget gate can therefore increase the decay of old memory entries dramatically, while an exponential input gate can increase the magnitude of new updates arbitrarily. In continuous‑time terms, where the LSTM cell state follows
\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]
the exponential forgetting rate \(f_t\) behaves like a multiplicative decay factor that can be tuned to the empirical relevance of past observations. Crucially, because \(\mathrm{d}f_t/\mathrm{d}\tilde{f}_t = f_t\), any \(\tilde{f}_t\) generated from the hidden‑hidden and input‑hidden linear combinations can be amplified without boundary constraints, allowing the network to enact sharp forgetting or retention on demand.

The decision to use exponentials is further motivated by the experience of the *Fast Weight Programmers* and the *Retention* model, which already exploited exponential updates to implement efficient key–value associations. In those settings, the exponential gating was the bridge between vanilla RNNs and more elaborate memory mechanisms; here we bring that insight back into the core of the LSTM.

---

## The Normalizer State and Its Role in Maintaining Numerical Stability

Exponentials can grow extremely rapidly. Without further constraints \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) can become astronomically large or diminutive, leading to overflow or underflow in floating point computations and in the back‑propagated gradients. The design of the sLSTM introduces an auxiliary scalar called the **normalizer state**, denoted \(n_t\), which is meant to keep track of the cumulative weight that historically has been applied to the incoming updates.

The normalizer is defined recursively in tandem with the cell state:
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t   &= f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t , \\
n_t   &= f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t ,
\end{aligned}
\]
with the convention \(c_{-1}=0, n_{-1}=0\). By construction,
\[
n_t \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^{t} \Bigl(\prod_{k=s+1}^{t} f_k\Bigr) \, i_s.
\]
Thus \(n_t\) records, in a floating point‑friendly manner, the total “influence mass” that should be summed when evaluating the current cell content.

To prevent runaway growth, we rescale the raw cell content by dividing through by the normalizer:
\[
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]
Because the same linear map of exponential gate coefficients appears in both numerator and denominator, the ratio remains bounded even when \(i_t\) or \(f_t\) individually diverge. This normalization, however, is not sufficient for back‑propagation: the exponential terms in the gradients still become unbounded. Therefore an additional **stabilizer state** \(m_t\) is computed as
\[
m_t \;=\; \max \!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr),
\]
and the gates are *renormalized* as
\[
\begin{aligned}
i'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\bigr), \\
f'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Because \(\tilde{i}_t\) may be negative, the subtraction of the stabilizer \(m_t\) caps the exponentials in the forward pass. Equivalently, in log‑domain these manipulated gates are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log i'_t &= \tilde{i}_t - m_t, \\
\log f'_t &= \log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
keeping their logarithms bounded from below. Plugging \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) in place of \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) in the forward equations is mathematically equivalent (the ratios \(\frac{c_t}{n_t}\) and the gradient flow remain unchanged), but numerically it guarantees that during training neither the activations nor their gradients leave the dynamic range of double precision.

---

## Derivation of the Stabilized Exponential Gating Equations

The derivation of the stabilized gates proceeds in three steps:

1. **Raw Exponential Gates.**  
   \[
   \tilde{g}_t = {\bf w}_g^\top {\bf x}_t + b_g,\quad
   g_t = \exp(\tilde{g}_t)\quad (g \in \{i,f\}).
   \]
2. **Normalizer and Stabilizer.**  
   The normalized cell state must be expressed in terms of \(\log i_t\) and \(\log f_t\). Let us denote \(\alpha_s = \log i_s\) and \(\beta_s = \log f_s\). Then
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   \log c_t &= \log \Biggl(\sum_{s=0}^{t} z_s e^{\alpha_s + \sum_{k=s+1}^t \beta_k}\Biggr)\\
   \log n_t &= \log \Bigl(\sum_{s=0}^t e^{\alpha_s + \sum_{k=s+1}^t \beta_k}\Bigr).
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   Introducing the stabilizer \(m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)\) ensures that the dominant term in the summations is always subtracted from each exponent, reducing the values inside the logarithm to \(\le 0\).  
3. **Stable Forward Equations.**  
   After stabilizing, we replace \(i_t, f_t\) by \(i'_t, f'_t\) everywhere. This yields the fully stabilized sLSTM equations:
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   c_t   &= f'_t\, c_{t-1} + i'_t \, z_t ,\\
   n_t   &= f'_t\, n_{t-1} + i'_t ,\\
   \tilde{h}_t &= \frac{c_t}{n_t},\\
   h_t   &= o_t\, \tilde{h}_t ,
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   with the same definitions for \(z_t\) and \(o_t\) as in the vanilla LSTM. The proof that these equations preserve the exact forward output is a straightforward induction on \(t\), leveraging the linearity of the recursions after incorporating the normalizer.

---

## Memory Mixing in the sLSTM: Recurrent Connections and Multi‑Head Design

A hallmark of the sLSTM is the **memory mixing** mechanism that exploits recurrent connections across multiple memory cells. In a conventional LSTM we may stack a vector of cell states \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\), each updated independently. The sLSTM allows each cell’s update to be informed by the hidden states of all other cells through a weight matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). Concretely, for head \(h\) and memory cell \(k\) the input to the cell equations becomes
\[
\tilde{z}_{t}^{(k,h)} = {\bf w}_z^\top {\bf x}_t + r_z^{(k,h)}\,h_{t-1}^{(h)} + b_z^{(k,h)},
\]
where \(h_{t-1}^{(h)}\) denotes the hidden state of the *same* head from the previous time step. Here, \(r_z^{(k,h)}\) is a *scalar* recurrent coefficient linking hidden state \(h_{t-1}^{(h)}\) to cell \(k\). Similar recurrent terms \(r_i, r_f, r_o\) appear in the gate computations. By learning a distinct set of \(r\)-coefficients for each cell, the network can **intertwine** cell updates, effectively redistributing learned information across memory slots in a fashion reminiscent of the *attention* mechanism but without explicit pairwise tokens.

The multi‑head design compounds this flexibility. sLSTM defines several independent lanes (heads) each with its own set of recurrent coefficients and head‑wise hidden state. Within a head, cells are allowed to mix, but heads do not communicate directly through mixing. This isolation is intentional: each head can specialise on a distinct pattern of information flow (e.g. syntactic versus semantic cues), while the inter‑head communication is limited to the *output* gating that aggregates the heads. Consequences of this design include:

- **Expressivity**: Multiple heads allow a richer representation of the past without linearly scaling the gating computations; only a modest increase in parameter count suffices.
- **Parallelism**: Even though memory mixing prevents full hardware‑level parallelization, the head‑wise independence permits vectorised operations within a head.
- **State‑tracking**: By coupling cell updates across cells via recurrent weights, sLSTM can encode and modify arbitrary trajectories in state space, a capability absent in standard LSTMs that lack mixing.

## Comparison of sLSTM to Vanilla LSTM on Synthetic State‑Tracking Benchmarks

The practical gains of exponential gating and memory mixing manifest starkly on controlled synthetic tasks engineered to expose the limitations of vanilla LSTMs.

### Nearest‑Neighbor Search

A canonical counter‑example is the *Nearest Neighbor Search* problem: a sequence contains a reference vector followed by a stream of candidate vectors; at the end the network must output the value attached to the nearest candidate. The vanilla LSTM struggles because once a candidate is stored, the forget gate cannot *recall* its relevance after a newer, more similar candidate arrives, owing to its bounded forgetting coefficient. In head‑aware sLSTM, the exponential forgetting gate can be sharply increased whenever a new candidate arrives, causing the old memory to evaporate. Simultaneously, the memory‑mixing recurrent weights allow the new vector to be *immediately* propagated into the other memory cells, thereby revising the previous storage decision. Empirical results show a dramatic reduction in mean squared error compared to the vanilla LSTM, validating that sLSTM successfully revises earlier memories.

### Parity and Regular Grammar Tasks

State‑tracking problems such as the parity or non‑regular language recognition tasks likewise benefit from sLSTM’s architecture. The exponential gates can encode the requisite modular arithmetic by learning to mask out or retain alternating bits precisely. The memory mixing further amplifies the ability to express the combinatorial state space needed to distinguish acceptability or violation conditions. In Synthetic benchmarks, sLSTM consistently achieves perfect or near‑perfect accuracy with fewer parameters than a comparable vanilla LSTM.

From a functional perspective, these empirical findings confirm that the *exponential gating* endows the cell with the ability to alter its update policy on the fly, while *memory mixing* equips the network with a built‑in combinatorial mechanism for coordinated updating of multiple memory slots. Together they lift the sLSTM over the expressive ceiling of standard LSTMs, enabling it to imitate or surpass the performance of architectures that rely on attention‑style conditioning (i.e. Transformers) in recall‑centric settings.

---

In summary, the adoption of exponential gate activations, coupled with the normalizer–stabilizer construction, permits the sLSTM to maintain a stable yet flexible memory dynamic. The enriched memory‑mixing structure and the multi‑head arrangement provide the necessary expressivity to track complex state trajectories while still fitting within the well‑understood LSTM formulation. The resulting sLSTM becomes the foundational building block from which *xLSTM* scales its architectures to billions of parameters, matching and in several regimes surpassing contemporary transformer‑based language models.

**Chapter 4 – Matrix Memory and the mLSTM**

The long‑standing reliance of LSTMs on a single scalar memory element has proven to be a bottleneck in modern large‑scale language modeling. Chapter 4 introduces the matrix‑valued memory cell that replaces the scalar with a full \(d\times d\) matrix. By doing so, the model can store entire key–value vectors directly in its memory and retrieve them using a single matrix–vector multiplication. This section explains why scalar memory is insufficient, derives the covariance update rule that allows the memory to accumulate key–value pairs, discusses how the memory is normalised and accessed, and shows how exponential gates are stabilised. Finally, we examine the parallel scan‑free formulation that makes the matrix‑LSTM trainable on current GPU hardware and analyse the trade‑off between memory capacity and computational cost.

---

### 4.1 Why Scalar Memory Limits Rare Token Representation

In an ordinary LSTM the memory at time step \(t\) is a single real value \(c_t\in\mathbb{R}\).  All information that the network wishes to remember must be compressed into this scalar.  When the vocabulary contains a wide range of token frequencies, such compression becomes problematic for two reasons:

1. **Poor discrimination for infrequent tokens.**  
   Rare tokens appear only a handful of times during training.  The network therefore has little opportunity to learn a robust scalar representation for these tokens.  They are inevitably mixed with frequent tokens in the scalar cell and lose signal strength.  Empirically, this causes a noticeable increase in perplexity for low‑frequency tokens (see the right panel of Figure 1).

2. **Limited context capacity.**  
   A single scalar memory cannot encode more than one distinct value at a time.  When a sequence contains an early token that needs to be remembered until a later position, the LSTM must purge the scalar before a new, unrelated token is processed.  Consequently, the network loses the ability to diferentially store multiple key–value pairs that coexist in a single sequence.

These shortcomings motivate a richer memory representation that can hold multiple key–value vectors simultaneously.  A matrix memory leads naturally to a covariance‑style update rule that accumulates outer products of key and value vectors, thereby constructing a latent space that can later be queried efficiently.

---

### 4.2 Deriving the Covariance Update Rule for Key–Value Storage

Suppose that at time step \(t\) the model produces a *key* vector \(\mathbf{k}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and a corresponding *value* vector \(\mathbf{v}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\).  The goal is to store the pair \((\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t)\) so that, at a later time \(t+\tau\), a *query* \(\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}\) can retrieve \(\mathbf{v}_t\) through inner‑product matching with \(\mathbf{k}_t\).

A convenient way to accumulate such associations is to maintain a symmetric matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) and update it by adding the outer product of the current value and key:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\;\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\;+\;\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}\;.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Equation 4.1 is precisely the covariance update used in Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs) and Fast‑Weight Programmers.  The update is linear in the number of stored pairs; after \(T\) updates the memory becomes a sum of \(T\) outer products.

The intuition is that the columns of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) become weighted linear combinations of stored values.  If we query with \(\mathbf{q}\) that is close to a stored key \(\mathbf{k}_i\), the product \(\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}\) will resemble \(\mathbf{v}_i\), because the term \(\mathbf{v}_i\,\mathbf{k}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}\) dominates the sum.  All other stored pairs contribute terms that are typically looser matches.

The choice of an outer product rather than an element‑wise product ensures *linear separability*: the dot product between a query and a key acts as a *retrieval weight* for the corresponding value.  This property is formally optimal under a binary retrieval objective, as argued in Sejnowski (1977) and Dayan (1991).  Thus, the covariance rule provides a principled mechanism to store arbitrary key–value pairs.

---

### 4.3 Normalising and Retrieving from a Matrix Memory

While adding raw outer products can lead to unbounded growth of the matrix norm, two techniques keep the size of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) under control:

1. **Normaliser State.**  
   The matrix memory is accompanied by a vector normaliser \(\mathbf{n}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) that aggregates the contribution of the keys weighted by the input gate and future forget gates:

   \[
   \mathbf{n}_t \;=\;f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1}\;+\;i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t\;.
   \tag{4.2}
   \]

   Here, \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) are the learning‑rate and decay‑rate gates, respectively (see Section 4.4).  The normaliser vector records the total “weight” of the stored keys up to time \(t\).

2. **Query‑Normalized Retrieval.**  
   The hidden state is obtained by applying the memory to the query and normalising by the *absolute* dot product \(|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t|\) (clipped to a minimum of 1, see Equation 4.3):

   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\;\frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}
   {\max\!\left(\,|\mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t|,\; 1\,\right)}\;,
   \qquad
   \mathbf{h}_t \;=\;\mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t\;.
   \tag{4.3}
   \]

   The denominator scales the retrieved vector relative to the amount of memory content that is *relevant* to the current query.  By using the absolute value, the network remains unaffected by the sign of the dot product, and the lower bound of 1 guarantees numerical stability even when the normaliser and query are nearly orthogonal.

The full set of forward equations for the mLSTM is summarised in Equation 4.4.  Every variable is standard notation: \(\tilde{\cdot}\) denotes a pre‑activation linear layer, \(\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{o}}\) is a matrix output gate, and the gates \(i_t\), \(f_t\), \(o_t\) are defined in Equations 4.5–4.7.  The query, key, and value projections are \( \mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{W}_v\), respectively, and are modulated by a (optional) layer‑norm.



### 4.4 Stabilisation of Exponential Gates in the mLSTM

The *input* and *forget* gates of the mLSTM are exponentials of linear functions of the input:

\[
i_t \;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t\bigr)\,,\qquad
f_t \;=\;\sigma\!\bigl(\tilde{f}_t\bigr)\;\text{or}\;\exp\!\bigl(\tilde{f}_t\bigr)\;.
\tag{4.5}
\]

Exponential activations are powerful because they allow the gates to grow or decay *multiplicatively*, matching the intuition behind add‑and‑forget mechanisms.  However, in practice, large values of \(\tilde{i}_t\) or \(\tilde{f}_t\) can cause overflow or underflow, destabilising both forward activations and backward gradients.

To resolve this, the mLSTM uses the *stabiliser state* \(m_t\) defined as

\[
m_t \;=\;\max\!\bigl(\,\log(f_t)\,+\,m_{t-1}\;,\;\log(i_t)\,\bigr)\;,
\tag{4.6}
\]

which keeps track of the current log‑scale of the gates.  The gates are then renormalised as

\[
i'_t \;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\log(i_t)-m_t\bigr)
\;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t-m_t\bigr)\;,\qquad
f'_t \;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t\bigr)\;.
\tag{4.7}
\]

Replacing \(i_t\) with \(i'_t\) and \(f_t\) with \(f'_t\) in the forward updates does **not** alter the network’s output or the gradients, because the normaliser \(\mathbf{n}_t\) and the hidden state \(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t\) undergo the same multiplicative scaling.  Consequently, the stabilisation preserves the mathematical properties of the original equations while keeping the numerics within a safe range.

---

### 4.5 Parallelisation of the mLSTM: Scan‑Free Formulation and GPU Considerations

A classic RNN needs to perform a *scan* over the sequence: the memory at time \(t\) depends on the memory at time \(t-1\).  For the mLSTM, the scan is captured by Equations 4.1 and 4.2.  Although the recurrence is linear, modern GPU architectures do not execute scans efficiently: a naive implementation would launch a kernel for each time step, forcing the device to wait for the result of the previous step before proceeding.

Fortunately, the linearity of the mLSTM update means that we can eliminate the costly serial scan and evaluate the entire sequence *in one batch*.  The key observation is that the memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\) can be expressed solely in terms of the *cumulative* product of forget gates:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\;\sum_{s=1}^{t} 
\Bigl(\prod_{r=s+1}^{t} f_r\Bigr)\;
i_s\,\mathbf{v}_s\,\mathbf{k}_s^{\!\top}\;.
\tag{4.8}
\]

An analogous expression holds for the normaliser \(\mathbf{n}_t\).  From this representation we can pre‑compute the cumulative decay products \(\prod_{r=s+1}^{t} f_r\) as a cumulative product along the sequence.  In PyTorch, the cumprod operation is highly optimised for GPUs and runs fully in parallel.  Once the gating products are available, the outer‑product sum can be calculated as a batched matrix multiplication, again exploiting GPU parallelism.

The resulting *scan‑free* algorithm has the following computational profile:

- **Memory complexity**: \(O(d^2)\) for the matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) and \(O(d)\) for the normaliser.
- **Time complexity**: \(O(T\times d^2)\) for a sequence of length \(T\), but all matrix multiplications happen in parallel; no serial dependencies remain.
- **Hardware utilisation**: The algorithm maps cleanly onto GPU tensor cores because the main operations are batched matrix additions and multiplications.

In toy implementations, the scan‑free mLSTM is roughly *4–8× faster* than a naive scan‑based implementation, and in practice it matches the throughput of FlashAttention‑style libraries when run on the same hardware.

---

### 4.6 Assessing the Trade‑off: Memory Capacity vs. Computation Cost

The matrix memory yields a dramatic increase in expressive power, but it comes with higher computational demands:

| Method | Parameter count | Memory size (per time step) | Computational cost |
|--------|-----------------|-----------------------------|---------------------|
| Scalar LSTM | \(O(d)\) | \(O(1)\) | \(O(d)\) matrix–vector multiplies |
| mLSTM | \(O(d^2)\) | \(O(d^2)\) | \(O(d^2)\) matrix–vector multiplies per step (but parallelisable) |

For modest hidden sizes (\(d \approx 128\)), the matrix memory adds only a small percentage to the parameter count but doubles the per‑step FLOPs.  When scaling to large language models (\(d\in[512, 2048]\)), the \(d^2\) term becomes significant.  Several practical strategies mitigate this cost:

1. **Reducing the number of stacked mLSTM blocks.**  
   Experiments show that a mix of mLSTM and sLSTM blocks (e.g. xLSTM[7:1]) balances capacity and speed, achieving state‑of‑the‑art perplexities while keeping training wall‑clock times comparable to Transformers.

2. **Leveraging mixed‑precision.**  
   The matrix operations are highly amenable to FP16 or BF16 without catastrophic accuracy loss.

3. **Caching.**  
   During inference, the matrix memory can be cached and reused across calls to the same context, reducing redundant recomputation.

4. **Sparse approximations.**  
   For future work, one could sparsify \(\mathbf{C}\) by thresholding small entries or by limiting the number of outer products that contribute to the sum for a given query.

In the end, the matrix memory delivers *Apparent* state‑tracking capabilities that were previously exclusive to Transformer‑style cross‑attention.  The mLSTM’s ability to retain multiple key–value pairs, to retrieve them through a single matrix multiplication, and to do all of this while remaining fully differentiable makes it a compelling building block for large language models.

---

### Summary

The mLSTM reformulates the classical hidden‑state recurrence by replacing a scalar memory with a full matrix that stores key–value associations via a covariance update.  The derivation of this update follows directly from associative memory theory and yields an architecture that is both theoretically sound and practically efficient.  Normalisation by a learned vector guarantees numerical stability and retrieves the desired value vector in a query‑normalised manner.  Exponential gates, when stabilised by a carefully designed log‑scale compensator, allow the model to learn highly dynamic learning rates and decays.  Thanks to the linearity of the recurrence, a scan‑free formulation turns the otherwise sequential operation into a fully parallel GPU workload.  While the matrix memory increases computational cost, the gains in memory capacity and the resulting performance improvements on challenging language modelling benchmarks confirm that the trade‑off is both justified and fertile ground for further optimisation.

# 5. Building Blocks: Residual xLSTM Modules

In modern large‑language‑model (LLM) backbones the neural computation is organized into *residual blocks* that interleave a main computational kernel with a skip connection, a layer normalisation, and a short‑range skip (gelu or other non‑linearity).  
This chapter explains how the two new xLSTM cells—**sLSTM** (scalar‑state LSTM with exponential gating) and **mLSTM** (matrix‑state LSTM with covariance updates)—fit naturally into such a block.  It also shows why the *post‑up‑projection* design is preferable for sLSTM and the *pre‑up‑projection* design for mLSTM, setting the stage for the full xLSTM architecture built from a mixture of these blocks, denoted xLSTM[$a$:$b$].  Finally, we discuss the hyper‑parameters that govern mixing, heads, and depth.

---

## Residual connections and layer‑normalization in modern LLMs

Residual networks were introduced to mitigate the vanishing‐gradient problem, allowing very deep models to train.  In an LLM each block typically takes an input \( \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d} \) and produces an output \( \mathbf{h}_{t} \).  
A residual block can be written

\[
\mathbf{h}_{t}\;=\;\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\;+\;\operatorname{Norm}\!\left(\mathcal{F}(\operatorname{Norm}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}))\right),
\]

where \( \mathcal{F} \) is the *main computation* (e.g. an LSTM cell) and \( \operatorname{Norm} \) is a layer‑normalisation.  
This pre‑normalised form (pre‑LayerNorm) is now standard in contemporary Transformers and State‑Space Models because it improves training stability and allows larger learning rates.  

The block also contains a *feed‑forward* sub‑module, often a 2‑layer MLP, that widens the feature space to an intermediate dimension (e.g. 4\(d\)), applies a non‑linearity, and projects it back.  
This two‑step expansion/compression is key to exploit **Cover’s Theorem**: embedding points in a higher‑dimensional space makes them linearly separable with higher probability.  We exploit this idea by letting the xLSTM cell operate in an *expanded* space for mLSTM, while sLSTM keeps the cell’s scalar state and therefore benefits from a *post‑up‑projection* that expands *after* the scalar summation.

---

## Post‑up‑projection blocks for sLSTM: summarizing in original space then expanding

The *sLSTM* cell has a single scalar memory \( c_{t} \in \mathbb{R} \).  Its forward equations (with exponential gates and normaliser) are  

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t   & = f_t\,c_{t-1}   + i_t\,z_t , \\
n_t   & = f_t\,n_{t-1}   + i_t ,\\
\tilde{h}_t & = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \\
h_t   & = o_t\, \tilde{h}_t .
\end{aligned}
\]

All gating signals \( (i_t, f_t, o_t) \) are computed from \( \mathbf{x}_t \) and the previous hidden state \( \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \).  Because the cell state is *scalar*, the cell’s computation is strictly *one‑dimensional* and cannot itself increase the representation dimensionality.  Therefore the **post‑up‑projection** strategy is adopted:

1. **Summarise** the input sequence into the scalar state via the sLSTM cell (cost \(O(1)\)).
2. **Project** this scalar (or the resulting hidden vector after the addition of the skip connection) into an intermediate dimensionality \(d_{\text{mid}}\) using a linear map \( \mathbf{W}_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mid}}\!\times\! d} \).
3. Apply a non‑linearity (typically GELU) and a second linear map \( \mathbf{W}_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\!\times\! d_{\text{mid}}} \) that returns the feature back to the original dimension.
4. Add the **residual** \( \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \) and normalise.

Mathematically:

\[
\text{ffn}(x) \;=\; \mathbf{W}_{\text{down}}\;\text{GELU}\!\big(\mathbf{W}_{\text{up}}\,x\big),
\]
\[
\mathbf{h}_t \;=\; \mathbf{h}_{t-1}\;+\;\operatorname{Norm}\!\left(\text{ffn}\!\big(\operatorname{Norm}(\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\big)\right).
\]

Because the cell’s **memory mixing** (recurrent weighting via \( \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}, \ldots \)) is performed *inside* the sLSTM cell before the up‑projection, the expanded dimensionality only operates on a *summary* of the entire past.  This keeps the core recurrent step lightweight, yet allows the downstream feed‑forward part to embrace a richer representation, essential for language modelling.

The post‑up‑projection block is illustrated on the left side of Figure \ref{fig:Backbones} in the paper.  In practice one chooses \( d_{\text{mid}} \) as a multiple of \( d \) (e.g. \(4d\)) and keeps the same number of layers as in a Transformer (often 48 or 96 blocks for medium‑sized models).

---

## Pre‑up‑projection blocks for mLSTM: expanding first to capture more context

The *mLSTM* cell stores a **matrix memory** \( \mathbf{C}_{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \).  Its update rule, incorporating exponential gates and a normaliser, is

\[
\mathbf{C}_t  = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\top}, \qquad
\mathbf{n}_t  = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t,
\]
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t 
    = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\lvert \mathbf{n}_t^{\top}\mathbf{q}_t\rvert,\,1\}}, 
\qquad   \mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t.
\]

The query, key and value vectors \( \mathbf{q}_t, \mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t \) are linear projections of the raw input \( \mathbf{x}_t \).  Because the cell’s **memory capacity grows with the input dimension** (the matrix is \(d\times d\) and each new key–value pair updates it), it is advantageous to *first* raise the dimensionality before the recurrent operation takes place.  This is precisely what a **pre‑up‑projection** block does:

1. **Project** the input \( \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \) to a larger intermediate space \( \mathbf{h}'_{t-1}= \mathbf{W}_{\text{up}}\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\) where \( \mathbf{W}_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mid}}\!\times\! d} \) and \( d_{\text{mid}} > d \).
2. **Feed** this high-dimensional vector into the mLSTM cell, which performs its matrix‑based update and retrieval entirely in the expanded space.  The cell’s weights (for key, value, query, and gates) are all \(d_{\text{mid}}\)-dimensional.
3. **Project** the hidden state back to the original dimension with \( \mathbf{W}_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\!\times\! d_{\text{mid}}}\).
4. **Add** the residual \( \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \) and apply layer‑norm.

Thus the cell’s *critical* memory operations are conducted in a richer feature space, which allows it to accommodate the quadratic memory capacity \(d_{\text{mid}}^2\) without overwhelming the scalar hidden vector.  Figure \ref{fig:Backbones} (center panel) shows this pre‑up‑projection layout.

The choice of \( d_{\text{mid}} \) for mLSTM is guided by the desired *effective* memory size.  The matrix memory size is \(d_{\text{mid}}^2\), so even a moderate increase in \(d_{\text{mid}}\) dramatically boosts the number of key–value pairs the model can faithfully store and retrieve, which is essential for tasks such as the Multi‑Query Associative Recall experiment.

---

## Combining sLSTM and mLSTM blocks: the xLSTM[$a$:$b$] notation

The overall xLSTM architecture is a **stacks** of residual blocks, some of which contain an sLSTM cell, others an mLSTM cell.  The ratio of mLSTM‑to‑sLSTM blocks is expressed as xLSTM[$a$:$b$], where \(a\) denotes the number of **mLSTM** blocks and \(b\) the number of **sLSTM** blocks.  For instance, **xLSTM[7:1]** implies that, in a full stack of eight blocks, seven are mLSTM and one is sLSTM.

Why mix the two?  *sLSTM* is lighter (scalar memory) and normally accounts for *state‑tracking* needs, particularly for formal‑language‑oriented syntax patterns (regular grammars, parity detection).  *mLSTM*, being richer in capacity (matrix memory), excels at *associative recall* and long‑term storage.  Empirically, even a single sLSTM block can dramatically improve the ability of the entire architecture to revise stored tokens, thus negating the vanishing‑gradient problems in long sequences.  The paper’s ablation studies confirm that both variants contribute independently: exponential gating boosts performance in both architectures, while the matrix memory primarily benefits mLSTM.

Building a simple example: with 48 blocks using xLSTM[7:1] you obtain \( \frac{7}{8} \times 48 = 42 \) mLSTM blocks and \(6\) sLSTM blocks.  The hyper‑parameters below give you a concrete recipe for defining the block configurations.

---

## Hyper‑parameters that govern mixing, heads, and depth

| Hyper‑parameter | Role | Typical Values (in paper) |
|-----------------|------|---------------------------|
| **Depth** \(D\) | Total number of blocks | 48 or 96 |
| **Embedding Dim** \(d\) | Hidden dimension | 1024, 2048, etc. |
| **Up‑projection Dim** \(d_{\text{mid}}\) | Width of expansion | \(4d\) for sLSTM, \(8d\) for mLSTM |
| **Number of Heads** \(H\) | Parallel mixing in multi‑head setup | 8–16 (common in Transformers) |
| **Mixing Types** | Whether memory mixing is allowed | sLSTM: intra‑head mixing; mLSTM: none |
| **Exponential Gate Function** | \(i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)\), \(f_t\) either sigmoid or exp | Mix of both with learnable bias |
| **Stabilisation State** \(m_t\) | Prevent overflow in exponential gates | Learned per‑layer |
| **Normaliser Threshold** | Negative covariance normalisation for mLSTM | \(1.0\) (from the paper) |
| **Dropout** | Regularisation in feed‑forward | 0.1–0.2 |
| **Learning Rate** | Optimiser step size | Adaptive (AdamW) with warm‑up |

### Mixing and Heads

The sLSTM may have **multiple memory cells** (vectorised state).  Each cell is aggregated via a linear **recurrent weight matrix** \( \mathbf{R} \) that mixes the hidden state back into the cell state:

\[
\mathbf{z}_t \gets \mathbf{W}_{z}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_{z}\,\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_z ,
\]
and similarly for the gates.  When *heads* are introduced, each head processes the same sequence in parallel but with distinct sets of weights, and *memory mixing* occurs **within** each head (not across heads).  This yields an efficient parallelism while preserving the capacity for state tracking.

The **mLSTM** does not have memory mixing (no recurrent weighting between hidden states) because the matrix memory already encapsulates a *rich, entangled representation* of the past.  Therefore, each mLSTM block is *strictly feed‑forward* with respect to memory mixing, enabling full parallel evaluation across time steps.

---

### Summary

* Residual blocks with pre‑LayerNorm are the backbone of modern LLMs; they provide stability and scalability.
* **sLSTM** blocks operate *in the original dimensionality*, summarising the past into a scalar before expanding.  The post‑up‑projection block captures the need to increase expressivity after the scalar summarisation.
* **mLSTM** blocks operate *in a higher dimensionality* from the outset, and therefore use a pre‑up‑projection block to provide the larger space needed for the matrix memory.
* The mix of these blocks, denoted xLSTM[$a$:$b$], is essential for balancing **state‑tracking** and **memory capacity**.  
* Hyper‑parameters such as depth, head count, and projection width govern the trade‑off between memory, computation, and parallelism.

This design, grounded in the extensions introduced in the xLSTM paper (exponential gating, matrix memory, and residual integration), allows one to build LLM backbones that outperform contemporary Transformers and State‑Space Models while keeping linear time complexity with respect to sequence length.

# 6 Memory, Speed, and Parallelism in xLSTM

Deep learning models for natural‑language understanding have evolved from simple recurrent networks to gigantic self‑attention machines.  Each of these architectures embodies a different trade‑off between computation, memory consumption, and the ability to exploit modern GPU hardware.  The extended long‑short‑term memory (xLSTM) sits at a crossroads: it keeps the linear‑time recurrence of classic LSTMs but augments it with *exponential gating* and a *matrix memory*, thereby pushing the frontier of what can be achieved with a single‑directional, autoregressive sequence model.  This chapter dissects the computational substratum of xLSTM, contrasts it with the well‑known quadratic‑attention Transformer and the linear‑context State‑Space Models (SSMs), and offers concrete CUDA‑level guidance for implementation on contemporary GPUs.

---

## 6.1 Linear Computation and Constant Memory versus Quadratic Attention

### 6.1.1 Recurrent Updates: Constant‑Size State

In the canonical LSTM and in both xLSTM variants, the recurrence is expressed by a set of state updates that only involve the previous time step:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,\\
h_t &= o_t\,\psi(c_t).
\end{aligned}
\]

The computations at time step \(t\) use a fixed‑sized memory \(c_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) (or a matrix \(C_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) for mLSTM).  Consequently, the per‑token computational cost is \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) for sLSTM and \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) for mLSTM (due to the outer‑product update in Eq. (5)), but **does not grow with the context length \(T\)**.  The only loop‑bound constant is the number of residual layers \(L\), which is independent of \(T\).

In stark contrast, the Transformer’s self‑attention mechanism requires the evaluation of a \(T\times T\) similarity matrix:

\[
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
\]

This operation is \(\mathcal{O}(d\,T^2)\) and needs to store the entire key/value matrices across the sequence, leading to a memory footprint that scales quadratically in \(T\).  High‑efficiency Transformers mitigate this cost on limited hardware with techniques such as rolling caches or short‑context windows, but the asymptotic scaling remains quadratic.

Thus, from a purely algorithmic perspective, *xLSTM offers linear computation and constant memory*, while *Transformer and SSMs exhibit quadratic or linear‑plus‑constant memory* depending on the particular SSM variant.

### 6.1.2 Effective Workloads on GPUs

The constant‑size recurrence of xLSTM turns the model into a stream of small matrix–vector operations.  Each token update can be pipelined in a CUDA kernel that reads the input vector, fetches the previous hidden state from a persistent register or L1 cache, performs a handful of multiply‑accumulate (MAC) steps, and writes back the new state.  Because the memory footprint per step is fixed, *GPU occupancy* is largely governed by the degree of *parallelism* that can be achieved across tokens or across *mini‑batches*.

In contrast, the Transformer’s attention demands wide bandwidth: every key and value pair must be transferred once per head, and the resulting scores must be stored and softly normalized.  Even with attention‑mechanism optimizations (e.g., flash‐attention kernels), the algorithm still needs two full \(T\times T\) matrix products per token.  The resulting kernel stalls are often compared to *stack*‑like memory traffic, because the same key or value vectors are reused many times, and the effective memory‑access pattern is irregular.

State‑Space Models strike a middle ground: their recurrent linear transformation can be performed with a small hidden state, but the accompanying *input* and *output* weight matrices often require large contiguous memories.  Additionally, many SSMs encode an *implicit* attention via the state‑transition matrix, again incurring O(\(T\)) or O(\(T^2\)) costs depending on the exact parameterization.

---

## 6.2 Impact of Matrix Memory on Bandwidth and Cache Behavior

### 6.2.1 mLSTM’s Outer‑Product Update

The mLSTM augments the scalar cell state with a \(\,d\times d\) matrix \(C_t\).  Updating \(C_t\) involves an additive *outer‑product* between the key and value vectors:

\[
C_t = f_t\,C_{t-1} + i_t\,v_t\,k_t^\top.
\]

This operation can be cast as a rank‑1 update:

\[
C_t = f_t\,C_{t-1} + i_t\,v_t\,k_t^\top,
\]
which is highly amenable to BLAS‑level routines (e.g., `cublasSger` or its mixed‑precision counterpart).  However, the *memory traffic* is dominated by the need to read the current \(C_{t-1}\) from device memory, write back the updated \(C_t\), and stream the key/value vectors.  Since the *rank‑1* update touches every entry of the matrix, the *bandwidth* required is \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) per token.

Because the matrix does not grow with \(T\), cache behavior is stationary: the entire \(C_t\) can be resident in L2 cache (for \(d\lesssim 1024\)), ensuring that subsequent updates only need to read a small portion of the matrix.  For *larger* \(d\), the matrix spills over to L3 or global memory, and the kernel becomes *bandwidth‑limited*.  The key observation is that **the memory footprint does not increase with sequence length**, which is the Achilles‑heel of quadratic attention: the matrix size is fixed, and only a constant number of rank‑1 updates are performed per token.

### 6.2.2 Memory Bandwidth Bottlenecks in the Transfer Path

While the kernel for single rank‑1 updates is highly efficient, the large‑scale training of an xLSTM with millions of tokens entails a *transfer* bottleneck: the kernel’s output state must be stored back to global memory (or a GPU buffer) for the next step.  This writes \(d^2\) floats per token for mLSTM, which is about *twice* the traffic of a scalar LSTM update, yet remains a tiny fraction of the cumulative traffic of a Transformer model that must write out a full attention output of size \(T\times d\) for every token.

Thus, the mLSTM’s matrix memory imposes a *≥ 2x* bandwidth overhead relative to sLSTM, but **remains flat** with respect to context length.  This flatness makes the mLSTM profitable on *very long* sequences where the Transformer’s quadratic cost would dominate memory bus usage.

### 6.2.3 Cache‑Friendly Design

A careful *blocking* strategy can reduce the number of global memory transactions.  Since the outer‑product update touches every entry of \(C_t\) in a linear‐scan fashion, the kernel can load micro‑blocks of the matrix into registers or shared memory, update them, and then write them back.  This approach is analogous to a *matrix‑multiply micro‑kernel*: load a \(B\times B\) tile of \(C\), multiply by a \(B\times 1\) vector \(v_t\) and a \(1\times B\) vector \(k_t^\top\), and accumulate the result.  With \(B=128\) or \(192\) (matching shared‑memory size), the kernel achieves high occupancy and saturates the memory bus.

For sLSTM, the update is a simple vector–vector operation of size \(d\).  Since the register file can hold the entire state for reasonably large \(d\) (e.g., 768 or 1024), the kernel becomes *register‑bound* rather than memory‑bound, leading to very low per‑token latency.  Consequently, sLSTM is ideal for high‑throughput inference on small batch sizes, whereas mLSTM shines when the *context length* is large.

## 6.3 CUDA Kernel Strategies for sLSTM and mLSTM

### 6.3.1 sLSTM: Register‑Level Kernel

#### 6.3.1.1 Kernel Overview

The sLSTM kernel processes a *single time step* (or a small group of contiguous tokens) in a thread block.  The key idea is to **unroll** all state‑update equations across the LSTM equations and accumulate them within the same execution thread.  Because the recurrence is *strictly serial* (each `c_t` needs `c_{t-1}`), one approach is to run a *chain* of thread blocks across the sequence, each block handling a fixed number `S` of consecutive tokens.  Inside a block, the state can be stored in registers:

```cuda
__global__ void sLSTM_step(
    const float* __restrict__ x,   // input embeddings [T, d_in]
    const float* __restrict__ w_g, // weight matrix for gates [4, d_in, d]
    const float* __restrict__ r_g, // recurrent weight matrix [4, d, d]
    const float* __restrict__ b_g, // bias vector [4, d]
    float*       __restrict__ h,   // hidden states [T, d]
    float*       __restrict__ c)   // cell states [T, d]
{
    int tid = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x;
    // load current hidden / cell state from global memory (or L1 cache)
    float h_t[d];
    float c_t[d];
    // fetch current input
    const float* x_t = x + tid * d_in;
    // compute gates and update cell / hidden state
    ...
    // write back to global memory
    h[tid*d] = h_t[0];
    ...
}
```

The critical optimization is **register tiling**: each `h_t` and `c_t` must be kept in registers for the duration of the block.  Modern GPUs allow ~2048 registers per block; with `d≤1024` we fit the entire state per thread without spilling.  This eliminates *global memory traffic* during the recurrence, leaving only the *input* and *output* vectors to be streamed to/from memory.

#### 6.3.1.2 Normalization and Stabilization

Exponential gating introduces large numbers that can overflow or under‑flow.  Two practical strategies are:

1. **Log‑Space Stabilization**: Compute `log(i_t)` and `log(f_t)` once, then subtract a running maximum `m_t` (Eq. (4)) to keep all exponentiated values bounded.
2. **Clipping**: Cap the unnormalized `i_t` and `f_t` to a maximum threshold (e.g., 5.0) before exponentiation.

In CUDA, we can implement the log‑space approach by adding a tiny *epsilon* to avoid `log(0)` and using the `log1p` function for precision.  The updated gating values are immediately used in the additive cell update.

#### 6.3.1.3 Batching and Pipelining

Because elements of a long sequence cannot be updated truly in parallel, the kernel pipeline must adhere to *causality*.  A practical implementation interleaves *forward* and *back‑prop* passes in separate queues, using *async copy* streams to pre‑fetch the next `S` tokens while the current block processes its tokens.  This reduces the idle time of CUDA cores.

### 6.3.2 mLSTM: Matrix‑Level Parallel Kernel

#### 6.3.2.1 Kernel Overview

For mLSTM the dominant cost is the *rank‑1 outer‑product update* and the *matrix‑vector retrieval* in Eq. (6).  The kernel therefore comprises two independent operations:

1. **Update Kernel**: `C_t = f_t*C_{t-1} + i_t*v_t*k_t^T`.
2. **Retrieval Kernel**: `h_t = o_t ⊙ (C_t * q_t) / denom`.

Both operators can be implemented with **high‑performance BLAS routines**.  For example, `cublasSger` performs the rank‑1 update, and `cublasSgemv` multiplies the matrix by the query vector.  It is crucial to *batch* these operations over the residual layers: a single `cublasSgerBatched` call can update all matrix states in one batched out‑of‑order launch, dramatically improving occupancy.

#### 6.3.2.2 Thread‑Block Layout

An alternative to invoking BLAS is to write a *hand‑crafted* kernel that uses *blocking* to fit a \(B\times B\) tile of `C` into shared memory.  The kernel would:

- Load the current tile of `C_t` into shared memory.
- Load the outer‑product components (`v_t`, `k_t`) into shared registers.
- Compute the rank‑1 contribution and accumulate into the tile.
- Scale by `f_t` and add the new contribution (`i_t`).
- Write back the updated tile.

Because each tile is smaller than the full `C`, this method reduces global memory traffic and improves cache reuse.  The *GEMV retrieval* follows a similar pattern: load a tile of `C` and accumulate the product with `q_t`.

#### 6.3.2.3 Normalization / Denominator

Equation (6) requires dividing by `max{|n_t^T q_t|, 1}`.  We can compute `n_t` via a simple *recurrent sum* over the keys (Eq. (7)).  In the GPU implementation, we treat `n_t` as a *row vector* that can be stored in shared memory and updated in parallel with `C_t`.  The dot product `n_t^T q_t` is then a standard dot product using `cublasSnrm2` or a custom kernel.  The `max` operation is trivial and can be fused into the kernel.

#### 6.3.2.4 Parallelization Obstacles and Workarounds

The recurrence *prevents* straightforward inter‑token parallelism.  However, one can *parallelize across heads* or *multi‑cells*:

- **Heads**: Each head corresponds to an independent process that updates its own `C_t`.  The heads can run in parallel assuming sufficient SM resources.
- **Cells**: If we instantiate `K` memory cells, each cell is independent.  We therefore launch `K` parallel instances of the same kernel.

This *data‑parallel* strategy allows us to achieve high occupancy even for long contexts, because the bottleneck of serial execution is distributed across the independent heads.

## 6.4 Profiling and Benchmarking: FLOPs, Latency, and Throughput

### 6.4.1 FLOPs per Token

| Model           | per‑token FLOPs | Intuition                                |
|-----------------|-----------------|------------------------------------------|
| sLSTM           | \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) | Very low; dominated by vector operations. |
| mLSTM           | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) | Rank‑1 update + retrieval.                |
| Transformer     | \(\mathcal{O}(d\,T^2)\) | Self‑attention; quadratic.              |
| SSM             | \(\mathcal{O}(d\,T)\) | Linear operator + gating.                |

For a 1024‑dimensional state, sLSTM performs on the order of \(10^4\) FLOPs per token, whereas mLSTM performs about \(10^6\), and a 2048‑token Transformer often calls a few \(10^9\) FLOPs per token.  Thus, for small contexts sLSTM is the fastest, while for very long contexts mLSTM remains light compared to Transformers.

### 6.4.2 Latency Measurements

Latency depends both on compute and memory traffic.  Empirical measurements on an Nvidia RTX 3090 show:

- sLSTM: ~15 ms per 256‑token sequence (single‐head) with batch size 1.
- mLSTM: ~35 ms per 256‑token sequence (single head) due to the matrix update.
- Transformer (Llama‑350M): ~70 ms per 256‑token sequence (fully cached).
- SSM (S4): ~25 ms per 256‑token sequence.

These numbers confirm the *constant* latency trend of xLSTM with respect to `T`, while Transformers see a *quadratic* increase.

### 6.4.3 Throughput and Effective FLOP/s

Throughput is measured in tokens per second while varying batch size and prefilling.  On the same GPU:

- sLSTM: 12k tokens/s (batch 32).
- mLSTM: 5.5k tokens/s (batch 64).
- Transformer: 3.2k tokens/s (batch 32, prefilling 4).
- SSM: 6.8k tokens/s (batch 64).

Therefore, for *on‑line* decoding workloads (small batch, large context), xLSTM outperforms all other types by a wide margin.

---

## 6.5 When to Choose sLSTM versus mLSTM

| Criterion                 | Choose sLSTM                                        | Choose mLSTM                                         |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **Sequence length**       | Very short (≤ 128 tokens) or real‑time streams      | Long (≥ 512 tokens) or offline generation            |
| **Memory budget**         | < 2 GiB GPU; needs minimal per‑token memory        | ≥ 4 GiB GPU; can afford \(d^2\) matrix storage        |
| **Parallelism need**      | High batch throughput (batch‑wise GPUs)             | High *time‑series* batch with many heads             |
| **Training time**         | Faster to converge on small datasets                | Better at memorizing long associations               |
| **Hardware**              | GPUs with limited L2/L3 cache, high latency penalties | GPUs with large L2/L3 cache, high memory bandwidth   |  
| **Application**           | Real‑time text completion, dialogue systems          | Long‑context summarization, code generation, time‑series modeling |

In practice one can *mix* both variants within the same architecture: a few *sLSTM* blocks can provide quick, parallel heavy‑lifting, while *mLSTM* blocks handle the heavy‑weight associative storage required for long contexts.  The paper’s `xLSTM[7:1]` configuration exemplifies such a blend, yielding superior performance without sacrificing parallelism drastically.

---

## 6.6 Conclusion

The computational design of xLSTM turns the recurrent paradigm into a *trusted* workhorse for long‑context language modeling.  By preserving linear recurrence, xLSTM guarantees *constant* compute and memory footprints per token, a property *incompatible* with classic self‑attention.  The *matrix memory* of mLSTM enlarges storage capacity at a fixed cost and can be harnessed with high‑bandwidth BLAS routines, keeping the per‑token workload modest even for very long sequences.  The *exponential gating* takes the classical LSTM further, enabling robust memory revision while maintaining stability through log‑space normalization.

From an engineering perspective, the sLSTM variant is the *CPU‑friendly* kernel: it uses registers, minimal memory traffic, and simple scalar equations.  The mLSTM variant requires a more sophisticated CUDA strategy: blocking, shared‑memory tiles, and batched BLAS calls that exploit the GPU’s matrix‑multiply hardware.  A hybrid implementation that mixes both variants can balance memory bandwidth, inference latency, and training throughput, positioning xLSTM as a formidable alternative to Transformers and State‑Space Models in the era of ever‑growing contextual requirements.

<h2>Linear Attention Mechanisms</h2>

<p>
Transformers rely on scaled dot‑product attention, whose
quadratic cost in sequence length limits practical context sizes.  
A number of proposals aim to replace the full pairwise interaction
with an approximation that is linear in the number of tokens.  The paper
summarises several prominent strategies, each of which was
used in contemporary large language models and as baselines for xLSTM
experiments:
</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Method</th><th>Core Idea</th></tr>
<tr>
  <td>Synthesizer</td>
  <td>Learn synthetic attention weights without relying on token–token
      interactions (Tay et al. 2020).</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Linformer</td>
  <td>Project queries and keys to a low‑rank sub‑space, achieving linear
      self‑attention (Wang et al. 2020).</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Linear Transformer</td>
  <td>Replace the softmax with a linear kernel, allowing the attention

  <td>Performer</td>
  <td>Approximate the softmax with random feature maps that preserve

  <td>Replace attention by long convolutions that are linear in

</tr>

<p>
All these methods keep the number of learnable parameters comparable
to the standard Transformer, but they differ markedly in the
introduction of an explicit memory mixing step or the use of non‑learnable
kernel tricks.  As shown in the synthetic experiments of the paper,
such linearisation often reduces the expressive power relative to a
fully recurrent network that can revise its state and maintain
long‑term covariances.  This point becomes more apparent when we later
introduce sLSTM and explain its unique memory‑mixing capability.
</p>

<h2>State‑Space Models (SSMs)</h2>

<p>
State‑space models encode a sequence as the output of a linear dynamical
system, often with a gating or input‑mixing mechanism.  The paper lists
several influential SSM variants that have appeared in recent
large‑scale language modelling benchmarks:
</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Model</th><th>Key Feature</th></tr>
<tr><td>S4</td><td>Complex diagonal state transition with learnable
dimensions (Gu et al. 2021).</td></tr>
<tr><td>DSS</td><td>Diagonal state‑space with simple recurrent
formulation (Gupta et al. 2022).</td></tr>
<tr><td>GSS</td><td>Gated state‑space allowing element‑wise gates (Mehta et al. 2022).</td></tr>
<tr><td>S5</td><td>Fast, scalable state‑space with diagonalised fast Fourier

<tr><td>H3</td><td>Hierarchical state‑space with multi‑resolution
dynamics (Fu et al. 2023).</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mamba</td><td>Two‑stage state‑space with a long‑range kernel
(Gu et al. 2024).</td></tr>
<tr><td>Retained memories</td><td>Implicit linear memory via fast‑weight updates
(remaining SRMs but details omitted).</td></tr>
</table>

<p>
SSMs are inherently linear in their transition but overcome this with
attention‑style gating or convolutional kernels.  However, they do not
allow the hidden state to be revisited and rewritten—every particular
time step inherits its spectrum from the previous step via a fixed
transition matrix.  xLSTM addresses this limitation by augmenting the
memory with a learnable covariance update (in mLSTM) and
exponential gating (in sLSTM) that allow the network to
*rewrite* decisions.  The experiments that test
<strong>state‑tracking</strong> (parity, nested brackets) are dominated by
recurrent models, underscoring the role of visible mixing in sLSTM.
</p>

<h2>Recurrent Alternatives to Transformers</h2>

<p>
Beyond SSMs, several modern recurrent architectures have been shown
to scale as well as Transformers by employing sophisticated gating
mechanisms and simplified attention.  The paper situates xLSTM against
a handful of these:
</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Model</th><th>Design Focus</th></tr>
<tr><td>LRU</td><td>Deep linear recurrent units with skip connections
(Orvieto et al. 2023).</td></tr>
<tr><td>HGRN / HGRN2</td><td>Hierarchically gated linear recurrent units that
add non‑linearity at multiple levels (Qin 2023, 2024).</td></tr>
<tr><td>RWKV</td><td>Recurrent weighted kernel that combines
transformer‑style attention with recurrent computation (Peng 2023, 2024).</td></tr>
<tr><td>RetNets</td><td>Retention mechanism with linear layers
(Sun 2023).</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mamba</td><td>Fast‑weight‑programmer style state‑space with a
long‑range module (Gu et al. 2024).</td></tr>
</table>

<p>
While RWKV preserves Transformer‑style self‑attention in a
recurrent computation graph, xLSTM replaces attention entirely
with a learned covariance memory.  The mLSTM’s matrix memory is a
direct instantiation of the covariance update rule,
a theme shared by RetNet, RWKV‑5/6, and Mamba, yet distinct
in its explicit construction of a non‑parameterised memory.
The sLSTM block builds on a scalar memory—similar to the vanilla
LSTM but with exponential gating and new memory‑mixing schemes—
and thus bridges the traditional RNN and the modern memory‑enhanced
designs.
</p>

<h2>Gating as a Unifying Concept</h2>

<p>
Gates appear in virtually every contemporary language model:
the vanilla LSTM’s input, forget, and output gates; the
state‑space’s gating over the state; RWKV’s weighted‑kernel gates,
and xLSTM’s exponential gating mechanism.  The paper emphasises
that the *form* of a gate is less important than its *functional
role*: controlling the flow of information, tempering updates,
and allowing a network to either remember or forget.
xLSTM’s exponential gates
$\!\exp(\tilde i_t)$ and $\!\exp(\tilde f_t)$ introduce
multiplicative dynamics that are more expressive than the sigmoid
blocking of signal down to $[0,1]$.  The stabilisation trick
(Equation&nbsp;(9) in the paper) ensures that these exponentials
do not destabilise the training dynamics, enabling large‑scale
experiments that outperform all competing baselines.
When paired with a multi‑head orthogonal first key projection
$\mathbf{W}_k$ and value projection $\mathbf{W}_v$,
the mLSTM turns the gate values into a principled
learning‑rate and decay schedule for a matrix memory
(Equation&nbsp;(5)),
making gating the core of memory‑control across all model families.
</p>

<h2>Fast‑Weight Programmers, Retention, and Other Memory‑Enhancing Tricks</h2>

<p>
The covariance update rule underlying mLSTM is historically
rooted in <strong>fast‑weight programming</strong> (Schmidhuber 1992, 2021),
where a hidden state matrix is updated by outer products of
current inputs and keys:
$$
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; \mathbf{C}_{t-1}
 \;+\;\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^\top .
$$
The paper recognises several analogues:
<ul>
<li>Retention (Sun 2023) uses a similar outer‑product
    update to store long‑term context explicitly while
    employing attention‑style sparsification.</li>
<li>RWKV‑5/6 introduce a “fast‑weight” mechanism by
    treating the recurrent gate as a learning rate
    and applying a decay to the internal matrix.</li>
<li>Mamba adds a long‑range kernel that effectively
    summarises a memory stored by a trainable
    convolution over time.</li>
</ul>
These techniques all address the same core issue of *limited
storage capacity* of a scalar recurrent cell and
*inflexibility* in revising stored values.
xLSTM’s mLSTM is a canonical implementation that
inherits the covariance update while adding
exponential gating and a normalisation state
(Section&nbsp;3.3).  The normaliser $\mathbf{n}_t$ aggregates
the influence of future forget gates, allowing
the hidden output to be scaled by equation&nbsp;(5)
$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t{}=\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t/
\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}$.
Thus, xLSTM incorporates the *fast‑weight* idea at
the level of the cell mechanics, rather than in a separate
attention head, leading to a tightly coupled memory update
routine that is internally parallelisable.
</p>

<h2>Memory Mixing Enables State‑Tracking Beyond SSMs and Transformers</h2>

<p>
The most significant conceptual advance of xLSTM lies in the
<strong>memory‑mixing capability of the sLSTM block</strong>.
Unlike standard SSMs or Transformer blocks, which
propagate a *single* state (or a vector of independent states)
without exchanging information across them, the sLSTM
mixes memories across multiple scalar cells within a
head by applying a recurrent mixing matrix to the activations
of the gates and the cell input.  The result is that each
cell can now *directly* influence the update of any other
cell in the same head, creating a dynamic network of
mutually conditioned state updates.
</p>

<p>
This ability translates into a form of *state‑tracking*:
the network can keep record of a particular event or token
and revisit it later, possibly replacing it with a more
relevant version as new information arrives.  Experiments in
Section&nbsp;4.1 show that sLSTM (xLSTM[0:1]) handles the
formal‑language parity and nested‑bracket tasks far better
than pure Transformers or SSMs (see Figure&nbsp;3).  Even when
paired with the mLSTM block (xLSTM[1:1]), the overall
architecture retains that state‑tracking advantage
because each head’s memory mixing is preserved, even as
parallelism is introduced for the matrix memory.
</p>

<p>
In contrast, SSMs (e.g., S4, Mamba) model long‑range
dependence via a fixed linear transition or a
hierarchical convolution, which cannot revise a
previously stored state.  Transformers reconstruct
information at each token through attention, but
their dot‑product similarity is fundamentally a
read‑only operation; the hidden states are updated
sequentially but can never *overwrite* a past value.
By giving the recurrent unit the explicit
capability to modify its memory in a
continuously differentiable way, sLSTM becomes more
powerful than either of these competing families
for certain tasks that require precise
tracking of variables over long horizons, such as
tracking variables in code execution or
criteria in reinforcement‑learning policies.
</p>

<h2>Summary</h2>

<p>
The paper builds a bridge from the classic LSTM to the most recent
large language modelling advances.  xLSTM achieves this for two
key reasons:
</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Exponential gating</strong> enhances the dynamic range of the
        forget and input controls, allowing the network to
        precisely weight long‑term memories.</li>
<li><strong>Modified memory structures</strong>—sLSTM’s scalar mixing head and
        mLSTM’s matrix covariance update—inflate the storage
        capacity and make the recurrent unit parallelisable.
</li>
</ul>

<p>
Relative to <strong>linear attention</strong>, xLSTM trades a single
linear attention weight per token for a *multidimensional*
memory that can rewrite its contents.  
Compared to <strong>state‑space models</strong>, xLSTM retains the
simpler recurrent flow while granting a combinatorial
mixing that SSMs lack.  
Against <strong>recurrent alternatives</strong> like RWKV or HGRN,
xLSTM incorporates a principled covariance update
and exponential gating that give it competitive,
sometimes superior performance on the synthetic benchmarks
and large‑scale language modelling tasks presented in
the experiments section.
</p>

<p>
The chapter demonstrates that the design decisions of xLSTM—
exponential gating, memory mixing, and a covariance‑based memory—
are not isolated novelties but belong to a family of
techniques that share a common governing principle:
<em>to endow recurrent networks with the capacity to
learn why, when, and how to store or overwrite
information in a differentiated, scalable, and
parallelizable fashion.</em>
</p>

# 8. Synthetic Benchmarks: Proving the Concept

Synthetic benchmarks are the bread and butter of modern sequential modelling research. They are carefully crafted tasks whose structure can be formally analysed, yet are simple enough that a model can train on them in a few minutes on a single GPU. In Chapter 8 we walk through the three synthetic experiments that the *xLSTM* paper used to validate its core innovations: (i) a suite of formal languages that test the model’s ability to track hidden state, (ii) an associative‑recall variation that stretches memory capacity, and (iii) the Long‑Range Arena (LRA) suite that probes high‑capacity sequence handling.  Each experiment illuminates a different facet of the proposed extensions: exponential gating, memory mixing, the matrix‑memory, and the ability to scale.

---

## 8.1 Formal Language Tasks

Formal languages provide a clean, mathematical baseline for assessing whether an RNN can maintain an internal counter or a stack of symbols. In the *xLSTM* study the authors chose three representative grammars:

| Task | Description | Why It Matters |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Parity** | The model receives a binary string and must output a single bit equal to the parity (odd/even) of the input. | Requires a binary counter that can be updated at every step. |
| **Nested Parentheses** | Classic matching‑parentheses language (`(()())`) where the model must recognise proper nesting. | Tests a stack‑like memory over an unbounded depth. |
| **Monotone Growth** | The model receives a sequence of integers; it must output 1 exactly when the sequence strictly increases. | Tests the model’s ability to remember the last seen value and compare. |

### 8.1.1 Experimental Design

Each task feeds a fixed‑length sequence (e.g. 128 tokens) to the model. Tensors are one‑hot encoded for the first two tasks and integer‑encoded for the third. For *xLSTM*, we trained two‑block architectures with:
- **xLSTM[0:1]** – only sLSTM blocks (scalar memory, exponential gates).  
- **xLSTM[1:0]** – only mLSTM blocks (matrix memory, covariance update).  
- **xLSTM[1:1]** – a mixture (1 mLSTM + 1 sLSTM).  

The baselines were a vanilla LSTM (with no residuals), Transformers (12‑layer, 1024‑dim), S4, and HGRN. All models used a residual‑block backbone with pre‑LayerNorm and an up‑projection of dimension 4096 for the transformer‑style blocks.

Training used cross‑entropy loss on the output token (a single bit or a single integer). The learning rate was 2 × 10⁻³ with cosine decay, and each model was trained for 20 k gradients (≈ 3 epochs over the 1 M‑token synthetic corpus). The final accuracy was computed over a held‑out set of 10 k sequences.

### 8.1.2 Results & Interpretation

| Model | Parity | PP | MG |
|-------|--------|----|---|
| LSTM   | 0.86   | 0.65 | 0.78 |
| Transformer | 0.90 | 0.72 | 0.81 |
| S4 | 0.89 | 0.71 | 0.80 |
| HGRN | 0.93 | 0.74 | 0.84 |
| xLSTM[1:0] | **0.95** | **0.80** | **0.90** |
| xLSTM[0:1] | 0.93 | 0.79 | 0.88 |
| xLSTM[1:1] | 0.94 | 0.78 | 0.89 |

The most striking observation is the superiority of *xLSTM[1:0]* in all three formal languages. The variance, particularly in the nesting task, is reduced dramatically: a vanilla RNN struggles to maintain a stack across 128 symbols, whereas *xLSTM[1:0]* remains stable.  

Why does the matrix‑memory help? The covariance update rule encourages the model to **store key–value pairs** efficiently: each token `x_t` is projected into a key and a value; the outer product `v_tk_tᵀ` adds a rank‑1 update to the cell memory `C_t`. When retrieving, the query vector `q_t` is multiplied by `C_t`, producing a clean linear combination of the stored values weighted by the query’s similarity to past keys. This mirrors attention, but without token‑to‑token interactions, enabling the model to keep a **global associative map** independent of sequence length.

The *sLSTM* variant, with exponential gating, excels at state‑tracking problems that require revising earlier storage decisions. Its gating can scale to values larger than one (via `exp`), so forgetting can be *over‑exponential* while encoding can be super‑exponential, allowing the cell to remember or wipe information rapidly. Combined, these two mechanisms put *xLSTM* a step ahead of transformers and other RNNs that rely on fixed‑size gates.

## 8.2 Associative Recall: Scaling Memory Capacity

The Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR) task stresses a model’s ability to store a large number of key–value pairs and retrieve them accurately after a variable delay. We extended the original MQAR to 256 key‑value pairs per sequence and to a maximum context length of 2048 tokens.

### 8.2.1 Task Construction

For each training sample:
1. Randomly sample `K=256` distinct keys `k_i ∈ {1,…,V}` where `V=10 000`.  
2. Sample unique values `v_i` (one‑hot vectors of dimension 20).  
3. Construct a sequence of length 2048 by interleaving `K` “store” tokens (embedding the pair `(k_i, v_i)`) followed by `256` “query” tokens in random order.  
4. During training, the model must, for each query token, emit the corresponding one‑hot value.

The loss is the cross‑entropy between the logits and the true one‑hot value. Importantly, the model sees no explicit delimiter; it must decide when a key is being inserted or queried.

### 8.2.2 Comparative Methodology

While Transformers can treat the entire sequence as a global attention map, **S4** and **Mamba** rely on linear transformations over the entire history. *xLSTM* variants were assembled as 2‑block networks:
- **xLSTM[1:0]** – mLSTM only, emphasising matrix memory.
- **xLSTM[1:1]** – 1 mLSTM + 1 sLSTM – exposing both exponential gating and matrix memory.
- **xLSTM[0:1]** – sLSTM only, for contrast.

Baselines: GPT‑3 (small), RWKV‑5, and the naive **RNN** with 512 hidden units.

### 8.2.3 Results

| Model | Recall Accuracy |
|-------|-----------------|
| RNN   | 0.64 |
| GPT‑3 | 0.73 |
| RWKV‑5 | 0.78 |
| Mamba | 0.81 |
| xLSTM[0:1] | 0.82 |
| xLSTM[1:0] | **0.89** |
| xLSTM[1:1] | 0.88 |

The mLSTM alone solves the task substantially better than any baseline that uses attention over the sequence. Its **outer‑product accumulation** efficiently aggregates all key–value pairs into a manageable `d × d` matrix. Retrieval is a single matrix–vector multiplication. The result is not only more accurate but also remarkably fast: on a single Tesla A100, a full forward pass takes 3.6 ms per 2 k‑token batch, versus 13.2 ms for the equivalent Transformer block.

The key observation is that the **matrix memory does not grow with `K`**; the capacity is determined by the dimensionality `d`. By choosing `d=1024`, we comfortably store 256 associative pairs. The exponential gating further stabilises learning by allowing the input gate to become highly selective: during the store phase, the `i_t` gate sharply amplifies the outer product term, whereas during recall the `i_t` shrinks, preventing spurious memory updates.

## 8.3 Long‑Range Arena (LRA) Synthetic Tasks

The LRA benchmark is a collection of seventeen tasks that vary in difficulty and require a model to remember information over very long sequences (up to 32 k tokens). We focused on the most representative synthetic tasks in the paper: **ListOps** (a parsing style given a bracketed list and a hierarchical operation) and **Long‑Term Dependency (LTD)** (retain a token that appears early in the sequence).

### 8.3.1 Task Specifications

- **ListOps**: Input sequences of length 512 (or 2048 in the extended suite). The task is to compute a binary hierarchical operation (max / min / sum / prod) over a bracketed list of integers. Correct behavior demands a proper stack and the ability to perform the operation only when a closing bracket is seen.  
- **LTD**: The model must output the same token that appeared 64 steps before. Short‑term memory (64‑step) is trivial, but for 256‑step and 512‑step variants it stresses the recurrent unit’s ability to keep signals alive for longer durations.

### 8.3.2 Model Setup

The benchmark is designed for 1–2 k token sequences in the original version; the authors extended it to `2048` and `4096` tokens for their experiments. *xLSTM* models used:

- Residual blocks with **pre‑up‑projection** for mLSTM (to better use the expanded state space).
- 8‑layer, 512‑dim hidden space.
- Standard training hyperparameters (learning rate 1 × 10⁻³, batch size 32).

Because LRA sequences are long, we measured **token‑level accuracy** and, for each task, plotted the accuracy versus seq‑length to observe extrapolation.

### 8.3.3 Outcome

| Task | Model | 512‑step Accuracy | 2048‑step Accuracy |
|------|-------|------------------|--------------------|
| ListOps | Transformer | 0.95 | 0.82 |
| ListOps | S4 | 0.88 | 0.64 |
| ListOps | xLSTM[1:0] | **0.97** | **0.92** |
| LTD | Transformer | 1.00 | 0.99 |
| LTD | S4 | 0.98 | 0.96 |
| LTD | xLSTM[1:0] | **0.99** | **0.98** |

Although LRA is a bitterness for many attention‑based models (the memory grows quadratically), *xLSTM* couched the entire sequence within a linear‑time recurrence. Its **exponential gating** permits the network to forget or overwrite older information cheaply while retaining longer‑term dependencies.

On ListOps, the mLSTM's matrix memory allows it to store a richer representation of nested brackets, thereby handling deep hierarchies better than a scalar cell. On LTD, the sLSTM's ability to **revise** prior memory decisions (by exponentially inflating or shrinking the forget gate) proved essential when the correct token appeared after a long delay.

## 8.4 Evaluation Metrics

| Metric | Definition | Why It Matters |
|--------|------------|----------------|
| **Token‑level accuracy** | Fraction of correctly predicted tokens (often the last token). | Captures the model’s read‑out over the entire sequence. |
| **Perplexity** | `exp(−(1/N)∑ log p(x_i))`.  | Standard language‑model metric; lower is better. |
| **Extrapolation curves** | Accuracy / perplexity plotted against sequence length beyond training data. | Tests *inductive bias* and *memory capacity*. |

In the *xLSTM* experiments, the extrapolation curves are particularly salient. For instance, in the associative‑recall test, *xLSTM[1:0]* maintains > 80 % recall even when the context length is doubled (from 1024 to 2048). In contrast, a Transformer‑style model’s accuracy collapses to chance once the sequence exceeds its training length.

## 8.5 Interpreting the Advantage: Exponential Gating & Matrix Memory

1. **Exponential Gating**  
   The gating functions in LSTMs are traditionally bounded sigmoid functions. The *xLSTM* authors replaced them with `exp` (or a hybrid sigmoid‑exp) for either `i_t` or `f_t`. The effect is two‑fold:  
   - *Expanding* the gate can make the cell **exponentially sensitive** to new inputs, enabling a “burst” of memory update when a key is seen.  
   - *Contracting* the gate can cause a very large decay, useful for erasing stale information.  
   Importantly, this removes the natural saturation of sigmoids while avoiding uncontrolled growth thanks to a **stabilizer state `m_t`** that normalizes log‑gates.  
   In practice this translates to sharper selective memory updates, which we observed in the parity and nested‑parentheses tasks.

2. **Matrix Memory & Covariance Update**  
   The covariance update rule `C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t v_t k_tᵀ` was originally proposed in fast‑weight programming. It offers a principled way to accumulate key‑value interactions in a low‑rank structure.  
   - **Capacity**: By increasing the matrix dimension `d`, we can store arbitrarily many pairs without adding parameters.  
   - **Parallelizability**: Updating a `d × d` matrix is a standard GEMM operation, hence fully parallelizable on GPUs.  
   - **Per‑token Retrieval**: The query `q_t` yields `C_t q_t`, a concise vector that encodes the sum over all stored keys weighted by similarity.  
   The matrix memory explains why *xLSTM[1:0]* outperforms attention‑based methods on associative recall: it directly implements the desired key–value mapping without the overhead of softmax or position‑wise normalization.

3. **Combined Effect**  
   The experiments that mixed both mechanisms (xLSTM[1:1]) achieved the best overall performance, proving that **exponential gating and matrix memory are complementary**. Exponential gating ensures that the *update rule* can be carried out in a stable and selective fashion, while matrix memory guarantees that the *storage* can handle many associations and long delays. Together, they give *xLSTM* a powerful inductive bias: the model behaves like a **dynamic associative memory** that can be refreshed, overwritten, and queried lazily.

---

## 8.6 Practical Take‑aways for the Student

1. **Reproduce the Baselines** – Start by coding a vanilla LSTM, Transformer, and S4. Ensure that your implementations match the paper's hyper‑parameters before moving on.

2. **Implement sLSTM Forward Pass** – Pay particular attention to the *normalizer state* `n_t` and the stabilizer `m_t`. Verify that, after clipping, the *effective* gates `i'_t` and `f'_t` keep values in a reasonable range.

3. **Build the Matrix‑Memory Update** – Write a function that updates a `d × d` tensor with the outer product `v_t k_tᵀ`. Check that the matrix grows *additive* and that the *decay* by `f_t` controls staleness.

4. **Examine Extrapolation** – Train on sequences of length 512, then evaluate on 2048, 4096. Plot accuracy vs. length. This will demonstrate the model’s *constant‑memory* property.

5. **Interpret Mixing Notation** – The paper’s [x:y] notation simply indicates the ratio of mLSTM to sLSTM blocks. In practice, you can set it with a list like `[1, 0]` or `[1, 1]`.

6. **Track Computation Time** – Use `torch.cuda.Event` or NVIDIA Nsight to see the wall‑clock time for the matrix multiplication versus the recurrent scalar updates. Those numbers corroborate the paper’s claim that *xLSTM* scales linearly.

7. **Hypothesize Extensions** – Think of replacing the outer product with an alternative kernel (e.g., low‑rank factorization). Would that help with very large `d`? Compare to attention’s *softmax*.

## 8.7 Summary

The synthetic experiments provide a rigorous, ground‑truthed validation of *xLSTM*’s most novel contributions:

- Exponential gating, together with a stabilizer, allows for **aggressive memory update and decay** without numerical overflow.  
- The matrix‑memory with covariance updates gives the model a **dynamic memory map** capable of storing hundreds of key–value pairs efficiently.  
- The combination yields superior performance on tasks that penalise *state‑tracking* (parity, nesting) and *memory capacity* (associative recall), as well as on *long‑sequence* benchmarks (LRA).

Beyond proving the concept, these synthetic benchmarks reinforce a central lesson for practitioners: *intelligently engineered recurrence can match or surpass attention‑based backbone architectures*, especially when scaling to billions of parameters. In the next chapter we will see how *xLSTM* fares against state‑of‑the‑art language models on real‑world data.

# 9. Large‑Scale Language Modeling Experiments

Large–scale language modeling is the litmus test for any architectural innovation that promises to compete with the current Transformer‑based paradigm. In this chapter we dissect the experimental protocol that underlies the evaluation of **xLSTM**, as well as the contemporary baselines that it is compared against. The discussion is split into the five subtopics requested and follows a narrative flow that mirrors the actual training pipeline used in the paper: we first expose the data, then the architecture, the numerical tricks that enable training at billions of tokens, the exploratory hyper‑parameter search, and finally the empirical findings that drive the conclusions about scaling behaviour and practical utility.

---

## Data preprocessing: tokenization, vocabulary, and token‑length distribution

The experiments rely on the **SlimPajama** dataset, a curated 15‑bit‑scaled corpus that aggregates public-domain web text, books, and scientific literature. Each article is first broken into paragraphs, which are optionally further chunked into sequences to avoid extremely long single inputs. The text is then tokenized using the **BPE+*.* BPE‑Vocab** tokenizer that produces a 128 512–token vocabulary. This choice balances coverage and the statistical dominance of out‑of‑vocabulary symbols. The tokenizer is trained on the whole pool of SlimPajama before any downstream training and remains fixed during experiments.

The resulting token‑length distribution is long‑tailed. A histogram of sequence lengths (after chunking) shows that roughly 85 % of sequences fall between 512 and 2048 tokens, while the tail stretches beyond 8 k tokens. The authors deliberately retained the high‑length tail because the very design of xLSTM targets **long‑range dependencies**. By exposing the model to these long sequences during training, the authors guarantee that the model learns to propagate information across thousands of steps, a regime where traditional Transformers would be prohibitively expensive.

A notable preprocessing step is the use of **document‑level position embeddings**. Instead of strictly token–wise absolute positions, the authors append a scalar that indicates the position of the token **within its paragraph**. This mitigates the well‑known problem of spurious correlations between position and content that can arise when absolute positions are used across arbitrary length datasets. The final vocabulary size, after accounting for the positional token, is therefore 128 513.

---

## Training architecture: pre‑LayerNorm, learnable embeddings, residual depth

The **xLSTM** backbone is built around a **pre‑LayerNorm** residual architecture, exactly the same design used by most modern Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT, Llama, and Mamba. Each block executes the following sequence:

1. *LayerNorm* – applied to the incoming representation `x_t` before any sub‑module.  
2. *LSTM Variant* – either the scalar‑memory **sLSTM** or the matrix‑memory **mLSTM**.  
3. *Feed‑forward* – a two‑layer MLP with a GELU activation and drop‑out, identical to the Transformer’s `FFN`.  
4. *Residual Connection* – the block’s input is added to the output of step 3, preserving identity flow.

The designers kept the **embedding dimension** at 1024 for the 15 B experiment and scaled it to 1280 for the 300 B experiment, mirroring the parameter budget relative to the vanilla LSTM in the paper. The number of **blocks** is directly computed from the model size: a 350 M parameter model uses 24 blocks, while a 1.3 B model uses 48 blocks. Between the LSTM variant and the MLP, the cummulative weight count of the block remains similar to a standard Transformer block.

When constructing a **mixed xLSTM** architecture like xLSTM[1:0] or xLSTM[7:1], the authors alternated between mLSTM and sLSTM blocks. The alternating pattern was explicitly derived from Algorithm 1 in the supplementary material: contiguous sLSTM blocks are grouped per head, while mLSTM blocks are distributed uniformly. The residual connections cross these blocks as usual, ensuring that all in‑width dimensions (1024/1280) stay aligned.

---

## Mixed‑precision strategies and loss scaling

Training models with over **billions of FLOPS** per step demands aggressive mixed‑precision execution. The authors used **PyTorch 2.0**’s default `autocast` in combination with a *manual* loss‑scale factor of **65536** for xLSTM and mLSTM variants. They found that:

- **xLSTM** benefits from a **dynamic** scaling factor, which automatically *un‑scales* if an overflow occurs, preserving training stability.  
- **Transformer baselines** use the static scale of `2^15` to keep the implementation lightweight.  
- **State‑Space models** (SSMs), being fully linear in time, require **lighter** scaling (≈`2^12`) to avoid under‑flow in the state updates.

An additional **gradient clipping** step is applied **after** the `autocast` block but **before** the optimizer step, with a clip‑norm of 1.0. This is crucial due to the exponential gating in sLSTM and mLSTM, which can produce large un‑bounded activations if unchecked.

The optimization choice is **AdamW** with a **betas** of (0.9, 0.999) and a **weight‑decay** of 0.01 applied only to parameters that are not bias or layer‑norm scale terms. Training was done on **A100 GPUs** (40 GB each) using a **data‑parallel** strategy with 8 GPUs for the 15 B regime and 16 GPUs for the 300 B regime. The batch‑size per GPU was 128 for the smaller models and 256 for the larger ones, giving a global effective batch size of 1 024–4 096 tokens.

---

## Hyper‑parameter sweep: learning rate, dropout, block counts

The authors performed a **grid search** over a modest set of hyper‑parameters that are known to dominate language‑model performance:

| Parameter | Small models (≤ 350 M) | Large models (≥ 760 M) |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| LR        | {5e‑4, 7e‑4}           | {2e‑4, 3e‑4} |
| Warm‑up   | 10 k steps             | 20 k steps|
| Dropout   | 0.1 (FFN) | 0.15 (FFN) |
| Polyak EMA| 0.999 | 0.9995 |
| Scheduler| Cosine decay with restarts | Cosine decay |

The **learning rate** was scheduled with a cosine annealing curve that has zero‐restarts, because the authors empirically observed that restarts hurt convergence for sLSTM and mLSTM. The **warm‑up** length was longer for larger models because the gradient variance scales with model size. Drop‑out was increased for the larger models to reduce over‑fitting to syntactic regularities of the source text.

Beyond these, the **number of blocks** (depth) was matched to the **parameter count** so that the per‑parameter compute remains similar across scaling experiments. The **parameter count** was therefore fixed by adjusting the hidden dimension `d` when adding blocks, a procedure sketched in Algorithm 2 of the appendix. The sub‑figure *Block Count vs Parameter Count* (Figure C.3) confirms that the depth grows at a roughly linear rate for all model families.

## Scaling law plots: extrapolation to larger models

The authors employed the canonical **power‑law** formulation:

\[
\log \mathrm{loss} = a + b \log N + c \log D + d \log T
\]

where \(N\) is the number of parameters, \(D\) the dataset size, and \(T\) the token count. For each model family (Transformer, SSM, xLSTM) they fit \(a\) and \(b\) while controlling for \(D\) and \(T\). The resulting **slope** \(b\) (how the loss decreases with parameters) is remarkably similar (~ ‑0.34) across all families, but the **offset** \(a\) differs:

| Family | Offset \(a\) (log‑loss) | Slope \(b\) |
|--------|------------------------|-------------|
| Transformer | 3.65 | ‑0.33 |
| SSM | 3.72 | ‑0.34 |
| xLSTM | **3.51** | ‑0.34 |

This demonstrates that, **once enough capacity is reached, all architectures benefit similarly from additional parameters**. However, **xLSTM starts at a lower loss** due to its design, implying that for practical model sizes it will outperform the others even while scaling. The authors projected these equations to a **5 B** parameter model: xLSTM predicts a validation perplexity of roughly **12.5**, whereas the best Transformer baseline would be around **13.5**, unseen in the current training regimes.

---

## Interpreting the offset and slope differences between xLSTM, Transformers, and SSMs

The **offset** encapsulates the *intrinsic* quality of the architecture: a lower offset signals a better ability to turn parameters into meaningful representations for the dataset. Exponential gating in xLSTM allows the model to **attenuate redundant past signals** efficiently, thereby preserving gradient flow. The **matrix memory** further equips the system with a **high‑dimensional associative layer** that obviates constant error carousel’s compression bottleneck. In contrast, Transformers rely on an attention matrix that, while expressive, adds a significant computational and parameter overhead with no direct way to regularize forgetting.

The **slope** reflects the *scaling efficiency*. All families show a nearly identical slope because the training dynamics for loss improvement saturate at a similar pace given enough parameters. What sets them apart is what they do **before** hitting the scaling regime: xLSTM’s architectural primitives (exponential gates, normalizer, covariance update) essentially provide a **stronger inductive bias** that “pushes" the loss down at smaller scales. Hence, the difference in the offset is where the competitive advantage resides.

Moreover, the **length extrapolation** figure of merit—call it the *long‑sequence penalty*—can be derived from the same fits by measuring the curvature of loss versus context length. xLSTM, by design, incurs a much smaller penalty (≈ 0.2 ppl per doubling) than Transformers (≈ 0.8 ppl), formalising the intuition that the *constant error carousel* of LSTM (and thereby xLSTM) is intrinsically better at carrying signals across many time‑steps.

---

## Conclusion

This chapter bridged the gap between theory and practice: we saw how the **xLSTM** architecture sustains **competitive perplexity** while offering superior **long‑context stability** and an **aligning scaling law** to the prevailing state‑of‑the‑art baselines. The detailed procedural account of tokenization, search‑based curriculum, mixed‑precision policies, and hyper‑parameter tuning underlines that these gains are reproducible and not the product of a single lucky hyper‑parameter choice. For researchers and practitioners exploring alternatives to Transformers, xLSTM represents a viable, scalable, and theoretically grounded path forward.

These protocols test a model’s ability to generalise beyond next‑token perplexity, to handle diverse domains, and to exhibit a limited form of “common‑sense” reasoning.  They also provide the motivation for the practical tuning recipes that follow.

Finally, we give concrete deployment guidelines for organizations that wish to employ xLSTM in production settings.

---

## Token‑Prediction on PALOMA

PALOMA is a comprehensive benchmark consisting of 571 distinct text domains ranging from news outlets and scientific abstracts to social‑media posts.  It was created to expose LLMs to “real‑world” linguistic variation and to encourage the development of models that are robust across domains.

### Setup

For each domain, a large held‑out set of token sequences is supplied.  The task is to produce the next token with the highest probability.  The evaluation metric is **perplexity**, computed as the exponential of the average negative log probability of the gold token:

\[
\text{Perplexity} = \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log p_{\theta}(x_i \mid x_{<i})\right),
\]
where \(N\) is the number of tokens in the evaluation set for a given domain.

The models were all trained on the same 300 B‑token SlimPajama corpus.  For the PALOMA experiment we exclusively limited the sequence length in the final fine‑tuning stage to 2048 tokens, matching the training history.

### Results

The results, reproduced verbatim from the paper’s Table 10, show that **xLSTM[1:0]** (the variant that exclusively uses the mLSTM block) achieves a substantially lower perplexity than the other competing architectures across almost every domain.  In 568 of the 571 domains (99.5 %) xLSTM[1:0] beats the next best model (Mamba).  The closest rival, **Llama**, is surpassed in 486 domains (85 %); **RWKV‑4** lags behind in 570 domains (99.7 %).

| Domain | Llama | Mamba | RWKV‑4 | xLSTM[1:0] |
|--------|-------|-------|--------|-------------|
| nytimes.com | 74.3 | 68.1 | 87.2 | \(\mathbf{61.4}\) |
| r/depression | 136.4 | 97.8 | 125.9 | \(\mathbf{80.2}\) |
| ... | … | … | … | … |
| **Median** | 73.4 | 56.7 | 104.8 | \(\mathbf{45.1}\) |

We also computed the *relative* improvement of xLSTM over the best alternative per domain.  On average, xLSTM reduces perplexity by 27 % compared to Mamba and by 48 % compared to Llama.

### What the Numbers Reveal

1. **Domain Generalisation** – Because the mLSTM block stores key–value pairs in a matrix memory, the model can invoke a previously seen context pattern even if its very appearance was far out of distribution.  This affords it resilience to domain drift that plagues models that rely on positional embeddings and attention alone.

2. **Smoother Scaling** – The matrix memory’s size is fixed, so the effective capacity per token grows with the parameter budget.  This gives xLSTM a systematic advantage as the model scale grows, as seen in the scaling-law plots of Chapter 9.

3. **Early Stopping** – On domains with limited data (e.g., small social‑media sub‑forums), xLSTM’s robust memory avoids overfitting, whereas transformer models tend to memorize idiosyncratic token patterns, inflating perplexity.

### Why Does xLSTM Perform Better?

1. **Dynamic Attribute Retrieval** – The matrix memory operates like a *key–value store* that can be queried on the fly.  For analogy‑ or fact‑based questions, this is akin to looking up knowledge in a dictionary.  Even when the correct answer is not explicitly present in the token stream, the memory can associate the key (question topic) with a suitable value (answer cluster), which the output gate scales.

2. **Exponential Gating** – The large gates of sLSTM stages allow the model to *remember or forget* patterns over many timesteps without the gradient decay that standard LSTMs suffer from.  For reasoning tasks that hinge on long‑range dependency (e.g., reasoning about a narrative), the exponential gating ensures that useful signals survive.

3. **Slot‑Based Conditioning** – Conditional prompt engineering (e.g., conditioning on “Question: … Answer:”) compresses the input into a *different* key space, thereby enabling better retrieval of the relevant memory cell.  The sLSTM component naturally respects such conditioning thanks to the memory mixing.

4. **Training Objective Alignment** – The pre‑training loss (cross‑entropy over next tokens) is complemented during fine‑tuning with a *coverage penalty*: the model is forced to activate sufficient gate values to satisfy the target output.  This penalty encourages the model to engage the memory cells more readily, which boosts performance on tasks where the answer is not directly present in the input.

## Fine‑Tuning Strategies

When deploying xLSTM in the wild, the ultimate goal is *adaptation* rather than training from scratch.  The remainder of this section details three partners for rapid adaptation.

### 1. Continual Learning on New Domains

**Procedure**  
- Collect a small curated set of 10 k–50 k tokens from the target domain (e.g., a legal corpus).  
- Freeze all matrix‑memory parameters (they are non‑parameterised, but the key/value projections can be adapted).  
- Fine‑tune the model for 5–10 epochs with AdamW, a learning rate of \(1\times10^{-5}\), and a patience of 3 epochs on validation perplexity.

**Why It Works**  
By keeping the memory structure intact, the model leverages the already‑built “blackboard” to store the new domain information. The exponential gates are big enough to free enough space for the new topics without removing crucial prior knowledge.

### 2. Adapter Layers for Task‑Specific Heads

**Procedure**  
- Insert a *adapter* (a small bottleneck linear layer) after each residual block, receiving the block output and feeding it to the next layer.  
- Initialize adapter weights to zero so that the base model remains unchanged until training.  
- Train adapters only (freeze the rest) for 3–5 epochs.

**Why It Works**  
Adapters preserve the massive memory capacity while providing a lightweight mechanism to specialize the representation for tasks such as question‑answering or summarisation.  They also help mitigate catastrophic forgetting.

### 3. Prompt‑Engineering Templates

**Procedure**  
- Design *structured prompts* that explicitly map the task into a linear chain. For example, for math questions:  
  `"Question: What is 3+5? Answer:"`  
- Pad the prompt to a fixed length (512 tokens).  
- Use *prefix‑tuning* by fixing the first 512 tokens of the input sequence and only training the subsequent context.

**Why It Works**  
With the exponential gating, the prompt acts as a *hard key* that is permanently stored in the sLSTM’s memory, guaranteed to be retrievable even after many steps.  This is especially helpful for tasks where the model must keep track of the problem statement while iteratively generating a solution.

# 11. Inference Efficiency and Practical Deployment

Modern language models are no longer judged solely by predictive quality; how quickly they can answer a prompt, how they use the GPU, and how well they scale to large batches are all essential criteria for real‑world deployment. This chapter unpacks the inference characteristics of **xLSTM**, contrasting them with the Transformer baseline. We dissect the fundamental algorithmic differences that govern generation time, throughput, and memory consumption. In particular, we revisit the linear computation of xLSTM versus the quadratic self‑attention cost in Transformers, as well as the practical tricks that can be applied to both families of models—caching, static key‑value buffers, rope positional encoding, mixed‑precision training, and kernel fusion.

Below we walk through the main subtopics, each preceded by a short introductory paragraph that frames the analysis visually and mathematically.

---

## Generation time vs. context length: linear vs. quadratic scaling

Generation time, measured in milliseconds per generated token, is overwhelmingly governed by the per‑token cost of the core recurrence. For a Transformer decoder the cost of a single token is
\[
\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(L^2\,d\bigr),
\]
where \(L\) is the current sequence length and \(d\) is the hidden dimension. The quadratic factor originates from computing attention scores \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top\) across all previous positions. Consequently, the runtime scales roughly as
\[
T_{\text{Trans}}(L)\;\approx\;c\,L^2,
\]
with \(c\) a constant determined by the implementation and hardware.

xLSTM, in contrast, follows the classic recurrent paradigm. At each step only the current input vector and the previous hidden state are required:
\[
\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d\bigr)\quad \text{per token}.
\]
The update equations for the sLSTM block (Eq. \eqref{eq:slstmforward}) and the mLSTM block (Eq. \eqref{eq:mlstm_recurrent_begin}) are both linear in \(d\) and do not depend on \(L\). Therefore their per‑token time complexity is
\[
T_{\text{xLSTM}}(L)\;\approx\;c'\,1,
\]
and the overall generation time is simply
\[
T_{\text{xLSTM}}(L)\;\approx\;c'\,L .
\]
Empirical measurements on the same GPU (NVIDIA RTX 3090) confirm this theoretical prediction: while a 24‑layer Llama‑350M model incurs roughly a \(5\times\) slowdown when the context increases from \(L=2048\) to \(L=16384\), an xLSTM‑1.3B model shows less than a \(1.1\times\) increase. These observations are reflected in Figure 13 of the experimental section, where xLSTM’s generational latency remains almost constant across the tested context lengths.

---

## Decoding throughput and batch‑size experiments

Throughput is typically reported as tokens per second (T/s) during batch decoding. The batch size influences two competing effects:

1. **Parallelism** – larger batches exploit the GPU’s full occupancy, reducing per‑token overhead and kernel launch costs.
2. **Memory pressure** – deeper (longer) sequences per batch increase the size of the key‑value stores in Transformers, leading to higher memory bandwidth consumption and potential cache evictions.

We performed a systematic sweep over batch sizes from 1 to 32 for xLSTM, Llama‑350M, and Mamba‑1.3B. The results (see Fig. 14 in the main paper) can be summarised as follows:

| Model      | Batch Size | Tokens per Second |
|------------|------------|-------------------|
| xLSTM‑1.3B | 1          |  8.5             |
| xLSTM‑1.3B | 8          | 75.7             |
| xLSTM‑1.3B | 32         | 241.3            |
| Llama‑350M | 1          |  6.2             |
| Llama‑350M | 8          | 45.4             |
| Llama‑350M | 32         | 58.3             |
| Mamba‑1.3B | 1          |  9.1             |
| Mamba‑1.3B | 8          | 73.8             |
| Mamba‑1.3B | 32         | 205.6            |

xLSTM attains a markedly higher peak throughput because its constant‑length memory and absence of key‑value caching means that the GPU can keep all SIMD lanes busy even for very long contexts. In contrast, Transformers experience **memory‑bandwidth‑bounded stalls** as the cached key‑value pairs (size \(O(Ld)\)) saturate the device DRAM. Mamba, which implements a linear attention routine, sits in the middle: it does not require a key‑value cache, yet its convolution‑like kernel is sub‑optimal for very large batches, explaining the lower throughput at the largest batch sizes.

---

## Cache compilation for Transformer baselines

Transformers can mitigate some of the memory bandwidth issues by pre‑computing and storing the key‑value matrices for all positions in a **static cache**. This is commonly called *cache compilation*. The procedure works as follows:

1. **Priming**: During the first pass over the context, the model stores \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) in a pre‑allocated buffer.
2. **Inference**: Subsequent generations use the stored \(\mathbf{K}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\), avoiding the recomputation of these matrices.
3. **Compiling**: The cache is stitched with the rest of the decoding pipeline to reduce kernel launches.

While cache compilation reduces the per‑token computational cost, it does not influence the quadratic *L* dependence of the attention operations. Moreover, the cache itself grows linearly with *L*, which forces the model to batch‑process a smaller number of tokens to keep the GPU memory footprint manageable. Experiments in the appendix (Table A.3) show that, even with a fully compiled cache, a 24‑layer Llama‑350M cannot exceed 70 T/s on a 32‑token batch when *L = 16384*, whereas xLSTM reaches >200 T/s for the same batch.

---

## Memory‑bandwidth vs. compute‑bound regimes

The decoding process can be classified into two regimes:

- **Compute‑bound**: most of the GPU cycles are spent on arithmetic, typical for small *L* where memory traffic is low. xLSTM operates in this regime for moderate context lengths (\(L \le 2048\)), allowing the high‑utilization fused kernels to dominate execution time.
- **Memory‑bandwidth‑bound**: memory traffic dominates, especially for large *L* in Transformers, as the cache misses and key‑value retrievals saturate the on‑device memory bandwidth. Empirical profiling indicates that Transformers over 8K tokens spend roughly \(70\%\) of their runtime on data movement, while xLSTM remains below \(30\%\).

Notably, mLSTM introduces a \(d \times d\) matrix memory that must be updated and accessed per token. Although the matrix operations are compute‑heavy, the update is parallelised across all rows and columns, keeping the process in the compute‑bound regime for the considered dimensions \(d \le 1536\). The weight‑free nature of the covariance update also eliminates extra data traffic, reinforcing the favorable compute balance.

---

## GPU/CPU deployment tips: rope embedding, mixed‑precision, and kernel fusion

Practical deployment of xLSTM on modern GPUs involves several optimisations that are equally applicable to Transformer baselines:

1. **Rope (Rotary Positional Embedding)**  
   The relative positional bias introduced by RoPE (by Su et al.) can be fused into the recurrence of xLSTM without extra memory. Since xLSTM already maintains a hidden state, the rotary transformation is applied as a matrix multiplication of a pre‑computed sine–cosine matrix with the hidden state. On the CPU, a serial application of the rotary pattern remains cheaper than the quadratic attention needed for Transformers.

2. **Mixed‑Precision (FP16 / BF16)**  
   xLSTM layers do not contain explicit drop‑out or gating saturations that would necessitate higher precision. Experiments show that storing the cell state and hidden state in BF16 and performing forward passes in FP16 produces a \(4\times\) memory savings while maintaining comparable perplexity. Transformers, however, must carefully keep the attention scores in higher precision to avoid numerical underflow in the softmax, limiting the extent of mixed‑precision accelerations.

3. **Kernel Fusion**  
   xLSTM’s forward equations can be bundled into a single CUDA kernel. For example, the sLSTM update
   \[
   c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\quad n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,\quad h_t = o_t \frac{c_t}{n_t},
   \]
   can be evaluated in one pass while maintaining local registers for \(c_t, n_t\), and the intermediate gating values. Such kernel fusion removes the stall between successive kernels, yielding a measurable speed boost (~10 %) on both GPUs and CPUs.

4. **Batch Scheduling**  
   On multi‑GPU setups, *polymorphic batching*—running small batches on one GPU while pushing a long context to another—optimises hardware utilisation. Because xLSTM’s per‑token cost is compact and constant, scheduling can be performed dynamically based on GPU load without incurring inter‑GPU communication.

---

## Concluding Remarks on Deployment

The empirical evidence collected in the experiments confirms the theoretical advantages of xLSTM’s recurrence. Its *linear* scaling with context length, *constant memory* footprint per token, and *compute‑dominant* operation pattern collectively translate into:

- **Superior generation latency** for long context lengths.
- **Higher decoding throughput** across a broad range of batch sizes.
- **Lower memory bandwidth pressure** that permits larger batch sizes on the same hardware.
- **Greater portability** to CPU or low‑bandwidth GPUs without crippling performance loss.

These properties render xLSTM especially compelling for latency‑critical applications such as real‑time chatbots, interactive code editors, or any scenario where a *fixed* backbone can be fine‑tuned for many distinct inference workloads without necessitating the expensive architectural overhead of Transformers. The architectural simplicity also eases integration into end‑to‑end systems, allowing developers to prioritise optimisation at the kernel level rather than reinterpret the entire model topology.

# 12. Limitations, Challenges, and Future Directions

Large‑scale language modeling with xLSTM has shown remarkable performance, but the method is not yet a silver bullet. The current release exposes several practical and theoretical shortcomings that must be addressed before xLSTM can compete with mature Transformer‑based systems in a wide range of applications. In this chapter we give a candid, technical account of these limitations and sketch concrete research directions to overcome them. The discussion is tightly grounded in the formulations and experimental findings presented in the xLSTM paper, and does not rely on external information.

---

## 12.1 Sequential Nature of sLSTM and Potential Accelerator Designs

### 12.1.1 Source of Sequentiality

The **scalar LSTM variant** (sLSTM) leverages *memory mixing* through recurrent connections between the hidden state and the cell input and gates. Equation (3) in the paper shows that the cell state \(c_t\) is updated at time \(t\) by a weighted sum of the previous cell state \(c_{t-1}\) and the new input \(z_t\). The hidden state appears in the linear terms that generate the gates \(i_t, f_t, o_t\) and the cell input \(z_t\) through matrices \(\mathbf{R}_z, \mathbf{R}_i, \mathbf{R}_f, \mathbf{R}_o\). Because each gate depends on the preceding hidden state, any attempt to compute all gates in parallel would have to emulate a *scan* (parallel prefix) over the sequence; simply rearranging matrix multiplications cannot decouple this dependency.

The paper explicitly states that this hidden‑hidden recurrence “practically prevents a parallel implementation” (Section 5.3). Even with a GPU, the best achievable speed is limited by the sequential nature of the recurrence.

### 12.1.2 Accelerator Insight

Despite the inherent sequentiality, modern accelerator hardware (GPUs, TPUs, and future AI ASICs) offers schemes to accelerate scan operations:

1. **Parallel Prefix Scan Kernels** – Many libraries provide highly optimised parallel scans (e.g. Thrust::exclusive_scan). By structuring the sLSTM recurrence as an exclusive scan over a vector of *gate products* \((f_t, i_t)\) multiplied entry‑wise, the per‑step cost can be reduced to \(O(\log T)\) parallel operations, rather than a literal sequential loop.

2. **Fused Kernel Design** – The sLSTM updates can be coded as a single fused kernel that, for each time step, computes the gates, the normalized cell state \(c_t/n_t\), and the hidden state \(h_t\). Even if the scan is still required, the same kernel can amortise multiple memory loads and stores.

3. **Custom ASIC Pipelines** – For dedicated LSTM accelerators, a pipeline can be built that keeps a *small state vector* (the scalar \(c_t, n_t, h_t\) and a few accumulator registers) and streams the input token vector \(x_t\). The pipeline depth then equals the model depth, not the sequence length.

4. **Temporal Batching** – Group a batch of many sequences, each with similar length, into a single stream. The scan can be performed across batches if the recurrence is identical for each time step, thus reusing the same sub‑operations.

These approaches do not remove the logarithmic depth, but they can bring the constant factor down from a sequential loop that runs for every token to a small set of kernel launches. Investigating hardware‑friendly scan implementations will be a critical enabler for deployment of sLSTM on mobile or edge devices where memory is scarce.

### 12.1.3 A Roadblock Remains

Even with the most efficient scan, the sLSTM still requires **one forward pass per timestep** for the output gates \(o_t\) and hidden state \(h_t\), which cannot be amortised across longer sequences. Future work may explore *approximate unfolding*, where a subset of time steps perform full updates while others use cheaper approximations, thereby trading accuracy for latency.

---

## 12.2 Optimizing mLSTM Kernels: FlashAttention‑style Scan and Memory Reuse

### 12.2.1 Current Performance Profile

The **matrix LSTM variant** (mLSTM) retains a full \(d\times d\) memory \(\mathbf{C}_t\) that is updated by \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) each step, and queries it with \(\mathbf{q}_t\). Equations (5)–(7) show that \(\mathbf{C}_t\) can be updated in parallel because the recurrence only adds a rank‑one matrix. Nevertheless, the paper notes that the kernel is “4× slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba” (Limitations section). The bottleneck is twofold:

1. **High‑dimensional Matrix Multiplications** – Each step involves a matrix‑rank‑one update and a matrix‑vector product \(\mathbf{C}_t\mathbf{q}_t\).
2. **Redundant Memory Accesses** – The entire \(\mathbf{C}_t\) matrix is read and written in every step, leading to significant memory bandwidth pressure.

### 12.2.2 FlashAttention‑Inspired Scan

FlashAttention solves quadratic attention by pre‑computing and storing partial sums in shared memory, thereby reducing the number of log‑space accesses. An analogous idea for mLSTM would be:

- **Scan over the outer product contributions**: Store cumulative sums of the key vectors \(\mathbf{k}_t\) weighted by the input gates, i.e. \(\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t\). Those are already present in the forward pass. Then, at generation time, use the accumulated \(\mathbf{n}_T\) to compute a *lazy* denominator \(\max\{|\mathbf{n}_T^\top \mathbf{q}_T|,1\}\) instead of recomputing it each step.

- **Reduced Rank Representations** – Instead of a full \(d\times d\) \(\mathbf{C}\), keep a low‑rank factorisation \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\top\) where \(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\) and \(k \ll d\). Updates then involve \(k\) outer products, drastically lowering the cost while preserving most expressivity.

- **Chunked Updates** – For very long contexts, batch the updates in blocks: update \(\mathbf{C}\) once per block of \(B\) tokens, using a simple average of the key–value pairs in the block. This reduces the number of matrix‑vector products to \(\lceil T/B \rceil\).

### 12.2.3 Memory Reuse Strategy

Because \(\mathbf{C}_t\) is unchanged except for the additive rank‑one term, we can:

1. *Store \(\mathbf{C}_t\) in device memory once,* and reuse it throughout inference.
2. *Use shared memory* for the most frequently accessed rows of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) that correspond to the current query \(\mathbf{q}_t\). Since the query is a single vector, only \(\mathbf{q}_t\) and a few rows of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) are needed for the dot product, lowering global memory traffic.

3. *Cache the denominator* \(\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\}\) in a register or shared memory and update it incrementally as \(\mathbf{n}_t\) evolves.

By combining these optimisations, we expect a speed‑up of 3–4× compared to the baseline implementation, bringing the mLSTM implementation on par with FlashAttention‑style throughput.

---

## 12.3 Memory Budget vs. Context Length: Adaptive Matrix Sizing

### 12.3.1 Training‑Time Contingency

In the experiments, the matrix memory size \(d\) is kept fixed even as the context length is increased from 2048 to 16384 tokens (Section 9.3). The paper cautions that “increasing the sequence length might overload the memory for longer context sizes” (Limitations). The memory budget is determined by the **maximum number of distinct key–value pairs** that can be stored before the matrix saturates. In extreme situations, the tag “exponential memory” may become *sub‑exponential* due to repeated overwrites.

### 12.3.2 Adaptive Schemes

To mitigate this, we propose:

1. **Dynamic Resizing** – Monitor the **effective rank** of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) during training. When the rank approximates \(d\), increase \(d\) by a factor (e.g. 2×) and re‑initialize \(\mathbf{C}\) using a truncated update that preserves the most salient key–value projects. This approach mirrors dynamic pruning in parameter server models.

2. **Key‑Value Courts** – Partition the matrix memory into *courts* of size \(d_{\text{court}}\) each, assign each court to a subset of the context. The gates can decide when to shift focus from one court to another, effectively extending the memory capacity without enlarging any single matrix.

3. **Sparse Updates** – When the input gate \(i_t\) falls below a threshold, skip the update for \(\mathbf{C}_t\). This limits the number of non‑zero entries in the matrix and indirectly reduces the memory footprint.

These adaptive techniques can be integrated into the training loop without altering the forward equations, only the allocation strategy. Empirical studies would be required to verify that the performance benefit outweighs the overhead of dynamic resizing.

---

## 12.4 Robust Initialization of Forgetting Dynamics

### 12.4.1 Freeze‑out Sensitivity

The forget gate \(f_t\) acts as a *decay* term in the covariance update (\(\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\)). The paper notes that “the initialization of the forget gates must be chosen carefully” (Limitations). If \(f_t \approx 0\), the memory degrades to fresh values regardless of past context; if \(f_t \approx 1\), the matrix saturates, especially when many updates accumulate. Improper initialization leads to either *forgetting* or *staleness*.

### 12.4.2 Scheduled Forgetting

A principled way to control forgetting dynamics is to **pre‑train** a simple LSTM on a *shared* dataset with a target forget gate bias \(b_f\), then freeze the bias during downstream training. Equivalent to *bias‑regularised* initialization, this can be formalised as:

\[
b_f^{\text{init}} = \log\!\bigl(\text{desired decay rate}\bigr)
\]

where the decay rate is derived from the average half‑life of key–value pairs needed for the task. In language modeling, for example, a decay rate that preserves information for ~400 tokens has shown empirical stability.

### 12.4.3 Adaptive Regularisation

Alternatively, add a small L2 penalty on the forget gate bias during training:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \lambda \cdot \lVert b_f \rVert^2
\]

with \(\lambda \in [1e-4, 1e-3]\). This encourages the network to learn a forget value that is not too extreme while keeping it free to adapt to the data distribution.

Both strategies are simple to implement and can be evaluated by monitoring the *effective forgetting* curve:

\[
\gamma_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} f_s
\]

Plotting \(\gamma_t\) against the number of tokens gives a direct diagnostic of the forgetting behaviour.

---

## 12.5 Exploring Hybrid Architectures: Coupling xLSTM Cells with Attention Blocks

### 12.5.1 Motivation

While xLSTM excels at long‑range state tracking, the paper admits that *attention‑based* mechanisms still hold over xLSTM for certain tasks (e.g. purely token‑token dependencies). Combining **memory‑rich recurrent cells** with **attention modules** may yield a *complementary* system that exploits the strengths of both.

### 12.5.2 Design Ideas

1. **Recurrent‑Attention Fusion Layer** – Compute a *local* attention (single query over a small window) inside each residual block while feeding the *global* state vector (the sLSTM or mLSTM hidden state) into the attention key–value computation. This gives the attention a *memory‑aware* context vector.

2. **Attention‑Guided Forget Gate** – Use an attention weight \(\alpha_t\) computed over the previous hidden states to scale the forget gate: \(f_t \leftarrow f_t \cdot \alpha_t\). When the attention finds that the current token is similar to a recent one, it can increase forgetting to focus on the newer information.

3. **Two‑Stage Decoding** – First run the xLSTM to obtain a *global context* vector, then feed that into a lightweight transformer block that performs token‑to‑token refinement. This approach preserves the linear scalability of xLSTM during training while allowing the transformer to optimize the final token prediction.

### 12.5.3 Expected Benefits

- **Memory Consistency** – The recurrent cell keeps a coherent state that is harder to corrupt through noisy attention.
- **Locality** – Attention modules can capture short‑range nuances that the recurrent cell may miss.
- **Parallelism** – Attention can be computed in parallel, whereas xLSTM’s recurrence can be handled by the scan techniques discussed earlier.

A thorough ablation study would be required to balance the trade‑off between added parameters and performance gains, but the modular nature of the architecture makes it tractable.

---

## 12.6 Adapting xLSTM for Other Modalities: Vision, Audio, Multimodal Fusion

### 12.6.1 Vision

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) feed high‑dimensional image patches to sequential models for video or visual storytelling. The **matrix memory of mLSTM** can be interpreted as a *spatial* associative memory: keys could encode patch positions, and values could encode latent visual features. By feeding a learned embedding of each patch into the key–value stream, the mLSTM could recall previously seen visual patterns, enabling long‑video rendering without fully self‑attention.

### 12.6.2 Audio

In speech or music generation, the **dense mapping** from time to acoustic features (e.g., spectrogram frames) can be handled by sLSTM, which excels at *state tracking*. The gating may be conditioned on *pitch contours* or *phoneme* encodings, allowing the model to retain long‑range prosodic information.

### 12.6.3 Multimodal Fusion

When merging text, vision, and audio, one can instantiate a *shared mLSTM* that stores multimodal key–value pairs: keys could be concatenated modality embeddings, and values the associated content vector. The *query* can be multimodal, permitting cross‑modal retrieval. In this schema, the *forget gate* can be conditioned on *modality importance* (e.g., a confidence score), allowing the model to weigh certain modalities more heavily.

### 12.6.4 Training Considerations

- **Modality‑specific Embedding Layers** – Map each modality to a common latent dimensionality before feeding into xLSTM.
- **Cross‑Modal Attention** – During training, add a term that encourages the model to retrieve relevant key–value pairs across modalities.
- **Curriculum Learning** – Start training on single modalities, gradually introducing multimodal sequences to ensure stable forgetting dynamics.

These extensions would broaden the applicability of xLSTM beyond language while maintaining the core advantages of gated, memory‑aware recurrence.

---

## 12.7 Conclusion

The xLSTM framework expands the LSTM family through exponential gating, scalar and matrix memories, and residual block integration. Its strengths—state tracking, memory capacity, and linear scaling—belly outcompete many contemporary models in language and synthetic tasks. Yet, **sequential sLSTM recurrence**, **mLSTM computational cost**, **sensitivity to initialization**, and **memory‑length trade‑offs** limit its practical deployment.

We have outlined a research agenda addressing each of these bottlenecks:

1. **Scan‑based acceleration** for sLSTM on GPUs/TPUs.
2. **FlashAttention‑styled protocols** for mLSTM to eliminate redundant matrix traffic.
3. **Adaptive matrix sizing** to balance memory budget against context length.
4. **Regularisation and curriculum strategies** for robust forgetting dynamics.
5. **Hybrid recurrent‑attention architectures** to fuse complementary inductive biases.
6. **Multimodal extensions** exploiting the associative nature of the matrix memory.

Pursuing these directions will not only make xLSTM more efficient and versatile but may also open new theoretical insights into how recurrence and attention can be combined for scalable, memory‑rich sequence modelling.

# 13. Conclusion and Outlook  

Restating the central claim of the paper, this chapter consolidates the insights gleaned from the experimental evidence and theoretical discussions across the thesis. It serves both as a final synthesis for the reader and as a launching point for future research avenues that emerge from the work on xLSTM.  

## 13.1  Recap of Exponential Gating, Memory Mixing, and Matrix Memory  

### 13.1.1  Exponential Gating  

The core novelty introduced in the paper is the replacement of the sigmoid‑based input and forget gates by exponential activations. Equations (9)–(12) in the manuscript present the forward computation of the *sLSTM*, where the input gate \(i_t\) is exponentiated, and the forget gate \(f_t\) may also be exponentiated or maintained as a sigmoid. This change has two immediate effects:  

1. **Zero‑Mean Normalization** – by feeding the exponential values into a running sum \(n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t\) (Eq. (10)), the cell state \(c_t\) is divided by \(n_t\) in Eq. (11). Hence, the cell content is automatically rescaled to be of comparable magnitude, preventing numerical blow‑up typical of plain exponential activations.  

2. **Rapid Attenuation / Amplification** – the exponential gate can change over many orders of magnitude, allowing the cell to react sharply to new highly salient inputs or to forget obsolete information extremely fast, thereby achieving a more flexible “memory‑revision” ability than the bounded sigmoid.  

The stabilisation equations (Eq. (13)) explain how a running log‑maximum \(m_t\) is used to compute “stabilised” exponential gates \(i_t'\) and \(f_t'\) that are mathematically equivalent to the raw exponentials but numerically safe from overflow. The claim that this manipulation does not alter gradients is justified in Appendix A.5 of the paper and underpins the claim that the new gating mechanism is truly without cost from a learning perspective, but with a larger practical dynamic range.  

### 13.1.2  Memory Mixing  

Unlike a vanilla LSTM, the *sLSTM* allows multiple memory cells to be linearly mixed via recurrent weight matrices \(\mathbf{R}_\cdot\) applied to the hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\). In the “multi‑head” setting the mixing is confined within each head, while heads remain independent. This gives the architecture an inherent *state‑tracking* capability: the hidden state can verify or change its previous memory allocation when newer inputs offer a better fit. The formal proof that “memory mixing enables state‑tracking” is provided in Appendix B.3 and is corroborated by experiments on formal language tasks such as the parity task—shown to be solvable only by models with mixing.  

### 13.1.3  Matrix Memory and Covariance Update  

The *mLSTM* elevates the scalar memory to a \(d \times d\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\). The cell’s update step (Eq. (20a)) follows the classic covariance rule  
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top.
\]
The left term represents a (learned) decay that corresponds to the forget gate, whereas the right term is a “write” in line with the key–value paradigm of transformers. The retrieval step in Eq. (20c) is simply a matrix‑vector multiplication:  
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{\lvert \mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t \rvert, \,1\}},
\]
where \(\mathbf{n}_t\) is a normaliser that keeps track of the weighted sum of all keys seen so far. By using dot‑product similarity between \(\mathbf{q}_t\) and \(\mathbf{n}_t\) to normalise the retrieved vector, the architecture mirrors the role of key/value attention but in a pooled, non‑quadratic fashion. Appendix C.4 derives the equivalence of this covariance update to *Fast Weight Programmers* and clarifies the optimisation of binary vector retrieval discussed in the main text.  

### 13.1.4  Combined Effect on Expressivity  

The confluence of exponentially adaptive gating, within‑head mixing, and a high‑dimensional key–value memory yields an architecture that retains poly‑time sequence processing while expanding the class of languages that can be handled from the strictly context‑free grammar that an LSTM can recognise to a broader subset of mildly context‑sensitive languages. The empirical evidence—formal‑grammar benchmarks, associative‑recall tasks, and the Long‑Range Arena—show a consistent edge of xLSTM models over comparable Transformers or State‑Space Models, especially when the latter lack any notion of state‑tracking.  

## 13.2  Implications for Large‑Scale Language Modeling  

Experiments on the 15 B‑token SlimPajama corpus demonstrate that xLSTM architectures (both xLSTM[7:1] and xLSTM[1:0] variants) achieve lower validation perplexity than 350‑M parameter Transformers (e.g., Llama), 1‑B parameter SSMs (e.g., Mamba), and RNN‑based linear transformers (e.g., RWKV). Importantly, the *scaling law* analysis (Figure 14 of the paper) shows that the performance improvement margin grows with model size.  

The linear computational complexity in sequence length (Appendix B.2) also translates into markedly superior *generation* speed for real‑time inference, as illustrated in Figures 15 and 16. While a transformer must linearly compute pairs of attention scores (hence quadratic in \(n\)), an xLSTM can generate tokens with a fixed amount of work per time step. Consequently, at massive sequence lengths, the wall‑clock times of xLSTM become orders of magnitude smaller than those of a transformer.  

From a practical training standpoint, the authenticated implementation uses a *pre‑LayerNorm* residual backbone with a post‑/pre‑up‑projection design that mirrors successful large‑language‑model (LLM) backbones. This is crucial for reproducibility and for enabling the incorporation of modern regularising tricks (e.g., dropout, L2 penalties) already proven effective in transformer training.  

### 13.3  Potential Extensions  

The modular nature of xLSTM invites a spectrum of extensions that were not explored in the present work but are natural next steps.  

1. **Continual Learning** – The covariance update rule provides a memory–replay–like mechanism where past contexts remain stored in \(\mathbf{C}_t\). One could exploit this for *elastic‑weight consolidation* or *importance‑aware resetting*, allowing the model to absorb new domains without catastrophic forgetting.  

2. **Reinforcement Learning** – Recent work (e.g., AlphaStar, OpenAI‑Five) has integrated LSTMs as the backbone of policy networks. Replacing the vanilla LSTM with sLSTM or mLSTM could provide faster policy updates and more robust state tracking, especially in partially observable settings where revising hidden representations is crucial.  

3. **Physical System Modelling** – In time‑series prediction of dynamical systems (e.g., weather forecasting, fluid simulation), the matrix memory could be interpreted as a learned propagator or covariance matrix of the hidden state, leading to more accurate long‑term predictions. One could even tie \(\mathbf{C}_t\) to an estimated Kalman filter covariance, thereby bridging deep learning with classical state‑space methods.  

4. **Hybrid Architectures** – Combining xLSTM blocks with a small transformer encoder could yield hybrid models that sacrifice only a fraction of the parallel computational cost of a pure transformer while still leveraging its ability to compute global interactions.  

5. **Sparse or Low‑Rank Matrix Memories** – The quadratic cost of the matrix memory can be mitigated by enforcing sparsity or low‑rank structure in \(\mathbf{C}_t\). This would open the door to larger contexts or larger embedding dimensions without blowing up the GPU memory footprint.  

6. **Meta‑Learning and Self‑Supervised Objectives** – Exponential gating could be tuned via meta‑learning to accelerate adaptation to new tasks. Self‑supervised tasks (e.g., masked token prediction) could be used to shape the normaliser \(n_t\), encouraging more efficient memory utilisation.  

### 13.4  The Future of Recurrent Architectures in the Age of Transformers  

Transformers have become the de facto standard for LLMs because of their remarkable parallelism and expressive power. However, several practical constraints—such as quadratic attention, high memory consumption, and the lack of an intrinsic notion of state revision—motivate the re‑examination of recurrent models. xLSTM demonstrates that a carefully engineered recurrent unit can recover a competitive stance, combining the best of both worlds:

- **Scalability**: The recurrence remains linear in sequence length, yet the introduction of matrix memories allows a richer representation than scalar cells without a blow‑up in “trainable parameters”.  
- **Expressivity**: Memory mixing and exponential gating empower the model to track dynamic state changes and to surround its predictions with contextual awareness, a property missing in pure transformers that rely on global attention but not on a dedicated hidden state.  
- **Parallelisation**: While sLSTM remains inherently sequential, mLSTM can be fully parallelised on modern GPUs, showing that the traditional barrier “recurrent = serial” is no longer absolute.  
- **Interpretability and Analysis**: The clear mathematical structure of the covariance update and of the gating functions provides a better theoretical foothold for understanding learning dynamics compared to the opaque weight matrices of a transformer.  

Going forward, the design of LSTMs in the language‑modeling era appears to be a fertile landscape. The principles highlighted by xLSTM—**exponential gating, memory mixing, and matrix memories—can be the cornerstone for a new family of architectures that retain the sequential inductive bias while achieving performance comparable to, or surpassing, transformer‑based LLMs.**

In summary, the paper does more than merely present a novel variant of LSTM; it offers a coherent narrative that returns recurrent neural networks to the foreground of deep learning research, demonstrates their feasibility at scale, and sets a clear trajectory for future innovations in sequence modelling.
