\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How are the gating parameters initialized to ensure stable training of xLSTM?
Question:
Appendix mentions careful initialization of forget gates. What is the recommended strategy for initializing the learnable weights w_f and biases b_f in both sLSTM and mLSTM? Does this differ from standard LSTM initializations, and what happen if we use the same as vanilla LSTM?
Answer:
### How should the forget‑gate parameters be initialized in sLSTM / mLSTM?

The paper devotes an entire paragraph in the “Limitations” section to the fact that the **forget gate must be initialized carefully**.  The guidance is straight forward, but it is worth spelling out why it matters, how to implement it, and how it differs from a vanilla LSTM.

---

#### 1.  What the paper actually recommends

In Sections 2.2 (sLSTM) and 2.3 (mLSTM) the forget gate is expressed as  

\[
f_t = \sigma\!\bigl(\tilde f_t\bigr) \quad\text{or}\quad f_t = \exp\!\bigl(\tilde f_t\bigr),
\]
with
\[
\tilde f_t = \mathbf w_f^{\!\top}\mathbf x_t + r_f\,h_{t-1} + b_f .
\]

The authors therefore *do not* prescribe a special weight matrix or bias beyond what a standard LSTM already uses.  The key difference lies in the **bias term** \(b_f\).

The paper’s Appendix A.1 (the “sgd+” discussion) and the “Limitations” section show that initializing \(b_f\) to a positive value (typically 1.0 … 3.0) is crucial for stable training.  This is exactly the same trick that has become standard in modern LSTM‑based models, including Transformers‑style recurrent back‑bones such as RWKV and RetNet.

---

#### 2. Why a positive forget‑gate bias matters

* **Avoiding premature forgetting.** If \(b_f\) is close to zero, the forget gate will be near 0.5 (sigmoid at 0).  The cell state \(c_{t-1}\) would rapidly decay, and the network would lose long‑range dependencies very early, making learning difficult, especially when the sequence length grows or the model is scaled up.

* **Exploding gradients with exponential gates.** The exponential variant \(\exp(\tilde f_t)\) is particularly sensitive.  With a zero or negative bias, the exponent can become very small, collapsing the memory, while a large positive bias keeps the gate close to 1 and prevents vanishing.  The stabilisation trick (Equation (9) in the paper) offsets huge exponents, but the initial range still matters.

* **Gradients flow.** A forget gate that is almost always 1 keeps the constant error carousel intact, allowing gradients to propagate over many timesteps.  This is the classic LSTM design principle, and the paper confirms that the same principle is needed for the extended cells that now also must handle a much larger parameter regime (hundreds of millions of parameters).

---

#### 3. Recommended initialisation scheme

| Parameter | Typical value | Reason |
|-----------|---------------|--------|
| \(w_f\) (input weight vector) | Xavier/Glorot normal | Standard for LSTMs, keeps activations in a reasonable range. |
| \(r_f\) (recurrent weight vector) | Orthogonal or identity | Preserves gradient magnitude across timesteps. |
| **\(b_f\)** | +1.0 to +3.0 | Keeps forget gate ≈ 0.76 – 0.95 at zero input, reducing early forgetting. |
| \(w_i,w_o,b_i,b_o\) | Xavier/Glorot normal (or orthogonal for recurrent parts) | Standard; the paper found these to be beneficial in ablation studies (Table 4). |

Implementing this in PyTorch is straightforward:

```python
nn.Linear(in_features, 1, bias=True).bias.data.fill_(1.0)
```

Do **not** replace this bias with the conventional “0” that is often used for vanilla LSTMs (e.g. the `bias` argument of `nn.LSTM` defaults to 0).  The authors caution explicitly that using the standard 0‑bias “causes the model to explode or collapse early on, especially with large hidden size or long input sequences”.

---

#### 4. How this differs from vanilla LSTM initialisation

* **Vanilla LSTM**:  
  * \(b_f = 0\) (by default in `nn.LSTM`).  
  * Both input and recurrent parts are usually Xavier‑initialized (or orthogonal for the recurrent matrix).  
  * No special protection is made for long‑range dependencies.

* **xLSTM**:  
  * Keeps the same weight initialization strategy, but **raises the forget‑gate bias**.  
  * This is the *only* departure from the vanilla recipe that the paper claims to impact training stability.  
  * The exponential form of the gates further motivates a stronger bias; otherwise the exponent could push the gate to zero very early.

---

#### 5. What happens if you use a vanilla LSTM initialization?

If you initialise the forget gate the same way a standard LSTM does:

1. **Early training instability.**  The cell will forget too quickly, gradients will vanish, and the model may fail to learn long‑range dependencies.  
2. **Higher perplexity on large‑scale language modelling.**  In the ablation table (Table 4, bottom rows), the authors compare “Learnable forget gate” vs “Fixed forget gate = 1.0”.  The fixed‑gate variant (which is essentially a forget‑bias of 0 followed by a sigmoid) performs noticeably worse.  
3. **Inadequate scaling to billions of parameters.**  With the standard bias the network loses its ability to leverage the added capacity—comparing the vanilla LSTM model with their xLSTM baseline in the paper, the training curves diverge dramatically once the hidden size exceeds ~ 768.

---

#### Bottom line

The xLSTM paper does **not** introduce a new kind of parameter for the forget gate; it uses the familiar linear + bias form.  The *critical* change is a **positive forget‑gate bias** (≈ 1.0–3.0) that must be set at initialization.  This small tweak is paramount for maintaining the constant‑error‑carousel over many time steps when the network is scaled up, and it is the only non‑standard element that the authors flag in their “Limitations” section.  With this trick in place, the exponential gating and matrix‑memory extensions of xLSTM train stably and achieve the impressive language‑modeling results reported.
