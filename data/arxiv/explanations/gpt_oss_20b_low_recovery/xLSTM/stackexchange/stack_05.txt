\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is the stabilizer state m_t defined as a max of log‑gate terms?
Question:
Equation (7) defines m_t = max(log(f_t)+m_{t‑1}, log(i_t)). This seems reminiscent of a log‑sum‑exp trick, but with a max instead. What numerical instability does it avert, and how does the subsequent re‑exponentiation in (8) guarantee that the overall forward pass remains unchanged?
Answer:
In the sLSTM (and in the mLSTM) the input and forget gates are allowed to grow exponentially.  
Historically the LSTM gates are bounded by the sigmoid, but the authors want the gates to range over \((0,\infty)\) so that a “forget‑gate decay’’ can be larger than one and an “input‑gate learning‑rate’’ can be arbitrarily large.  
When an exponential of a large pre‑activation is taken, the raw gate value can easily overflow a 32‑bit float (and under‑flow to zero otherwise).  Moreover, a very large gate value will dominate the recurrence, producing a cell state that is the product of many such terms and quickly blowing up or vanishing.  

To avoid these numerical catastrophes the authors keep a **log‑domain sum** that represents the cumulative effect of all previous forget gates and the most recent input gate.  The stabilizer state is defined as  

\[
m_t \;=\; \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr).
\]

This is exactly the same as the classic “log‑sum‑exp’’ trick used to add numbers that are very large or very small, except that here we only need the *max* because the recurrence contains a product:  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t \;=\; e^{\,m_{t-1}}\bigl(e^{\,\tilde f_t}c_{t-1} + e^{\,\tilde i_t}z_t\bigr),
\]

so that the terms inside the parentheses are on a comparable scale.  By storing the running log‑sum \(m_t\) we can rewrite the gates as

\[
i'_t \;=\; \exp(\log i_t - m_t) \;=\; \exp(\tilde i_t - m_t), \qquad
f'_t \;=\; \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

Because \(m_t\) is the *maximum* of the two log‐terms, at least one of \(i'_t\) or \(f'_t\) will be exactly \(1\) (or numerically close to it) while the other will be a number in \((0,1]\).  This guarantees that the updated gates never explode or collapse to zero; the product of a series of such stabilised gates remains numerically well‑behaved.

Re‑exponentiating these stabilised gates is safe because the following identity holds algebraically for the cell update:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t
&= f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t                              \\
&= e^{m_{t-1}}\bigl(e^{\,\tilde f_t}c_{t-1} + e^{\,\tilde i_t}z_t\bigr) \\
&= e^{m_t}\Bigl( e^{\,m_{t-1}-m_t}\,e^{\,\tilde f_t}c_{t-1}
                + e^{\,m_t-m_t}e^{\,\tilde i_t}z_t \Bigr)   \\
&= e^{m_t}\bigl( f'_t\,c_{t-1} + i'_t\,z_t \bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]

and, because \(m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\log i_t)\), the two terms in the maximum are exactly the two terms that appear inside the parentheses.  Thus the factor \(e^{m_t}\) is the same across all time steps and can be absorbed into a global scaling of the cell state.  The normaliser \(n_t\) (Eq. (6)) is updated with the same stabilised gates, so the ratio \(c_t / n_t\) – which is the quantity fed to the activation \(\psi\) – is unchanged.  Consequently the forward output \(h_t\) is identical to the un‑stabilised version, and gradients with respect to parameters remain the same because the replacement is an exact algebraic equivalence, not a numerical approximation.

In short, the *max* in \(m_t\) prevents any component from becoming too large or too small, keeping all intermediate values in a safe numerical range.  The re‑exponentiation simply rewrites the recurrence in a numerically stable form without altering the mathematical behaviour of the network.
