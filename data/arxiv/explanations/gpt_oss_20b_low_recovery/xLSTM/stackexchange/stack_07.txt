\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What exactly does 'memory mixing' mean in the context of sLSTM?
Question:
The paper states that new sLSTM can mix memory across cells via recurrent matrices R_z, R_i, R_f, R_o, and that memory mixing is a new feature. Could someone formalize what operations are performed on the cell state vector when multiple cells are present? How does this differ from the inter‑cell mixing used in standard multi‑dimensional LSTM?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In a multi‑cell sLSTM, the hidden‑to‑cell recurrent weights do not merely generate the gate‐inputs \(z_t,i_t,f_t,o_t\) from the previous hidden state; because the gates are exponential, each cell’s update also depends on the *full vector* of all other cells’ update terms.  Thus the cell‑state vector \(\mathbf c_t\in\mathbb R^d\) is updated by a *non‑linear, coupling* operation that mixes the states of all cells at once, whereas in the classic multi‑dimensional LSTM the recurrent matrices only linearly mix the *linear* pre‑activations of the gates before the sigmoid or tanh.

Below is a more detailed but concise explanation, with the key equations from the paper, and a comparison to the standard multi‑ dimensional LSTM (MD‑LSTM).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  What “memory mixing” means in sLSTM

For a one‑layer sLSTM with \(d\) scalar cells, let

* \(\mathbf c_{t-1}\in\mathbb R^{d}\) – previous cell state vector  
* \(\mathbf h_{t-1}\in\mathbb R^{d}\) – previous hidden state (usually \(\mathbf h_{t-1}=\mathbf o_{t-1}\odot\psi(\mathbf c_{t-1})\))

The input‑generated pre‑activations are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf z}_t &= W_z\,\mathbf x_t + \mathbf b_z,\\
\tilde{\mathbf i}_t &= W_i\,\mathbf x_t + \mathbf b_i,\\
\tilde{\mathbf f}_t &= W_f\,\mathbf x_t + \mathbf b_f,\\
\tilde{\mathbf o}_t &= W_o\,\mathbf x_t + \mathbf b_o,
\end{aligned}
\]
where \(W_{\bullet}\in\mathbb R^{d\times m}\).

The *recurrent* part uses four **diagonal** matrices (one per gate)  
\(\mathbf R_{\bullet}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) (typically implemented as learned scalars per cell).  
The recurrence formulas in the paper (Eq. (3) with Eq. (4) for the exponential gates) read

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{\mathbf z}_t + \mathbf R_z\,\mathbf h_{t-1}), \\
\mathbf i_t &= \exp(\tilde{\mathbf i}_t + \mathbf R_i\,\mathbf h_{t-1}), \\
\mathbf f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf f}_t + \mathbf R_f\,\mathbf h_{t-1}) \,\text{or}\,
            \exp(\tilde{\mathbf f}_t + \mathbf R_f\,\mathbf h_{t-1}),\\
\mathbf o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf o}_t + \mathbf R_o\,\mathbf h_{t-1}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because the gates are exponentiated (or masked by the sigmoid), the *effective* update for the cell state is

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf c_t \;=\; \mathbf f_t \odot \mathbf c_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf i_t \odot \mathbf z_t\;}
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise product.

Now, note that \(\mathbf i_t,\mathbf f_t,\mathbf z_t\) themselves involve \(\mathbf h_{t-1}\).  Since \(\mathbf h_{t-1}= \mathbf o_{t-1}\odot \psi(\mathbf c_{t-1})\) contains all components of \(\mathbf c_{t-1}\), the recurrence in (1) **mixes all cells together in a single, non‑linear update**.  The mixing is *not* a linear combination of the previous cell states; it is a *multiplied* and *exponentiated* interaction that allows one cell’s gate curve to be steered by the current hidden activation of every other cell.

The authors call this operation **memory mixing** because the cell‑state vector at time \(t\) is constructed from a *mix* of the previous full vector \(\mathbf c_{t-1}\) (via \(\mathbf f_t\)) and the current cell inputs \(\mathbf z_t\), both of which have been **mixed** by the hidden‑to‑cell recurrent matrices.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Contrast with the multi‑dimensional LSTM (MD‑LSTM)

In a standard MD‑LSTM (see Greff et al., 2015), the recurrence for a vector of \(d\) cells is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf z}_t &= W_z \mathbf x_t + U_z \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_z,\\
\tilde{\mathbf i}_t &= W_i \mathbf x_t + U_i \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_i,\\
\tilde{\mathbf f}_t &= W_f \mathbf x_t + U_f \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_f,\\
\tilde{\mathbf o}_t &= W_o \mathbf x_t + U_o \mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_o,
\end{aligned}
\]
followed by the usual sigmoid/tanh activations and linear state update

\[
\mathbf c_t = \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf f}_t)\odot\mathbf c_{t-1} + \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf i}_t)\odot \tanh(\tilde{\mathbf z}_t).
\]

Here the recurrent matrices \(U_{\bullet}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) **only mix the pre‑activations** before the activation functions.  After the gates are applied individually to each cell, the cell‑state update (the “Add‑and‑Forget” rule) remains a *point‑wise* operation on the vector.  In other words, the mixing is linear and *pre‑gate*.

In contrast, sLSTM mixes **after** the gates have been applied (thanks to the exponential gates) and *non‑linearly* (multiplicatively).  This gives the model the ability to rearrange information across cells on the fly; one cell can *completely overwrite* or *override* another by letting its input gate grow while another’s forget gate shrinks.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Intuition: why the difference matters

* **State‑tracking capability.**  The authors show (Sec. 4.1) that sLSTM with memory mixing solves state‑tracking tasks that baseline transformers or MD‑LSTMs fail on.  This is because the exponential gates can make forgetting *exactly* zero and remembering *exactly* one, while the mixing lets a single “key” cell focus all the information when it appears, and other cells quickly forget irrelevant history.

* **Non‑linear capacity increase.**  With \(d\) cells, a classic LSTM can represent at most a \(d\)-dimensional linear subspace of histories.  sLSTM effectively creates a highly non‑linear manifold of states because each cell’s contribution can be exponentiated and intertwined with all other cells’ activations.

* **Parallelization constraints.**  MD‑LSTM’s linear mixing is a simple matrix multiplication and thus trivially parallelizable.  sLSTM’s memory mixing (exponential and element‑wise product with a mixed hidden vector) breaks this parallelism; the paper notes this as a trade‑off for the expressive power.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  Formal recap of the operations for \(d\) cells

1. **Compute gate pre‑activations** with input and hidden recurrence:
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf g}_{t} = W_g \mathbf x_t + \mathbf R_g\,\mathbf h_{t-1} + \mathbf b_g
   \quad (g\in\{z,i,f,o\}).
   \]
2. **Apply activation (exponential for \(i,f\))**:
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   \mathbf z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{\mathbf z}_t),\\
   \mathbf i_t &= \exp(\tilde{\mathbf i}_t),\\
   \mathbf f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf f}_t)\text{ or }\exp(\tilde{\mathbf f}_t),\\
   \mathbf o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{\mathbf o}_t).
   \end{aligned}
   \]
3. **Mix cell states**:
   \[
   \boxed{\;\mathbf c_t = \mathbf f_t \odot \mathbf c_{t-1} + \mathbf i_t \odot \mathbf z_t\;}
   \]
4. **Compute hidden state** (with normalizer \(n_t\) for stability):
   \[
   \mathbf h_t = \mathbf o_t \odot \frac{\mathbf c_t}{n_t},\qquad
   n_t = \mathbf f_t \odot n_{t-1} + \mathbf i_t.
   \]

The *coupling* comes from \(\mathbf h_{t-1} = \mathbf o_{t-1}\odot \psi(\mathbf c_{t-1})\) appearing in \(\tilde{\mathbf g}_{t}\).  Since \(\mathbf h_{t-1}\) is a full‑vector, every gate for every cell is influenced by all other cells’ previous hidden activations.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5.  Bottom line

- **Memory mixing in sLSTM** is the **element‑wise, exponentiated interaction** of the previous cell‑state vector (modulated by the forget gate) with a *mixed* set of cell‑inputs, where the mixing originates from recurrent connections that feed the hidden state back into every gate.  
- **Classic MD‑LSTM** only mixes **linearly before the gates**; the gate outputs stay independent across cells except for the simple point‑wise state update.  
- The new mixing gives sLSTM a stronger state‑tracking power at the cost of less parallelizability—exactly the trade‑off the paper stresses.

This formalisation should make it clear how the sLSTM differs from conventional multi‑dimensional LSTMs and why the authors call the operation “memory mixing”.
