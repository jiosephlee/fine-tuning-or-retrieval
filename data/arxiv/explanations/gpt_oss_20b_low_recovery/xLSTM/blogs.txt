# From LSTM to xLSTM: The Big Leap in Recurrent Modeling

## The LSTM legacy

Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) cells have been the backbone of sequence modeling for over thirty years. An LSTM cell keeps a *cell state* \(c_t\) that is updated additively:

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1}\;+\; i_t\,z_t,
\]

and a *hidden state* \(h_t\) that is a gated, squashed version of the cell state. Three sigmoid gates – forget, input and output – decide how much past information to keep, how new information to write, and how much of the cell content to expose. This structure has solved the vanishing‑gradient problem and powered early successes in speech, handwriting, machine translation, and even games like StarCraft II.

Yet the LSTM is not a silver bullet. Three core limitations keep it from matching the performance of modern Transformers or State‑Space Models:

| Limitation | Illustration | Consequence |
|------------|--------------|-------------|
| **Static storage** | Once a value is written, the memory can’t be revised. | Struggles with tasks that require re‑evaluation of past information (e.g., nearest‑neighbor search). |
| **Scalar memory** | The cell is a single number. | Poor handling of rare tokens or high‑dimensional signals. |
| **Sequential computation** | Hidden state at \(t\) depends on \(\,h_{t-1}\). | Linear time and memory in sequence length, limiting throughput. |

These weaknesses have paved the way for self‑attention and linear‑attention Transformers. The question we ask in this post: *Can we keep the elegant RNN form but fix these flaws?* The answer is **yes**—enter **xLSTM**.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Exponential gating: give the gates a growth factor

The first innovation is to replace the traditional sigmoid activation on the forget and input gates with an *exponential* activation:

\[
i_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad   
f_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

Exponential gates can grow or shrink much faster than sigmoids, allowing the cell to *forget* irrelevant past information abruptly or *retain* useful information for a long time.  

But unchecked exponentials can cause numerical overload. xLSTM introduces a *stabilizer state* \(m_t\) that keeps gate logs bounded:

\[
m_t \;=\;\max\!\bigl(\log f_t+m_{t-1}, \ \log i_t\bigr), 
\]

and uses this to renormalize the gates:

\[
i'_t=\exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t-m_t\bigr), \qquad
f'_t=\exp\!\bigl(\tilde{f}_t+m_{t-1}-m_t\bigr).
\]

The key insight is that the cell update \((c_t)\) and the stabilizer \((m_t)\) are *intertwined*, but the final hidden state remains unchanged:

\[
h_t \;=\; o_t\,\frac{c_t}{n_t},
\]

where the *normalizer state* \(n_t\) tracks the cumulative gate effect:

\[
n_t \;=\; f_t\,n_{t-1}\;+\; i_t.
\]

In practice, this small extra state yields a remarkable ability to **revise memory** on the fly—something vanilla LSTMs simply cannot do.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## sLSTM: new memory mixing without sacrificing capacity

The next split–in the sugar‑coat of xLSTM is the *scalar‑memory* cell called **sLSTM**. Its forward pass is almost identical to the traditional LSTM but with the exponential gates and the normalizer:

```text
c_t   = f_t * c_{t-1}   + i_t * z_t
n_t   = f_t * n_{t-1}   + i_t
h_t   = o_t * (c_t / n_t)
```

sLSTM re‑introduces **memory mixing** across multiple cells. Hidden states at one step can influence gates and inputs at the next, creating a sophisticated *state‑tracking* capability.  
This mixing, however, keeps sLSTM **sequential**: we cannot arbitrarily hop across time steps, but it offers completeness for tasks where revisiting storage decisions matters (e.g., nested context, rare‑token spotting).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## mLSTM: matrix memory for explosive capacity

To tackle the **scalar‑memory** limitation we upgrade the cell content from a number \(c_t\) to a full \(d\times d\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t\). Think of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) as a *key‑value memory*. Each time step stores a *key* \(\mathbf{k}_t\) and a *value* \(\mathbf{v}_t\):

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]

Retrieval is a simple matrix–vector product:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\bigr)},
\]
where \(\mathbf{q}_t\) is the query vector. The *normalizer* \(\mathbf{n}_t\) accumulates the influence of past keys, just as in the scalar case.

Because there is no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence, mLSTM is **fully parallelizable**—all updates can be expressed as matrix multiplications that modern GPUs execute in bulk. The price is a quadratic cost in the hidden size for the memory matrix, but this cost scales with the dimension rather than the sequence length, keeping the overall algorithm linear in the number of tokens processed.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## xLSTM blocks and a residual backbone

An **xLSTM block** stacks either an sLSTM or an mLSTM inside a standard residual neural network:

1. **Pre‑up‑projection (for mLSTM)**: project the input into a higher‑dimensional space, apply the mLSTM, then project back.  
2. **Post‑up‑projection (for sLSTM)**: process in the original dimension, up‑project, apply a non‑linearity, back‑project.

The residual connection and *pre‑LayerNorm* (used in GPT‑3, Llama, etc.) are kept intact to benefit from mature training stability tricks.

Stacking dozens of these blocks builds an **xLSTM architecture** that can match the depth of contemporary large language models while preserving the RNN’s linear‑time property.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Empirical evidence: outpacing Transformers and SSMs

### 1. Synthetic tasks

- **Formal languages** (parity, nested brackets): xLSTM solved tasks that even SSMs and Transformers failed to learn, thanks to memory mixing.  
- **Multi‑Query Associative Recall**: mLSTM’s matrix memory achieved higher recall accuracy than Transformers on 256 key‑value pairs.  
- **Long‑Range Arena**: xLSTM maintained strong performance across diverse long‑sequence benchmarks, confirming its scalability.

### 2. Real‑world language modeling

On **15 B tokens** of SlimPajama, a 48‑block xLSTM (350 M parameters) reached a validation perplexity of **~20.8**, beating the best Transformer (GPT‑3 style) at ~23.1 and all SSMs. Scaling up to **300 B tokens** confirmed that larger xLSTMs keep lower perplexities across model sizes.

### 3. Sequence‑length extrapolation

When trained with a context of 2 048 tokens but tested up to 16 384 tokens, xLSTM’s perplexity rose only modestly, while Transformers’ performance degraded sharply. This reflects the constant‑memory property—xLSTM does not need to resize its internal state as the input sequence grows.

### 4. Downstream tasks

On a battery of common-sense reasoning and open‑domain language tasks, xLSTM matched or surpassed GPT‑3‑style Transformers and SSMs across the board, proving not just better raw perplexity but also real-world applicability.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why does this matter for practitioners?

| Concern | How xLSTM addresses it |
|---------|------------------------|
| **Hardware limits on sequence length** | Constant‑time, constant‑memory per token → linear throughput. |
| **Extreme context handling** | Exponential gates enable rapid forgetting/keeping → better long‑range reasoning. |
| **Sparse or rare token generalization** | Matrix memory captures fine‑grained key‑value associations. |
| **Training stability** | Normalizer and stabilizer states keep exponentials in check; residual, LayerNorm follow established practices. |
| **Implementation simplicity** | Light‑weight CUDA kernels for sLSTM and mLSTM; fully compatible with PyTorch and HuggingFace pipelines. |

In short, xLSTM offers a *drop‑in* alternative to Transformers for anyone who needs:

- **Long‑context coherence** (e.g., dialogue systems, code generation).  
- **High throughput on GPUs/Tensor‑cores** (no quadratic attention).  
- **Resource‑constrained inference** (fixed memory footprint).  

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Future directions

- **CUDA kernel optimization**: mLSTM is already fast on GPUs; sLSTM kernels can be further streamlined.  
- **Hybrid architectures**: Ensembles of sLSTM and mLSTM blocks (e.g., xLSTM[7:1]) can balance memory mixing with capacity.  
- **Domain‑specific adaptions**: Reinforcement learning agents (AlphaStar, OpenAI Five) could benefit from xLSTM’s state‑tracking without huge attention overhead.  
- **Physics‑inspired models**: The covariance‑update rule links to fast‑weight programmers and associative memories—potential for time‑series and system dynamics.  

---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Bottom line

xLSTM turns the venerable LSTM into a modern, scalable sequence model that rival’s Transformers and State‑Space Models, but with:

- **faster sequential processing**,
- **higher memory capacity**,
- **easier scaling** to billions of parameters, and
- **robustness to long‑term context**.

If you’re building or deploying large‑scale language models, give xLSTM a try. The gains may surprise you—especially when you need both **performance and efficiency**.



# Why Transformers Aren’t the Only Boss of Language Models

## A Quick Recap: LSTMs vs. Transformers

When the language‑modeling world exploded two decades ago, **Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM)** networks were the de‑facto standard. Their clever gating scheme lets a cell remember information for a long time, and that was enough to crack early challenges like generating handwritten text or translating short sentences. Then came the 2017 Transformer paper, and with it an architecture that allows tokens to talk to each other in parallel by means of self‑attention. The Transformer’s ability to scale in a parameter‑efficient way turned it into the winner and, eventually, into the backbone of the first few *Large Language Models* (LLMs) such as GPT‑3 and Llama.

But the Transformer’s success has also revealed its hidden Achilles’ heels:

| Limitation | What it hurts | Why it matters |
|------------|---------------|----------------|
| **Quadratic memory in context length** | Long conversations, code reviews, or scientific articles | Every token pair needs an attention weight, so the cost grows as *O(T²)*. |
| **No built‑in state‑tracking** | Tracking variable scopes in code or nested parentheses | Transformers treat each token independently; once a piece of information is absorbed, there’s no mechanism to revise what was stored. |
| **Limited write‑only capacity** | Weird rare tokens that need a specialized representation | The peripheral attention matrix can be very large but still compresses messages into a handful of vectors. |

These shortcomings are not just academic; they translate directly into higher training costs and weaker generalisation on very long or highly structured inputs. The paper “xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory” sets out to answer a simple question: *Can we build an LSTM‑style model that competes with Transformers on size and accuracy, yet keeps the linear cost in sequence length?* The answer is yes – and the answer is xLSTM.

## Enter xLSTM: Two New Ingredients for a Classic

xLSTM is arguably one of the most honest attempts to bring back the LSTM into the LLM spotlight. Two core ideas lift the traditional cell to the surface:

1. **Exponential Gating** – Instead of using the usual sigmoid (the classic 0…1 gate), the new gates can be exponentiated. This change allows the cell to **revise** what it has written: if a later token demands a stronger memory slot, the forget‑gate can exponentially erase old contributions, and the input‑gate can strengthen the new content accordingly. Think of it as giving the memory cell a *“reset button”* that can fire on demand.

2. **Matrix Memory (mLSTM)** – At the heart of the LSTM lived a single‑dimensional scalar cell. xLSTM turns this into a `d × d` matrix `C`. Storing a key‑value pair is no longer an addition to a scalar but a rank‑one matrix update `v_t k_t^⊤`. This is a *covariance‑update* rule that is optimal for separating the memory content from the noise. The matrix does **not** keep extra parameters; it is just a memory buffer that is read out in a linear fashion. Because the update is a matrix–vector product, the cost stays linear in `T`, yet the model gains a *quadratic* capacity in terms of distinct patterns it can store.

These two ideas are grafted onto the familiar **residual‑block** design that has become a hallmark of modern LLMs. A residual block can either *post‑up‑project* (first operate in the input space and then map to a higher dimension) or *pre‑up‑project* (map up first, then process). xLSTM chooses the former for the scalar‑cell sLSTM, which benefits from strong “memory mixing” across the hidden states, and the latter for the matrix‑cell mLSTM, which already has a richer storage.

The end product is the **xLSTM block**. Stack these blocks in a pre‑LayerNorm backbone, and you obtain a full xLSTM architecture that scales with the number of blocks (just like a Transformer or a State Space Model). The key point is that each block’s forward pass is **O(T)** because it only involves *linear* operations on the input sequence. There is **no quadratic attention**.

## What Do the Numbers Say?

The authors evaluated xLSTM against Transformers, State‑Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba and H3, and a host of other linear‑Transformer approaches on 15 B and 300 B token‐scale language‑modeling experiments from the SlimPajama corpus.

| Metric | Transformer (e.g. Llama) | SSM (Mamba) | **xLSTM** |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------|-----------|
| Validation perplexity (15 B) | 29.4 | 28.8 | **26.8** |
| Validation perplexity (300 B) | 27.9 | 26.5 | **25.3** |
| Extrapolation to longer contexts (1.3 B model, 16 k tokens) | ↑ 2–3× | ↑ 1.5× | **≈ CONSTANT** |
| Fast‑token throughput (1.3 B, GPU) | 35 t/s | 210 t/s | **380 t/s** |

xLSTM consistently lands out in front on the validation perplexity while maintaining or beating the speed of other **recurrent‑style** models. Its crucial advantage is that the **quadratic bottleneck disappears**. A Transformer needs to rebuild the entire attention map when the context grows, while xLSTM only needs to scan the new inputs once. That explains why its extrapolation to 16K tokens does not degrade as sharply as the others.

## The Blame the Small Gaps – where xLSTM Still Struggles

- **sLSTM Parallelism** – The scalar‑cell block can’t be fully parallelised because the hidden state feeds back into itself. The authors address this with a custom CUDA kernel that’s only 2× slower than the matrix‑cell version; still, the Maths of their custom kernel are a bit opaque to casual users.
- **mLSTM Computational Overhead** – Even though the memory is cheap, each update involves a `d × d` matrix multiplication. At 1.3 B, the per‑token cost is about `4×` higher than a FlashAttention block. Still, for many edge deployments where memory is a stricter bottleneck than compute, this trade‑off is attractive.
- **Scale‑up Validity** – The paper trains up to 1.3 B parameters—still modest compared to the 175 B of GPT‑3. The scaling curves look promising, but a full 10× jump should be taken with caution until the community reproduces the results.

## Take‑Away: When LSTMs Bling‑Back

xLSTM shows that a *revived* recurrent unit can **match or beat** Transformers on a wide range of language‑modeling metrics while keeping the runtime linear in sequence length. For scenarios where:

- **Memory** is more precious than raw parallelism (e.g. mobile or embedded deployment),
- **Long‑range context** is a requirement (think code, scholarship papers, or telemetry logs),
- or **Explainability** wins (LSTMs expose their cell states more transparently than self‑attention maps),

xLSTM offers a compelling alternative.

In the grand narrative of deep learning, we’ve seen the rise and peak of RNNs, the jump to Transformers, and now the **second act** of the LSTM. The key lesson? *Architecture choices should be driven by the problem’s structural constraints, not just the wish to flirt with the newest paradigm.* xLSTM reminds us that sometimes a well‑engineered old idea can still compete on modern scales.



# The Science Behind Exponential Gating: Giving LSTMs a Memory Revision Superpower

## Why Memory Revision Matters

Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been a staple of sequential modelling for decades. Their design—cell states coupled with input‑, forget‑, and output‑gates—helps them remember information over long horizons and learn long‑range dependencies.  
Despite this, classic LSTMs struggle with a subtle but important capability: **revising what they have stored**. 

Imagine you scan a sentence that contains a reference word “apple” and later encounter “apple tree”. A conventional LSTM keeps the first word in its cell state and, because the update is additive, it cannot *overwrite* that entry when a more relevant token appears. This limitation shows up in problems such as nearest‑neighbor search or rare‑token prediction, where the best‑matching memory slot should be dynamically updated.  

The *exponential gating* mechanism introduced in **xLSTM** remedies this. It allows the LSTM to “forget” older entries exponentially, evicting them from memory, and to *identify* the most recent, most relevant information. Below we unpack the mathematics, the intuition, and the role of the **normalizer state** that keeps the system stable.

---

## 1. Revisiting the LSTM Update Equations

The canonical cell update for an LSTM at time step \(t\) is

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1}\; +\; i_t\,z_t,
\]

where:

* \(f_t\) is the **forget gate** (sigmoid output),
* \(i_t\) is the **input gate** (sigmoid output),
* \(z_t\) is the **cell input** (usually \(\tanh\)-activated linear combination of the current input and previous hidden state).

The term \(f_t\,c_{t-1}\) “carries over” the previous memory, while \(i_t\,z_t\) injects new information. **Because both gates are sigmoid‑bounded, the contribution of past memory decays only linearly**; the model has to learn huge weights to effectively *drop* old information.

---

## 2. Exponential Gating: From Sigmoid to Exponential

xLSTM replaces the sigmoid activation of the input and forget gates with an **exponential activation**:

\[
i_t \;=\; e^{\tilde i_t}, \qquad
f_t \;=\; e^{\tilde f_t},
\]

where \(\tilde i_t,\,\tilde f_t\) are the raw gate pre‑activations.  
Exponentials possess a crucial property: a small change in the gating pre‑activation can produce a *large* change in the gate value. In practice this means that the network can set \(f_t \ll 1\) (effectively wiping the old memory) or \(i_t \gg 1\) (strongly pushing in new content) in a single time step.

### What does this buy us?

* **Rapid forgetting**: If the model detects that the current token suggests the previous entry is no longer relevant, it can set \(f_t\) near zero, causing the term \(f_t\,c_{t-1}\) to vanish instantly.  
* **Rapid updating**: Similarly, a large \(i_t\) overwhelms the old memory with the new input, effectively *rewriting* the cell state on the fly.

In short, exponential gates give the LSTM the “memory revision” superpower needed for tasks where the most recent mention should trump earlier ones.

---

## 3. The Normalizer State: Keeping Things in Check

Exponentials can blow up numerically, especially when the network learns to use very large values. To prevent overflow and ensure stable training, xLSTM introduces a **normalizer state** \(n_t\). For the scalar sLSTM variant the update equations become:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t   & = f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t,\\
n_t   & = f_t\,n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t,\\
h_t   & = o_t\,\tilde h_t, \qquad
\tilde h_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\end{aligned}
\]

* The normalizer, \(n_t\), is essentially a running weighted sum of all past input‑gate activations, scaled by subsequent forget gates.
* By dividing the cell state \(c_t\) by \(n_t\) before applying the output gate, we **renormalize** the memory. This prevents exponential growth of \(c_t\) from exploding the hidden state.

A stabilizing trick (derived from stabilization in Zeiler‑style residual networks) involves keeping a running maximum of the log‑guars \(m_t\) and re‑scaling the input and forget gates:

\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t & = \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr),\\
i'_t & = e^{\,\tilde i_t - m_t}, \qquad
f'_t = e^{\,\tilde f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Replacing \(i_t\) by \(i'_t\) and \(f_t\) by \(f'_t\) in the forward pass does not change the network’s output or gradients. The trick simply keeps the gates in a numerically safe range.

---

## 4. Memory Mixing and Multiple Heads

xLSTM’s exponential gating works hand‑in‑hand with a new form of **memory mixing**. If the network has multiple scalar memory cells (as in the original multi‑cell LSTM), hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections \(\mathbf{R}\) can mix the outputs of different cells. Exponential gates sharpen this mixing: when a fresh input necessitates a more relevant memory slot, the gating can instantaneously drop the weaker memories while preserving or even magnifying the stronger ones.

For a *single* sLSTM cell the architecture is usually built into a **post‑up‑projection residual block**: first a linear transform projects the input, then the LSTM attends in the original space, and finally another transform maps back. For a *matrix* mLSTM (see below) a **pre‑up‑projection block** is used because the memory capacity sits in the high‑dimensional space.

---

## 5. Matrix Memory (mLSTM) for Increased Capacity

While exponential gating patches the *revision* issue, xLSTM also introduces a **matrix‑valued memory** \( \mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) to boost capacity. The update rule mimics the covariance update used in *fast‑weight programmers*:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t \;=\; f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\;+\; i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top},
\]

where \(\mathbf{v}_t\) and \(\mathbf{k}_t\) are respectively the value and key vectors derived from the input. Retrieval is then:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}
          {\max\bigl( | \mathbf{n}_t^{\!\top}\mathbf{q}_t | , 1 \bigr)} ,
\]

with \(\mathbf{q}_t\) the query vector and \(\mathbf{n}_t\) a normalizer analogous to the scalar case.

Because \(\mathbf{C}_t\) grows with \(d\), it can store many key‑value pairs simultaneously—exhibiting a richer memory repertoire. Crucially, the matrix memory **does not interfere with the exponential gating**: both mechanisms coexist because they address different limitations of the classic LSTM.

---

## 6. Putting It All Together – xLSTM Blocks

An **xLSTM block** encapsulates either an sLSTM or an mLSTM inside a residual backbone:

1. **Post‑up‑projection block (for sLSTM):**  
   * Linear layer → *exponential‑gated* LSTM → linear layer.  
2. **Pre‑up‑projection block (for mLSTM):**  
   * Linear layer → *matrix‑memory* LSTM → linear layer.

The block is wrapped with *pre‑LayerNorm* and a residual connection, matching the prevalent design of modern large language models (LLMs). A deep stack of such blocks—say, 48 layers—constitutes an **xLSTM architecture** that can be scaled to billions of parameters.

---

## 7. Intuition in a Nutshell

| Problem | Traditional LSTM | Exponential Gating | Benefit |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------|---------|
| **Cannot overwrite past memory** | Additive memory with sigmoid gates | Exponential gates can set forget \(\ll 1\) or input \(\gg 1\) instantly | **Live revision** of memory cells |
| **Catastrophic forgetting** | Gradual decay, hard to reset | Exponential forget can wipe memory in one step | **Graceful forgetting** |
| **Numerical instability** | Sigmoid gates bounded, but cell can still blow up with extreme \(\mathbf{W}_{z, h}\) | Normalizer state and gate scaling | **Stable training** |
| **Limited capacity for key‑value pairs** | Scalar cell, heavy compression | Matrix memory with covariance update | **Exponentially larger memory** |

---

## 8. Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Implement exponential gates** if you need an LSTM that must *revisit* or *overwrite* its stored state (e.g., in reinforcement‑learning policies where state estimates need to be continuously refined).
2. **Add a normalizer state** to prevent overflow; it’s a lightweight extra that scales linearly with the number of cells.
3. **Consider the matrix memory** if your application requires storing many key‑value pairs (e.g., associative recalls, memory‑augmented networks). The mLSTM version is naturally more parallelizable at train‑time.
4. **Use residual Block + LayerNorm** for easy scaling to larger models, mirroring the success of Transformers and State‑Space models.

---

## 9. Closing Thoughts

Exponential gating transforms the classic LSTM from a fixed‑memory processor into a *dynamic memory manager*. By learning to either forget or overwrite old entries instantly, the network can handle tasks that demand immediate memory revision—tasks where traditional LSTMs falter. Combined with the richer matrix memory of mLSTM, the **xLSTM family** demonstrates that carefully engineered gating and memory structures can bring LSTMs back into the conversation as viable blocks for modern large language models.

Happy revising!



# Matrix Memories in Neural Networks: A New Take on Long‑Term Storage

## The LSTM Legacy and Its Limits

Between the 1990s and today, the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) has been the go‑to recurrent architecture for sequential data. Its two core ideas—constant error carousel (the additive cell update) and gating—eliminate the vanishing‑gradient problem that crippled vanilla RNNs. An LSTM cell keeps a **scalar** memory \(c_t \in \mathbb{R}\) that is updated time‑step by time‑step:

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,
\]

where the forget gate \(f_t\), input gate \(i_t\), and a candidate input \(z_t\) are all scalar values. The hidden output \(h_t\) is a gated, squashed version of this memory. While this simple machinery has produced remarkable results in handwriting generation, machine translation, and even high‑stakes games, it has a fundamental bottleneck: **storage capacity**. Because everything is compressed into a single value, every time step must trade off what it remembers and what it forgets. This becomes acute for rare tokens or long‑range dependencies that require keeping multiple pieces of information simultaneously.

The new breed of large language models has largely moved away from LSTMs in favour of Transformers and State‑Space Models, both of which can process long contexts with parallelizable operations. Yet they, too, hit limitations—most notably on tasks that require *state tracking* (e.g. maintaining the identity of previously seen entities). The paper *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* proposes a concrete remedy: replace the scalar memory with a **matrix‑valued memory** and use a principled **covariance update rule** for key‑value pairs. This simple tweak gives the model a theoretical and empirical edge in long‑term storage. Below we unpack this new idea.

---

## From Scalars to Matrices: The mLSTM Cell

### What Is a Matrix Memory?

Instead of a single number, the mLSTM keeps a full \(d \times d\) matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) at each time step. Think of \(\mathbf{C}_t\) as a high‑dimensional *knowledge base* that records interactions between vectors. In practice, the network learns *keys* \(\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and *values* \(\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) from the input \(\mathbf{x}_t\), and stores their outer product into \(\mathbf{C}_t\). The forward update is

\[
\boxed{\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \, \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^{\!\top}} \tag{1}
\]

where \(f_t\) is a forget gate (a scalar in \((0,1]\)) and \(i_t\) is an **exponential input gate** (i.e. \(i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)\)). The forget gate acts as a *decay factor* for the whole memory matrix, while the input gate scales how strongly a new key–value pair is written.

To read from the memory, the cell receives a *query* vector \(\mathbf{q}_t\) (derived from the current input) and applies a matrix‑vector product:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1\}} , \qquad
\mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t ,
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\mathbf{n}_t\) is a *normalizer* that tracks the total influence of the gates (defined analogously to Eq. (1) but with scalar key factors), and \(\mathbf{o}_t\) is the output gate. This retrieval is exactly the same mechanism used in the Transformer’s attention, except that the **key/value pair is stored once** in the matrix instead of being repeated across many tokens.

### The Covariance Update Rule

Equation (1) is nothing more than the classic **covariance update** from associative memory theory:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]

Historically, such outer‑product memories trace back to the Hopfield network and later the **Fast‑Weight Programmers** (Fisher, Sejnowski) and self‑supervised learning methods that exploit the outer product of hidden states. The idea is that the matrix captures *co‑occurrence statistics* between keys and values; each new pair incrementally steers the stored representation toward that correlation. Because outer products are rank‑one, a single update breathes new linear information into the matrix, while the decay gate \(f_t\) ensures that old, weakly relevant data fades away gradually.

Mathematically, if we view the memory as \(\mathbf{C}_t = \sum_{\tau \le t} i_\tau f_\tau^{t-\tau}\, \mathbf{v}_\tau \mathbf{k}_\tau^\top\), it is clearly a weighted sum of past outer products. The weights are controlled entirely by the gates, and thanks to the exponential nature of \(i_t\) and the soft decay provided by \(f_t\) (which may itself be an exponential), the network can *prioritize* certain memories while still keeping the update rule stable.

---

## Why a Matrix Outsmarts a Scalar

### 1. **Higher Capacity for Key–Value Associations**

In an LSTM’s scalar cell, every input is projected onto a single number. To recover a high‑dimensional key‑value pair later, the network has to encode the entire vector in that number, essentially squeezing a continuous space into one dimension—an impossible task when the space is huge or tokens are rare. A matrix memory, by contrast, can store *joint* information across all dimensions. Think of it as a table where each row/column pair records how a particular key component interacts with a particular value component. The resulting capacity scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) in the number of trainable *parameters* inside the matrix (although the memory itself does not have learned parameters; the gates do). This means a 512‑dimensional key can theoretically capture up to \(512^2 = 262,144\) independent interactions—far beyond any scalar’s reach.

### 2. **Exact Retrieval Without Redundant Attention**

Transformers achieve high memory capacity by attending to each query across *every* position in the sequence, thus effectively reconstructing the key‑value pairs at inference time. This incurs a quadratic cost in the sequence length. mLSTM, with its matrix memory, **stores** the associations *once*, so retrieving a value reduces to a single matrix‑vector product (linear in the hidden dimension, constant in the sequence length). Therefore, the model can handle very long contexts without the quadratic penalty.

### 3. **Seamless Integration with Gating**

The forget gate \(f_t\) acts as a **global decay** of the matrix, shrinking the influence of old key‑value pairs. The exponential input gate \(i_t\) can sharply increase the weight of a new key‑value pair when the model deems it important. Because both are scalars, the gates remain lightweight while exerting powerful control over the high‑dimensional memory. This is reminiscent of amortized forgetting in biology: a *per‑step* tone that gradually reduces memory strength unless reinforced.

---

## Practical Implications for Language Modelling

In the experiments reported in the paper, the **mLSTM branch of xLSTM** outperformed Transformer‑based models on a range of demanding synthetic tasks:

| Task | Best Performer | Meter |
|------|----------------|-------|
| Multi‑Query Associative Recall (256 keys, 2048 tokens) | xLSTM[1:0] | 92.3 % |
| Long‑Range Arena (e.g. Secret Number, Image Captioning) | xLSTM | +0.4 BLEU over Mamba |
| Token prediction on 571 diverse domains (PALOMA) | xLSTM[1:0] | 99.5 % reduction in perplexity vs. Mamba |

These results show that the **matrix memory** not only boosts theoretical capacity but also delivers tangible performance gains when the model is *scaled up* to billions of parameters. Compared to plain LSTMs, which struggle to remember rare symbols or track multiple entities simultaneously, xLSTM’s mLSTM cells can hold an extensive *memory graph* that is queried efficiently with a linear operation.

Furthermore, the **covariance update** provides a principled way to integrate new evidence while preserving prior knowledge, akin to Bayesian updating but in a neural‑network-friendly form. This aligns with recent trends toward *memory‑augmented* neural networks and *fast‑weight* learning, but with a less burdensome parameter count and a cleaner mathematical foundation.

## How to Build an mLSTM Cell (Pseudocode)

```python
def mLSTM_step(x_t, h_prev, C_prev, n_prev):
    # Project input to key, value and query
    k_t = (W_k @ x_t + b_k) / sqrt(d)
    v_t = W_v @ x_t + b_v
    q_t = W_q @ x_t + b_q

    # Compute gates
    i_t = exp(w_i.T @ x_t + b_i)          # exponential input gate
    f_t = sigmoid(w_f.T @ x_t + b_f)      # forget gate
    o_t = sigmoid(W_o @ x_t + b_o)        # output gate

    # Update matrix memory and normalizer
    C_t = f_t * C_prev + i_t * np.outer(v_t, k_t)
    n_t = f_t * n_prev + i_t * k_t

    # Retrieval
    denom = max(abs(n_t.T @ q_t), 1.0)
    h_tilde = C_t @ q_t / denom
    h_t = o_t * h_tilde

    return h_t, C_t, n_t
```

The algorithm is **fully parallelizable** across time steps; one can pre‑compute all keys, values, and queries and then perform a batched matrix‑vector product since the recurrence is only on the *memory* state \(\mathbf{C}_t\). Modern GPUs can handle the \(d \times d\) updates efficiently, making mLSTM competitive in wall‑clock time with FlashAttention or Mamba.

---

## Takeaways

1. **Matrix memories** break the scalar bottleneck of traditional LSTMs, giving the model a quadratic‐in‐dimension handling of associations while keeping the parameter budget low.
2. The **covariance update rule** is a mathematically grounded, parameter‑free way to add and decay key–value pairs, aligning with fast‑weight learning concepts.
3. In practice, mLSTM cells deliver strong performance on both *synthetic* associative recall tests and *real* language modeling benchmarks, rivaling the best Transformer‑based large language models.
4. Because the memory is independent of sequence length, mLSTM scales well to contexts of several thousand tokens—a crucial property for many downstream applications (e.g., code understanding, dialogue systems).

In sum, the matrix‑based memory of mLSTM is a compelling step toward *re‑introducing gated recurrent architectures* into the age of large language models. It shows that with the right memory structure and update rule, recurrent networks can still outshine their Transformer cousins in storage capacity and long‑range inference.



# Building an xLSTM‑Based Large Language Model from Scratch

## 1. Why xLSTM?

Long short‑term memory (LSTM) networks were once the backbone of language modelling.  
Since 2017 the Transformer architecture has taken over, mainly because it is
parallelisable and can be scaled to billions of parameters.  Yet LSTMs still
offer three attractive properties that Transformers lack:

| Limitation of vanilla LSTM | xLSTM remedy |
|---------------------------|--------------|
| Inability to revise a stored entry | **Exponential gating** + normaliser |
| Scalar memory limits capacity | **Matrix memory** (covariance update) |
| Serial processing because of hidden‑hidden connections | **mLSTM** (no memory mixing) is fully parallelisable |

xLSTM fuses these two extensions – the *exponential gates* of **sLSTM** and the
*matrix memory* of **mLSTM** – inside a residual block backbone.  The resulting
architecture matches the compute‑ and memory‑scalability of Transformers
while retaining the expressiveness of RNNs.  In practice, an xLSTM trained on
300 B tokens outperforms contemporary Transformers (e.g. Llama, Mamba) in
perplexity, downstream benchmarks and inference throughput.

Below you’ll see how to assemble an xLSTM‑based large language model from the
ground up, how to tune its hyper‑parameters, and how to optimise inference
speed.

---

## 2. Architectural Blueprint

### 2.1 The building block

An xLSTM block takes a sequence of hidden states **h** as input,
embeds them into a higher‑dimensional space, applies a non‑linear
summarisation, and projects back to the original dimension:
```
Input ──► (optional up‑projection) ──►  *xLSTM cell*  ──► (optional down‑projection) ──►
          └—residual‑connection—┘
```
Two flavours of the cell exist:

| Cell | Key‑operations |
|------|----------------|
| **sLSTM** | scalar cell, exponential input and forget gates, normaliser *n* = f n̂ + i, hidden state *h* = o ⋅ c/n |
| **mLSTM** | matrix memory **C** ∈ ℝᵈˣᵈ, covariance update **C** ← f C + i v kᵗ, query **q** = Wq x, hidden *h* = o ⋅ C q / max{|nᵗ q|,1} |

Both cells expose *input*, *forget* and *output* gates that can be either
sigmoid or exponential; we find exponential gates (with a stabiliser *m*) to
give the best trade‑off between expressiveness and training stability.

### 2.2 Mixing heads and blocks

The xLSTM architecture is a stack of *N* residual blocks.

* **Heads per block** – each block can contain *H* independent heads that do
  not communicate at all (no cross‑head mixing).  Heads act like separate
  memory streams and can be tailored for different grammar or semantic
  sub‑tasks.

* **Block ratio *a:b*** – we denote an architecture by
  `xLSTM[a:b]`, where *a* / *b* is the ratio of mLSTM‑ to sLSTM‑based
  blocks.  For example, `xLSTM[7:1]` means 7 × mLSTM blocks and 1 × sLSTM
  block out of 8 total.  Empirically, a 7:1 split often gives the best trade‑off
  between speed (≈ mLSTM) and state‑tracking power (≈ sLSTM).

### 2.3 Residual scaffolding

The residual backbone follows the pre‑LayerNorm pattern that is ubiquitous
in modern LLMs:

```
x + LayerNorm( Block(x) )
```

Pre‑LayerNorm gives better stability for large hidden dimensions and is
friendly to the computational graph of the mLSTM.

The entire stack is wrapped inside a **GPT‑style decoder**:
* token‑embedding layer, positional‑embedding layer,
* **N** xLSTM blocks,
* a final Language‑Model head that maps hidden states back to token logits.

---

## 3. Hyper‑parameter Tuning

| Hyper‑parameter | Typical value | Why |
|------------------|----------------|-----|
| **Embedding dim** | 1024 | Matches Transformer baselines |
| **Hidden dim (per head)** | 128 | For 8 heads → 1024 hidden dimension |
| **Number of blocks** | 48 | Empirical sweet‑spot from our 300 B training |
| **Sequence length** | 2048 (train), up to 16384 (eval) | Allows long‑context extrapolation |
| **Learning rate** | 3 × 10⁻⁶ | AdamW with warm‑up 10 000 steps |
| **Warming‑up** | 10 000 steps | Avoids early gradient explosion |
| **Weight‑decay** | 0.1 | Regularises parameters |
| **Gradient clipping** | 1.0 | Keeps gradients stable |
| **Batch size** | 256 | Determined by GPU memory (mix of 8× A100 or 4× V100) |
| **Max‑norm of *n*** | 1.0 | In mLSTM normaliser *n* keeps dot‑products bounded |
| **Gate type** | Exponential (for *i*, *f*) | Gives better state‐revision capability |
| **Stabiliser *m*** | Log‑max of past gates | Prevents overflow in exp gates |

### 3.1 Staged warm‑up

Start by training a small xLSTM (e.g. 125M params) for 10 k steps to gather
gradient statistics. Then use the computed *m* and *n* statistics to initialise
larger models.

### 3.2 Ratio sweep

Run 3‑fold cross‑validations with `xLSTM[1:1]`, `xLSTM[7:1]` and `xLSTM[1:0]`.
Pick the ratio that gives the lowest validation perplexity on a held‑out
validation set after 300 B steps.  In our experiments, `xLSTM[7:1]` consistently
wins in perplexity, while `xLSTM[1:0]` offers a 10‑20 % throughput boost.

---

## 4. Training on 300 B Tokens

### 4.1 Data pipeline

| Step | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Chunking | Tokenise with a BPE vocabulary (≈ 50 k tokens). Store as 32‑bit ints. |
| Sharding | Split into 1‑M‑token shards, read on‑the‑fly via `torch.utils.data.Dataset`. |
| Batching | Use a `TensorDataset` that yields tensors of shape (batch, seq_len). |
| Padding | All shards already fixed length (2048). No padding needed. |

### 4.2 Distributed training

* Use **DeepSpeed** or **FairScale** for ZeRO‑3 optimisation – this keeps all
  parameters on‑device while partitioning gradients across workers.  
* For 1.3 B model train on 8× A100 GPUs (40 GB).  
* Train for **≈ 400 k steps** (≈ 300 B tokens).  
* Log loss, perplexity and learning‑rate dynamics on Weights & Biases.

### 4.3 Loss smoothing

Apply **label‑smoothed cross‑entropy** with ε = 0.1 to improve calibration.

### 4.4 Checkpoints

Persist checkpoints every 2 M steps.  Include the full state (model,
Optimizer, scheduler, *m* and *n* statistics) to enable training resumption.

---

## 5. Inference Speed Optimisations

### 5.1 Parallelisation map

* **sLSTM** – sequential due to hidden‑hidden connections.  Still
  implementation‑fast: custom CUDA kernel (register‑level optimisation)
  runs ~ 2× slower than mLSTM but scales linearly with context.
* **mLSTM** – fully parallelisable.  Replace the GPU kernel with
  **Flash‑style matrix multiply** (torch.nn.functional.linear +
  torch.cond).  The matrix memory **C** is kept in shared memory for
  quick access.

### 5.2 Caching strategy

Unlike the Transformer, xLSTM does not need a large KV cache:

* For *mLSTM*, compute **C** incrementally with a sliding window.  
* For *sLSTM*, keep a small *n* buffer (size 32) – this is trivial to cache
  in GPU registers.

### 5.3 Batch‑level optimisation

* Use **torch.compile** (or JIT) for the full decoder pipeline.
* Reduce batch‑size to **1** for interactive generation, but scale to
  **≥ 32** for greedy decoding in batch inference.

### 5.4 Throughput benchmark

| Model | Batch | Context | Per‑token time (ms) | Throughput (tokens/s) |
|-------|-------|--------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **xLSTM[7:1]** | 32 | 2 048 | 0.62 | 516 |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** | 32 | 2 048 | 0.42 | 761 |
| **Mamba** | 32 | 2 048 | 0.57 | 559 |
| **Llama‑2‑7B** | 32 | 2 048 | 2.4 | 133 |

The numbers match our paper’s inference curves: xLSTM preserves a
constant‑memory profile and scales linearly, whereas Llama shows a clear
quadratic slowdown with larger batch or context sizes.

---

## 6. Putting It All Together

Below is a minimal PyTorch skeleton for an xLSTM block (mLSTM flavour).  It is
pure‑Python for clarity; replace `torch.nn.functional.linear` by your
GPU‑optimised kernels when deploying.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class MLSTMBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, heads=8, bias=True):
        super().__init__()
        self.heads  = heads
        self.d      = d
        self.init_state(d)

        # gate parameters
        self.w_i   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)
        self.w_f   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)
        self.w_o   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)

        # key/value/query projections
        self.W_k   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)
        self.W_v   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)
        self.W_q   = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=bias)

    def init_state(self, d):
        self.C = torch.zeros(d, d, device='cuda')
        self.n = torch.zeros(d, device='cuda')
        self.m = torch.zeros(1, device='cuda')  # stabiliser

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (B, T, d)
        B, T, D = x.shape
        C, n, m = self.C, self.n, self.m

        for i in range(T):
            xi = x[:, i, :]
            k   = self.W_k(xi) / math.sqrt(D)
            v   = self.W_v(xi)
            q   = self.W_q(xi)

            i_gate = torch.exp(self.w_i(xi))          # exp gate
            f_gate = torch.sigmoid(self.w_f(xi))     # sigmoid gate
            o_gate = torch.sigmoid(self.w_o(xi))

            # stabilise
            m = torch.max(torch.log(f_gate) + m, torch.log(i_gate))
            i_gate = torch.exp(self.w_i(xi) - m)
            f_gate = torch.exp(self.w_f(xi) + m - m)

            # update memory
            C = f_gate[:, None, None] * C + i_gate[:, None, None] * torch.ger(v, k)
            n = f_gate * n + i_gate * k

            # retrieval
            denom = torch.max(torch.abs(torch.bmm(n[None], q[:, None])) , 1.0)
            h = o_gate * torch.squeeze(C @ q.unsqueeze(-1) / denom, -1)

            # (here you would normally store *h* to restore the final
            #   hidden state sequence, but for brevity the code stops.)

        return h
```

> **Tip**: Pre‑compute the factor `i_gate * k` for the matrix update to
> avoid repeated linear algebra calls inside the `for` loop – this is the
> core optimisation in practice.

### 6.1 Training loop

model = XLMLEncoder(...)

for step, batch in enumerate(dataloader):

## 7. Conclusion

Building a large language model with xLSTM is surprisingly straightforward:
slots of modular, residual blocks, a handful of additional parameters, and
the same training recipe that works for Transformers.  The trade‑offs are
clear:

* **xLSTM** keeps the *constant‑error carousel* that makes LSTMs friendly
  to long‑range dependencies.
* **Exponential gating** improves state‑revision and keeps gate values in
  a numerically stable range.
* **Matrix memory** gives each block a luxury of representational
  capacity that scales with *d²* but **does not** increase model
  parameters.
* **Parallelisable mLSTM** yields throughput comparable to
  transformers for medium‑size contexts; **sLSTM** offers the best
  state‑tracking performance at a small cost.

Once you have the kernel optimised (Flash‑style matrix multiply for
mLSTM, register‑level CUDA for sLSTM) you can reach a 300 B token
training run on a single node of 8×A100 for a few weeks, and the resulting
model will rival and, in several benchmarks, surpass the current
state‑of‑the‑art transformer‑based LLMs.

Happy modelling!



# xLSTM vs. State‑Space and Attention: Who Wins the Long‑Context Game?

## The long‑context war – what's the real edge?

When the transformer era burst onto the scene, the excitement was clear: self‑attention now lets models look back arbitrarily far, and the whole field leapt toward billion‑parameter systems. Yet the practical cost of that quadratic attention and the loss of truly “memory‑centric” recurrence left a gap. Enter **xLSTM**, a modern upgrade to the classic Long‑Short‑Term Memory that tackles the same problems many times the way the original LSTM was built to solve them.

In this post we’ll walk through the empirical evidence that xLSTM not only keeps up with state‑space models (SSMs) and transformers on long‑range benchmarks, it actually outperforms them on the hardest tasks. We’ll also tease out *why* that happens, without getting lost in technical weeds.

---

## Quick recap: what’s new in xLSTM?

xLSTM is not a single model but a family of two variants, each beefing up the core LSTM idea in a different direction:

| Variant | Core Idea | Key Benefit |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **sLSTM** | *Exponential gating* + *memory mixing* (confusing as that sounds) | Allows the unit to *revise* earlier memorized values – useful for state‑tracking problems. |
| **mLSTM** | *Matrix memory* with a *covariance update rule* | Holds large key‑value stores without squeezing them into a scalar – great for associative recall. |

Both variants fit inside a residual block with optional up‑projection – exactly the kind of “backbone” most modern language models use.

---

## Two wars, two prizes

### 1. The Long‑Range Arena (LRA)

LRA is a composite benchmark that puts models to the test on a set of synthetic tasks (e.g., list‑processing, program‑execution, parity checking) where the model must remember information from far earlier in the sequence. The challenge? The scaffolding of the task requires *true state tracking* – a capability that most pure attention models lack.

| Model | LRA Micro‑Average (lower is better) |
|-------|-------------------------------------|
| **xLSTM[0:1]** (pure sLSTM) | **6.1** |
| xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) |  9.3 |
| xLSTM[1:1] (mix) | 8.8 |
| Transformer (e.g. GPT‑3) |  13.9 |
| SSM (e.g. Mamba) | 12.5 |
| Classic LSTM | 15.6 |

**Why xLSTM steals the show.**  
The sLSTM’s exponential gates allow the forget gate to *decay* previous states in a learnable, continuous way. When the series contains a pattern that needs to be “updated” – say a repeated variable that changes later – sLSTM can replace the stored value. Transformers, oblivious to the notion of “memory mixing”, simply rewrite attention scores every time. SSMs are good at propagating linear dynamics but they lack the kind of state‑tracking that sLSTM supplies.

### 2. Associative Recall (AR)

In AR a model must memorize a list of key‑value pairs and later be queried for the right value. The more pairs you pack in, the more the system is forced to use its memory capacity smartly.

| Model | Top‑1 Recall (avg over 256 pairs) |
|-------|-----------------------------------|
| **xLSTM[1:0]** | **89.4 %** |
| xLSTM[1:1] | 84.7 % |
| Mamba | 82.1 % |
| RWKV‑5 | 63.4 % |
| Classic LSTM | 24.3 % |

**Why xLSTM wins on AR.**  
The matrix memory in mLSTM stores each new key‑value pair as an outer product \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^{\top}\). This awakens a *covariance update* that’s proven to be the optimal way to separate distant keys. Transformers also have an implicit key‑value structure, but they rely on dot‑product attention – essentially a *sample* of that matrix. xLSTM, meanwhile, updates the full matrix each step, giving it an edge when the number of pairs explodes. The result is higher recall and robustness to overload.

---

## The practical take‑away

| Trait | Transformer | State‑Space Model | xLSTM |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------|
| **Sequential vs. Parallel** | Viable, but quadratic memory | Linear, but not true memory updates | Linear *and* offers state‑tracking or massive key‑value storage |
| **Scaling to billions of params** | Straight‑forward, but expensive | Straight‑forward | Straight‑forward, *but* requires careful GPU kernel tuning |
| **Long‑context performance** | Good, but fickle when true state tracking is needed | Good, but limited to linear dynamics | Consistently top‑performing on formal languages and associative recall |

If your project demands *long‑context* reasoning or *in‑sequence memory updates* (think code generation, complex dialogue agents, or even some RL scenarios), xLSTM has a structural advantage that vanilla transformers simply don’t possess. Conversely, if you’re on a tight GPU budget and only need *big‑scale statistical patterns* (like typical next‑token prediction), a transformer or SSM may still win on raw speed.

---

## Bottom line

The empirical evidence squares with a simple intuition: **give the network a way to *write* to a durable memory while keeping the gate‑controlled *update* logic intact, and you get far better long‑range performance.** xLSTM does just that—two concrete enhancements that revive the classic LSTM spirit and turn it into a competitive torch against the state‑of‑the‑art. Whether you’ll adopt it will depend on your workloads, but for anyone wrestling with stubborn long‑context dependencies, it’s worth a deeper look.



# Scaling Laws for **xLSTM**: What to Expect as Model Size Grows

In the past few years the community has spent a great deal of effort quantifying how the performance of language models scales with the amount of compute or the number of parameters. The canonical reference is the *scaling law* paper by Kaplan et al., which shows that a log‑log plot of perplexity versus the product of data, model size, and context length collapses into a straight line. That discovery has guided the choice of architecture, environment, and training budget for every new large‑scale model.

With the advent of **xLSTM**—the extended Long‑Short‑Term‑Memory that adds exponential gating, matrix memory, and a flexible residual backbone—it is natural to ask: *Does xLSTM obey the same power‑law scaling?* And if so, how does its trajectory compare to that of Transformers and State‑Space Models?

Below we dissect the empirical results from the latest paper on xLSTM, explain the key findings in plain terms, and outline what you should expect when you scale the model up.

---

## 1. Re‑visiting the Scaling Framework

The scaling law used in modern LLM research can be written as

\[
\mathcal{L} = A\; \bigl(\theta\, D\bigr)^{-\alpha}
\]

where:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $\mathcal{L}$ | validation perplexity (or other loss) |
| $\theta$ | number of trainable parameters |
| $D$ | number of tokens seen during training |
| $A$ | a method‑specific constant |
| $\alpha$ | the decay exponent (typically $0.07$–$0.12$ for language models) |

On a log‑log plot, each learning system traces a straight line with slope $-\alpha$; the **offset** $A$ indicates how large the loss starts. A smaller offset means *better performance at the same scale*.

Figure 1 (re‑created from the xLSTM paper) shows the perplexity curves for four major families on a $15$ B‑token training set:

- **Transformers** (e.g. a GPT‑3 style model)
- **State‑Space Models** (Mamba, H3)
- **Linear Transformers** (GLA, RetNet)
- **xLSTM** (both sLSTM‑only and mixed‑block versions)

The lines are remarkably parallel—their slopes (i.e., $\alpha$) are all within $0.03$ of each other. What distinguishes xLSTM is its *lower starting point*: the $A$ value is roughly $15$–$20$ % lower than the nearest Transformer.

A similar picture emerges when we train on a much larger corpus of $300$ B tokens (Fig. 2). Here the power‑law slopes are almost identical, but the offset for xLSTM is $5$–$10$ % smaller than for Mamba and almost $20$ % smaller than for Llama.

---

## 2. What Does a Lower Offset Mean?

Imagine you’re building a speeder through a gravel field. The slope tells you how quickly you can go faster when you add more horsepower, but the offset tells you how many pedals you need to be comfortable at a given speed.

*Practically speaking*:

- **Smaller offset → lower perplexity at every scale**. This translates to better next‑token predictions for the same compute budget.
- **Better cut‑off performance**: At low model sizes, xLSTM already competes with or beats other methods. For example, a $350$ M‑parameter xLSTM matches a $1$ B‑parameter Transformer on the same validation set.
- **Consistent extrapolation**: A model that starts lower tends to stay lower as you keep adding data or parameters because the slope is nearly the same. In other words, the *relative advantage* remains robust.

---

## 3. The Role of Exponential Gating and Matrix Memory

The architecture explains the offset advantage. Two key innovations drive the better scaling:

1. **Exponential Gating**  
   - Allows the model to *revise* what it has stored; forgetting can be as aggressive as remembering.
   - Stabilizes gradients even at large gate values, which is essential when you push the recurrence length.

2. **Matrix Memory (mLSTM)**  
   - Stores key–value pairs in a $d\times d$ matrix instead of a single scalar.  
   - Proven to approach the retrieval capacity of Transformers while keeping parameter count modest.  
   - Adds almost no extra *parameter* cost, but provides a *higher effective capacity*.

Because neither mechanism adds extra trainable weights, the *parameter budget* of an xLSTM can stay comparable to a Transformer while the *effective capacity* of its memory and gating mechanisms is larger. That explains why xLSTM achieves a lower $A$ without changing the learning‑rate schedule or other training hyper‑parameters.

---

## 4. Practical Take‑aways for Scaling Up

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **Do I need more data or more parameters to see xLSTM’s advantage?** | Both are true, but the advantage shows up even at **$15$ B tokens** and **$350$ M parameters**. |
| **How does the perplexity drop with doubling the parameters?** | Roughly follows the power‑law: $\Delta\mathcal{L}\approx-0.1$. For instance, going from $1$ B to $2$ B parameters reduces perplexity by about $10$ %. |
| **Is the advantage preserved at longer contexts?** | Yes. The paper reports *sequence‑extrapolation* experiments up to $16\,384$ tokens, where xLSTM retains lower perplexity than Transformers or Mamba. |
| **Will xLSTM still win when I go beyond $1$ B parameters?** | The *scaling slope* is essentially identical to other families, so the relative margin (~$5$–$10$ %) should persist. |
| **Do I need to redesign my training pipeline?** | Mostly no. xLSTM can be trained with the same mix‑precision, AdamW, and learning‑rate schedule as a Transformer. The only difference is the use of exponential gating, which is handled by a small CUDA kernel in the research code. |

---

## 5. Future Outlook

The scaling curves suggest that *xLSTM is a serious competitor* for teams chasing next‑generation LLMs. Coupled with its linear‑time context handling and low memory footprint, it offers a sweet spot for:

- **On‑device inference** (constant memory, no quadratic attention cost)
- **Long‑context tasks** (e.g., document summarization, code generation)
- **Transfer learning** (the extra memory capacity seems to transfer nicely to downstream tasks)

There is still room for improvement. The mLSTM component is computationally heavier than the Transformer’s softmax, but the paper’s CUDA kernels have already cut the gap by 50 % compared to a naive implementation. Further parallelization of the matrix updates could bring xLSTM’s decoding latency on par with Transformers on high‑throughput inference servers.

---

## 6. Summary

- **xLSTM follows the same power‑law slope** ($\alpha \approx 0.09$) as Transformers and SSMs.  
- **Its offset is lower by 5–10 %** in perplexity, meaning it consistently outperforms the other families at *all* scales tested.  
- The offset advantage comes from **exponential gating** (dynamic forgetting) and **matrix memory** (high retrieval capacity) that do not inflate the parameter count.  
- The performance advantage persists at *longer context lengths* and *larger token budgets*.  

If you’re planning to scale a language model, consider xLSTM as a compelling alternative to the Transformer. The curves say it will grow with you—just like a Transformer, but with a height advantage that can stay in place.



# Optimizing xLSTM Inference: From CUDA Kernels to High Throughput

High‑performance inference is as important for a new model as its training performance.  
For the xLSTM family, whose core innovations are exponential gating and a novel matrix‑memory, the authors had to address two main engineering challenges:

1. **Sequential dependence in sLSTM blocks** – the hidden‑to‑hidden mixing forces a scan through the time‑steps, which is the main bottleneck for real‑time inference.  
2. **Large \(d\times d\) matrix operations in mLSTM** – the covariance‑style memory update and retrieval involve full‑matrix multiplications, which can be expensive on commodity GPUs if not properly parallelized.

Below we walk through the problem space and the key solutions presented in the paper.

---

## 1. The Inference Bottleneck in xLSTM

### 1.1 sLSTM: Memory Mixing vs. Parallelism  
Standard LSTM cells mix the previous hidden state into the current gate computations.  
In the sLSTM variant this mixing is **within each head** and is weighted by exponential gates. The recurrence requires a left‑to‑right scan:

\[
h_t = o_t \odot \frac{c_t}{n_t}\;,\qquad 
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t
\]

Because each time step depends on the previous one, a naïve implementation must run one scan per generation step. On a GPU this translates to a performance penalty that scales linearly with the context length.

### 1.2 mLSTM: Large Matrix Ops  
The matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is updated by

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\, \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\]

Both the update and the read operation \(\tilde{h}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{|\mathbf{n}_t^\top\mathbf{q}_t|,1\}\) are \(O(d^2)\). While parallelizable in principle, early implementations exposed a 4‑fold slowdown compared to FlashAttention‑style kernels because all matrix operations were executed in a single custom kernel without exploiting lower‑precision math or shared memory.

---

## 2. CUDA Optimizations for sLSTM

To reduce the scan latency, the authors crafted a **register‑level GPU kernel** that respects the dependence order but keeps the per‑time‑step work minimal.

| Design Decision | Why It Helps |
|-----------------|--------------|
| **Lane‑wise scan** | The kernel processes all time‑steps in a warp, reusing registers for \(c_{t-1}\) and \(n_{t-1}\). This keeps the time‑step pipeline fully occupied. |
| **Exponential gating stabilization** | The stabilizer \(m_t\) is computed as a simple max logarithm, staying in registers and avoiding expensive `log`/`exp` calls. |
| **Memory‑local combiners** | All gate expressions \(\tilde{i}_t, \tilde{f}_t, \tilde{o}_t\) are fused into a single launch to reduce global memory traffic. |
| **Batch‑wise fusion** | When generating multiple sequences in parallel, the kernel can bundle them into a single kernel launch, cutting launch overhead by up to 50 %. |

The net result is a sLSTM inference kernel that is *less than two times* slower than the parallel mLSTM kernel, a significant win given that sLSTM blocks often dominate the deep xLSTM stack.

---

## 3. Parallelizing mLSTM in the CUDA Realm

The mLSTM matrix memory can be updated and queried in parallel, leading to a **scan‑free inference mode**:

1. **Pre‑compute all keys, values, and queries** – each token is mapped to \((\mathbf{k}_t, \mathbf{v}_t, \mathbf{q}_t)\) in a single forward pass.  
2. **Parallel accumulation of \(\mathbf{C}\)** – using a *reduction* pattern, each token contributes \(\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) weighted by its input gate \(i_t\).  
3. **Batch‑wise matrix‑vector products** – the read step \(\tilde{h}_t = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{q}_t\) is performed via a batched GEMM, taking full advantage of highly‑optimized cuBLAS kernels.

The authors avoided a custom kernel for the matrix construction; instead, they leveraged **cuBLAS’s batched matrix multiplication** and a few lightweight element‑wise ops. By keeping all tensors in **mixed precision (bf16)** they achieved a speed‑up of ~20 % over full‑precision versions while maintaining numerical stability thanks to the stabilizer state \(m_t\).

---

## 4. Resulting Throughput Numbers

The paper reports the following key metrics for a 1.3 B‑parameter xLSTM on an NVIDIA A100:

| Model | Generation Latency (ms/step) | Throughput (tokens/sec) |
|-------|-----------------------------|------------------------|
| xLSTM (sLSTM + mLSTM mix) | **≈ 6 ms** | **≈ 170 k/tokens** |
| Mamba (SSM) | **≈ 8 ms** | **≈ 125 k** |
| RWKV‑4 (RNN) | **≈ 7 ms** | **≈ 140 k** |
| Llama‑7B (Transformer) | **≈ 35 ms** | **≈ 30 k** |

The linear scaling of xLSTM’s latency with respect to context length is a direct consequence of the scan‑free design for mLSTM and the efficient per‑step kernel for sLSTM. In contrast, the quadratic cost of the Transformer attention mechanism causes the Llama baseline to grow rapidly as the context expands.

---

## 5. Take‑Away: Working Recurrent vs. Transformer

* If you need **constant‑time per step** and **high batch throughput**, xLSTM’s mixed gate & matrix memory approach is a compelling alternative to Transformers, especially on GPUs where GPU‑friendly matrix ops dominate performance.  
* For **very long sequences** (tens of thousands of tokens) the sLSTM’s sequential scan can become a bottleneck; moving more blocks to the parallel mLSTM flavor (e.g., xLSTM[7:1] vs. xLSTM[1:0]) can offset this.  
* Because the matrix memory is independent of sequence length, **cache‑efficiency** is naturally higher than token‑wise key‑value storage in Transformers, leading to *higher decoding throughput* when batching multiple prompts.

---

## 6. Future Directions

The authors anticipate that **FlashAttention‑style kernels** for mLSTM could shave another 10–15 % off latency, while a fully **SIMD‑friendly sLSTM** implementation could bring scan overhead below 10 ms/step on consumer GPUs. Combined with hyperparameter tuning and deeper architectural search, xLSTM has the potential to match or surpass transformer‑based LLMs in both training *and* inference regimes.

---

### TL;DR

- **Challenge**: sLSTM scanning and mLSTM matrix ops hurt inference speed.  
- **Solution**: Register‑level scan‑efficient sLSTM kernel + batched cuBLAS for mLSTM.  
- **Result**: ~6 ms/step and > 170 k tokens/sec on A100, outpacing large transformers in throughput.  
- **Implication**: xLSTM offers a practical, high‑performance alternative for large‑scale language modeling and deployment.
