\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Exponential Gating and the sLSTM

The long‑term research heritage of the LSTM hinges on its gated state‑carrying structure, with the forgetting, inputting, and outputting behaviors governed by sigmoid activations. In *xLSTM* the first major departure from this LSTM canon is the replacement of the sigmoid with an exponential function in the input and forget gates. This seemingly modest change unlocks a host of new dynamical properties: the capacity to revise earlier storage decisions, the ability to keep critical signals from dissipating over long horizons, and the support for a novel form of memory mixing that directly encodes state‑tracking in the recurrent dynamics.

In what follows we dissect this idea in depth, beginning with an intuitive justification for the exponential choice, proceeding to the formal definition of the sLSTM, and ending with a discussion of its implications on the expressive power of recurrent models via synthetic benchmarks.

---

## Motivation for Replacing Sigmoid with Exp on Gates

The standard LSTM employs sigmoid activations, \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))\), for every gate, which bound gate values to the compact interval \([0,1]\). Although this boundedness is beneficial for numerical stability, it also induces an undesirable saturation effect: when a gate approaches either extreme, its gradient collapses, effectively freezing the corresponding signal. This property hampers the network’s ability to *revise* a memory location once it has been written. In a typical state‑tracking task, the network must replace a stored value with a newer, more relevant one; a saturated forget gate, by definition, cannot forget.

If instead we allow the gates to grow unboundedly through an exponential activation, \(\exp(\tilde{g})\), we obtain an unbounded non‑linear mapping that preserves sign and grows fast with its input. An exponential forget gate can therefore increase the decay of old memory entries dramatically, while an exponential input gate can increase the magnitude of new updates arbitrarily. In continuous‑time terms, where the LSTM cell state follows
\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]
the exponential forgetting rate \(f_t\) behaves like a multiplicative decay factor that can be tuned to the empirical relevance of past observations. Crucially, because \(\mathrm{d}f_t/\mathrm{d}\tilde{f}_t = f_t\), any \(\tilde{f}_t\) generated from the hidden‑hidden and input‑hidden linear combinations can be amplified without boundary constraints, allowing the network to enact sharp forgetting or retention on demand.

The decision to use exponentials is further motivated by the experience of the *Fast Weight Programmers* and the *Retention* model, which already exploited exponential updates to implement efficient key–value associations. In those settings, the exponential gating was the bridge between vanilla RNNs and more elaborate memory mechanisms; here we bring that insight back into the core of the LSTM.

---

## The Normalizer State and Its Role in Maintaining Numerical Stability

Exponentials can grow extremely rapidly. Without further constraints \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) can become astronomically large or diminutive, leading to overflow or underflow in floating point computations and in the back‑propagated gradients. The design of the sLSTM introduces an auxiliary scalar called the **normalizer state**, denoted \(n_t\), which is meant to keep track of the cumulative weight that historically has been applied to the incoming updates.

The normalizer is defined recursively in tandem with the cell state:
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t   &= f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t , \\
n_t   &= f_t \, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t ,
\end{aligned}
\]
with the convention \(c_{-1}=0, n_{-1}=0\). By construction,
\[
n_t \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^{t} \Bigl(\prod_{k=s+1}^{t} f_k\Bigr) \, i_s.
\]
Thus \(n_t\) records, in a floating point‑friendly manner, the total “influence mass” that should be summed when evaluating the current cell content.

To prevent runaway growth, we rescale the raw cell content by dividing through by the normalizer:
\[
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]
Because the same linear map of exponential gate coefficients appears in both numerator and denominator, the ratio remains bounded even when \(i_t\) or \(f_t\) individually diverge. This normalization, however, is not sufficient for back‑propagation: the exponential terms in the gradients still become unbounded. Therefore an additional **stabilizer state** \(m_t\) is computed as
\[
m_t \;=\; \max \!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\; \log i_t\bigr),
\]
and the gates are *renormalized* as
\[
\begin{aligned}
i'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\tilde{i}_t - m_t\bigr), \\
f'_t &= \exp\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
Because \(\tilde{i}_t\) may be negative, the subtraction of the stabilizer \(m_t\) caps the exponentials in the forward pass. Equivalently, in log‑domain these manipulated gates are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log i'_t &= \tilde{i}_t - m_t, \\
\log f'_t &= \log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
keeping their logarithms bounded from below. Plugging \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) in place of \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) in the forward equations is mathematically equivalent (the ratios \(\frac{c_t}{n_t}\) and the gradient flow remain unchanged), but numerically it guarantees that during training neither the activations nor their gradients leave the dynamic range of double precision.

## Derivation of the Stabilized Exponential Gating Equations

The derivation of the stabilized gates proceeds in three steps:

1. **Raw Exponential Gates.**  
   \[
   \tilde{g}_t = {\bf w}_g^\top {\bf x}_t + b_g,\quad
   g_t = \exp(\tilde{g}_t)\quad (g \in \{i,f\}).
   \]
2. **Normalizer and Stabilizer.**  
   The normalized cell state must be expressed in terms of \(\log i_t\) and \(\log f_t\). Let us denote \(\alpha_s = \log i_s\) and \(\beta_s = \log f_s\). Then
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   \log c_t &= \log \Biggl(\sum_{s=0}^{t} z_s e^{\alpha_s + \sum_{k=s+1}^t \beta_k}\Biggr)\\
   \log n_t &= \log \Bigl(\sum_{s=0}^t e^{\alpha_s + \sum_{k=s+1}^t \beta_k}\Bigr).
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   Introducing the stabilizer \(m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t-1}, \log i_t)\) ensures that the dominant term in the summations is always subtracted from each exponent, reducing the values inside the logarithm to \(\le 0\).  
3. **Stable Forward Equations.**  
   After stabilizing, we replace \(i_t, f_t\) by \(i'_t, f'_t\) everywhere. This yields the fully stabilized sLSTM equations:
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   c_t   &= f'_t\, c_{t-1} + i'_t \, z_t ,\\
   n_t   &= f'_t\, n_{t-1} + i'_t ,\\
   \tilde{h}_t &= \frac{c_t}{n_t},\\
   h_t   &= o_t\, \tilde{h}_t ,
   \end{aligned}
   \]
   with the same definitions for \(z_t\) and \(o_t\) as in the vanilla LSTM. The proof that these equations preserve the exact forward output is a straightforward induction on \(t\), leveraging the linearity of the recursions after incorporating the normalizer.

## Memory Mixing in the sLSTM: Recurrent Connections and Multi‑Head Design

A hallmark of the sLSTM is the **memory mixing** mechanism that exploits recurrent connections across multiple memory cells. In a conventional LSTM we may stack a vector of cell states \(\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\), each updated independently. The sLSTM allows each cell’s update to be informed by the hidden states of all other cells through a weight matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). Concretely, for head \(h\) and memory cell \(k\) the input to the cell equations becomes
\[
\tilde{z}_{t}^{(k,h)} = {\bf w}_z^\top {\bf x}_t + r_z^{(k,h)}\,h_{t-1}^{(h)} + b_z^{(k,h)},
\]
where \(h_{t-1}^{(h)}\) denotes the hidden state of the *same* head from the previous time step. Here, \(r_z^{(k,h)}\) is a *scalar* recurrent coefficient linking hidden state \(h_{t-1}^{(h)}\) to cell \(k\). Similar recurrent terms \(r_i, r_f, r_o\) appear in the gate computations. By learning a distinct set of \(r\)-coefficients for each cell, the network can **intertwine** cell updates, effectively redistributing learned information across memory slots in a fashion reminiscent of the *attention* mechanism but without explicit pairwise tokens.

The multi‑head design compounds this flexibility. sLSTM defines several independent lanes (heads) each with its own set of recurrent coefficients and head‑wise hidden state. Within a head, cells are allowed to mix, but heads do not communicate directly through mixing. This isolation is intentional: each head can specialise on a distinct pattern of information flow (e.g. syntactic versus semantic cues), while the inter‑head communication is limited to the *output* gating that aggregates the heads. Consequences of this design include:

- **Expressivity**: Multiple heads allow a richer representation of the past without linearly scaling the gating computations; only a modest increase in parameter count suffices.
- **Parallelism**: Even though memory mixing prevents full hardware‑level parallelization, the head‑wise independence permits vectorised operations within a head.
- **State‑tracking**: By coupling cell updates across cells via recurrent weights, sLSTM can encode and modify arbitrary trajectories in state space, a capability absent in standard LSTMs that lack mixing.

## Comparison of sLSTM to Vanilla LSTM on Synthetic State‑Tracking Benchmarks

The practical gains of exponential gating and memory mixing manifest starkly on controlled synthetic tasks engineered to expose the limitations of vanilla LSTMs.

### Nearest‑Neighbor Search

A canonical counter‑example is the *Nearest Neighbor Search* problem: a sequence contains a reference vector followed by a stream of candidate vectors; at the end the network must output the value attached to the nearest candidate. The vanilla LSTM struggles because once a candidate is stored, the forget gate cannot *recall* its relevance after a newer, more similar candidate arrives, owing to its bounded forgetting coefficient. In head‑aware sLSTM, the exponential forgetting gate can be sharply increased whenever a new candidate arrives, causing the old memory to evaporate. Simultaneously, the memory‑mixing recurrent weights allow the new vector to be *immediately* propagated into the other memory cells, thereby revising the previous storage decision. Empirical results show a dramatic reduction in mean squared error compared to the vanilla LSTM, validating that sLSTM successfully revises earlier memories.

### Parity and Regular Grammar Tasks

State‑tracking problems such as the parity or non‑regular language recognition tasks likewise benefit from sLSTM’s architecture. The exponential gates can encode the requisite modular arithmetic by learning to mask out or retain alternating bits precisely. The memory mixing further amplifies the ability to express the combinatorial state space needed to distinguish acceptability or violation conditions. In Synthetic benchmarks, sLSTM consistently achieves perfect or near‑perfect accuracy with fewer parameters than a comparable vanilla LSTM.

From a functional perspective, these empirical findings confirm that the *exponential gating* endows the cell with the ability to alter its update policy on the fly, while *memory mixing* equips the network with a built‑in combinatorial mechanism for coordinated updating of multiple memory slots. Together they lift the sLSTM over the expressive ceiling of standard LSTMs, enabling it to imitate or surpass the performance of architectures that rely on attention‑style conditioning (i.e. Transformers) in recall‑centric settings.

---

In summary, the adoption of exponential gate activations, coupled with the normalizer–stabilizer construction, permits the sLSTM to maintain a stable yet flexible memory dynamic. The enriched memory‑mixing structure and the multi‑head arrangement provide the necessary expressivity to track complex state trajectories while still fitting within the well‑understood LSTM formulation. The resulting sLSTM becomes the foundational building block from which *xLSTM* scales its architectures to billions of parameters, matching and in several regimes surpassing contemporary transformer‑based language models.
