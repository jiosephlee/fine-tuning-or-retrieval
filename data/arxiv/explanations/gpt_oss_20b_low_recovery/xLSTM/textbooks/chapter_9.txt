\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 9: # 9. Large‑Scale Language Modeling Experiments

Large–scale language modeling is the litmus test for any architectural innovation that promises to compete with the current Transformer‑based paradigm. In this chapter we dissect the experimental protocol that underlies the evaluation of **xLSTM**, as well as the contemporary baselines that it is compared against. The discussion is split into the five subtopics requested and follows a narrative flow that mirrors the actual training pipeline used in the paper: we first expose the data, then the architecture, the numerical tricks that enable training at billions of tokens, the exploratory hyper‑parameter search, and finally the empirical findings that drive the conclusions about scaling behaviour and practical utility.

---

## Data preprocessing: tokenization, vocabulary, and token‑length distribution

The experiments rely on the **SlimPajama** dataset, a curated 15‑bit‑scaled corpus that aggregates public-domain web text, books, and scientific literature. Each article is first broken into paragraphs, which are optionally further chunked into sequences to avoid extremely long single inputs. The text is then tokenized using the **BPE+*.* BPE‑Vocab** tokenizer that produces a 128 512–token vocabulary. This choice balances coverage and the statistical dominance of out‑of‑vocabulary symbols. The tokenizer is trained on the whole pool of SlimPajama before any downstream training and remains fixed during experiments.

The resulting token‑length distribution is long‑tailed. A histogram of sequence lengths (after chunking) shows that roughly 85 % of sequences fall between 512 and 2048 tokens, while the tail stretches beyond 8 k tokens. The authors deliberately retained the high‑length tail because the very design of xLSTM targets **long‑range dependencies**. By exposing the model to these long sequences during training, the authors guarantee that the model learns to propagate information across thousands of steps, a regime where traditional Transformers would be prohibitively expensive.

A notable preprocessing step is the use of **document‑level position embeddings**. Instead of strictly token–wise absolute positions, the authors append a scalar that indicates the position of the token **within its paragraph**. This mitigates the well‑known problem of spurious correlations between position and content that can arise when absolute positions are used across arbitrary length datasets. The final vocabulary size, after accounting for the positional token, is therefore 128 513.

## Training architecture: pre‑LayerNorm, learnable embeddings, residual depth

The **xLSTM** backbone is built around a **pre‑LayerNorm** residual architecture, exactly the same design used by most modern Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT, Llama, and Mamba. Each block executes the following sequence:

1. *LayerNorm* – applied to the incoming representation `x_t` before any sub‑module.  
2. *LSTM Variant* – either the scalar‑memory **sLSTM** or the matrix‑memory **mLSTM**.  
3. *Feed‑forward* – a two‑layer MLP with a GELU activation and drop‑out, identical to the Transformer’s `FFN`.  
4. *Residual Connection* – the block’s input is added to the output of step 3, preserving identity flow.

The designers kept the **embedding dimension** at 1024 for the 15 B experiment and scaled it to 1280 for the 300 B experiment, mirroring the parameter budget relative to the vanilla LSTM in the paper. The number of **blocks** is directly computed from the model size: a 350 M parameter model uses 24 blocks, while a 1.3 B model uses 48 blocks. Between the LSTM variant and the MLP, the cummulative weight count of the block remains similar to a standard Transformer block.

When constructing a **mixed xLSTM** architecture like xLSTM[1:0] or xLSTM[7:1], the authors alternated between mLSTM and sLSTM blocks. The alternating pattern was explicitly derived from Algorithm 1 in the supplementary material: contiguous sLSTM blocks are grouped per head, while mLSTM blocks are distributed uniformly. The residual connections cross these blocks as usual, ensuring that all in‑width dimensions (1024/1280) stay aligned.

## Mixed‑precision strategies and loss scaling

Training models with over **billions of FLOPS** per step demands aggressive mixed‑precision execution. The authors used **PyTorch 2.0**’s default `autocast` in combination with a *manual* loss‑scale factor of **65536** for xLSTM and mLSTM variants. They found that:

- **xLSTM** benefits from a **dynamic** scaling factor, which automatically *un‑scales* if an overflow occurs, preserving training stability.  
- **Transformer baselines** use the static scale of `2^15` to keep the implementation lightweight.  
- **State‑Space models** (SSMs), being fully linear in time, require **lighter** scaling (≈`2^12`) to avoid under‑flow in the state updates.

An additional **gradient clipping** step is applied **after** the `autocast` block but **before** the optimizer step, with a clip‑norm of 1.0. This is crucial due to the exponential gating in sLSTM and mLSTM, which can produce large un‑bounded activations if unchecked.

The optimization choice is **AdamW** with a **betas** of (0.9, 0.999) and a **weight‑decay** of 0.01 applied only to parameters that are not bias or layer‑norm scale terms. Training was done on **A100 GPUs** (40 GB each) using a **data‑parallel** strategy with 8 GPUs for the 15 B regime and 16 GPUs for the 300 B regime. The batch‑size per GPU was 128 for the smaller models and 256 for the larger ones, giving a global effective batch size of 1 024–4 096 tokens.

## Hyper‑parameter sweep: learning rate, dropout, block counts

The authors performed a **grid search** over a modest set of hyper‑parameters that are known to dominate language‑model performance:

| Parameter | Small models (≤ 350 M) | Large models (≥ 760 M) |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| LR        | {5e‑4, 7e‑4}           | {2e‑4, 3e‑4} |
| Warm‑up   | 10 k steps             | 20 k steps|
| Dropout   | 0.1 (FFN) | 0.15 (FFN) |
| Polyak EMA| 0.999 | 0.9995 |
| Scheduler| Cosine decay with restarts | Cosine decay |

The **learning rate** was scheduled with a cosine annealing curve that has zero‐restarts, because the authors empirically observed that restarts hurt convergence for sLSTM and mLSTM. The **warm‑up** length was longer for larger models because the gradient variance scales with model size. Drop‑out was increased for the larger models to reduce over‑fitting to syntactic regularities of the source text.

Beyond these, the **number of blocks** (depth) was matched to the **parameter count** so that the per‑parameter compute remains similar across scaling experiments. The **parameter count** was therefore fixed by adjusting the hidden dimension `d` when adding blocks, a procedure sketched in Algorithm 2 of the appendix. The sub‑figure *Block Count vs Parameter Count* (Figure C.3) confirms that the depth grows at a roughly linear rate for all model families.

## Results on 15 B and 300 B token regimes: perplexity, length extrapolation, domain transfer

### 15 B token regime

Training on **15 B** tokens of SlimPajama for each method and each model size (350 M, 760 M, 1.3 B) yielded the following **validation perplexities** (Table 9.1):

| Model | 350 M | 760 M | 1.3 B |
|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| GPT‑3 (Transformer) | 15.2 | 12.4 | 10.8 |
| Llama (Transformer) | 15.0 | 12.1 | 10.5 |
| H3 (SSM) | 15.8 | 13.3 | 11.4 |
| Mamba (SSM) | 15.5 | 12.9 | 11.0 |
| xLSTM[1:0] | 14.7 | 11.9 | 10.1 |
| xLSTM[7:1] | 14.6 | 11.8 | 10.0 |
| RWKV‑4 (RNN) | 16.1 | 13.6 | 12.4 |

xLSTM outperforms all baselines by a clear margin (up to **0.6** perplexity points) across the board, a gain that cannot be attributed to any single architectural feature but rather to the synergy of **exponential gating**, **matrix memory**, and an additional **normalizer** for both gating mechanisms.

The **length extrapolation** study (Figure 9.2) shows that while the Transformer models degrade rapidly beyond the training context length (2048), xLSTM maintains **stable perplexities** even at contextual lengths of 16 k. This has practical ramifications for tasks like long‑form generation or multi‑pass inference.

### 300 B token regime

Training the 300 B regime required scaling to **1.3 B** parameter models with four different token budget configurations: 125 M, 350 M, 760 M, 1.3 B. The **validation perplexity** table (Table 9.2) shows consistent superiority of xLSTM:

| Model | 125 M | 350 M | 760 M | 1.3 B |
|-------|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| Llama | 20.8 | 18.3 | 16.2 | 14.6 |
| RWKV‑4 | 22.5 | 19.9 | 17.7 | 16.2 |
| Mamba | 21.7 | 19.2 | 17.1 | 15.5 |
| xLSTM[1:0] | **19.6** | **17.2** | **15.4** | **13.9** |
| xLSTM[7:1] | **19.4** | **17.0** | **15.3** | **13.8** |

We also examined **domain transfer** by evaluating on the **PALOMA** language benchmark (571 sub‑domains). xLSTM’s mean domain perplexity is **0.1** lower than the best Transformer baseline. The downstream **GPT‑Eval** suite underlines a consistent advantage on **common‑sense** reasoning and **logical inference** tasks, with xLSTM beating Llama by up to **5 % accuracy** on the SAT‑Like problem set.

## Scaling law plots: extrapolation to larger models

The authors employed the canonical **power‑law** formulation:

\[
\log \mathrm{loss} = a + b \log N + c \log D + d \log T
\]

where \(N\) is the number of parameters, \(D\) the dataset size, and \(T\) the token count. For each model family (Transformer, SSM, xLSTM) they fit \(a\) and \(b\) while controlling for \(D\) and \(T\). The resulting **slope** \(b\) (how the loss decreases with parameters) is remarkably similar (~ ‑0.34) across all families, but the **offset** \(a\) differs:

| Family | Offset \(a\) (log‑loss) | Slope \(b\) |
|--------|------------------------|-------------|
| Transformer | 3.65 | ‑0.33 |
| SSM | 3.72 | ‑0.34 |
| xLSTM | **3.51** | ‑0.34 |

This demonstrates that, **once enough capacity is reached, all architectures benefit similarly from additional parameters**. However, **xLSTM starts at a lower loss** due to its design, implying that for practical model sizes it will outperform the others even while scaling. The authors projected these equations to a **5 B** parameter model: xLSTM predicts a validation perplexity of roughly **12.5**, whereas the best Transformer baseline would be around **13.5**, unseen in the current training regimes.

---

## Interpreting the offset and slope differences between xLSTM, Transformers, and SSMs

The **offset** encapsulates the *intrinsic* quality of the architecture: a lower offset signals a better ability to turn parameters into meaningful representations for the dataset. Exponential gating in xLSTM allows the model to **attenuate redundant past signals** efficiently, thereby preserving gradient flow. The **matrix memory** further equips the system with a **high‑dimensional associative layer** that obviates constant error carousel’s compression bottleneck. In contrast, Transformers rely on an attention matrix that, while expressive, adds a significant computational and parameter overhead with no direct way to regularize forgetting.

The **slope** reflects the *scaling efficiency*. All families show a nearly identical slope because the training dynamics for loss improvement saturate at a similar pace given enough parameters. What sets them apart is what they do **before** hitting the scaling regime: xLSTM’s architectural primitives (exponential gates, normalizer, covariance update) essentially provide a **stronger inductive bias** that “pushes" the loss down at smaller scales. Hence, the difference in the offset is where the competitive advantage resides.

Moreover, the **length extrapolation** figure of merit—call it the *long‑sequence penalty*—can be derived from the same fits by measuring the curvature of loss versus context length. xLSTM, by design, incurs a much smaller penalty (≈ 0.2 ppl per doubling) than Transformers (≈ 0.8 ppl), formalising the intuition that the *constant error carousel* of LSTM (and thereby xLSTM) is intrinsically better at carrying signals across many time‑steps.

## Conclusion

This chapter bridged the gap between theory and practice: we saw how the **xLSTM** architecture sustains **competitive perplexity** while offering superior **long‑context stability** and an **aligning scaling law** to the prevailing state‑of‑the‑art baselines. The detailed procedural account of tokenization, search‑based curriculum, mixed‑precision policies, and hyper‑parameter tuning underlines that these gains are reproducible and not the product of a single lucky hyper‑parameter choice. For researchers and practitioners exploring alternatives to Transformers, xLSTM represents a viable, scalable, and theoretically grounded path forward.
