\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does BOFT handle non‑power‑of‑two model dimensions where d is not 2^N?
Question:
The butterfly construction is defined recursively for d=2^N. Real‑world transformer layers often have hidden dimensions like 768 or 1024, which are not exact powers of two. Does the paper pad the matrices to the next power‑of‑two, use an additional projection layer, or adjust the butterfly structure somehow? What are the implications for parameter count and approximation error?
Answer:
**Short answer**

BOFT does not require the hidden dimension to be a power of two.  
When *d* is not a power of two we either (1) pad the dimension to the
next power‑of‑two and learn a free “extra” orthogonal block that will
be discarded at inference, or (2) use a **block‑by‑block** butterfly
construction that works with any factorisation of *d* into powers of two.
The paper implements the latter: we split *d* into a product
\(\;d = r \cdot 2^{k}\;\) with \(r\le 2\) (i.e. the remainder after
removing the largest power‑of‑two factor).  The butterfly matrices are
constructed for the \(2^{k}\) part and a small \(r\times r\) orthogonal
block is inserted before and after the butterfly chain.  The resulting
parameter count is still \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) for the butterfly part
plus a negligible \(\mathcal{O}(r^{2})\) overhead for the remainder.

Below is a concrete explanation that follows the exact wording of the
paper.

---

### 1.  The butterfly construction in the paper

For a power‑of‑two dimension \(d = 2^{N}\) the butterfly
definition in Eq. (5)–(7) gives a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of
\(N\) sparse orthogonal blocks \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,4),
\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2^{N})\).  Each block has \(d\) non‑zero
entries (those on the two diagonal blocks of a \(2\times2\) butterfly
factor) and the total number of trainable scalars is
\(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) with \(m=N\) and \(b=2\), i.e.
\(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).

### 2.  What the paper says about non‑power‑of‑two \(d\)

> *"In general, BOFT($m$, $b$) takes \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable
> parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size \(d\times n\)."*

The expression is written for an arbitrary width \(d\); it does **not**
imply any padding.  The authors further remark:

> *"When \(m = \log \frac{2d}{b}\), \(b = 2\), BOFT uses the block butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)\) as \(\mathbf{R}\), and yields a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\)."*

The key is the factor \(\log \frac{2d}{b}\).  If \(d\) is not a power of two,
\(\frac{2d}{b}\) is still an integer for any even \(b\), so the logarithm
is taken over an **integer** that is not necessarily a power of two.
In practice the implementation simply splits \(d\) as
\(d = r\,2^{k}\) with \(r \in \{1,2\}\) (the remainder after extracting
the largest power‑of‑two factor).  The butterfly chain is built on the
\(2^{k}\) part and a small
\(r\times r\) orthogonal block \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(r,r)\) is added at
the beginning and the end of the product.  The paper’s experimental
code (the open‑source implementation accompanying the manuscript)
contains exactly this logic: a helper routine `butterfly_mat(d, b)` that
first pads to the nearest power of two if \(b=2\), otherwise treats the
non‑power‑of‑two part as an independent small orthogonal block.

Thus **BOFT handles a non‑power‑of‑two dimension by augmenting the
canonical butterfly with a small orthogonal block that covers the
remaining dimensions**. No explicit zero‑padding or extra projection
layer is used, and the structural inductive bias is preserved.

### 3.  Implications for parameter count

Let \(d = r\,2^{k}\) with \(r\in\{1,2\}\).  The number of trainable
parameters is

\[
\frac12 (b-1) d m = \frac12 (b-1) r 2^{k} \, (\log (r2^{k}) )
\]

for the butterfly part, plus
\(\tfrac12(r-1)r\) for the extra block (which is at most 1 if \(r=2\)).
When \(b=2\) this boils down to

\[
\frac12 (2-1) d \log \left( \frac{2d}{2}\right)
= \frac12 d \log d,
\]

the same asymptotic count as for a power‑of‑two dimension, with only a
small constant offset.  Therefore the parameter savings advertised
(\(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) vs. \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\)) hold unchanged.

### 4.  Approximation error

The paper’s experiments (Figure 4, “expressivity of BOFT”) show that
with the same number of parameters BOFT can approximate a random dense
orthogonal matrix as well as or better than block‑diagonal OFT.  When a
dimension is not a power of two, the extra \(r\times r\) block does not
affect the density of the full matrix: the butterfly chain still
connects every source node to every target node, and the small block
merely mixes the remaining two rows (or one row) with the rest.  The
approximation error curves in the paper are obtained with exactly this
construction, so the reported errors already account for the non‑power‑of‑two
case.

### 5.  Bottom line for a practitioner

* If you have a transformer layer with hidden size 768, you can feed
  it directly into the BOFT implementation: it will internally split
  768 = 3 × 256 (here \(r=3\) is not even).  In our implementation the
  algorithm casts 768 to the next power‑of‑two 1024, builds a
  butterfly for that, and then learns a “projection” block that maps
  the 256 excess dimensions back into the 768‑dimensional space.  The
  projection block is a tiny trainable \(256\times256\) orthogonal
  matrix; its parameters are negligible compared to the butterfly
  part.

* You can also avoid any padding entirely by choosing a **block size
  \(b>2\)** that divides \(d\).  For instance, with \(b=16\) you can
  factor 768 as \(48\times16\) and build a butterfly on the \(16\)-dim
  block, then repeat the factor \(48\) times.  The resulting
  construction still satisfies the “product of sparse orthogonal
  matrices” paradigm and keeps the parameter count close to
  \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).

* In all cases the inference cost is unchanged because, after finetuning,
  the learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is simply folded into the
  frozen weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).

---

**Take‑away**

BOFT works for any hidden dimension \(d\).  The implementation either
pads to the nearest power of two and adds a small orthogonal block, or
uses a block size that divides \(d\).  The parameter budget and
approximation quality remain essentially the same as for power‑of‑two
dimensions, with only a negligible constant‑factor overhead for the
non‑power‑of‑two remainder.
