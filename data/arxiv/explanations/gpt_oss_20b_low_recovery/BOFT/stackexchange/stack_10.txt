\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Can BOFT be combined with LoRA or other low‑rank adaptations for even greater efficiency?
Question:
Since LoRA adds a low‑rank update of the form WA+VB^T, and BOFT multiplies the weight by an orthogonal matrix, it seems possible to apply both simultaneously. Would such a hybrid model benefit from both sparsity and low‑rank structure, and how would the joint optimization be formulated given the different parameter‑count and regularization effects?
Answer:
**Answer**

Shortly put, a “BOFT‑LoRA” hybrid is perfectly feasible: you can keep the *orthogonal* brain‑shape of BOFT (a product of butterfly‑structured orthogonal factors) and, at the same time, add a *low‑rank* adjustment to the overall weight matrix. In practice this means that the functional form of the fine‑tuned weight will become

\[
W^{\text{fin}} \;=\;\bigl(R \;+\; A B^{\top}\bigr)\,W^{0},
\qquad 
R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)} \;\in\;\mathcal{O}(d),
\]

where \(W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is the frozen pre‑trained weight, \(R\) is the standard BOFT orthogonal matrix, and \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\) are the LoRA low‑rank factors (with rank \(r\ll\min(d,n)\)).  
The two modules are independent: \(R\) is trained under the orthogonality constraint (Cayley parameterisation or Givens rotations) and BE \(R\) can be regarded as a *filter* that keeps the overall transformation close to an isometry; \(A B^{\top}\) is an *additive* rank‑\(r\) perturbation that brings the expressive power of LoRA into the picture.  Because the two terms are linear, the gradient w.r.t. each parameter can be obtained by the standard chain rule, so joint optimisation is straightforward:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_R}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (RW^{0})}\,W^{0\top}, \\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (AB^{\top}W^{0})}\,W^{0\top}B,\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial B}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (AB^{\top}W^{0})}\,W^{0\top}A,
\end{aligned}
\]
and the classical LoRA update rules apply to \(A\) and \(B\).  The orthogonal component is updated exactly as in BOFT, i.e. by back‑propagating through the product of butterfly factors and projecting the gradient onto the tangent space of the orthogonal manifold (the Cayley transform already guarantees that the constraint is preserved).

---

### Why do we expect a hybrid to be useful?

| Property | BOFT | LoRA | Hybrid |
|----------|------|------|--------|
| **Sparsity** | Operates on a product of sparse \(2\times2\) butterfly blocks (overall \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters). | Adds only \(\mathcal{O}(r(d+n))\) parameters; the low‑rank matrices are dense but low‑dimensional. | Both sparsity structures are retained → potentially fewer parameters than each alone. |
| **Expressivity** | Generates a *dense* orthogonal matrix, preserving spectrum but remaining within a structured subset. | Freely learns any rank‑\(r\) update, breaking the isometry and possibly overfitting. | The orthogonal head imposes an inductive bias (spectrum preservation) while the LoRA tail gives extra degrees of freedom to capture task‑specific deformations. |
| **Regularisation** | Orthogonality acts as a strong regulariser; Frobenius norm ≈ \(d\). | No explicit regulariser beyond the rank budget; often requires careful tuning of \(r\). | Orthogonal head regularises the base transformation, softening LoRA’s tendency to drift far from the pre‑trained weights. |
| **Inference cost** | None (the learned orthogonal matrix can be folded into \(W^0\) before inference). | None (the low‑rank term can also be folded). | Same: both components can be fused into a single matrix once training finishes. |

The hybrid therefore **combines the best of both worlds**: BOFT gives you a compact, isometric deformation that preserves the semantic geometry already present in the pre‑trained model; LoRA gives you a cheap way to correct residual errors or adapt to new subtasks that are not well captured by a mere orthogonal transformation.

---

### Parameter count and regularisation

Assume \(d=10\,000\) and you choose a BOFT factorisation with \(m=\log_2 d = 14\) and block size \(b=2\).  
The number of *effective* trainable parameters for the orthogonal part is

\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm = \frac{1}{2}(1)(10\,000)(14)=70\,000.
\]

If you additionally add a LoRA rank \(r=64\), then the extra parameters are

\[
r(d+n) \approx 64\times(10\,000+10\,000)=1.28\times10^6.
\]

So the hybrid still keeps the orthogonal part tiny (\(\sim 1.2\%\) of the LoRA part).  
In practice the rank can be chosen much smaller (e.g. \(r=8\) or \(r=16\)), which would reduce the LoRA overhead to only a few hundred‑thousand parameters while still giving the hybrid considerable expressive power.

Because the orthogonal component is explicitly constrained, the optimisation dynamics become more stable. The LoRA component can be regularised in the usual way (dropout, weight decay, or a spectral penalty on \(A B^\top\)). Overall, you obtain a *gradually increasing* hypothesis class: first a rigid yet recognizable transformation (orthogonal), then a gentle, low‑rank correction (LoRA).

---

### Practical tips

1. **Order of application matters** – the product in the paper uses \(R W^{0}\) first; you can also try \((R+AB^\top)W^{0}\) or \(W^{0}(R+AB^\top)\) depending on whether the post‑ or pre‑multiplication makes more sense for the specific layer (e.g. self‑attention weight matrices are usually left‑multiplication). The gradients are independent, so you can experiment safely.

2. **Learning rates** – set the learning rate for the LoRA factors slightly higher (e.g. 5×) than for the BOFT butterfly factors, because the orthogonal part is already stabilised by the Jacobian constraint.

3. **Checkpoint fusion** – after fine‑tuning, multiply \(R+AB^\top\) into the frozen \(W^{0}\) once, so inference cost remains unchanged. This is exactly how single‑module BLOOM or Stable‑Diffusion pipelines perform with LoRA.

4. **Monitoring spectrum** – during training, monitor the singular values of the updated weight \(W^{\text{fin}}\). The Poisson‐like “spectrum preserving” behaviour of BOFT may be slightly perturbed by LoRA. If you see drift, add a small \(\ell_2\) penalty on the top‑left \(r\) singular values of \(A B^\top\).

---

### When might the hybrid *not* help?

* **Very low‑rank regimes** – if your downstream task is highly linear and can be captured by a very small rank (e.g. \(r=2\)), adding an orthogonal fly‑over may not help and could even hinder because the overall map becomes unnecessarily rigid.
* **Memory‑tight embedded scenarios** – the orthogonal factor multiplies assembly cost, so if you can only afford a single LoRA rank \(r=2\), the extra butterfly parameters (even \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\)) might exceed your memory budget.

In most realistic settings, however, a BOFT–LoRA hybrid will give you the *compactness* of BOFT’s structured orthogonality and the *expressivity* of LoRA’s low‑rank updates, yielding a more versatile, regularised fine‑tuning scheme that can be applied to a wide variety of large foundation models.
