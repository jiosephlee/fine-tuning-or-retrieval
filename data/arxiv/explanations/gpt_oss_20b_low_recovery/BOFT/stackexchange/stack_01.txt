\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley parameterization ensure the learned orthogonal matrix stays exactly orthogonal during BOFT training?
Question:
In BOFT we define each butterfly component using a skew‑symmetric parameter ∫ such that R=(I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1}. I understand that if Q is skew‑symmetric then (I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1} is orthogonal, but could the Jacobian or numerical errors during back‑propagation cause the constraint to drift? How is orthogonality enforced in practice, and are there any stability tricks (e.g. re‑projection, step‑size restrictions) that the paper uses to keep ∫ in the skew‑symmetric manifold?
Answer:
**Short answer**

BOFT keeps the orthogonal constraint *exactly* during training.  
Each butterfly component is parameterised by a skew–symmetric matrix \(Q\) and a Cayley transform  

\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\; .
\]

Because the transform is a rational function, the orthogonality identity  

\[
R^{\top}R = I
\]

holds for *any* real \(Q\) (as long as \(I-Q\) is invertible).  The only thing that matters for the optimization is that the **numerical evaluation** of \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) remains accurate during forward and backward passes.  BOFT therefore relies on the following two practical measures:

1. **Small learning rates & identity initialization** – the paper initializes every \(Q\) with zeros (so \(R=I\)) and applies a very small step size. This keeps \(\|Q\|\) small → the matrix \((I-Q)\) stays well‑conditioned and the inversion is numerically stable.
2. **Automatic differentiation of the Cayley formula** – PyTorch’s autograd takes the exact derivative of the function above.  Because the Jacobian is obtained through the same numerical routine that computes the forward pass, any drift that might happen if one merely updated \(R\) directly is avoided.  The derivative is

   \[
   \frac{\partial R}{\partial Q} \;=\; 2(I-Q)^{-T}(I+Q) (I-Q)^{-1},
   \]

   and this is applied exactly in back‑prop.  No re‑projection or clipping is needed.

If, in a very unlikely event, the condition number of \(I-Q\) becomes large (e.g. due to an accidental blow‑up of some \(Q_{ij}\)), the division by zero or overflow would trigger a NaN in the loss; the usual training‑time safety checks (e.g. loss‑clipping or gradient clipping) would immediately stop the optimisation so the parameter never drifts.

---

### Why the constraint does not “drift”

A matrix \(R\) is orthogonal iff \(R^{\top}R = I\).  
Suppose we compute \(R\) as a floating‑point expression from \(Q\).  Even after many forward–backward mini‑steps, as long as the *forward* evaluation of the Cayley transform is exact (up to machine precision), the resulting \(R\) will still satisfy \(R^{\top}R = I\) up to round‑off error.  The underlying reason is that the Cayley transform is *exactly* orthogonal algebraically; the numerical error only comes from the finite‑precision inversion of \((I-Q)\).  Because \((I-Q)\) is kept nonsingular and well‑conditioned by the small learning rate, the round‑off remains on the order of machine epsilon (≈\(10^{-16}\) in double precision), which is far below any training‑time drift.

In contrast, methods that simply apply a gradient update on \(R\) (e.g. multiplying by a random matrix) would need a *sub‑step* projection back onto the orthogonal set after every update; otherwise the orthogonality would degrade.

---

### Practical tricks that appear in the paper

| Trick | What it does | Why it matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Identity initialization** | \(Q=0 \Rightarrow R=I\) | Starts the optimisation exactly at the identity, so the first few gradients are modest. |
| **Very small learning rate (e.g. \(10^{-4}\))** | Keeps \(\|Q\|\) small | Prevents \((I-Q)\) from becoming ill‑conditioned. |
| **Drop‑out is *multiplicative*** | Randomly replaces butterfly components with identities | Helps avoid over‑fitting without breaking the orthogonality constraint. |
| **No projection or clipping** | The orthogonality is maintained by the transform itself | Avoids expensive re‑projection operations. |

These are the only stability tricks reported in the BOFT paper.  Nothing else (e.g. re‑projection, line‑search, special optimisers) is needed.

---

### Final take‑away

- The Cayley parameterisation guarantees analytic orthogonality; it is a *hard* constraint that does not depend on the particular values of \(Q\).  
- Numerical drift is practically negligible because the update step keeps \(Q\) small and the inversion of \((I-Q)\) stays well‑conditioned.  
- The paper therefore does **not** re‑project, nor does it impose any additional step‑size restrictions beyond a modest learning rate; it relies on the inherent orthogonality of the Cayley transform and on careful numerical implementation.  

So, in short, BOFT keeps the learned matrix exactly orthogonal by construction, and the only practical safeguards are a clean initialization, a small learning rate, and the multiplicative‑dropout trick to maintain regularisation.
