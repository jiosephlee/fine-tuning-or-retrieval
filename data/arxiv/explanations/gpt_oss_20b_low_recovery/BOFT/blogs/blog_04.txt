\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# The Trade‑off Between Expressivity and Generalization in Finetuning

Modern foundation models such as GPT‑4, ViT‑Large and Stable Diffusion can contain hundreds of billions of parameters.  Fine‑tuning these giants on a specific downstream task is no longer a simple “plug‑and‑play” problem – we have to conserve as many of the pre‑training learned signals as possible while still allowing the model to adapt.  Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA, BitFit and, more recently, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) have made it possible to update *just a few* knobs while keeping the rest of the network frozen.

The paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, a PEFT technique that replaces the dense orthogonal matrices in OFT with a product of sparse butterfly matrices.  At first glance, this looks like a purely engineering trick, but behind the scenes lies a neat theory that explains **why a carefully chosen structure can actually generalise better than an unconstrained one**.

In this post we unpack that theory in plain English, tie it back to experimental evidence, and finish with concrete take‑aways for anyone who wants to nudge a large model toward a new task without breaking the bank (or the model).

---

## 1. The two sides of the equation

| **Expressivity** | **Generalisation** |
|------------------|--------------------|
| Ability to represent *any* transformation (e.g., a full dense orthogonal matrix) | Ability to stay close to the pre‑training knowledge and avoid over‑fitting to a small downstream dataset |
| Larger hypothesis space | Stronger inductive bias |

### Expressivity in a nutshell

Think of the weight update as a *matrix* \( R \) that we multiply the frozen weight \( W^{0} \) on.  If we allow \( R \) to be any \( d\times d \) orthogonal matrix, we can reach **every** orthogonal transformation because:

\[
R \in O(d) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{any pair of orthonormal columns can be rotated into any other pair.}
\]

So in theory, the model has *complete flexibility* to re‑shape its features to fit the new task.

### Generalisation in a nutshell

However, too much flexibility is often the enemy of generalisation, especially when the downstream dataset is small.  Imagine you have a kilogram‑weight model trained on an enormous dataset; you suddenly ask it to predict stocks for the next day.  If you let the weights wiggle freely, they will likely over‑fit to noise in that tiny stock‑price sample, losing the general pattern it learned from the big data.

PEFT methods strike a balance: they keep most of the original weights *exactly* the same, while allowing a *structured* change that is still expressive enough to solve the new problem.

---

## 2. Why a *structured* orthogonal matrix can be better

### The classic OFT bottleneck

OFT keeps the new weight \( W^{\text{new}} = R W^{0} \) with a *single* dense orthogonal matrix \( R \).  
- **Pros**: Maximal expressivity.  
- **Cons**: \( d^2 \) trainable parameters – not parameter‑efficient; and the update can be noisy, especially early in training.

A simple fix is to split \( R \) into blocks (\( R = \text{diag}(R_{1}, …, R_{r}) \)).  
- **Pros**: Fewer parameters (only \( br \) non‑zero entries if block size is \( b \)).  
- **Cons**: The update is *too brittle* – each block sees only one part of the feature space, so the model can’t express transformations that mix those parts.

This sparse, block‑diagonal update works pretty well, but it forces the model to remember that “features 1–32 should never talk to features 33–64”.  That is an inductive bias that may be *wrong* for some downstream tasks.

### Enter the butterfly

The *butterfly structure* is a classic construction from the Cooley‑Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).  A butterfly matrix is built as a *product* of many **dense but highly sparse** matrices.  Each factor only couples a few pairs of neurons, but after a few stages every neuron can influence every other one.

Key facts:

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Sparse** | Each factor has only \( O(d) \) nonzero entries. |
| **Dense composite** | The product yields a *full* orthogonal matrix (no zero entries). |
| **Parameter‑efficient** | We need only \( O(d\log d) \) free parameters (one per butterfly factor). |
| **Structured inductive bias** | The shape mirrors classic fast transforms (FFT, Hadamard), which are known to capture many useful symmetries. |

Thus, BOFT uses a *product* of \( m=\log d \) orthogonal butterfly components, each parameterised by just a few \( 2\times2 \) rotational blocks, to obtain an overall transformation that is both expressive *and* regularised.

---

## 3. Theoretical backing: BOFT is “more expressive” **but** with “better bias”

In the paper’s Theorem 1, the authors prove:

> **Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT).**  
> For any block size \( b \), a BOFT matrix \( R(m,b) \) is *at least as expressive* as a block‑diagonal OFT matrix with the same \( b \).  
> Moreover, by chaining enough butterfly components (\( m = \log d \)), BOFT can approximate *any* orthogonal matrix of size \( d \).

Put differently:  
- If you use **one** butterfly component (\( m=1 \)), you have the same number of free parameters as the block‑diagonal OFT but with a *lighter* sparse pattern that still connects across groups.  
- If you use **more** components (\( m>1 \)), you get more degrees of freedom while still keeping the total parameter count low (\( O(d\log d) \)).

But *more expressivity* is not a free lunch.  The authors argue that BOFT finds a **sweet spot**:  

1. **Expressive enough** to finish the downstream task.  
2. **Structured enough** to avoid over‑fitting.  

The reasoning is that the butterfly’s sparse factors act like a *tutor* that encourages the model to learn transformations that respect a *global pattern* (like the Fourier basis).  This pattern discourages arbitrary noise in the parameter updates while still allowing the model to rotate between any two points in feature space eventually.

---

## 4. Experiments that confirm the theory

| Task | Method | Params (≈)* | Accuracy (%) |
|------|--------|------------------|---------------|
| ImageNet (ViT‑Large) | OFT (full) | 0.8 M | 84.3 |
| ImageNet | OFT (full) | 0.8 M | 83.7 |
| ImageNet | BOFT, \(m=\log d, b=2\) | 0.06 M | 84.1 |
| GLUE (BERT‑Base) | LoRA | 0.53 M | 81.2 |
| GLUE | BOFT | 0.04 M | 82.4 |
| Stable Diffusion (text‑to‑image) | OFT (block) | 0.92 M | 74.5 |
| SD | BOFT | 0.08 M | 75.7 |

\*Params count only the *trainable* part of the PEFT module.

Key observations:

- **BOFT achieves comparable or better accuracy** with an **order of magnitude fewer** trainable parameters.  
- In text‑to‑image generation, the butterfly-based BOFT improves FID scores, showing better generalisation to new prompts.  
- Adding a *multiplicative dropout* to BOFT’s butterfly components further prevents over‑fitting on tiny datasets – a direct benefit of the structured sparsity.

---

## 5. Why do “simpler” patterns often win?

1. **Implicit Bias** – A butterfly structure embeds a *single* well‑studied transform (FFT).  Models trained with such a bias remember global symmetries that are often present in visual or language data (e.g., periodicity, locality).  
2. **Reduced Search Space** – With fewer free parameters, the optimisation problem is easier; stochastic gradients discover a good solution faster and stick to it.  
3. **Stability** – Orthogonal transformations preserve norms, which keeps the optimisation trajectory bounded and mitigates the exploding/vanishing gradient problem.  

In short, *less is more* when the “less” still covers the *right* parts of the hypothesis space.  BOFT’s answer is *yes, modern tasks benefit from a controlled shuffling of features*.

---

## 6. Practical take‑aways

1. **Use BOFT for fine‑tuning large vision or language models** when you only have a few thousand labelled examples.  
2. **Start with the minimal butterfly (b=2, m=log d)** – this gives you \( O(d\log d) \) parameters and usually outperforms block‑diagonal OFT.  
3. **Initialize all butterfly components as identity** (Cayley transform with zero skew‑symmetric matrix).  
4. **Add multiplicative dropout** (drop whole butterfly blocks) if your downstream dataset is extremely small.  
5. **Post‑training, fuse the learned orthogonal matrix into the base weights** – no extra inference cost.

---

## 7. Open questions

- **Training speed:** Each butterfly factor adds a matrix multiplication per forward pass.  Fast implementation or lattice‑accelerated inference can help.  
- **Topology choice:** Butterfly is one of many efficient information‑exchange graphs.  Exploring other network topologies might yield even better trade‑offs.  
- **Theory‑to‑practice gap:** While BOFT can represent *any* orthogonal matrix with enough factors, in practice fewer factors may suffice.  When is “just enough”?

---

### Bottom line

Orthogonal Butterfly demonstrates that **structured, sparse updates can match or exceed the generalisation performance of unconstrained methods**.  It shows that *expressivity* and *generalisation* are not opposites – you can have a highly expressive transformation as long as it’s regularised by a meaningful structure.  For anyone looking to adapt large foundation models with limited compute or data, BOFT offers a clean, theoretical, and empirically sound recipe.
