# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Needs Butterfly – An Easy‑to‑Understand Primer

Large foundation models—think GPT‑4, Stable Diffusion, or ViT‑Large—have become the bedrock of modern AI.  Their sheer size (hundreds of billions of weights) makes training from scratch impossible for most teams, which means *adapting* a pre‑trained model to a new task is the most practical way to get speed and performance.  Several fine‑tuning recipes exist, but many trade‑off between **how many parameters you tweak** and **how well you keep the original knowledge**.  One principled approach to this trade‑off is *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT).  Unfortunately, OFT can still bring a large parameter burden.  In this post we’ll explain two things:

1.  Why preserving the *angles* between neurons is a good idea for fine‑tuning.
2.  How a classic butterfly pattern from the fast Fourier transform turns a dense orthogonal matrix into a *parameter‑efficient* form.

We’ll keep the math light, use intuition‑heavy analogies, and stay grounded in the original research paper.

---

## 1. Orthogonality: The “Keep the Same Vibe” Rule

### 1.1 The Pre‑training Knowledge

When a model like GPT‑4 learns, each linear layer can be visualised as a matrix of weights.  Think of each column of that matrix as an *expert neuron*: given an input vector, that neuron contributes a weighted dot‑product to the output.  During pre‑training the model discovers a rich set of these experts, and the angles (i.e., pair‑wise dot products) between every pair of them capture the relationships that the model has learned.

### 1.2 What Happens When We Fine‑Tune?

When we start fine‑tuning on a new dataset we usually tweak many parameters.  If we change the weights arbitrarily, we risk **shaking all those angles**.  The model may forget useful relations it discovered before—like how “cat” and “animal” are related in language or how a particular visual pattern correlates with texture in vision.  To mitigate this, we restrict the fine‑tuning transformation to an *orthogonal matrix*.

### 1.3 Orthogonal matrices preserve angles

Mathematically, an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$.  Multiply a vector $\mathbf{x}$ by $\mathbf{R}$ and its length stays the same; all pairwise angles between vectors (and thus between neurons) stay exactly the same.  That means the pre‑training knowledge encoded in the geometry of the weight matrix remains intact.

### 1.4 How OFT Works in Practice

Suppose the original weight matrix is $\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.

| Original Model | After OFT |
|----------------|------------|
| $\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$ | $\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}$ |

* $\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is a learned orthogonal matrix.
* The forward pass is unchanged other than multiplying $\mathbf{R}$ on the left.
* Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal, the *shape* of the weight matrix (and thus the angles between its columns) is preserved exactly.

## 2. The Parameter‑Bottleneck of OFT

### 2.1 Why OFT Needs Many Parameters

An arbitrary $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has roughly $d^2$ degrees of freedom.  For a transformer layer with $d=12\,288$ (a common dimension), that is **hundreds of millions** of trainable values – far too many for a “parameter‑efficient” fine‑tuning scheme.

### 2.2 What the Original OFT Does

The original paper introduced a *block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrix: split the $d$ dimensions into $r$ blocks and learn an independent orthogonal matrix for each block.  If each block is of size $b=d/r$, then we only have $r \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ trainable parameters (thanks to the skew‑symmetric Cayley parameterization).  This shrinks the memory cost dramatically.

### 2.3 The Trade‑Off

- **Pros**: Huge reduction in parameters, easy to implement.
- **Cons**: The block‑diagonal pattern forces neurons from different blocks to evolve independently; many useful cross‑block interactions are lost.  In practice, this limits the *expressivity* of the fine‑tuning transformation.

We can think of a block‑diagonal matrix as letting each department in a company work in isolation even though the whole organization benefits from collaboration.

---

## 3. A New Angle: Dense Orthogonal From Sparse Pieces

The key insight: *We can build a fully dense orthogonal matrix not by storing all its entries, but by multiplying together a handful of “sparse” orthogonal matrices.*  Imagine writing a long, complex recipe, but each step only uses a few ingredients.  The overall dish is rich, yet you never needed a gigantic pantry.

But how can multiplying sparse matrices yield a *dense* product?  The answer lies in **information transmission** and, specifically, the *butterfly* structure from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

---

## 4. Information Transmission View

### 4.1 The Picture

Visualise a $d \times d$ matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as a connectivity graph:

* Nodes on the left side: $d$ source neurons.
* Nodes on the right side: $d$ target neurons.
* An edge (non‑zero entry) indicates that information can flow directly.

If $\mathbf{R}$ is dense, every source can talk directly to every target—like a full mesh network.  Now think of factorising $\mathbf{R}$ into $\mathbf{B}_m\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_1$.  Each $\mathbf{B}_i$ can be seen as a *layer* of a multi‑stage network, where edges only connect adjacent layers.  The composition of all layers still lets every source reach every target, but the *total number of edges* (trainable parameters) may be much smaller.

### 4.2 Desired Properties

1. **Dense connectivity** – the overall network must let any source talk to any target.
2. **Minimal free edges** – the total number of edges (or non‑zero entries) should be as small as possible while still respecting orthogonality.

With orthogonality, each matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ must be *invertible* and preserve norms, which adds a subtle constraint on how many edges can be free.  But it turns out we can shortcut the construction using the celebrated **butterfly** graph.

---

## 5. Butterfly Structure – The Fast‑Fourier Secret

### 5.1 What is a Butterfly?

In the Cooley–Tukey FFT, a $2^N$‑point transform is broken down recursively into smaller $2^{N-1}$ transforms.  The multiplication pattern yields a *butterfly* network: a layered graph where each node in one layer connects to exactly two nodes in the next layer, with a characteristic “X” pattern—hence the name *butterfly*.

### 5.2 Why This Helps

- The **number of edges** needed is $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, far less than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges of a naively dense matrix.
- Despite sparsity, the butterfly network can still achieve *dense* global connectivity after composition.
- Each “butterfly” block is a $2\times2$ orthogonal matrix (a rotation or reflection), multiplied in a specific order defined by the network.

In short, the butterfly is a *structured* way to tile a big matrix with many small orthogonal pieces.

---

## 6. Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT)

### 6.1 Putting It All Together

1. **Start with the pre‑trained weight $\mathbf{W}^0$.**
2. **Compose an orthogonal matrix** as a product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components:
   \[
   \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_m \mathbf{B}_{m-1} \cdots \mathbf{B}_1
   \]
3. **Fine‑tune** the *tiny* set of parameters inside those butterfly components.
4. **Forward pass**:
   \[
   \mathbf{z} = \bigl( \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\bigr)^\top \mathbf{x}
   \]
   The inference time cost remains the same as the original model because $\mathbf{R}$ can be multiplied into $\mathbf{W}^0$ after training.

### 6.2 Parameter Count

- Each butterfly block of size $2$ uses $\frac{1}{2}(2-1) \cdot 2 = 1$ effective trainable parameter (just the rotation angle).
- If we need $m = \log_2 d$ butterfly layers, the total number of trainable parameters is $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$.
- For a large transformer layer ($d=12\,288$), this drops the parameter count from **hundreds of millions** to **a few million**—a big win!

### 6.3 How It Looks?

| Scheme | Parameters per Layer | Key Idea |
|--------|---------------------|----------|
| Full fine‑tune | $d \times n$ | Naïve, huge |
| LoRA | $d \times n \times r$ (low‑rank) | Reduce rank $r$ |
| OFT (block‑diag) | $r \times \frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ | Independent small orthogonal blocks |
| **BOFT** | $\frac{1}{2}(b-1) d m$ | Product of $m$ sparse butterfly orthogonal blocks |

BOFT sits in the sweet spot: it keeps the *full* expressive power of a dense orthogonal matrix while only using $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable values.

---

## 7. Practical Take‑aways

1. **Angles Matter** – Keeping neuron angles unchanged during fine‑tuning preserves pre‑training knowledge and stabilises training.
2. **Sparsity + Structure** – By arranging orthogonal matrices in a butterfly cascade, we can get the benefits of a dense transform without the heavy memory load.
3. **No Latency Penalty** – After fine‑tuning, we fold the learned orthogonal matrix into the original weights; inference speed is unchanged.
4. **General‑Purpose** – The method works for vision, language, and generative models.  The paper reports state‑of‑the‑art across several downstream tasks.
5. **Future Roadmap** – The butterfly pattern is just one of many possible sparse network topologies.  Exploring other “compilers” of dense matrices could yield even more efficient parameterizations.

---

## 8. In Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning tackles the otherwise crude “small‑learning‑rate” hack by enforcing a mathematically principled *rotational* update that preserves angles between neuron vectors.  But the naïve implementation exploded parameters like a bad aquarium leak.  By re‑imagining the dense orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse! pieces—specifically butterfly‑structured blocks—we squeeze the parameter count back down to a manageable size while keeping the full expressive power.  Think of it as building a complicated highway system using only a few well‑placed bridges.

So, next time you want to adapt a giant language model or a vision network, consider **BOFT**: a small change in the parameter landscape that yields large gains in efficiency and generalisation.
