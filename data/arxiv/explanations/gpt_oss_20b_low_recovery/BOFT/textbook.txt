# Introduction to Parameter‑Efficient Adaptation

Large foundation models such as GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion have become a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence: they can generate human‑like text or produce high‑quality images from textual prompts, and their performance improves steadily as the number of parameters grows. However, the sheer scale of these models—often hundreds of billions of parameters—renders training from scratch prohibitively expensive in terms of compute, memory, and energy. Consequently, research and industrial applications increasingly rely on the ability to **adapt** an already‑trained foundation model to a new downstream task with only a modest amount of additional computation and storage. This chapter introduces the motivations behind such adaptation, outlines the main paradigms used in the community (model‑based finetuning, adapters, and prompt tuning), and emphasizes why any successful approach must preserve the knowledge encoded by the pretrained weights while remaining feasible for large‑scale models.

## Why Foundation Models Need Adaptation

The most compelling reason to adapt foundation models rather than train anew is **cost efficiency**. Training a transformer with 175 B parameters from the ground up can cost tens of millions of dollars and several weeks of GPU time. In contrast, fine‑tuning typically requires only a few thousand GPU hours and a tiny fraction of the memory footprint. Beyond cost, adapting a frozen backbone also guarantees that the **semantic structure** learned during massive pretraining is largely retained, ensuring that the downstream model benefits from the linguistic, visual, or multimodal understanding already encoded.

Another reason stems from the **breadth of downstream applications**. A single well‑trained vision transformer might be fine‑tuned for image classification, object detection, semantic segmentation, or image‑to‑image translation. Similarly, a large language model can be repurposed for question answering, summarization, or code generation. Rather than training separate neural architectures for each task, practitioners prefer to **share** the base model and only modify a small part to capture task‑specific nuances. This sharing also promotes reproducibility and eases deployment, because the underlying transformer can be reused across different services.

Finally, from a research standpoint, efficient adaptation methods enable rapid experimentation. A scientist can apply a new loss function or a novel regularizer to a pre‑trained network, significantly accelerating the cycle of hypothesis testing without re‑engineering the entire model.

## Relation to Large‑Scale Models

Large‑scale models are the testbed for modern adaptation strategies. GPT‑3, for example, hosts about 175 B parameters and was trained on a variety of open‑source text corpora. Conversely, Stable Diffusion, a diffusion‑based text‑to‑image model, contains hundreds of millions of parameters distributed across a generative backbone and a U‑Net. Both models serve as archetypes when evaluating adaptation techniques:

- In language models, correct fine‑tuning is often judged by perplexity or downstream task accuracy, but more nuanced metrics—such as the degree to which the emergent linguistic representations survive—have become increasingly popular.
- In diffusion models, practitioners care not only about image fidelity but also about controllability (e.g., steering generation with different prompts or attributes). 

Because these models already embody intricate statistical structure, any adaptation method that fails to preserve this structure typically collapses quickly or suffers from catastrophic forgetting. Accordingly, recent efforts have focused on **principled constraints** (e.g., orthogonality) that implicitly enforce the preservation of relational properties among neurons.

## Model‑Based Finetuning vs. Structured Regularization

A **theorem**, originally introduced in the high‑performance reinforcement learning community, states that a linear transformation preserving pairwise angles between input vectors maintains the geometric relationships between learned features. In the context of model‑based finetuning, this translates to learning a **shared orthogonal transformation** that is applied to each layer of the backbone. Mathematically, if the original weight matrix is \( W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), the adapted matrix is parameterized as \( R\,W^{0}\) where \( R\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is orthogonal \((R^{T}R=I_{d})\). This strategy, known as **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, has been shown to preserve spectral norms and pairwise angles more faithfully than additive adjustments like LoRA.

However, the **cost** of OFT is the \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameters required to store a general orthogonal matrix for a large layer. Block‑diagonal approximations reduce this cost but impose a rigid sparsity pattern that can degrade expressivity. Hence, the community has turned to **alternative parameterizations**: either *adapters*, which insert lightweight residual modules (typically a few projection layers) between the existing layers, or *prompt tuning*, which appends learnable tokens to the input representation. Both approaches exhibit lower parameter budgets but may lose the guarantee of perfect orthogonality or the ease of merging the fine‑tuned weights back into the backbone.

An emerging approach is the **butterfly factorization** of orthogonal matrices, where a dense orthogonal matrix is expressed as a product of \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse orthogonal factors. This yields a \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameter set, striking a balance between the expressivity of OFT and the efficiency of adapter or prompt tuning. The key insight is that each sparse factor can be constructed as a block‑diagonal matrix composed of small orthogonal blocks (for instance, 2 × 2 rotations). When stacked according to the butterfly pattern, the product achieves dense connectivity across all neurons while keeping the number of trainable entries manageable.

By the end of the chapter, readers will have a solid grasp of **why** adaptive methods are essential, **what** the major choices are, and **how** orthogonality and sparsity can be used to engineer a parameter‑efficient, yet expressive, finetuning recipe.

# Principles of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a principled framework developed to adapt large foundation models to downstream tasks while preserving the geometric structure of the pretrained model. The core idea is to apply an orthogonal transformation to every linear layer of the frozen backbone. Because orthogonal matrices preserve pairwise angles between vectors, OFT guarantees that the semantic relationships encoded during pretraining remain intact after adaptation. This chapter dissects the mathematical underpinnings of OFT, explains how orthogonality is enforced via the Cayley transform, and discusses the block‑diagonal variant that was introduced to reduce the parameter budget. We also highlight the trade‑offs that arise when confronting the limitations of dense versus block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices.

---

## Orthogonal transformations and pairwise angles

In deep neural networks each linear layer can be viewed as a matrix \( \bm{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) acting on an input vector \( \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} \), yielding an output \( \bm{z} = \bm{W}^{\top}\bm{x}\). The columns of \( \bm{W}\), typically called neurons, span a subspace of the input space. The pairwise angles between columns \( \bm{w}_i,\bm{w}_j\) encode how the neurons discriminate among different directions of the input. 

Orthogonal transformations preserve these angles: for any orthogonal matrix \( \bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (\( \bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}\)), the transformed columns \( \bm{R}\bm{w}_i \) satisfy
\[
\langle \bm{R}\bm{w}_i , \bm{R}\bm{w}_j \rangle = \langle \bm{w}_i , \bm{w}_j \rangle.
\]
Hence, an orthogonal transformation rotates the whole set of neurons without changing their mutual geometry. OFT exploits this fact by reparameterizing the finetuned weight matrix as 
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{fin}} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0,\qquad \bm{R}\in O(d)
\]
where \( \bm{W}^0 \) is the frozen pretrained weight and \(O(d)\) denotes the orthogonal group. Because \(\bm{R}\) acts left‑multiplicatively, the output \( \bm{z}^{\text{fin}} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\top}\bm{x}\) is simply a rotated version of the original pretraining forward pass. The invariance of pairwise angles guarantees that the network can reuse the structure learned during pretraining, yet still be adaptable to the new task through a constrained, low‑neck adaptation.

---

## Cayley transform for orthogonality enforcement

Directly optimizing over all matrices \( \bm{R}\) with the constraint \( \bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}\) is notoriously difficult because the orthogonality constraint is non‑convex. The Cayley transform offers an elegant way to embed this constraint into the parametrization itself.

Let \( \bm{Q}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be a skew‑symmetric matrix (\( \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^{\top}\)). The Cayley transform maps \(\bm{Q}\) to an orthogonal matrix:
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}.
\]
This mapping is smooth and differentiable everywhere except at matrices \(\bm{Q}\) that cause \(\bm{I}-\bm{Q}\) to become singular. In practice, \(\bm{Q}\) is initialized as the zero matrix, which yields \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), the identity transformation. Because the Cayley transform preserves orthogonality by construction, we can write every OFT update as
\[
\bm{R}(\bm{Q}) = (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},\qquad \bm{Q}^{\top} = -\bm{Q}.
\]
When learning, we treat the free parameters as the upper‑triangular entries of \(\bm{Q}\), guaranteeing that the computed \(\bm{R}\) will always remain orthogonal. This eliminates the need for projection steps or penalty terms that attempt to enforce orthogonality during training.

---

## Block‑diagonal OFT and parameter budget

A full orthogonal matrix with dimension \(d\) contains \(\frac{d^2-d}{2}\) independent degrees of freedom, which is prohibitive when \(d\) is large (e.g. \(d\in[10^3,10^4]\)). OFT mitigates this cost by constraining \(\bm{R}\) to a block‑diagonal form:
\[
\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\ldots,\bm{R}_r),\qquad
\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br = d.
\]
Each block \(\bm{R}_i\) is an independent orthogonal matrix parameterized by a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}_i\) via the Cayley transform. Because each block has \(\frac{b^2-b}{2}\) degrees of freedom, the total number of learnable parameters scales as
\[
\frac{b^2-b}{2}\times r = \frac{b^2-b}{2}\times \frac{d}{b}
= \mathcal{O}(bd).
\]
Thus, by fixing \(b\) one can keep the parameter count linear in the dimension \(d\). The block‑diagonal form also has an intuitive interpretation: each block rotates a subgroup of neurons together, preserving angles within the subgroup while decoupling interactions across subgroups. This reduced coupling effectively reduces overfitting, leading to improved generalization in downstream tasks.

---

## Limitations of dense vs block‑diagonal matrices

While block‑diagonal OFT conserves the pairwise‑angle principle, it imposes a rigid sparsity pattern that can be too restrictive. Two main limitations arise:

1. **Expressivity loss**: Because interactions between neurons in different blocks are entirely absent, the adaptation cannot model transformations that mix these subspaces. For example, a global rotation or a permutation that swaps neurons across blocks cannot be represented. As a consequence, the hypothesis class is a strict subset of the full orthogonal group, potentially leaving out beneficial transformations for the downstream task.

2. **Inductive bias complications**: The partitioning of neurons into blocks is based solely on their tensor indices, not on any semantic or functional grouping. There is no principled way to decide the optimal block size or their arrangement. Choosing too few blocks (large \(b\)) reduces parameter efficiency, while choosing too many blocks (small \(b\)) over‑constrains the model, preventing it from reaching the necessary flexibility for adaptation.

These limitations motivated the introduction of more expressive but still parameter‑efficient parametrizations—most notably the butterfly factorization, which we introduce in subsequent chapters. By allowing an interleaved, multi‑stage mixing of all neurons while keeping the number of trainable parameters manageable, butterfly‑based OFT (BOFT) strikes a better balance between expressivity and regularity.

# Information Transmission Perspective  

In this chapter we reformulate Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) as an information‑transmission problem on a grid‑graph, clarify how the sparsity pattern of a matrix factorization corresponds to edges in this graph, and establish the two key desiderata—dense connectivity and minimal free edges—that guide the design of parameter‑efficient orthogonal transforms.  

## Grid‑graph interpretation of matrix multiplication  

Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and a trainable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ that OFT introduces. The forward computation is  
\[
    \bm{z} = \bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},\quad \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.  
\]
Suppose we factorize $\bm{R}$ into a product of $m$ square matrices
\[
    \bm{R} = \bm{B}_{m}\,\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_{1},\qquad \bm{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
\]
Each factor $\bm{B}_{i}$ can be viewed as a connectivity matrix between two consecutive layers of a directed graph.  
Let us construct a $(m+1)\times d$ grid: the first row corresponds to the $d$ source nodes (the columns of $\bm{I}_{d}$), the last row corresponds to the $d$ target nodes (the columns of $\bm{R}$), and each intermediate row $i$ ($1\le i\le m$) corresponds to the $d$ nodes after the $i$‑th factor.  
An entry $(p,q)$ of $\bm{B}_{i}$ is non‑zero precisely when there is a directed edge from node $q$ in layer $i$ to node $p$ in layer $i+1$.  
By composing the factors, every path in the graph corresponds to a product of non‑zero entries leading from an input node to an output node.  
Consequently, the $(p,q)$ entry of the full matrix $\bm{R}$ equals the sum of products of edge weights along all paths from node $q$ in the first layer to node $p$ in the last layer.  
Thus, the sparsity pattern of $\bm{R}$ is determined by the union of edge sets of all $\bm{B}_{i}$, and the presence of an edge determines whether a path exists.

## Edge counts for dense vs sparse factorization  

A fully dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ contains $d^{2}$ entries, all of which are potentially non‑zero.  
In graph terms, this corresponds to a complete directed bipartite subgraph between the first and last layers, requiring $d^{2}$ distinct edges.  
However, in a factorization, the total number of trainable edges is the sum of the numbers of non‑zero entries across all $\bm{B}_{i}$.  
If each $\bm{B}_{i}$ is also dense, their joint edge count equals $m\,d^{2}$, which is far larger than required.  
The goal is to reduce the number of trainable edges while ensuring that the induced final matrix $\bm{R}$ is still dense, i.e., every source node can reach every sink node by at least one path.  

In practice, each factor is chosen sparse: a typical choice is that each $\bm{B}_{i}$ has $d$ non‑zero entries, forming a permutation matrix.  
With $m$ such permutations, the total number of trainable edges is $m\,d$, which is optimal for representing any permutation.  
Yet a permutation alone is insufficient to generate a dense $\bm{R}$ because it preserves the same sparsity pattern: the product of permutations is still a permutation.  
To obtain density, we need to introduce additional non-zero patterns across the factors so that multiple distinct paths connect each pair of source–sink nodes.

## Orthogonality constraints on edges  

All matrices $\bm{B}_{i}$ must be orthogonal for OFT to preserve pairwise angles between neurons.  
An orthogonal matrix satisfies $\bm{B}_{i}^{\!\top}\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{I}_{d}$, which imposes linear constraints on its entries.  
These constraints translate into restrictions on the edge set:  

1. **Bijectivity** – Each factor must map the $d$ source nodes injectively to the $d$ destination nodes, which means each column (and each row) must contain at least one non-zero entry.  
2. **Zero–row/column coupling** – If a particular entry $(p,q)$ of $\bm{B}_{i}$ is forced to be zero, orthogonality often forces other entries to be zero or to take values that compensate for the loss of magnitude in the corresponding row/column.  
3. **Structured dependencies** – In a $2\times 2$ orthogonal block the pattern of zeros and the signs of non‑zeros are coupled; e.g., setting $(1,1)=0$ implies $(2,2)=0$ and $(1,2)=(2,1)=\pm1$.  

Because of these coupling effects, not every arbitrary sparse pattern can be realized by an orthogonal matrix.  
The information‑transmission view must therefore simultaneously satisfy the following: every node in the first layer can reach every node in the last layer (dense connectivity), and the non‑zero pattern satisfies the orthogonality constraints of each $\bm{B}_{i}$ (inducing dependencies among edges).  

## Desired properties: dense connectivity & minimal free edges  

From the perspective of a grid‑graph with $d$ nodes per layer, a factorization achieves **dense connectivity** if for each pair of nodes $(p,q)$ there exists at least one directed path from node $q$ in layer 0 to node $p$ in layer $m$.  
In the graph, this means that the union of all path‑sets forms a complete bipartite directed subgraph between the first and last layers.  
Only then can every entry of $\bm{R}$ become non‑zero after full multiplication.

Simultaneously, we want to use as few trainable edges as possible to reduce the parameter budget.  
We distinguish two types of edges:

- **Free edges** – entries whose value is forced by orthogonality (e.g., $\pm1$ in a $2\times2$ orthogonal block).  
  These do not count toward the trainable parameter count.  
- **Free trainable edges** – entries whose value is not fixed by orthogonality and therefore must be learned during finetuning.

The minimal edge count criterion demands that the number of free trainable edges be as small as possible while still permitting dense connectivity.  
This leads to the design principle underlying orthogonal butterfly factorization: by arranging factors in a logarithmic sequence of butterfly patterns, we achieve dense connectivity with only $O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.  
In contrast, a block‑diagonal structure yields $O(bd)$ parameters but cannot guarantee dense connectivity because each block operates independently, limiting inter‑block information flow.

By formulating parameter efficiency as an information‑transmission problem, we transform the abstract question “How can we generate a dense orthogonal matrix using few trainable parameters?” into a concrete combinatorial optimization on a grid‑graph, paving the way for principled design of orthogonal butterfly matrices.

**Butterfly Structures for Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization**

The efficient adaptation of large foundation models hinges upon our ability to generate dense orthogonal matrices while keeping the number of trainable parameters tractable. A powerful tool in this endeavor is the so‑called *butterfly* structure, borrowed from the classic radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm. By carefully arranging sparse building blocks, a butterfly matrix can be composed as a product of only \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) non‑zero transforms, each of size \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). Because each constituent matrix can be constrained to be orthogonal, the final product remains orthogonal and yet requires only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters. The following sections unpack this construction from first principles, explore how to enforce orthogonality with the Cayley transform, and quantify the sparsity that results.

---

# Definition of Butterfly Factor & Matrix

At the most elemental level, a *butterfly factor* is a \(k\times k\) matrix composed of four diagonal blocks of size \(\frac{k}{2}\times \frac{k}{2}\). Let \(\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) be arbitrary vectors. The factor is

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
}\tag{1}
\]

Each diagonal block performs a component‑wise scaling on half of the vector coordinates, and the off‑diagonal blocks mix the two halves. Importantly, the pattern of non‑zeros in \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\) contains exactly \(k\) non‑zero entries: two on the diagonal of each half and two off‑diagonal (one in each half). This sparsity becomes the cornerstone of the butterfly recursion.

Define a *butterfly component* of size \(d\times d\) by block‑diagonal concatenation of \(d/k\) butterfly factors:

\[
\boxed{
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k),\mathbf{B}^{F}(k),\dots,\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\bigr)
}\tag{2}
\]

where each diagonal block is a \(k\times k\) factor. The resulting matrix retains the sparsity pattern of a single factor, merely repeated across disjoint coordinate ranges.

A full *butterfly matrix* of dimension \(d\) is constructed recursively by multiplying a chain of such components. When the radix is powers of two, i.e. \(d=2^{N}\), we obtain

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{B}(d)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{2})\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,\tfrac{d}{4})\dots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2) 
}\tag{3}
\]

The recursion mirrors the divide‑and‑conquer strategy of the Cooley–Tukey algorithm: each level halves the sub‑problem size and introduces a new butterfly component that exchanges information between complementary halves of the vector. The last level, with block size \(2\), performs pairwise mixing and thus amounts to a set of independent \(2\times 2\) rotations or reflections.

---

# Recursive Construction and Block‑Butterfly

The recursive definition in (3) can be unpacked level by level. Assume we have already computed \(\mathbf{B}(\tfrac{d}{2})\). Then

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\,
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{B}(\tfrac{d}{2}) & \mathbf{0}\\ 
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{B}(\tfrac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}
\]

Because \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2)\) couples the two \(\tfrac{d}{2}\)‑dimensional halves via its off‑diagonal blocks, the whole matrix is dense once we go through all levels. Each recursion step adds a \(\tfrac{d}{2}\) butterfly component that operates on all \(d\) coordinates, but only uses \(\frac{d}{2}\) non‑zero entries: two per pair. Consequently, at level \(i\) (counting from the top \(i=0\) down to the bottom), we introduce \(\frac{d}{2^{i}}\) butterfly components, each of size \(\frac{d}{2^{i}}\). The total number of non‑zero entries across all levels is

\[
\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2} d-1} \frac{d}{2^{i}}\cdot 2
= d\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2} d-1}\frac{2}{2^{i}}
= 2d \Bigl(1-\frac{1}{2^{\log_{2} d}}\Bigr)
\approx 2d\,,
\]

which demonstrates that the final butterfly matrix has only \(O(d)\) non‑zeros. The product of \(\log_{2} d\) such matrices yields a dense transformation, but the number of trainable parameters—those unique to the diagonals of the butterfly factors—grows only as \(O(d\log d)\).

The block‑butterfly extension arises when we replace the simple diagonal vectors \(\mathbf{d}_{i}\) in (1) with full \(b\times b\) diagonal blocks \(\mathbf{D}_{i}\). The factor then becomes a \((k b)\times (k b)\) matrix:

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}_{\text{blk}}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{2})\\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{D}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\]

with each \(\mathbf{D}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\). By choosing \(b>1\), we increase the expressive power of each factor while retaining the same sparsity pattern: the total number of trainable blocks remains \(\frac{d}{b} \log_{2} d\), but each block contains \(b^{2}\) parameters. This *block‑butterfly* construction is particularly useful in neural network finetuning, where a small block size (e.g. \(b=2\)) supplies a rich set of directional interactions, and a larger block size (e.g. \(b=4\) or \(8\)) can accelerate convergence at the cost of more parameters.

---

# Ensuring Orthogonality via Cayley per Block

A central requirement for our finetuning scheme is that every factor in the butterfly product be an *orthogonal* matrix. Orthogonality guarantees that the overall transformation preserves norms and angles, a property that underlies the strong generalization observed in orthogonal finetuning. However, naïvely assigning arbitrary diagonal weights to a butterfly factor risks breaking orthogonality. To enforce orthogonality while keeping each factor sparse, we first solve the problem for the simplest block: a \(2\times 2\) rotation.

Any \(2\times 2\) special orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}_{2}\) can be expressed using a scalar angle \(\theta\):

\[
\mathbf{R}_{2}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\[2pt]
\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Instead of storing \(\theta\) explicitly, we embed the rotation into the Cayley transform. For a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\) satisfying \(\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}\),

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{R}_{2} = (\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
}\tag{4}
\]

the inversion is trivial because \(\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{Q}\) is invertible for small \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\). The advantage of the Cayley parameterization is that \(\mathbf{Q}\) contains only a single trainable scalar (the off‑diagonal element). Pinning such a scalar to zero yields the identity matrix, providing the desired initialization for finetuning.

For block‑size \(b>2\), each diagonal block \(\mathbf{D}_{i}\) is a \(b\times b\) matrix. We decompose \(\mathbf{D}_{i}\) into a pairwise product of \(2\times 2\) rotations across the \(b\) rows and columns. Concretely, let \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) be skew‑symmetric; then the Cayley transform

\[
\mathbf{R}_{b} = (\mathbf{I}_{b}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}_{b}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
\]

produces a full \(b\times b\) orthogonal matrix. Because \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric, it has \(\frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) free parameters, which is the minimal number required to parameterize an orthogonal matrix up to sign. By inserting \(\mathbf{R}_{b}\) into every diagonal block of a butterfly factor, we guarantee orthogonality for the entire factor.

Finally, since the product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal, the full butterfly matrix

\[
\mathbf{B}_{\text{ortho}}(d) = \prod_{i=1}^{\log_{2} d}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\text{ortho}}(d,2^{i})
\]

is guaranteed to be orthogonal. In practice, we initialize all \(\mathbf{Q}\) matrices to zero, yielding \(\mathbf{B}_{\text{ortho}}(d)=\mathbf{I}\) at the onset of finetuning.

---

# Counting Non‑Zero Parameters

Because each butterfly factor is highly structured, we can count the effective trainable parameters systematically. Consider a butterfly factor \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\text{ortho}}(d,k)\) with block size \(k\) and block‑size \(b\) inside each factor as described above. The number of independent parameters per block is

\[
\frac{b(b-1)}{2},
\]

the degrees of freedom of a skew‑symmetric matrix. There are \(\frac{d}{k}\) such blocks, so the parameters contributed by the factor are

\[
\frac{d}{k}\cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2}.
\]

Summing over all levels \(k=2,4,\dots,d\) in the butterfly recursion yields

\[
\sum_{k=2}^{d}\!\frac{d}{k}\cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2}
= \frac{b(b-1)}{2}\, d\!\!\sum_{k=2}^{d}\!\frac{1}{k}
= \frac{b(b-1)}{2}\, d \, \bigl(H_{d}-1\bigr),
\]

where \(H_{d}\) is the \(d\)-th harmonic number. Since \(H_{d}\approx \ln d + \gamma\), the total number of effective trainable parameters scales as

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(d\,b\,\ln d\bigr)
}\tag{5}
\]

For the standard butterfly (\(b=2\)), we recover the commonly cited \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) bound. The dependence on \(b\) is linear because each block introduces roughly \(\frac{b^{2}}{2}\) parameters, yet the overall sparsity pattern remains linear in \(d\).

This parameter count is dramatically lower than the \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameters required to store a dense orthogonal matrix or the \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) parameters required for the block‑diagonal factorization used in the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). The reduction is achieved at the expense of a slight overhead in forward and backward passes: each butterfly layer involves \(\log d\) sparse matrix–vector multiplications, but the overall computational complexity stays linear in \(d\log d\).

---

Through the lens of these carefully engineered sparse structures, a dense orthogonal transformation becomes tractable even for models with millions of parameters. The butterfly construction sits at the intersection of signal‑processing intuition, linear‑algebraic rigor, and deep‑learning efficiency, providing a principled pathway to parameter‑efficient adaptation of the most powerful neural architectures.

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) Framework

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a principled way to finetune large pretrained models by learning a *dense* orthogonal transformation that is expressed as a product of several *sparse* orthogonal matrices. By carefully choosing the sparsity pattern, BOFT achieves a dramatic reduction in the number of learnable parameters while still preserving the key property of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): the pairwise angles between neurons are virtually unchanged. This chapter exposes the mathematical foundation of BOFT, explains its construction, and discusses the practical implications of its design choices.

---

## 1. The BOFT Matrix Product \( \mathbf{R}(m,b) \)

Let \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) be a frozen weight matrix from a pretrained layer. Orthogonal Finetuning replaces the left multiplication \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\mapsto \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\), where \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is a learnable orthogonal matrix. In BOFT we constrain \( \mathbf{R}\) to take a specific form:
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} ,
\tag{1}
\]
where

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| \(m\)  | Number of butterfly factors (depth). |
| \(b\)  | Block size within each butterfly factor. |
| \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) | Orthogonal butterfly component at depth \(i\). |

### 1.1 Butterfly Components

A *butterfly factor* is a \(k\times k\) matrix (\(k=2^{\ell}\) for some \(\ell\)) composed of four diagonal blocks:
\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
   \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
   \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{2}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{d}_j\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) contains scalars to be learned. A full \(d\times d\) butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is built recursively by embedding two smaller butterfly matrices at every level:
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\left(d,d\right)\,
               \tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\left(d,\tfrac{d}{2}\right)\,
               \cdots\,
               \tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\left(d,2\right),
\tag{3}
\]
with
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\left(d,k\right)=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{B}^F_1(k),\dots,\mathbf{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]
Each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is a block‑diagonal matrix of size \(d\times d\); its non‑zero pattern is a permutation of the Frobenius‑rank‑\(k\) butterfly pattern.

In BOFT we *block‑lift* this construction by replacing scalars with \(b\times b\) matrices. A *block‑butterfly component* \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,k)\) has the same sparsity pattern as \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) but each non‑zero entry is a \(b\times b\) dense block. The small value of \(b\) controls the number of trainable parameters inside each block.

### 1.2 Orthogonality Constraint

For each depth \(i\), the component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) must be orthogonal:
\[
\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\bigr)^\top\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}_d.
\tag{5}
\]
This is enforced by Cayley parameterization:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\bigl(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}_i\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_i\bigr)^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) is skew‑symmetric. Each non‑zero block in \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) contains the learnable parameters; the size of a block is \(b\times b\).

**Effective parameter count.**  
If a block contains \(b^2\) entries and the scalar diagonal blocks are replaced by \(b\times b\) matrices, the number of non‑zero trainable entries in one butterfly component is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d\). Consequently, for \(m\) components the total number of *effective* trainable parameters is
\[
N_{\!\text{params}}=\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m.
\tag{6}
\]
Special choices recover known finetuning strategies:

| Choice | Interpretation |
|--------|----------------|
| \(m=1,\ b=d\) | Original OFT (full orthogonal matrix). |
| \(m=1,\ b\) any | Block‑diagonal OFT (as in the original paper). |
| \(m=\log_2 d,\ b=2\) | Pure butterfly BOFT: \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters. |

---

## 2. Forward Pass and Gradient Flow

Given a training example \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and a target output \(\mathbf{y}\), the forward pass of a linear layer under BOFT is
\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{z}
=
\bigl(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\,\mathbf{W}^0\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
}
\tag{7}
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is computed as in (1). The computation can be carried out recursively: first multiply \(\mathbf{x}\) by \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,1)}\), then by \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,2)}\), and so on. Since every \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\) is sparse, each multiplication costs \(\mathcal{O}((b-1)d)\) operations; thus the overall per‑layer cost is \(\mathcal{O}(d\,m)\).

Back‑propagation automatically differentiates through the product chain, adjusting the skew‑symmetric parameters in each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}\). Because the orthogonality constraint is preserved exactly during training (thanks to Cayley), the gradients respect the geometry of the Stiefel manifold and do not drift the learned matrix away from orthogonality.

---

## 3. Initialization – Identity & Zero Skew

The standard approach for finetuning is to start from a model that exactly matches the pretrained one. For BOFT this requires
\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\mathbf{I}_d.
\]
Every butterfly component must thus be initialized to the identity matrix. In Cayley terms, the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_i\) is initialized to zero:
\[
\mathbf{Q}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{0} \quad \forall i.
\tag{8}
\]
With \(\mathbf{Q}_i=\mathbf{0}\), the Cayley transform yields \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}_d\), guaranteeing that the initial weights are precisely the pretrained weights. This zero‑initialization is crucial for stability: it ensures that the dynamic range of activations and gradients at the start of training matches that of the frozen model.

---

## 4. Multiplicative Dropout

Dropout is a regularization mechanism that randomly deactivates neurons during training. In the context of BOFT, the orthogonal matrix is *multiplicative*; therefore, applying conventional dropout to the matrix entries would destroy orthogonality. Instead, **multiplicative dropout** acts *before* the matrix multiplication, striking a controlled compromise:

1. **Component sampling.** With probability \(p_1\), we *skip* a butterfly component entirely, treating it as the identity matrix. This is equivalent to setting all its parameters to zero.
2. **Block sampling.** For each butterfly component that remains active, we independently drop a fraction \(p_2\) of the individual \(b\times b\) diagonal blocks, replacing them by identity blocks.

Formally, let \(\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}\) be the set of indices for active components. For each \(i\in\mathcal{S}\) and each block \(j\) in component \(i\), we define a Bernoulli variable \(b_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}\) with \(\mathbb{P}(b_{i,j}=1)=1-p_2\). Then the effective component is
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b,\,\text{drop}}_{(d,i)} = 
\begin{cases}
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)} & \text{if } i\in\mathcal{S}\ \text{and}\ b_{i,j}=1,\\
\mathbf{I}_d & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Multiplicative dropout preserves orthogonality of the *active* components and keeps the overall transformation orthogonal when all active components are multiplied together. The stochasticity injects a robustness to over‑fitting, as the model must learn to perform well under random block removals.

---

## 5. Runtime vs Parameter Trade‑offs

| Variant | \(m\) | \(b\) | Parameters | Forward Cost | Overhead vs OFT |
|---------|-------|-------|------------|--------------|------------------|
| OFT \((1,b)\) – block‑diagonal | 1 | \(b\) | \(\tfrac12(b-1) d\) | \(\mathcal{O}((b-1)d)\) | Minimal |
| BOFT \((1,d)\) – full orthogonal | 1 | \(d\) | \(\tfrac12(d-1)d\approx\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) | Same as OFT |
| BOFT \((m,b)\) | \(m\) | \(b\) | \(\tfrac12(b-1)dm\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d\,m)\) | Slightly larger, but \(b=2\) keeps cost minimal |

**Key observations**

- **Parameter scaling.** Setting \(b=2\) and \(m=\log_2 d\) yields roughly \(d\log d\) parameters, a severe reduction from \(d^2\) in the full orthogonal case.
- **Computational overhead.** Each additional butterfly component incurs a linear cost in \(d\); thus, increasing \(m\) dilutes parameter savings by slight runtime overhead. In practice, the sparsity of each component keeps per‑iteration time small, and inference time is unaffected because the learned \(\mathbf{R}\) is collapsed into the frozen weights after training.
- **Expressiveness.** As \(m\) grows or \(b\) increases, BOFT can approximate a larger subset of the orthogonal group. Experiments (see the discussion chapter) show that even modest values such as \(b=4,\,m=5\) achieve error rates comparable to block‑diagonal OFT with \(b=16\).

---

## 6. Special Cases and Relationships

### 6.1 BOFT\((1,b)\): Block‑Diagonal OFT

With a single butterfly component and block size \(b\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,1)}\) is block‑diagonal and orthogonal. The product reduces to a single block‑diagonal matrix:
\[
\mathbf{R}(1,b)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\bigr), \quad
r=\tfrac{d}{b}, \quad \mathbf{R}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
\]
exactly reproducing the design of the original OFT paper. Thus, BOFT contains block‑diagonal OFT as a degenerate case.

### 6.2 BOFT\((1,d)\): Full Orthogonal OFT

Choosing block size \(b=d\) eliminates the block‑diagonal structure, leaving a single dense orthogonal matrix. The parameter count reverts to \(\tfrac12(d-1)d\), and the method becomes identical to the original OFT, which optimizes a full orthogonal left multiplier.

### 6.3 BOFT\((\log_2 d,2)\): Pure Butterfly

The most compact instantiation uses the smallest possible block size \(b=2\) and the maximum number of components \(m=\log_2 d\). Each component is a *two‑by‑two* orthogonal butterfly factor. The product of all \(\log_2 d\) components yields a dense orthogonal matrix with only \(d\log_2 d\) learnable parameters. This is a typical choice when one seeks the highest level of parameter efficiency while maintaining strong expressiveness.

### 6.4 Full OFT as a Limit

When \(b\) is allowed to grow arbitrarily and \(m\) grows accordingly, the union of all products of butterfly components covers the entire orthogonal group. In the extreme case \(b=d,\,m=1\), every orthogonal matrix can be represented exactly. Therefore, BOFT is a *generative* framework: it can interpolate between the heavy‑weight full‑matrix OFT and the highly structured block‑diagonal OFT, setting a continuum of trade‑offs between **expressiveness** and **parameter budget**.

---

## 7. Summary

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a flexible, structured approach to learn dense orthogonal transformations with a small number of trainable parameters. Its core ideas can be distilled as follows:

1. **Matrix Product:** \(\mathbf{R}(m,b)\) is a product of \(m\) orthogonal butterfly components, each parametrized as a block‑diagonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) with \(b\times b\) dense blocks.
2. **Identity Initialization:** All components are initialized to the identity via zero skew‑symmetric parameters, ensuring the fine‑tuned weights start from the pretrained model.
3. **Multiplicative Dropout:** Randomly disables entire components or blocks during training, preserving orthogonality while regularizing the model.
4. **Runtime vs Parameter Trade‑offs:** For fixed \(d\), choosing smaller \(b\) and larger \(m\) yields a highly parameter‑efficient, yet expressive, approximation of the full orthogonal group; the converse yields more flexible but heavier models.
5. **Special Cases:** BOFT subsumes block‑diagonal OFT (\(m=1\)), full‑matrix OFT (\(b=d\)), and pure butterfly BOFT (\(m=\log d, b=2\)).

With these ingredients, BOFT provides a principled, scalable, and theoretically grounded finetuning scheme that is well‑suited for large foundation models across vision, language, and generative domains.

# Theoretical Properties of BOFT

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) sits at the intersection of structured linear algebra and efficient parameter adaptation.  Its theoretical appeal stems from three pillars: (i) a guaranteed expressive power that strictly surpasses the block‑diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with the same block size; (ii) a spectral preservation property that keeps the dominant singular value of the pretrained weight untouched; and (iii) an implicit regularization that nudges the model toward classical fast linear transforms.  In this chapter we unpack these properties, present proof sketches and empirical illustrations, and it that we situate BOFT within the broader landscape of expressibility in orthogonal re‑parameterizations.

---

## Expressivity Theorem and Experimental Validation

### 1. Formal expressivity statement

\begin{theorem}[Expressivity of BOFT]
Let \( \mathcal{O}_{\text{BF}}(m,b) \) denote the family of orthogonal matrices realizable by BOFT with \(m\) butterfly layers and block size \(b\).  The set \( \mathcal{O}_{\text{BF}}(m,b) \) is a strict superset of the block‑diagonal orthogonal family with block size \(b\) and is, for any fixed \(b\), more expressive than the family obtained when \(m=1\).
\end{theorem}

*Sketch of proof.*  
A block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix is a product of \(r = d/b\) independent \(b \times b\) orthogonal matrices.  BOFT replaces each block with a product of \(\log_2(b)\) butterfly factors, each of which is itself a product of \(\log_2(b)\) block‑diagonal 2‑by‑2 rotations.  Since \(\log_2(b) > 1\) for all \(b > 2\), the space of admissible parameters strictly expands.  By induction on the block size, each additional butterfly layer introduces coupling between previously independent blocks, thereby enlarging the reachable set of orthogonal matrices.  Finally, because the entire product remains orthogonal, the space is a proper subset of the full \(\mathcal{O}(d)\).

### 2. Numerical approximation study

In the paper’s Figure 8, the authors approximated a random dense orthogonal matrix \( \bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times 512}\) using BOFT configurations of varying \((m,b)\).  The approximation error was measured in Frobenius norm relative to \(\|\bm{Q}\|_F\).  The experimental curve for BOFT(\(9,2\)) lay well below the curve for BOFT(\(1,16\)), despite the former using far fewer trainable parameters (\(\frac12 (b-1)d m = 11{,}520\) vs. \(204{,}800\)).  Similarly, BOFT(\(6,4\)) matched the performance of BOFT(\(2,16\)), again with an order‑of‑magnitude fewer parameters.  These results empirically confirm the theorem: for a fixed budget of trainable weights, depth in the butterfly factorization yields a richer expressivity.

---

## Spectral Norm Preservation and Singular Values

### 1. Left‑side orthogonal multiplication preserves singular values

Let \( \bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}\) be the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix, with \(\bm{U},\bm{V}\) orthogonal and \(\bm{\Sigma}\) diagonal containing \( \sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_{\min(d,n)} \ge 0\).  BOFT (or OFT) replaces \(\bm{W}^0\) by
\[
    \bm{W}^\prime = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}.
\]
Because \(\bm{R}\) is orthogonal (\(\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R} = \bm{I}\)), the product \(\bm{R}\bm{U}\) is again an orthogonal matrix.  Consequently, the singular values of \(\bm{W}^\prime\) are identical to those of \(\bm{W}^0\); only the left singular vectors are rotated.  In particular, the spectral norm
\[
    \|\bm{W}^\prime\|_2 = \sigma_1(\bm{W}^\prime) = \sigma_1(\bm{W}^0) = \|\bm{W}^0\|_2
\]
remains strictly unchanged throughout training.

### 2. Training stability implications

Preserving the top singular value alleviates the risk of vanishing or exploding gradients.  In deep residual architectures, the dominant singular value controls the Lipschitz constant locally; keeping it fixed ensures that the representational capacity of the pretrained area does not deteriorate as parameters flow through the finetuned layers.  Empirical studies (Figure 9 of the paper) demonstrate that BOFT attains higher training loss minima than LoRA, corroborating the theoretical advantage.

---

## Bilinear Similarity Interpretation

### 1. Reformulation of the forward pass

For a single linear layer, the pre‑trained forward operation is
\[
    \bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}.
\]
After BOFT, this becomes
\[
    \bm{z}^\prime = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}
    = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{x}.
\]
Hence each neuron’s output corresponds to a bilinear similarity:
\[
    z_i^\prime = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top (\bm{R}^\top \bm{x}) = \bm{w}_i^0 \cdot (\bm{R}\bm{x}),
\]
where \(\bm{w}_i^0\) is the \(i\)-th column of \(\bm{W}^0\).  The right‑multiplication by \(\bm{R}\) constitutes a linear transformation of the input embedding before similarity computation.

### 2. Connection to metric learning

In distance‑metric learning, one seeks a positive‑definite matrix \(\bm{M}\) to define a Mahalanobis distance \(d_{\bm{M}}(\bm{x},\bm{y}) = \sqrt{(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^\top \bm{M}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}\).  If we restrict \(\bm{M} = \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\), the bilinear similarity implicitly imposes a rotation of the input space, guaranteeing that \(\bm{M}\) remains the identity.  This extreme regularization aligns with the idea of learning a *directional* adjustment rather than an arbitrary metric, thereby preventing the model from drifting too far from the pretraining geometry.

---

## Inductive Bias via Classical Transforms

### 1. Butterfly matrices recover fast transforms

A butterfly matrix \(\bm{B}(d)\) (Equation (3)–(4) in the paper) is constructed as a product of sparsely interleaved \(2 \times 2\) orthogonal blocks.  Many well‑known linear transforms—Discrete Fourier, Discrete Cosine, Hadamard—can be expressed exactly as a butterfly matrix (often multiplied by a permutation).  Consequently, the hypothesis set of BOFT contains these transforms as special cases.

### 2. Regularization effect

Because the parameter space is constrained to forms that admit efficient fast transforms, the model is *naturally* biased toward sparse, low‑depth, and regular patterns.  Such inductive bias mirrors that of convolutional or transformer attention weights, which are designed to be communicative yet sparse.  The bias can be considered implicit regularization: by making the family of admissible weight transformations sub‑exponential in dimension, BOFT narrows the search space to matrices likely to generalize well on tasks that share structural similarities with those transforms (e.g., frequency‑aware vision tasks, language models with local context).

### 3. Empirical validation

Across vision and language downstream tasks, BOFT consistently outperforms LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT.  The advantage is more pronounced in *low‑data* regimes, suggesting that the inductive bias is preventing overfitting—a hallmark of a well‑reset theoretical prior.

---

## Kaleidoscope Hierarchy and Full Expressivity

### 1. From BOFT to the full orthogonal group

The *kaleidoscope* construction (Equation (6) in the paper) concatenates alternating left–right BOFT blocks:
\[
    \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
    = \prod_{j=1}^{l}
        \bigl(\bm{R}_{j,1}(m_1,b_1)\bigr)
        \bigl(\bm{R}_{j,2}(m_2,b_2)^\top\bigr).
\]
When \(m_1=m_2=\log_2 d\), \(b_1=b_2=2\), and \(l=d-1\), the factorization spans the entire orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\).  This follows from the fact that a product of \(d-1\) alternating orthogonal caps (each a full general orthogonal matrix) generates all rotations and reflections in \(d\) dimensions.

### 2. Trade‑off considerations

While the kaleidoscope hierarchy guarantees *universal* expressiveness, it also dramatically enlarges the parameter count (proportional to \(d^2\)).  In practice, we observe that *partial* kaleidoscope, e.g. taking \(l\) much smaller than \(d-1\), offers a flexible sweet‑spot: the model can still generate nearly any orthogonal transform, yet the number of trainable parameters remains modest (\(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\)).  This mirrors the philosophy behind *parameter‑efficient finetuning*: adopt sufficient flexibility to capture task‑specific nuances without abandoning the core pretraining knowledge.

### 3. Comparison to other expressive schemes

Low‑rank methods (LoRA) and hybrid low‑rank & orthogonal schemes (e.g. Luong et al.) can achieve universal expressivity, but at a higher risk of catastrophic forgetting due to the unrestricted parameter space.  BOFT’s block‑sparse factorization offers a *structured bottleneck* that keeps the retained knowledge intact, while the optional kaleidoscope layers allow the model to unfold the full representational power when necessary.

---

## Synopsis

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning achieves a delicate equilibrium in the space of model adaptation methods:

* **Expressivity**: BOFT superset of block‑diagonal OFT, as formalized above, and can be augmented to the full orthogonal group via the kaleidoscope hierarchy.
* **Spectral fidelity**: Left‑side orthogonal multiplication preserves the spectral norm and all singular values, ensuring training stability.
* **Bilinear view**: The method can be reinterpreted as a constrained Mahalanobis‑style similarity, flanking the pretrained weight with a rotation.
* **Inductive bias**: Butterfly factorization embeds classical fast linear transforms into the hypothesis set, acting as a regularizer without explicit penalties.
* **Scalability**: The parameter budget scales with \(d\log d\), a drastic reduction over dense orthogonal or full‑rank approaches.

These theoretical guarantees, corroborated by empirical findings in vision, language, and diffusion domains, establish BOFT as a principled, versatile, and efficient tool for adapting large foundation models without sacrificing performance or inducing undue training instability.

# Practical Implementation and Training Details

Fine‑tuning large foundation models is a delicate art: a single hyper‑parameter choice can decide whether a method converges, stalls, or overfits. In this chapter we provide a detailed, hands‑on guide to turning the theoretical contribution of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) into a robust, production‑ready recipe. Each section walks through the practicalities of implementation, hyper‑parameter tuning, and debugging, while staying grounded in the definition of BOFT presented in the paper. The material is tailored for a college student who has already worked through the earlier chapters, so we can assume familiarity with the fundamentals of weight‑space re‑parameterization and orthogonal matrices.

## Implementing BOFT in PyTorch / JAX

Below is a minimal, end‑to‑end example that demonstrates how to wrap any linear or dense layer with a BOFT module. The code is kept intentionally succinct so that you can focus on the mechanics of the butterfly factorization rather than on auxiliary project‑level boilerplate.

### 1. PyTorch Implementation

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class ButterflyComponent(nn.Module):
    """
    A single orthogonal butterfly component of size (d, d).
    Parameters: m – number of butterfly stages (log2(d) when block_size=2).
                b – butterfly block size (2, 4, 8, …).
    """
    def __init__(self, d, stage, block_size=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = d
        self.stage = stage
        self.block_size = block_size

        # Number of 2x2 sub‑blocks inside a block_size x block_size block.
        k = block_size // 2
        num_blocks = d // block_size

        # Each sub‑block is parameterised by an angle (rotate) so that the
        # overall block stays orthogonal.  We initialise with zeros → identity.
        theta = torch.zeros(num_blocks * k, dtype=torch.float32)
        self.theta = nn.Parameter(theta)

    def forward(self, x):
        """
        Forward pass for a single butterfly component.
        Input:  x of shape (batch, d)
        Output: transformed x of shape (batch, d)
        """
        # Build 2x2 rotation matrices on the fly using torch.cos/sin
        cos = torch.cos(self.theta)
        sin = torch.sin(self.theta)

        # Reshape to (num_blocks, k, 2) to create 2x2 rotation sub-matrices
        rot = torch.stack([cos, -sin, sin, cos], dim=2)  # (B, K, 4)
        rot = rot.view(-1, 2, 2)                         # (B, 2, 2)

        # Reshape the input to interleave blocks
        x = x.view(-1, self.d // self.block_size, self.block_size)  # (B, NB, BS)

        # For a given stage, we need to permute which pairs of rows
        # receive the rotation.  The ButterflyFactor has a fixed pattern
        # derived from the Cooley–Tukey algorithm.
        perm = torch.arange(self.block_size)
        # For stage s (s=0 is the first stage), we toggle pairs explain below.
        if self.stage % 2:
            perm1 = perm.clone()
            perm1[::2], perm1[1::2] = perm1[1::2], perm1[::2]
            perm = perm1

        # Now mix the rows according to the rotation matrix
        # We shuffle the rows by block and apply the 2x2 rotations.
        x = x.transpose(1, 2).contiguous()  # (B, BS, NB)
        # group sub‑blocks according to perm
        x = x[:, perm]                      # (B, BS, NB)
        # Merge pairs to form 2 x 2 sub‑blocks; each will be multiplied
        # by a rotation matrix
        x = x.reshape(-1, self.block_size // 2, 2, self.d // self.block_size)
        # reshape to (B*NB, 2, BS, ...) - perform rotation
        # we skip numeric details here for brevity
        # ...
        # Return to (B, d)
        return x.reshape(-1, self.d)

class BOFT(nn.Module):
    """
    Full BOFT layer that wraps a pretrained weight matrix W0 of shape
    (d, n).  It replaces W0 with R @ W0 where R is a product of
    orthogonal butterfly components.
    """
    def __init__(self, d, n, m, block_size=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = d
        self.n = n
        self.m = m
        self.block_size = block_size
        self.Qt = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d, d, dtype=torch.float32))  # skew‑symmetric

        # Store each butterfly component
        self.components = nn.ModuleList([
            ButterflyComponent(d, stage=i, block_size=block_size)
            for i in range(m)
        ])

        # Pretrained weight W0 (frozen)
        self.register_buffer("W0", torch.empty(d, n))
        # initialise W0 later with your pretrained model's weights

    def forward(self, x):
        """
        x: (batch, d)
        Returns y: (batch, n) computed by (R @ W0)^T x
        """
        # Build R by successive matrix multiplications
        R = torch.eye(self.d, dtype=x.dtype, device=x.device)
        for comp in self.components:
            R = torch.matmul(comp(x), R)  # comp(x) is a matrix function

        # Compute transformed weight
        W = torch.matmul(R, self.W0)       # (d, n)
        return torch.matmul(x, W)          # (batch, n)
```

> **Note.** The code above is intentionally schematic: implementing the exact butterfly permutation and rotation efficiently requires careful tensor reshapes and broadcasting that are standard in autonomous differentiation libraries. A production implementation will utilise fused kernels and avoid explicit matrix multiplications inside the loop.

The JAX variant follows the same logic but leverages functional programming. Jax’s `jax.experimental.stax` or `haiku` libraries provide a convenient way to construct similarly structured modules, and the purely functional approach allows you to use `jax.vmap` or `jax.jit` to accelerate training.

### 2. Memory and FLOP Footprint

Each butterfly component contains `α = (d / block_size) * (block_size / 2)` angles. Hence, the total number of trainable parameters is

\[
p_{\text{BOFT}} = m \times \alpha = \frac{1}{2} m (block\_size - 1) d.
\]

By contrast, a full orthogonal matrix needs `d (d-1)/2` parameters. For `d=4096`, `block_size=2`, and `m = log_2 d = 12`, BOFT uses only 49 000 parameters versus ~8 million.

The FLOP count per forward pass is linear in `d` plus `m * (something)` due to the butterfly permutation. On a GPU, this overhead is negligible compared to the cost of applying the full weight matrix of the network (state‑of‑the‑art vision or language models typically have >1 billion parameters).

---

## Choosing **m**, **b** and Block Sizes

BoFT provides a two‑dimensional hyper‑parameter space:

| Parameter | Meaning | Typical Values | Effect |
|-----------|---------|----------------|--------|
| `b` (block size) | Size of each non‑zero block in the butterfly component. | 2, 4, 8, 16 | Controls the intrinsic sparsity; smaller `b` ⇒ fewer parameters but also lower expressiveness. |
| `m` (stage count) | Number of butterfly stages (i.e., number of matrix productions). | `ceil(log_2(d/b))` | Controls how many times you interleave 2×2 rotations; larger `m` increases expressiveness. |
| `m * b` | Total number of parameters | N/A | Should stay within your budget. |

**Guidelines.**

1. **Budget First.** If your target task allows only 0.1 % of the original model’s parameters to be adapted, start by picking `b=2` and compute `m` accordingly. For example, for a `4096`‑dimensional linear layer and a 0.1 % budget, `b=2` gives `m=12` (ℰ ≈ 49 000 params).  

2. **Sensitivity to Layer Size.** Layers closer to the input or output typically benefit from a richer parameterization because they aggregate more raw features; consider a higher `b` or an extra stage (`m+1`) for those layers.  

3. **Classic Transforms as Starting Points.** If you know that your task relies heavily on Fourier‑like features (e.g., image inpainting, audio synthesis), initialize `b` to 2 and `m` to log₂d, which aligns with the classic FFT butterfly.  

4. **Empirical Sweeps.** Perform a short sweep over `b={2,4,8}` and `m={log₂d, log₂d-1, log₂d-2}` while monitoring the validation loss. The sweet spot is often one or two stages less than the full possible depth, as the extra stages yield diminishing returns while adding a small overhead.

---

## Warm‑up and Learning Rates

Orthogonal finetuning enjoys a strong inductive bias, but it is still prone to transient exploding or vanishing gradients if the learning rate is too high. The recommended schedule follows a two‑stage cosine learning‑rate decay with a linear warm‑up.

1. **Warm‑up.**  
   * Duration: 1 %–3 % of total training steps (e.g., 100–300 steps).  
   * Schedule: Linear from `lr_start = 1e-5` to `lr_peak = lr_base`.  

2. **Base Learning Rate `lr_base`.**  
   * For large language models (LLMs): `3e-5`.  
   * For vision transformers (ViT): `1e-4`.  
   * For diffusion generative models: `5e-5`.  

3. **Cosine Decay.**  
   * From `lr_base` down to `1e-5` over the remaining steps.  
   * Smoothing at the end helps prevent overshooting the optimum.  

4. **Per‑Layer Rate Scaling.**  
   * Since BOFT is shared across the entire layer, we typically keep a single schedule for all butterfly components.  
   * If you observe that the early layers over‑adapt, apply a factor of `0.8` to their learning rate; conversely, multiply by `1.2` for deeper layers if the gradient vanishes.

---

## Optimizer Selection (AdamW, Adam, ‑…)

The BOFT parameterization preserves the *shape* of the weight matrix but not its *magnitude*. Therefore, the optimizer should handle both adaptive learning rates and weight‑decay properly.

| Optimizer | Key Properties | Recommended Settings |
|-----------|----------------|----------------------|
| `AdamW` | Weight decay decoupled from gradient step; 
Adaptive per‑parameter learning rates. | `betas=(0.9,0.95)`, `weight_decay=0.01` for BOFT parameters; `weight_decay=0` for frozen backbone weights. |
| `Adam` | Weight decay applied via L2 penalty. | Use the same hyper‑parameters as AdamW but be cautious: may penalize the orthogonal parameters too heavily. |
| `LAMB` | Helpful in training massive character‑level LLMs with very large batch sizes. | Use if you have >32 GB GPU; otherwise, AdamW suffices. |

> **Why AdamW?** The orthogonal matrices are being modified multiplicatively. AdamW’s decoupled weight decay treats the orthogonal parameters as learnable “bends” of the rotation, allowing the optimizer to keep them close to `I` without shrinking the magnitude artificially. In experiments, AdamW consistently outperformed Adam on BOFT.

---

## Monitoring Convergence and Overfitting

Because BOFT operates with fewer degrees of freedom than LoRA, it is less likely to over‑fit raw gradients, but it can still exhibit pathological behavior if the orthogonality constraints fail or if the warm‑up is too aggressive. A set of diagnostics and checkpoints can catch these issues early.

### 1. Orthogonality Loss

Although the parameterization guarantees that each butterfly component is orthogonal by construction, finite‑precision arithmetic may drift. Compute an orthogonality loss after each update:

\[
L_{\text{ortho}} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \| \tilde{B}_i^{\top}\tilde{B}_i - I \|_F^2 .
\]

If `L_ortho` exceeds \(10^{-5}\), the Cayley transform parameters have gone out of range. In practice, we observe this rarely once the initial learning rate is capped at `lr_base`.

### 2. Gradient Clipping

A simple clip budget on the norm of the gradient of the BOFT parameters stabilises training:

\[
\|\nabla_{\theta} L\|_2 \leq 1.0 .
\]

This clipping is especially useful when fine‑tuning diffusion models with high learning rates.

### 3. Learning Curves and Early Stopping

Plot the loss and any task‑specific metric (e.g., BLEU, PSNR) per epoch. Since BOFT has significantly fewer trainable parameters, it typically converges in fewer epochs than LoRA. Use **early stopping** with a patience of 5 epochs: if no improvement on the validation metric is seen, halt training.

### 4. Checkpointing and Reproducibility

Because BOFT introduces a new weight matrix `R` that is learned jointly with the downstream model, it is easy to accidentally overwrite the pretrained backbone. Save checkpoints that include:

* `state_dict` of the frozen backbone.  
* `state_dict` of the BOFT parameters.  
* Optimizer state (for `AdamW`).  

Re‑loading a checkpoint should regenerate the exact same output on a deterministic batch.

---

## Practical Tips for Stable Training

| Issue | Symptom | Fix |
|-------|---------|-----|
| **Froze Layer & BOFT Weight Drift** | As training progresses, the effective weight diverges from the original distribution of the frozen backbone. | Introduce a small L2 penalty on `R` toward identity: \( \lambda \| R - I \|_F^2 \) with \( \lambda \in [1e-3, 1e-2] \). |
| **Under‑utilisation of BOFT Capacity** | Validation performance plateaus early. | Increase `m` by one stage or increase `b` if budget allows. |
| **Training Instability on Very Small Datasets** | Loss oscillates, gradient norms explode. | Reduce `lr_base` to `1e-5` and add a more aggressive gradient clipping threshold (`0.5`). |
| **Numerical Symmetry Breakdown** | Orthogonality loss starts to increase after 10k steps. | Re‑initialize the forward angles to zero every 5k steps; the orthogonal constraint will guide them back. |
| **Inference‑time Latency** | None; `R @ W0` is fused post‐training. | Ensure you store the pre‑multiplied weight matrix \( R W^0 \) in the final checkpoint. |

## Summary

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning offers a disciplined, mathematically grounded way to adapt foundation models with a drastically reduced parameter budget. By factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of butterfly components, one achieves:

1. **Parameter Efficiency.** \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable weights per linear layer versus \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) for a naive orthogonal matrix or \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) for LoRA.
2. **Implicit Regularisation.** The product of orthogonal matrices preserves both the spectral norm and the angular geometry of the pretrained weight matrix.
3. **Flexibility.** The two hyper‑parameters `m` and `b` enable fine‑grained control over the capacity and the inductive bias.

This chapter has taken you from the abstract definition of BOFT to an actionable, PyTorch‑ready implementation, illustrated how to choose hyper‑parameters, how to stabilise training, and how to monitor convergence. Armed with these tools, you’re ready to apply BOFT to vision transformers, language models, and diffusion generative pipelines with confidence.

# Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

In this chapter we delve into the empirical evidence that illustrates the practical impact of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT).  The experiments span three distinct classes of foundation models—vision transformers, large language models, and text‑to‑image diffusion generators—each evaluated on a representative suite of downstream tasks.  The goal is to demonstrate that BOFT attains state‑of‑the‑art transfer performance while remaining highly parameter‑efficient, and to analyze how its design hyper‑parameters (\(m\), \(b\)) and regularization (drop‑out) affect generalization.

## 1. Vision Tasks – Image Classification and Segmentation

### 1.1 Datasets and Experimental Protocol

We evaluated BOFT on the ImageNet‑1K classification benchmark and the COCO 2017 segmentation challenge.  Vision‑transformer backbones of varying sizes were selected: the 12‑layer ViT‑S (384 M parameters) and the 24‑layer ViT‑B (632 M parameters).  All models were first pretrained in an unsupervised contrastive setting following [CLIP] and then adapted to the downstream tasks.

Finetuning was conducted for 30 epochs (ImageNet) or 20 epochs (COCO), with a batch size of 512.  The learning rate schedule followed the “linear warm‑up followed by cosine decay” pattern, with the base learning rate set to \(10^{-3}\).  No learning‑rate decay was applied to the orthogonal parameters, in accordance with the theory that orthogonal transforms preserve the spectral norm of the frozen weights.

Comparators included:
- **LoRA** (rank‑16 update, uniform across layers).  
- **OFT** (block‑diagonal, block size \(b=d/4\)).  
- **Full finetuning** (all weights trainable).  
- **Adapter tuning** (small bottleneck adapters, rank‑8).

All experiments were repeated three times with different random seeds to report mean ± standard deviation.

The BOFT configuration with a moderate depth (\(m=4\) or \(6\)) and small block size (\(b=8\) or \(12\)) almost matches full finetuning while using less than a quarter of the trainable parameters. ## 4. Performance Metrics and Statistical Analysis

### 4.1 Parameter‑Efficiency Metric

We define the *Effective Parameter‑Efficiency* (EPE) as:

\[
\mathrm{EPE} = \frac{(1 - \frac{P_{\text{trainable}}}{P_{\text{full}}}) \times \Delta \text{Metric}}{\log_2 P_{\text{full}}},
\]

where \(P_{\text{trainable}}\) is the trainable parameter count, \(P_{\text{full}}\) is the full model parameter count, and \(\Delta \text{Metric}\) is the absolute performance improvement over the baseline (e.g., reduction in FID).  BOFT consistently scores higher LEE across all modalities compared to OFT and LoRA.

### 4.2 Significance Testing

For each benchmark, we calculated a two‑tailed Student’s \(t\)-test between BOFT and the next best method.  In all cases, the \(p\)-value was below 0.01, indicating that the performance gains are statistically significant.  For instance, on ImageNet classification with ViT‑B, BOFT’s 86.7 % top‑1 accuracy was significantly better than OFT’s 85.1 % (p = 0.004).

## 7. Outlook

The empirical evidence underscores the promise of orthogonal butterfly finetuning as a versatile, parameter‑efficient method.  Future work may explore:
- **Adaptive butterfly depth** per layer, allowing early layers to use shallow butterflies and deeper layers to exploit full orthogonality.

These directions will further cement BOFT’s role as a foundational tool for efficient adaptation of large‑scale foundations to ever‑shifting real‑world tasks.

# Discussion and Future Directions

The advancement of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) brings a new perspective to the continual adaptation of large foundation models. By factorizing dense orthogonal transformations into a cascade of sparse butterfly components, BOFT achieves a remarkable compression of trainable parameters while preserving the theoretical guarantees of orthogonal finetuning—most notably the preservation of pair‑wise angles among neurons and the spectral norm of pretrained layers. Nevertheless, like any nascent methodology, BOFT invites a deeper examination of its practical costs, unexplored design avenues, and potential synergies with complementary approaches. This chapter sheds light on several open questions and proposes concrete directions for future research, all grounded in the insights presented in the foundational paper.

## 1. Training Overhead versus Inference Speeds

### 1.1 The Core Trade‑off

BOFT replaces a single dense orthogonal update with a chain of \(m\) sparse butterfly matrices. While each butterfly component is computationally lightweight (owing to its block‑diagonal structure), the overall forward and backward propagation must traverse \(m\) multiplicative stages. Practically, this inflates the training time by roughly a factor of \(m\) compared to standard OFT. In contrast, inference benefits are neutral: once the orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}(m,b)\) is learned, it can be fused with the pretrained weight \(\bm{W}^0\), and the model can be executed exactly like its unfine‑tuned counterpart.

### 1.2 Quantifying the Overhead

Empirical profiles on vision transformers (ViT‑L/32 and ViT‑H/14) and large language models (GPT‑3 175B proxy) reveal that BOFT with \(m=4\) incurs approximately \(3\times\) slower forward passes, whereas the same training regime with OFT (block‑diagonal size \(b=128\)) incurs only a \(1.2\times\) slowdown. Memory usage, however, remains comparable because both methods maintain a single copy of the orthogonal parameters per layer and do not allocate extra activations beyond what is required for intermediate butterfly multiplications.

### 1.3 Targeted Optimizations

Several engineering paths may mitigate the training cost:

| Technique                                 | Description                                                                                     | Expected Impact        |
|-------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **JIT Acceleration**                       | Fuse consecutive butterfly multiplications into a single kernel using GPU kernels, e.g. cuBLAS  | Reduce kernel launch overhead |
| **Mixed Precision Training**              | Store butterfly parameters in FP16 while computing in FP32 or BF16                              | Decrease memory & compute |
| **Re‑use of Intermediate Activations**    | Cache butterfly outcomes when the same matrix is applied repeatedly (e.g. across multiple layers) | Minor, but cumulative gain |
| **Parallel Butterfly Execution**          | Exploit the independence between different butterflies across layers                           | Speed‑up due to concurrency |

Moreover, the parameter calendar of BOFT—i.e., the depth \(m\) and block size \(b\)—offers a free hyper‑parameter to balance fidelity versus speed. Choosing \(m = \log_2(d/b)\) yields a dense orthogonal matrix with the minimal number of butterfly stages for a given \(d\), thereby granting the most efficient training route.

## 2. Selecting Efficient Network Topologies

### 2.1 Extending Beyond Butterfly

The paper’s information‑transmission framework conceptualizes the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as the design of a connectivity graph on a \(d \times (m+1)\) grid. Although the butterfly graph is optimal for the number of edges (\(O(d \log d)\)), it is not necessarily optimal in wall‑time or hardware utilization. Alternative topologies with different combinatorial properties may yield superior performance.

| Topology                         | Edge Count \(E\) | Path Length | Comments                                      |
|----------------------------------|-------------------|-------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Butterfly                        | \(\Theta(d \log d)\) | \(\Theta(\log d)\) | Classical; can be implemented via radix‑2.  |
| Hyper‑cube                       | \(\Theta(d \log d)\) | \(\Theta(\log d)\) | Similar to butterfly, but different adjacency. |
| Random Sparsity (Cayley-like)    | \(\Theta(d)\)    | \(\Theta(d)\) | Fewer edges, but may fail to satisfy connectivity. |
| Planted Random Regular Graph     | \(\Theta(d)\)    | \(\Theta(\log d)\) | Provides uniform degree distribution.       |
| Small‑world (Watts–Strogatz)      | \(\Theta(d)\)    | \(\Theta(\log d)\) | Low average path length, high clustering.   |

A systematic search across these topologies, guided by the information‑transmission constraints (complete connectivity, orthogonality of each factor), could uncover configurations that reduce both edge count and runtime. For instance, a small‑world topology may enable fewer butterfly stages while preserving dense connectivity.

### 2.2 Learning the Topology

Instead of hand‑crafting the network, we can let the factorization itself be learnable:

1. **Mask‑Learning**: Introduce binary mask parameters for each potential edge, and learn their sparsity patterns jointly with the matrix values, perhaps regularized by a L1 penalty to enforce sparsity.
2. **Neural Architecture Search (NAS)**: Define a search space over directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) describing butterfly factorization, and use reinforcement learning or differentiable NAS to identify a high‑quality topology.

Preliminary experiments revealed that allowing the masks to adapt to the task can yield a savings of up to \(20\%\) in effective trainable parameters without sacrificing accuracy in vision‑classification benchmarks.

## 3. Generalizing BOFT to Other Orthogonal Structures

### 3.1 Block‑Diagonal + Butterfly Hybrid

The paper demonstrated that BOS (Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal + Butterfly) matrices lie between OFT’s block‑diagonal and BOFT’s purely butterfly cases. An explicit hybrid design—where the first few butterfly stages are full with block size \(b\), followed by smaller butterfly stages—has the potential to capture more expressive transformations with a moderate parameter budget.

### 3.2 Structured Orthogonal Transform Bases

Beyond just butterfly factors, there exists a wealth of structured orthogonal bases recognized in numerical linear algebra:

- **Hadamard Transform**: an orthogonal matrix with entries \(\pm1\) and structure that can be recursively defined. Incorporating Hadamard factors can accelerate computation.
- **Discrete Sine/Cosine Transforms**: useful for encoding signal band‑limitedness.
- **Wavelet Transforms**: support multi‑scale representations.

Embedding these as preludes or postludes to the butterfly chain—i.e., \(\bm{R} = \bm{H} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}_{i} \cdot \bm{C}\) where \(\bm{H}\) and \(\bm{C}\) are Hadamard / Cosine—could explicitly endow the orthogonal transformation with a particular inductive bias (spatial locality, frequency emphasis).

### 3.3 Non‑Unitary Orthogonal Variants

The Cayley transform restricts butterfly components to be strictly orthogonal. Relaxations to orthonormal but non‑unitary matrices (e.g., oblique transforms) might enlarge the hypothesis space while still preserving a form of angle preservation. This line of inquiry would require new convergence proofs and may impact inference speed due to potential loss of normality.

## 4. Leveraging Low‑Rank Adapters in Tandem with BOFT

While BOFT excels at preserving the spectral properties of pretrained layers, it remains agnostic to low‑rank information that may be strongly task‑specific. A promising direction is to combine BOFT with a low‑rank fine‑tuning arm:

1. **Orthogonal Plus Low‑Rank**: Update weights as \(\bm{W} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 + \bm{U}\bm{V}^\top)\), where \(\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\).
2. **Orthogonal Plus ADAPTERS**: Add small bottleneck layers after each oriented block (i.e., \(\bm{z} = \bm{W}\bm{x} + \bm{A}\bm{S}\bm{B}\bm{x}\)), where \(\bm{A},\bm{B}\) are lightweight adapter matrices and \(\bm{S}\) represents the projection to a low‑rank subspace.

Such hybrid schemes can capture both global rotational invariances (via BOFT) and local, dimension‑specific adjustments (via LoRA or adapters). Early experiments on VQA and text‑to‑image synthesis illustrate a 5–10% accuracy boost over BOFT alone when the rank \(r\) is set to 32.

## 6. Open Research Questions and Outlook

1. **Optimal Edge‑Count for Dense Orthogonal Realization**: Is \(\Theta(d\log d)\) the theoretical lower bound for connectivity under orthogonality constraints, or can we find sparse topologies with \(\Theta(d)\) edges that still yield dense matrices?
2. **Convergence Guarantees for Multi‑Stage Butterfly Training**: While Cayley transforms guarantee orthogonality, does the product of multiple butterfly factors preserve desirable curvature properties (e.g., Lipschitz continuity) that facilitate training stability?
3. **Transferability Across Domains**: Traditional butterfly transforms are tailored for image and signal data. Can BOFT be adapted to more exotic modalities (e.g., graph neural networks, reinforcement learning policies) where adjacency differs substantially?
4. **Theoretical Expressivity Bounds**: The Expressivity Theorem hints that a product of butterfly matrices can span the full orthogonal group given enough stages. Formalizing the minimum number of stages needed for prescribed approximation accuracy remains an open challenge.
5. **Integration with Model Compression**: Combining BOFT with structured pruning or quantization may yield models that are both parameter‑efficient and compute‑efficient during inference—an avenue ripe for exploration.

By framing the design of sparse orthogonal factorizations as an information‑transmission problem, the paper unlocks a rich design space where network topology, low‑rank adapters, and structured bases can be synergistically combined. Future work that rigorously tackles the aforementioned open questions will not only refine BOFT itself but also illuminate broader principles governing efficient, stable, and generalizable model adaptation.

Key Take‑aways  
--------------  

The central idea of BOFT is the realization that a dense orthogonal matrix—necessary for preserving angular relations in the weight space—can be parameterized as a product of several sparse matrices, each of which is orthogonal. This factorization offers two complementary advantages. First, it reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, where $d$ is the hidden dimension of a linear layer. Second, because each factor is itself a valid rotation, the low‑rank product remains exactly orthogonal throughout training.  

To design a factorization that is both compact and expressive, the authors reframed the problem as an *information transmission* task on a grid graph. With this view, each sparse factor represents a directed edge set that shuttles information from one layer of nodes to the next. The goal is to ensure *dense connectivity*—every source node can reach every sink node—while keeping the total number of free edges as small as possible. The butterfly topology, originating from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform, satisfies both goals with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. Embedding the butterfly into the orthogonal parameterization yields BOFT.  

A practical extension of BOFT includes *identity initialization* of all butterfly components (i.e., the Cayley parameters are set to zero) and a *multiplicative dropout* scheme that randomly replaces selected butterfly blocks with identity matrices. These techniques mirror the successful practices in LoRA and improve regularization without compromising the orthogonal constraint.  

Empirical evaluations across vision, language, and diffusion domains confirm that BOFT not only matches but often surpasses existing PEFT baselines such as LoRA and the original block‑diagonal OFT. Crucially, BOFT does so with fewer edits to the pretrained weights, thereby preserving the knowledge encoded during pre‑training more faithfully.  

Impact on Foundation Model Research  
-----------------------------------  

The authors position BOFT as a *generic* finetuning framework. Unlike previous PEFT methods that target a narrow class of downstream tasks (e.g., controllable text‑to‑image generation for OFT), BOFT demonstrates strong performance across a spectrum of modalities—vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models. This breadth suggests that preserving angular relationships via orthogonal transformations is a universally valuable inductive bias for foundational representations.  

From a theoretical standpoint, BOFT offers a new lens for studying the *expressivity* of structured parameterizations. By proving that a product of butterfly matrices can approximate any orthogonal matrix when augmented with additional mirrored components (Theorem 1), the paper bridges the gap between imprecise heuristic regularizers and formal coverage of the orthogonal group. This insight invites further research on hybrid structures that blend butterfly sparsity with low‑rank factors, potentially yielding even tighter parameter budgets.  

Moreover, the proposed *information transmission* viewpoint opens a cross‑disciplinary dialogue with network topology analysis. Future work may harness advanced routing heuristics from communication networks or coding theory to design novel sparsity patterns that balance expressivity, trainability, and computational overhead.  

Future Impact on Industry Deployments  
-----------------------------------  

In industrial settings, the boundary between research and production is increasingly tight, as companies strive to localize large foundation models without incurring prohibitive computational costs. BOFT’s parameter efficiency has two concrete benefits:  

1. **Reduced Storage Footprint** – Replacing a full weight matrix with a sequence of butterfly components cuts the number of updatable entries from $d^2$ to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$. For a 12‑billion‑parameter transformer, the savings can amount to millions of dimensions, which directly translates into faster storage, quicker upload times, and reduced model distribution costs.  

2. **Zero‑Latency Inference** – Although training a BOFT‑fine‑tuned model requires an extra matrix multiplication (the product of $m$ butterfly factors), this cost is only incurred during training. Once training ends, the composite orthogonal matrix can be fused into the base weights. The inference path remains identical to the unmodified foundation model, ensuring that latency budgets are unaffected—a critical requirement for real‑time applications such as chatbots, image‐generation APIs, and recommendation engines.  

Because BOFT can be paired with existing prompt or adapter mechanisms, it offers a modular layer that can be toggled on for domain adaptation, personalization, or compliance controls (e.g., ensuring that downstream models operate within a bounded spectral norm). This flexibility positions BOFT as a foundational building block for growth‑scaled ML platforms.  

In summary, Orthogonal Butterfly elevates orthogonal finetuning from a niche technique to a versatile, theoretically grounded, and practically efficient strategy. By marrying the mathematical elegance of butterfly factorization with the pragmatic demands of modern AI deployment, BOFT charts a promising path for the next generation of foundation‑model adaptation research.
