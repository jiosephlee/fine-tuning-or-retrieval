\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 8: # Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

In this chapter we delve into the empirical evidence that illustrates the practical impact of Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT).  The experiments span three distinct classes of foundation models—vision transformers, large language models, and text‑to‑image diffusion generators—each evaluated on a representative suite of downstream tasks.  The goal is to demonstrate that BOFT attains state‑of‑the‑art transfer performance while remaining highly parameter‑efficient, and to analyze how its design hyper‑parameters (\(m\), \(b\)) and regularization (drop‑out) affect generalization.

## 1. Vision Tasks – Image Classification and Segmentation

### 1.1 Datasets and Experimental Protocol

We evaluated BOFT on the ImageNet‑1K classification benchmark and the COCO 2017 segmentation challenge.  Vision‑transformer backbones of varying sizes were selected: the 12‑layer ViT‑S (384 M parameters) and the 24‑layer ViT‑B (632 M parameters).  All models were first pretrained in an unsupervised contrastive setting following [CLIP] and then adapted to the downstream tasks.

Finetuning was conducted for 30 epochs (ImageNet) or 20 epochs (COCO), with a batch size of 512.  The learning rate schedule followed the “linear warm‑up followed by cosine decay” pattern, with the base learning rate set to \(10^{-3}\).  No learning‑rate decay was applied to the orthogonal parameters, in accordance with the theory that orthogonal transforms preserve the spectral norm of the frozen weights.

Comparators included:
- **LoRA** (rank‑16 update, uniform across layers).  
- **OFT** (block‑diagonal, block size \(b=d/4\)).  
- **Full finetuning** (all weights trainable).  
- **Adapter tuning** (small bottleneck adapters, rank‑8).

All experiments were repeated three times with different random seeds to report mean ± standard deviation.

### 1.2 Image Classification Results

| Backbone | Task | Method | # Trainable Params | Top‑1 Accuracy |
|----------|------|--------|--------------------|----------------|
| ViT‑S  | ImageNet | Full | 36 M | 85.4 % |
| ViT‑S  | ImageNet | LoRA | 1.1 M | 83.8 % |
| ViT‑S  | ImageNet | OFT (b = 384/4) | 1.1 M | 84.3 % |
| ViT‑S  | ImageNet | **BOFT (m=4,b=8)** | 0.69 M | **85.0 %** |
| ViT‑B  | ImageNet | Full | 241 M | 87.2 % |
| ViT‑B  | ImageNet | LoRA | 1.3 M | 84.5 % |
| ViT‑B  | ImageNet | OFT (b = 632/4) | 1.3 M | 85.1 % |
| ViT‑B  | ImageNet | **BOFT (m=6,b=12)** | 0.88 M | **86.7 %** |

The BOFT configuration with a moderate depth (\(m=4\) or \(6\)) and small block size (\(b=8\) or \(12\)) almost matches full finetuning while using less than a quarter of the trainable parameters.  Importantly, the parameter budget is substantially lower than that of OFT because the multiplication of \(\log d\) butterfly components incurs only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters.

### 1.3 Segmentation Results

On COCO pan‑optic segmentation, we fine‑tuned the same ViT backbones by inserting a lightweight decoder on top.  While the decoder introduces additional parameters, we focus on the adapter cost of the backbone.

| Backbone | Method | Trainable Params | MAP |
|----------|--------|------------------|-----|
| ViT‑S | Full | 25 M | 55.2 % |
| ViT‑S | LoRA | 0.8 M | 52.6 % |
| ViT‑S | OFT | 0.8 M | 53.9 % |
| ViT‑S | **BOFT (m=4,b=8)** | 0.5 M | **55.1 %** |
| ViT‑B | Full | 186 M | 60.4 % |
| ViT‑B | LoRA | 1.1 M | 57.3 % |
| ViT‑B | OFT | 1.1 M | 58.8 % |
| ViT‑B | **BOFT (m=6,b=12)** | 0.76 M | **60.1 %** |

Again, BOFT approaches full finetuning performance with roughly one‑sixth of the trainable parameters.  The pairwise‑angle preservation property of orthogonal transformations appears to help maintain the internal feature geometry that the decoder relies on.

## 2. Language Tasks – Fine‑tuning GPT‑style Models

### 2.1 Model and Data

We fine‑tuned a 7 B parameter GPT‑3 style decoder on two canonical benchmarks:
- **WIKITEXT‑103** (language modelling).  
- **GLUE** (mixed classification and regression).

The base GPT‑3 model was pretrained in a massive unsupervised corpus.  We adopted the same block‑diagonal OFT strategy as the vision experiments for a baseline, and then compared to BOFT.

### 2.2 Results

| Task | Method | Trainable Params | Perplexity / Accuracy |
|------|--------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Wikitext‑103 | Full | 102 M | 5.21 |
| Wikitext‑103 | LoRA (rank‑32) | 1.6 M | 6.45 |
| Wikitext‑103 | OFT (b = 768) | 1.6 M | 6.12 |
| Wikitext‑103 | **BOFT (m=3,b=32)** | 0.94 M | **5.94** |
| GLUE | Full | 102 M | 84.7 % |
| GLUE | LoRA | 1.6 M | 78.9 % |
| GLUE | OFT | 1.6 M | 80.5 % |
| GLUE | **BOFT (m=4,b=64)** | 1.24 M | **82.8 %** |

The absolute gains on GLUE are modest because the dataset is small relative to the model size; however, BOFT nevertheless approaches the performance of full finetuning with significantly fewer parameters, while maintaining a smoother optimization trajectory thanks to the orthogonality constraint.

### 2.3 Training Stability

During language finetuning, orthogonal transformations prevent the gradient explosion commonly seen when fine‑tuning large sequential models.  We observed that the orthogonal learning rate could be set higher for BOFT without incurring divergence, whereas LoRA tended to overshoot the optimum quickly.

## 3. Diffusion Tasks – Controllable Image Generation

### 3.1 Settings

We adopted the Stable Diffusion v2.1 diffusion backbone (\(~3.5\) B parameters) and fitted a conditioning‑based U‑Net controller.  The diffusion model was fine‑tuned on the **DALLE‑2 ControlNet** dataset, which consists of 100 K paired images and prompt–control embeddings.  Evaluation focused on the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) for generated samples and on task‑specific metrics such as DSC for segmentation‑guided generation.

### 3.2 Results

| Method | Trainable Params | FID@50 | DSC |
|--------|------------------|--------|-----|
| Full finetune | 200 M | 14.2 | 68.5 % |
| LoRA (rank‑64) | 5.6 M | 18.7 | 63.2 % |
| OFT (b = 768) | 5.6 M | 17.4 | 65.1 % |
| **BOFT (m=5,b=8)** | 3.2 M | **15.1** | **66.8 %** |

BOFT reduces the per‑parameter impact on the final image quality, achieving a “lower is better” FID value comparable to fine‑tuning with roughly a third of the trainable weight budget.

### 3.3 Control Precision

We also measured the **Semantic Alignment Score (SAS)**, a metric that quantifies how well the generated image adheres to the user‑supplied control.  BOFT consistently outperformed OFT and LoRA, underscoring the importance of orthogonal transformations for preserving the pre‑trained conditioning logic embedded in the diffusion network.

## 4. Performance Metrics and Statistical Analysis

### 4.1 Parameter‑Efficiency Metric

We define the *Effective Parameter‑Efficiency* (EPE) as:

\[
\mathrm{EPE} = \frac{(1 - \frac{P_{\text{trainable}}}{P_{\text{full}}}) \times \Delta \text{Metric}}{\log_2 P_{\text{full}}},
\]

where \(P_{\text{trainable}}\) is the trainable parameter count, \(P_{\text{full}}\) is the full model parameter count, and \(\Delta \text{Metric}\) is the absolute performance improvement over the baseline (e.g., reduction in FID).  BOFT consistently scores higher LEE across all modalities compared to OFT and LoRA.

### 4.2 Significance Testing

For each benchmark, we calculated a two‑tailed Student’s \(t\)-test between BOFT and the next best method.  In all cases, the \(p\)-value was below 0.01, indicating that the performance gains are statistically significant.  For instance, on ImageNet classification with ViT‑B, BOFT’s 86.7 % top‑1 accuracy was significantly better than OFT’s 85.1 % (p = 0.004).

## 5. Ablation Studies on \((m, b)\) and Drop‑out

### 5.1 Effect of Butterfly Depth \(m\)

We varied the depth \(m\) while fixing the block size \(b=16\) on both ViT‑S and Stable Diffusion.  Table 1 below shows the trend.

| \(m\) | Trainable Params | ViT‑S Top‑1 | Diffusion FID |
|------|------------------|-------------|---------------|
| 2 | 1.6 M | 84.0 % | 18.1 |
| 4 | 0.81 M | 85.0 % | 15.1 |
| 6 | 0.43 M | 85.4 % | 14.4 |

For \(m \geq 4\), the marginal benefit of increasing \(m\) diminishes, suggesting a sweet spot where the product of butterfly components captures sufficient interactions without excessive multiplication cost.

### 5.2 Effect of Block Size \(b\)

Next, we fixed \(m=4\) and varied \(b\).  Larger \(b\) reduces the depth of butterflies needed but increases per‑matrix sparsity.

| \(b\) | Trainable Params | ViT‑S Top‑1 | Diffusion FID |
|------|------------------|-------------|---------------|
| 8 | 0.69 M | 85.3 % | 15.0 |
| 16 | 0.44 M | 85.4 % | 14.8 |
| 32 | 0.22 M | **85.6 %** | 14.5 |

The performance peaks around \(b=16\), balancing expressivity and parameter count.

### 5.3 Drop‑out Ablation

We introduced **multiplicative dropout** during BOFT training, randomly zeroing out percentages of butterfly components.  Figure 2 plots FID versus dropout ratio.

- Zero dropout: 15.1 FID
- 10 % dropout: 15.0 FID
- 20 % dropout: 15.3 FID
- 30 % dropout: 16.2 FID

A moderate dropout (≈ 10 %) slightly improves generalization, likely by preventing overfitting to the few orthogonal degrees of freedom.  Excessive dropout degrades performance due to the intrinsic regularization being too strong.

## 6. Comparative Summary

| Method | Domains | Trainable Params (≈ % of full) | Typical Accuracy / FID | Strengths |
|--------|---------|-------------------------------|------------------------|-----------|
| LoRA | Vision, Language, Diffusion | 0.5–1 % | Good, but limited by additive rank‑bias | Simple, fast, minimal overhead |
| OFT | Vision, Language, Diffusion | 0.5–1 % | Better than LoRA, but block‑diagonal rigidity | Angle‑preserving by design |
| BOFT | Vision, Language, Diffusion | 0.1–0.4 % | Matches or surpasses OFT; ≈ +1 % accuracy | Parameter‑efficient; dense orthogonal; structured inductive bias |

In essence, BOFT leverages the butterfly factorization to construct a dense orthogonal transformation with only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters, thereby achieving performance that is competitive with full finetuning while drastically reducing the learnable footprint.  The structured sparsity introduces an inductive bias that aligns with common linear operators (Fourier, Hadamard), which empirically translates into better generalization across a spectrum of downstream tasks.

## 7. Outlook

The empirical evidence underscores the promise of orthogonal butterfly finetuning as a versatile, parameter‑efficient method.  Future work may explore:
- **Adaptive butterfly depth** per layer, allowing early layers to use shallow butterflies and deeper layers to exploit full orthogonality.  
- **Hybrid regularization** combining BOFT with low‑rank adapters (LoRA) to capture both angle‑preserving and subspace‑reparameterization benefits.  
- **Hardware acceleration** for the successive butterfly multiplications to mitigate training overhead.  

These directions will further cement BOFT’s role as a foundational tool for efficient adaptation of large‑scale foundations to ever‑shifting real‑world tasks.
