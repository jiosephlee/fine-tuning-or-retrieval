\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley parameterization ensure the learned orthogonal matrix stays exactly orthogonal during BOFT training?
Question:
In BOFT we define each butterfly component using a skew‑symmetric parameter ∫ such that R=(I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1}. I understand that if Q is skew‑symmetric then (I+Q)(I−Q)^{-1} is orthogonal, but could the Jacobian or numerical errors during back‑propagation cause the constraint to drift? How is orthogonality enforced in practice, and are there any stability tricks (e.g. re‑projection, step‑size restrictions) that the paper uses to keep ∫ in the skew‑symmetric manifold?
Answer:
**Short answer**

BOFT keeps the orthogonal constraint *exactly* during training.  
Each butterfly component is parameterised by a skew–symmetric matrix \(Q\) and a Cayley transform  

\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\; .
\]

Because the transform is a rational function, the orthogonality identity  

\[
R^{\top}R = I
\]

holds for *any* real \(Q\) (as long as \(I-Q\) is invertible).  The only thing that matters for the optimization is that the **numerical evaluation** of \((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) remains accurate during forward and backward passes.  BOFT therefore relies on the following two practical measures:

1. **Small learning rates & identity initialization** – the paper initializes every \(Q\) with zeros (so \(R=I\)) and applies a very small step size. This keeps \(\|Q\|\) small → the matrix \((I-Q)\) stays well‑conditioned and the inversion is numerically stable.
2. **Automatic differentiation of the Cayley formula** – PyTorch’s autograd takes the exact derivative of the function above.  Because the Jacobian is obtained through the same numerical routine that computes the forward pass, any drift that might happen if one merely updated \(R\) directly is avoided.  The derivative is

   \[
   \frac{\partial R}{\partial Q} \;=\; 2(I-Q)^{-T}(I+Q) (I-Q)^{-1},
   \]

   and this is applied exactly in back‑prop.  No re‑projection or clipping is needed.

If, in a very unlikely event, the condition number of \(I-Q\) becomes large (e.g. due to an accidental blow‑up of some \(Q_{ij}\)), the division by zero or overflow would trigger a NaN in the loss; the usual training‑time safety checks (e.g. loss‑clipping or gradient clipping) would immediately stop the optimisation so the parameter never drifts.

---

### Why the constraint does not “drift”

A matrix \(R\) is orthogonal iff \(R^{\top}R = I\).  
Suppose we compute \(R\) as a floating‑point expression from \(Q\).  Even after many forward–backward mini‑steps, as long as the *forward* evaluation of the Cayley transform is exact (up to machine precision), the resulting \(R\) will still satisfy \(R^{\top}R = I\) up to round‑off error.  The underlying reason is that the Cayley transform is *exactly* orthogonal algebraically; the numerical error only comes from the finite‑precision inversion of \((I-Q)\).  Because \((I-Q)\) is kept nonsingular and well‑conditioned by the small learning rate, the round‑off remains on the order of machine epsilon (≈\(10^{-16}\) in double precision), which is far below any training‑time drift.

In contrast, methods that simply apply a gradient update on \(R\) (e.g. multiplying by a random matrix) would need a *sub‑step* projection back onto the orthogonal set after every update; otherwise the orthogonality would degrade.

---

### Practical tricks that appear in the paper

| Trick | What it does | Why it matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Identity initialization** | \(Q=0 \Rightarrow R=I\) | Starts the optimisation exactly at the identity, so the first few gradients are modest. |
| **Very small learning rate (e.g. \(10^{-4}\))** | Keeps \(\|Q\|\) small | Prevents \((I-Q)\) from becoming ill‑conditioned. |
| **Drop‑out is *multiplicative*** | Randomly replaces butterfly components with identities | Helps avoid over‑fitting without breaking the orthogonality constraint. |
| **No projection or clipping** | The orthogonality is maintained by the transform itself | Avoids expensive re‑projection operations. |

These are the only stability tricks reported in the BOFT paper.  Nothing else (e.g. re‑projection, line‑search, special optimisers) is needed.

---

### Final take‑away

- The Cayley parameterisation guarantees analytic orthogonality; it is a *hard* constraint that does not depend on the particular values of \(Q\).  
- Numerical drift is practically negligible because the update step keeps \(Q\) small and the inversion of \((I-Q)\) stays well‑conditioned.  
- The paper therefore does **not** re‑project, nor does it impose any additional step‑size restrictions beyond a modest learning rate; it relies on the inherent orthogonality of the Cayley transform and on careful numerical implementation.  

So, in short, BOFT keeps the learned matrix exactly orthogonal by construction, and the only practical safeguards are a clean initialization, a small learning rate, and the multiplicative‑dropout trick to maintain regularisation.

### What is the intuition behind the 'minimum free edges' requirement in the information‑transmission framework?
Question:
The paper states that each orthogonal factor must contain at least d edges to maintain full rank, and these edges are counted separately from trainable parameters because they are fixed to ±1. I’m puzzled: why can we consider those edges as ‘free’? Wouldn’t any non‑zero entry in a sparse orthogonal matrix still contribute to the parameter count if it is learnable? How does the authors’ counting of non‑zero edges reconcile with the usual parameter‑counting in sparse matrices?
Answer:
The “free edges” discussion appears in the **information‑transmission view** of Section 3.1.  
There the authors regard the factorisation  

\[
\mathbf R=\mathbf B_m\mathbf B_{m-1}\dots\mathbf B_1
\]

as a path‑finding problem on a \(d\times(m+1)\) grid. Each matrix \(\mathbf B_i\) is a sparse \(d\times d\) matrix and its non‑zero pattern is interpreted as a set of directed edges between adjacent levels of the grid.  
For \(\mathbf R\) to be a *dense* orthogonal matrix we need a path from every node in the first level to every node in the last level.  
The “freedom” that the authors talk about is the *minimal number of edges that must be present between two consecutive levels* for the whole network to be fully connected **and** for \(\mathbf B_i\) to be orthogonal.

---

### Why are some edges *free*?

An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf B^\top\mathbf B=\mathbf I\).  
A basic property of orthogonal matrices is that each row (and each column) must contain at least one non‑zero entry; otherwise \(\mathbf B\) would have a zero row and could not be invertible.  
Because of orthogonality, the sparsity pattern is highly coupled: if the entry \((1,1)\) is zero, then the entry \((2,2)\) must also be zero, and so on.  
In fact, for a *full‑rank* sparse orthogonal matrix the smallest possible non‑zero pattern is a \(d\times d\) matrix with exactly \(d\) non‑zeros per column (or per row). Those \(d\) non‑zeros can always be chosen to be \(\pm1\). This is the classical “permutation‑plus‑sign” matrix and it is already orthogonal.

Thus, **those \(d\) non‑zeros required for each \(\mathbf B_i\) are not learnable**: they are fixed to \(\pm1\) by the orthogonality constraint.  
The authors call these “free edges” because they do not contribute to the *parameter budget*—there is nothing to optimise for them. Whenever a sparse orthogonal matrix is constructed, we can guarantee the existence of a set of \(d\) mandatory edges that satisfy orthogonality and which can be left fixed.  

---

### How does this reconcile with the usual notion of sparse‑matrix parameters?

In a generic sparse matrix, every non‑zero entry is a free scalar that the optimiser will adjust.  
Two consequences follow:

1. **Counting non‑zeros**: In the usual sparse‑matrix setting you would count every non‑zero element as a parameter.  
2. **Learnability**: Each non‑zero entry can, in principle, take any real value (subject to any regulariser), so the optimiser has freedom over that element.

For *orthogonal* sparse matrices the situation is different:

* The orthogonality constraint *removes degrees of freedom*: once the pattern is chosen, every non‑zero that belongs to the minimal “permutation‑plus‑sign” backbone must be fixed to \(\pm1\).  
* The remaining non‑zeros (those that are not part of this minimal backbone) are the **only** terms that can actually change during training.  
* Consequently, when the authors state that an orthogonal factor must contain at least \(d\) edges, they mean that \(d\) edges are *inherent* because of orthogonality and therefore do not count toward the trainable parameters.  

Mathematically, if we write the Cayley parameterisation \(\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) with \(\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^\top\), the \(\pm1\) entries arise from the identity part (\(\mathbf I\)) in the factorisation. These entries are never updated. The learnable part of \(\mathbf Q\) contributes \(\frac{1}{2}d(d-1)\) free parameters for a dense \(\mathbf Q\), but after imposing the sparsity pattern of \(\mathbf B_i\) the number is reduced accordingly.  

Therefore, when the paper says *“minimum free edges”* they refer to **the obligatory \(\pm1\) edges that an orthogonal sparse matrix must have so that the matrix can be full‑rank, and these edges are not counted in the parameter budget**.

---

### Intuition from a simple example

Consider the smallest non‑trivial case \(d=2\).  
An arbitrary orthogonal matrix looks like

\[
\mathbf B=
\begin{bmatrix}
c & s\\
-s & c
\end{bmatrix},
\]

with \(c^2+s^2=1\).  
If we insist on a sparse pattern with only two non‑zeros per column, we have to choose

\[
\mathbf B=
\begin{bmatrix}
\pm1 & 0\\
0 & \pm1
\end{bmatrix},
\]

which fulfils the orthogonality constraint and uses exactly two edges (one per column).  
The signs \(\pm1\) are fixed, so there are **no free parameters** in this pattern.  
If we wanted a different sparse pattern (e.g. a single “outer‑product” structure), we would add *one* learnable non‑zero per column; the matrix would then have a total of three non‑zeros, but only one of them would be free. The two fixed \(\pm1\) entries are the “free edges” that the authors mention.

---

### Bottom line

The counting scheme used in the paper is tailored to **orthogonal** sparse matrices. Because orthogonality forces a minimal set of \(\pm1\) entries, those entries are considered *fixed* and thus *free* in the sense that they do not contribute to the parameter count.  
When you see a statement like “each orthogonal factor must contain at least \(d\) edges, and these edges are not counted”, it is a reminder that you should only count the *learnable* non‑zeros. This convention aligns with the authors’ information‑transmission graph, where the goal is to reduce the number of *trainable* edges while still guaranteeing full connectivity and orthogonality.

### Why does a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix fail to approximate classic linear transforms like the Fourier matrix?
Question:
In the discussion on inductive bias, the authors claim that block‑diagonal OFT cannot recover any classic linear transform, whereas butterfly‑based BOFT can. Could you clarify mathematically why block‑diagonal structure prevents representing transforms that mix all input dimensions, while butterfly structure still allows global mixing?
Answer:
**Why a block–diagonal orthogonal matrix can never emulate a transform like the Fourier matrix, while a butterfly‑factorized orthogonal matrix can**

---

### 1.  What does “mixing all dimensions” mean?

A linear transform \(T\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) *mixes all dimensions* if every output coordinate is a linear combination of **every** input coordinate, i.e. no entry of \(T\) is identically zero.  
The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix \(F\) has this property:  
\[
F_{k,j}=e^{-2\pi i kj/d}\neq 0 \quad\forall\, k,j .
\]

Hence, if an orthogonal matrix \(R\) is to approximate \(F\) up to a small error, the pattern of zeros in \(R\) must be the same as in \(F\) (none).

---

### 2.  Block–diagonal orthogonal matrices

A block–diagonal orthogonal matrix is of the form
\[
R=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(R^{(1)},R^{(2)},\dots,R^{(r)}\right),
\qquad
R^{(t)}\in\mathbb R^{b\times b},\;br=d .
\]
The sparsity pattern is that the set \(\{1,\ldots d\}\) is split into \(r\) disjoint blocks and entries that connect two different blocks are exactly zero.  
Consequently, for any two indices \(i\) and \(j\) belonging to different blocks one has
\[
R_{ij}=0\quad\text{and}\quad (R^T)_{ij}=0 .
\]
Thus, the matrix *fails to mix* any two dimensions that fall into distinct blocks.

Because the Fourier matrix has every entry non‑zero, no block‑diagonal matrix can be close to it in Frobenius or spectral norm: the block‑diagonal structure forces a block of zeros that the DFT simply never contains.  
Formally, for any block‑diagonal \(R\)

\[
\|F-R\|_F^2 \;\ge\; \sum_{\text{block‑off‑diagonal}}\bigl|F_{ij}\bigr|^2
= \frac{d^2}{2},
\]
since half of the entries of \(F\) lie between different blocks.

So, **the inductive bias of block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices is that they keep each block independent and cannot model global interactions** – exactly why they cannot approximate “classic” transforms that mix all coordinates.

---

### 3.  Butterfly‑factorized orthogonal matrices

A butterfly matrix \(B(d)\) is defined recursively as a product of \(\log_2 d\) sparse factors whose non‑zero pattern looks like the well‑known Cooley–Tukey “butterfly” diagram.  
Each factor
\[
\tilde B(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(B^F_1(k),\dots,B^F_{d/k}(k)\bigr)
\]
has only \(2k\) non‑zeros per row, yet the *global* product
\[
R = \tilde B(d,2)\,\tilde B(d,4)\,\dots\,\tilde B(d,d/2)
\]
has no structural zeros.  

Intuitively, the butterfly factors are **interleaving permutations and 2‑by‑2 rotations**. After the first factor, every input dimension influences two intermediate nodes; after the second factor, each of those intermediate nodes influences two new nodes, and so on. After \(\log_2 d\) stages, every input has a path to every output – the concatenated graph is a fully connected bipartite graph, which guarantees that the resulting matrix can have non‑zero entries everywhere.

Mathematically, for a butterfly factorization with \(\log_2 d\) stages,
\[
R = \prod_{i=1}^{\log_2 d} \tilde B(d,2^i),
\]
each \(\tilde B(d,2^i)\) is orthogonal (parameterized by a skew‑symmetric matrix via Cayley). Because each factor is invertible, the product \(R\) is orthogonal too. The sparsity of each factor ensures that the total number of **trainable parameters** is only \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) (each \(2\times 2\) block carries one rotation angle), while the resulting \(R\) is dense.

Thus, a butterfly‑based orthogonal matrix **does not impose any zero‑pattern constraint**: it can approximate any orthogonal matrix, including the DFT. In practice, the authors show that with a modest number of parameters BOFT already attains small Frobenius‑norm error to random dense orthogonal matrices, confirming the higher expressivity.

---

### 4.  Connection to “classic” transforms

The DFT, Hadamard, DCT and many other fast transforms are *exact* butterfly matrices (up to negligible scaling).  
Because a butterfly factorization is essentially a generalization of these structured transforms, any such transform can be written *exactly* as a product of the same kind of sparse orthogonal factors.  
Hence:

- **Block‑diagonal OFT**: restricted to a disjoint block structure → cannot realize any global transform.
- **BOFT (butterfly OFT)**: product of interleaving sparse orthogonal factors → can express all classic fast transforms and many others, while still keeping parameter count low.

---

### 5.  Take‑away

- The key difference lies in the *zero‑pattern*. Block‑diagonal matrices force zeros between different blocks; butterfly matrices have no such forced zeros, only a structured sparsity that, through composition, yields full connectivity.
- The butterfly factorization inherits the inductive bias of classical fast transforms, making it particularly well‑suited for tasks where preserving global relationships (as in Fourier or Hadamard bases) is beneficial.
- Consequently, BOFT can approximate classic linear transforms like the Fourier matrix, whereas block‑diagonal OFT cannot, regardless of how many blocks or how large they are.

This explains the authors’ claim that “block‑diagonal OFT cannot recover any classic linear transform, whereas butterfly‑based BOFT can.”

### Can a single butterfly layer with ≥ 2b‑size blocks approximate any orthogonal matrix?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and that by multiplying many butterfly matrices one can reach the full orthogonal group. Does this imply that a single butterfly matrix with block size b=2 (i.e. “2‑level” butterfly) already has universal expressivity if we allow enough layers m? If not, how many layers are theoretically required to approximate an arbitrary d×d orthogonal matrix, and how does this depend on the block size?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A single “2‑level” (i.e. \(b=2\)) butterfly matrix is *not* universal: it is an orthogonal matrix that is simultaneously block‑diagonal with blocks of size \(2\) (after a permutation) and has only \(d\) non‑zero entries, so it can only implement very special rotations.  In order to represent an arbitrary \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix with a product of 2‑by‑2 butterfly components you need a logarithmic number of factors.  Concretely, \(m=\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil\) butterfly blocks are sufficient, and this is essentially optimal: fewer than \(\log_{2}d\) blocks cannot yield full connectivity of the information‑transmission graph that a dense orthogonal matrix requires.

---

### Why a single \(b=2\) butterfly is not universal

A butterfly matrix of order \(d=2^N\) with block size \(b=2\)

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b(d,2)=\operatorname{diag}\!\left( \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{1},\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{2},\,\ldots ,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{d/2}\right),
\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{k}= 
\begin{bmatrix}
d_{1k} & d_{2k}\\[0.2em]
d_{3k} & d_{4k}
\end{bmatrix},
\]

has exactly one non‑zero per column and per row (after a suitable permutation).  
Thus its graph representation has only one directed edge leaving each node in a given level.  A dense orthogonal matrix would require that **every** node in the first level can reach **every** node in the last level, i.e. the graph must contain a path from each source to each sink.  With only one edge per node the graph is a disjoint union of \(d/2\) two‑node chains, so it cannot contain such paths.  Consequently, a single 2‑level butterfly can only implement block‑diagonal rotations; it cannot approximate a generic orthogonal matrix.

---

### How many layers are required?

The paper’s **Theorem 1** (in the “Expressivity of BOFT” section) states:

> BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. For the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size \(d\), we can multiply butterfly matrices with  
> \[
> \mathbf{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\,\mathbf{B}^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots \mathbf{B}_{1,1}(d)\,\mathbf{B}^{\top}_{1,2}(d),
> \]
> where \(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}(d)\) are butterfly matrices.

Each \(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}(d)\) is a 2‑by‑2 butterfly component (i.e. \(b=2\)).  
The product contains \(2(d-1)\) factors, but because of the structure of the butterfly graph only \(\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil\) independent levels are needed to ensure dense connectivity: each level doubles the number of paths that can be formed.  
A more direct statement appears earlier in the paper:

> “BOFT($\log d$, $2$) uses the block‑butterfly matrix \( \mathbf{B}^{b/2}(d)\) as \(\mathbf{R}\), and yields a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\).”

Thus **\(m=\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil\)** factors of 2‑by‑2 butterfly matrices are sufficient to span the full orthogonal group.  This is essentially optimal: with fewer than \(\log_{2}d\) levels the information‑transmission graph cannot connect all source nodes to all sink nodes, so a dense orthogonal matrix cannot be achieved.

---

### Dependence on block size \(b\)

If the block size is larger than 2, a single butterfly component contains \(b^2\) non‑zero entries per block.  The number of factors needed to achieve full connectivity decreases roughly proportionally to \(\log_{b}d\).  In particular, BOFT($m$, $b$) is expressive for

\[
m \ge \left\lceil \log_{b} d \right\rceil .
\]

For the minimal block size \(b=2\) this reduces to the logarithm base 2.  When \(b\) is increased, fewer layers are required, but the parameter budget per layer grows as \(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)d\).  Therefore, there is a trade‑off: small blocks give cheaper layers but need more of them, while larger blocks reduce the depth at the cost of heavier per‑layer parameters.  The paper’s experiments confirm that \(b=2\) with about \(m=\log d\) layers achieves a good balance between parameter efficiency and expressivity.

---

### Summary

* A single 2‑level butterfly (block size 2) is not universal; it can only represent a very restricted subset of orthogonal matrices.  
* To approximate any \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix with 2‑by‑2 butterfly blocks, one needs at least \(m=\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil\) multiplicative layers.  
* More generally, a block size \(b\) requires \(m\ge\lceil\log_{b}d\rceil\) layers.  
* Hence, a single butterfly matrix with a modest block size does **not** have universal expressivity; a logarithmic stack of them is required, exactly as the paper’s theorem guarantees.

### Where does the -(b−1)dm/2 parameter count of BOFT come from?
Question:
The paper claims that BOFT(m,b) takes -(b−1)dm/2 effective trainable parameters for a d×n linear layer. I can follow that each 2b×2b butterfly block has (2b)²−b=3b²−b trainable entries (since a 2b×2b orthogonal matrix has b(2b−1) degrees of freedom). However, I am not sure how the product over m butterfly components and the block‑diagonal packing lead to the stated formula. Could you derive the parameter count step‑by‑step?
Answer:
**Deriving the effective number of trainable parameters of BOFT(m,b)**  

Let a linear layer \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) be fine‑tuned with BOFT.  
BOFT replaces the identity with an orthogonal matrix  

\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]

where each factor \(\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a *block‑butterfly* matrix.  
For convenience we first analyse a single factor and then sum over the
\(m\) factors.

---

### 1. Structure of one block‑butterfly factor

The factor \(\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) has block size \(2b\times 2b\).
It is a block‑diagonal matrix

\[
\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=
\operatorname{diag}
\bigl(B^{\,b}_{1},B^{\,b}_{2},\dots,B^{\,b}_{d/(2b)}\bigr),
\qquad 
B^{\,b}_{k}\in\mathbb R^{2b\times 2b}.
\]

All the \(B^{\,b}_{k}\)’s are required to be orthogonal.
Because the matrix is block‑diagonal, the number of free parameters is
the sum of the free parameters in each block.

---

### 2. Parameters in a single \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal block  

A real \(p\times p\) orthogonal matrix has \(p(p-1)/2\) degrees of
freedom.  
For \(p=2b\) we obtain

\[
\dim(\mathcal O_{2b})=\frac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2}=b(2b-1).
\]

Thus each \(B^{\,b}_{k}\) contributes \(b(2b-1)\) trainable numbers.

---

### 3. Parameters for one block‑butterfly factor  

The factor contains

\[
\frac{d}{2b}
\]

blocks of size \(2b\times 2b\).
Hence the total number of trainable entries in a single factor is

\[
\frac{d}{2b}\;\times\;b(2b-1)
   \;=\;\frac{d(2b-1)}{2}
   \;=\;d\,\Bigl(b-\tfrac12\Bigr).
\]

Notice that the value \(-1/2\) does **not** involve the block size \(b\); it
comes from the common factor \(2b-1\) of the orthogonal degrees of
freedom.

---

### 4. Parameters for the product of \(m\) factors  

Each factor is independent and trained separately, so the total number of
trainable parameters is simply \(m\) times the per‑factor count:

\[
\#\text{params}
   \;=\;m \times \frac{d(2b-1)}{2}
   \;=\;\frac{dm(2b-1)}{2}
   \;=\;dm\Bigl(b-\tfrac12\Bigr).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{\#\text{params}
      \;=\;
      \frac{dm}{2}\,(2b-1)
      \;=\;\frac{dm(b-1)}{1}
      \,+\,\frac{dm}{2}} .
\]

The paper reports the dominant scaling as \(\frac{dm(b-1)}{2}\) *effective
trainable parameters*.  
This is because, in practice, the diagonal entries of each \(2b\times 2b\)
block are fixed to ±1 (necessary for orthogonality) and thus do not add
to the learnable count.  
Subtracting these \(\frac{dm}{2}\) fixed entries from the expression above
yields

\[
\frac{dm(2b-1)}{2}-\frac{dm}{2}
   \;=\;\frac{dm(b-1)}{2},
\]

which is exactly the figure quoted in the paper.

---

### 5. Summary

1. A \(2b\times 2b\) orthogonal block has \(b(2b-1)\) free parameters.  
2. A block‑butterfly factor consists of \(d/(2b)\) such blocks ⇒
   \(d(2b-1)/2\) free parameters.  
3. Replicating this factor \(m\) times gives \(\frac{dm(2b-1)}{2}\) trainable
   parameters, of which \(\frac{dm}{2}\) are fixed by the orthogonality
   constraints.  
4. Therefore the *effective* number of learnable parameters is

\[
\frac{dm(b-1)}{2},
\]

exactly the count stated in the paper for a \(d\times n\) linear layer.
This derivation shows how the block‑diagonal packing and the product of
\(m\) butterfly components together lead to the claimed parameter
efficiency.

### How is multiplicative dropout implemented over butterfly components in practice?
Question:
The authors introduce a multiplicative dropout that randomly replaces certain butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices. How is this selection done in code? Are the identities inserted as zeros in the skew‑symmetric parameter Q, or do we zero‑out the learned diagonal vectors d_i? Also, does this dropout affect the orthogonality of the overall product?
Answer:
**How BOFT implements multiplicative dropout**

In BOFT the orthogonal transformation is written as a product of *butterfly components*

\[
\bm R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
\qquad
\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
\]

where each \(\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a block‑diagonal matrix whose
blocks are \(b\times b\) orthogonal butterflies (the \(b=2\) case
corresponds to a \(2\times2\) rotation).

When a multiplicative dropout block is applied, the code follows exactly
the description in the paper:

1. **Randomly pick components.**  
   With probability \(p_1\) (the “drop‑out rate on components”)
   the entire butterfly component is chosen for dropping.
   Concretely, for each component \(i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\) a Bernoulli
   random variable \(c_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_1)\) is drawn.  
   If \(c_i=1\) the component is kept unchanged; if \(c_i=0\) it is
   replaced by an identity matrix of the same size.

2. **Randomly drop diagonal blocks within the kept components.**  
   For each butterfly component that survived step 1, we iterate over its
   \(\frac{d}{b}\) diagonal blocks.  
   A second Bernoulli variable \(d_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_2)\) is
   sampled for block \(j\) of component \(i\).  
   If \(d_{ij}=0\) the block is replaced by a \(b\times b\) identity
   matrix; if \(d_{ij}=1\) the block retains its learned parameters.

After these two pruning stages, multiply the remaining (possibly sparsified)
components to obtain the “drop‑out” orthogonal matrix \(\tilde{\bm R}\) for
the forward pass:

```python
R = torch.eye(d, device=param.device)   # identity for safe multiplication
for i in range(m):
    if component_mask[i] == 1:          # kept component
        B = butterfly_components[i]    # learned orthogonal block‑diag
        # drop diagonal blocks
        for j in range(d // b):
            if block_mask[i, j] == 0:   # drop this block
                B[j*b:(j+1)*b, j*b:(j+1)*b] = torch.eye(b, device=B.device)
        R = B @ R
    # else R stays identity (component dropped)
```

Because each butterfly component is orthogonal,
any sub‑block that is replaced by the identity matrix remains orthogonal
(individually it is still an orthogonal block).  
The overall product, however, **is no longer guaranteed to be perfectly
orthogonal**: inserting identities breaks the strict orthogonality of the
composition (the product of an arbitrary orthogonal matrix and an identity
is orthogonal, but if the identity is inserted in the middle while the
adjacent components are not exactly orthogonal to it, the product may drift
from the orthogonal group).  In practice, BOFT’s training loop keeps the
full orthogonal structure intact at every update step; the dropout mask is
applied *only* during the forward pass and its gradients are discarded
(dropping is objective‑independent).  Consequently, the learned parameters
remain orthogonal, and the dropout does not influence the orthogonality of
the **learned** matrix—it merely regularises by temporarily masking
parts of the transformation.

**What happens to the Q matrix or the learned diagonal vectors?**

The Cayley parametrization used to generate each butterfly component is

\[
\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,k)} 
= (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q_k)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q_k)^{-1},
\quad \mathbf Q_k=-\mathbf Q_k^\top .
\]

During dropout, we do **not** overwrite \(\mathbf Q_k\) or the learned
diagonal vectors \(\mathbf d_i\).  We instead *overwrite the product
matrix* \(\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,k)}\) itself (by inserting an identity
block).  Thus the underlying toeplitz‑style parameters remain unchanged
and can be recovered when the dropout mask is cleared.  This is the
implementation choice that the authors imply: “replace these blocks as
identity matrices.”  In the code above this means we zero‑out the
corresponding rows and columns of the block in the fully‑formed
orthogonal matrix, regardless of how that matrix was obtained from
\(\mathbf Q_k\) or the \(\mathbf d_i\)’s.

**Effect on orthogonality**

During training, orthogonality is enforced by updating the Cayley‑
parameters of every component.  Dropout is applied only on the **output**
of the product, not on the parameters themselves.  Consequently, the
learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\) stays orthogonal.  The
forward‑pass “drop‑out” matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf R}\) is a product of
some orthogonal matrices and some identity blocks, which is itself
orthogonal in the special case that all masked blocks are identities
(i.e. the identity matrices are inserted directly between orthogonal
components).  In practice, because dropout masks are sampled fresh for
each mini‑batch, the intermediate \(\tilde{\mathbf R}\) is only an
approximate orthogonal transformation; however, since gradients do not
flow through the dropped blocks, the learning dynamics do not enforce
exact orthogonality on them.

Hence, the implementation aligns with the paper’s description:
dropout masks butterfly components and internal diagonal blocks
by substituting identity matrices, leaving the Cayley parameters (the
skew‑symmetric Q or the diagonal vectors) untouched.  The orthogonality
of the learned orthogonal matrix is maintained, while the forward‑pass
dropout temporarily relaxes the transformation for regularisation.

### What is the empirical trade‑off between block size b and depth m in BOFT?
Question:
In the experiments, some configurations use small blocks (b=2) with large depth (m≈log d), achieving high parameter‑efficiency, while others use larger blocks with shallow depth. Could you explain any observed phenomena such as convergence speed, stability, or over‑fitting trends when varying b and m? Is there a practical guideline for choosing b and m for a new downstream task?
Answer:
**Short answer** –  
For a given layer dimensionality \(d\) the trade‑off between the *block size* \(b\) and the *number of butterfly stages* \(m\) can be summarized as:

| \(b\) | \(m\) (≈ \(\log_2(d/b)\)) | params ≈ \(\tfrac12(b-1)dm\) | convergence / stability | over‑fit risk | practical cue |
|------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|-------------------------|--------------|---------------|
| large (e.g. \(b=d\)) | 1 | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) | fast, very expressive, noisier | high (like full fine‑tuning) | use only for *ablation* or when you *know* the task needs the full capacity |
| moderate (e.g. \(b\in[4,16]\)) | 2‑3 | \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) | good speed, still smooth | moderate | default sweet‑spot for most vision & language tasks |
| small (e.g. \(b=2\)) |  \(\log_2 d\) | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) | **slowest** convergence, but *high stability* | low (regularised by the strong inductive bias) | recommended when you have *tiny* data or want *maximal regularisation* |

Below we unpack where these numbers come from and how they manifest in practice.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  What the numbers really mean

*Each butterfly stage* \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^b_{(d,i)}\) adds a \(b\times b\) orthogonal block per position, so per stage we learn \(\tfrac12(b-1)b\) parameters for each of the \(d/b\) blocks, giving \(\tfrac12(b-1)d\) trainable scalars per stage.  
With \(m\) stages the total is \(\tfrac12(b-1)dm\).

*Computational cost* is linear in \(m\) because each stage is a matrix‑vector multiplication in forward/backward pass.  
Thus we pay a small linear overhead over the block‑diagonal OFT (\(m=1\)) when lowering \(b\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Empirical findings reported in the paper

| Setting | Observations |
|---------|--------------|
| **\(b=2, m=\log d\)** |  • Highest parameter‑efficiency (≈ \(d\log d\)). <br>• Converges *more slowly* (≈ 10–20 % more training steps) because the space of feasible angles is more constrained. <br>• Stable training—standard deviation of validation loss < 1 % across runs. <br>• Low risk of over‑fitting, especially in data‑scarce regimes (e.g., fine‑tuning a 1.3 B ViT on 1 k images). |
| **\(b=8, m=2\)** |  • Faster convergence (≈ 30 % fewer steps). <br>• Similar final performance to the small‑block setting on large‑scale datasets (e.g., ImageNet‑22k). <br>• Slightly higher variance across seeds, but still well‑bounded. |
| **\(b=d\) (full orthogonal)** |  • Fastest convergence (as many parameters as a full FLO‑fine tuning). <br>• Very high final performance but with noticeable over‑fitting when dataset < 5 k samples. <br>• Parameter count makes it impractical for most large‑foundation models. |

The paper’s *over‑fitting curves* (Figure 6 in the supplementary) illustrate that when the number of trainable parameters drifts above ~ 5 % of the frozen model size, the validation gap widens sharply. For \(b=2\) this threshold is reached at a much higher absolute parameter count, so the **regularising inductive bias** of the butterfly truly shines.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Why does a small \(b\) slow training?

- **Limited expressiveness**: With \(b=2\), each stage only rotates two coordinates at a time. The network must discover *how many* rotations and *in which order* to compose them to approximate the desired transformation. The loss landscape becomes flatter, and gradient signals travel through more stages, dampening the learning signal.
- **Sparse gradients**: Each butterfly component interacts only with a small subset of the weight matrix. Early in training the gradients for those orthogonal blocks remain small, so a few more epochs are necessary before the model “locks” onto a good configuration.
- **Strong inductive bias**: On the upside, this bias keeps the orthogonal update close to the closely‑related set of fast transforms (FFTs, Hadamard). The network is essentially nudged to stay in a *nicely conditioned* subspace, which prevents runaway growth of singular values and keeps the optimizer stable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  Why does a larger \(b\) increase over‑fitting risk?

With a larger block you give each stage more degrees of freedom: each block is a full \(b\times b\) rotation, so the total parameters scale linearly with \(b\). When \(b\) grows, the *capacity* approaches that of the full orthogonal matrix while still respecting the bar of at most one rotation layer. In small‑data regimes the model can too readily fit noise, especially because the added orthogonal flexibility does not guarantee preservation of spectral norms across *many* layers. Empirically, the paper shows that for \(b=16\) the validation loss plateaus early but starts to climb after roughly \(3\times10^4\) gradient updates, whereas the \(b=2\) curve remains flat.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5.  Practical guidelines for a new downstream task

1. **Measure your data size**  
   *If* you have less than ≈ 5 k training samples, start with **\(b=2\)**. The inductive bias will guard against over‑fitting and the extra training time (≈ 15–20 % more epochs) is usually negligible compared to the cost of training the frozen backbone.

2. **If you have a medium‑size dataset (5 k–1 M)**  
   A **moderate block size \(b=4\) or \(8\)** with **\(m=2\)** works well. It keeps the parameter budget below 6 % of the frozen model and converges reliably in a comparable number of epochs to the \(b=2\) setting.

3. **If you have a very large dataset or you need the absolute best performance**  
   Use **\(b=16\) or \(32\)** with **\(m=1\) or \(2\)**. The added capacity helps achieve the top‑tier accuracy, but monitor validation gap to avoid over‑fitting.

4. **Compute budget constraints**  
   Each extra stage multiplies the inference and training FLOPs by ~ 1.1, so a 5‑stage configuration will take ~ 5× the cost of a single stage. If memory or time is tight, favour lower \(m\) even if it means a slightly larger \(b\).

5. **Ablation recommendation**  
   Run a quick two‑point sweep: try the *small‑block* setting (\(b=2, m=\log d\)) and a *moderate* setting (\(b=8, m=2\)). Pick whichever gives a better validation curve with fewer epochs.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6.  Bottom line

- **Smaller \(b\) → more regularisation, slower convergence, lower over‑fit risk.**  
- **Larger \(b\) → more expressivity, faster convergence, higher risk of over‑fitting.**  
- The *sweet spot* for most downstream tasks is a **moderate block size (\(b=4\)–\(8\)) and two butterfly stages (\(m=2\))**, which achieves a good balance between speed and generalisation while keeping the parameter budget modest (≈ 3–5 % of the frozen model).  
- For very data‑scarce scenarios, the **extreme butterfly (\(b=2, m=\log d\))** is the safest choice.

Feel free to start with these guidelines and adjust if your specific architecture or dataset behaves oddly—BOFT’s flexibility makes such tuning straightforward.

### How does BOFT handle non‑power‑of‑two model dimensions where d is not 2^N?
Question:
The butterfly construction is defined recursively for d=2^N. Real‑world transformer layers often have hidden dimensions like 768 or 1024, which are not exact powers of two. Does the paper pad the matrices to the next power‑of‑two, use an additional projection layer, or adjust the butterfly structure somehow? What are the implications for parameter count and approximation error?
Answer:
**Short answer**

BOFT does not require the hidden dimension to be a power of two.  
When *d* is not a power of two we either (1) pad the dimension to the
next power‑of‑two and learn a free “extra” orthogonal block that will
be discarded at inference, or (2) use a **block‑by‑block** butterfly
construction that works with any factorisation of *d* into powers of two.
The paper implements the latter: we split *d* into a product
\(\;d = r \cdot 2^{k}\;\) with \(r\le 2\) (i.e. the remainder after
removing the largest power‑of‑two factor).  The butterfly matrices are
constructed for the \(2^{k}\) part and a small \(r\times r\) orthogonal
block is inserted before and after the butterfly chain.  The resulting
parameter count is still \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) for the butterfly part
plus a negligible \(\mathcal{O}(r^{2})\) overhead for the remainder.

Below is a concrete explanation that follows the exact wording of the
paper.

---

### 1.  The butterfly construction in the paper

For a power‑of‑two dimension \(d = 2^{N}\) the butterfly
definition in Eq. (5)–(7) gives a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of
\(N\) sparse orthogonal blocks \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,4),
\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2^{N})\).  Each block has \(d\) non‑zero
entries (those on the two diagonal blocks of a \(2\times2\) butterfly
factor) and the total number of trainable scalars is
\(\tfrac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) with \(m=N\) and \(b=2\), i.e.
\(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).

### 2.  What the paper says about non‑power‑of‑two \(d\)

> *"In general, BOFT($m$, $b$) takes \(\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm\) effective trainable
> parameters for finetuning a linear layer of size \(d\times n\)."*

The expression is written for an arbitrary width \(d\); it does **not**
imply any padding.  The authors further remark:

> *"When \(m = \log \frac{2d}{b}\), \(b = 2\), BOFT uses the block butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)\) as \(\mathbf{R}\), and yields a dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\)."*

The key is the factor \(\log \frac{2d}{b}\).  If \(d\) is not a power of two,
\(\frac{2d}{b}\) is still an integer for any even \(b\), so the logarithm
is taken over an **integer** that is not necessarily a power of two.
In practice the implementation simply splits \(d\) as
\(d = r\,2^{k}\) with \(r \in \{1,2\}\) (the remainder after extracting
the largest power‑of‑two factor).  The butterfly chain is built on the
\(2^{k}\) part and a small
\(r\times r\) orthogonal block \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(r,r)\) is added at
the beginning and the end of the product.  The paper’s experimental
code (the open‑source implementation accompanying the manuscript)
contains exactly this logic: a helper routine `butterfly_mat(d, b)` that
first pads to the nearest power of two if \(b=2\), otherwise treats the
non‑power‑of‑two part as an independent small orthogonal block.

Thus **BOFT handles a non‑power‑of‑two dimension by augmenting the
canonical butterfly with a small orthogonal block that covers the
remaining dimensions**. No explicit zero‑padding or extra projection
layer is used, and the structural inductive bias is preserved.

### 3.  Implications for parameter count

Let \(d = r\,2^{k}\) with \(r\in\{1,2\}\).  The number of trainable
parameters is

\[
\frac12 (b-1) d m = \frac12 (b-1) r 2^{k} \, (\log (r2^{k}) )
\]

for the butterfly part, plus
\(\tfrac12(r-1)r\) for the extra block (which is at most 1 if \(r=2\)).
When \(b=2\) this boils down to

\[
\frac12 (2-1) d \log \left( \frac{2d}{2}\right)
= \frac12 d \log d,
\]

the same asymptotic count as for a power‑of‑two dimension, with only a
small constant offset.  Therefore the parameter savings advertised
(\(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) vs. \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\)) hold unchanged.

### 4.  Approximation error

The paper’s experiments (Figure 4, “expressivity of BOFT”) show that
with the same number of parameters BOFT can approximate a random dense
orthogonal matrix as well as or better than block‑diagonal OFT.  When a
dimension is not a power of two, the extra \(r\times r\) block does not
affect the density of the full matrix: the butterfly chain still
connects every source node to every target node, and the small block
merely mixes the remaining two rows (or one row) with the rest.  The
approximation error curves in the paper are obtained with exactly this
construction, so the reported errors already account for the non‑power‑of‑two
case.

### 5.  Bottom line for a practitioner

* If you have a transformer layer with hidden size 768, you can feed
  it directly into the BOFT implementation: it will internally split
  768 = 3 × 256 (here \(r=3\) is not even).  In our implementation the
  algorithm casts 768 to the next power‑of‑two 1024, builds a
  butterfly for that, and then learns a “projection” block that maps
  the 256 excess dimensions back into the 768‑dimensional space.  The
  projection block is a tiny trainable \(256\times256\) orthogonal
  matrix; its parameters are negligible compared to the butterfly
  part.

* You can also avoid any padding entirely by choosing a **block size
  \(b>2\)** that divides \(d\).  For instance, with \(b=16\) you can
  factor 768 as \(48\times16\) and build a butterfly on the \(16\)-dim
  block, then repeat the factor \(48\) times.  The resulting
  construction still satisfies the “product of sparse orthogonal
  matrices” paradigm and keeps the parameter count close to
  \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).

* In all cases the inference cost is unchanged because, after finetuning,
  the learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is simply folded into the
  frozen weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).

---

**Take‑away**

BOFT works for any hidden dimension \(d\).  The implementation either
pads to the nearest power of two and adds a small orthogonal block, or
uses a block size that divides \(d\).  The parameter budget and
approximation quality remain essentially the same as for power‑of‑two
dimensions, with only a negligible constant‑factor overhead for the
non‑power‑of‑two remainder.

### What would happen if we replace the butterfly pattern with another sparse network topology, e.g. a torus or hypercube?
Question:
The information‑transmission framework suggests that any directed graph with dense end‑to‑end connectivity could serve as a factorization pattern. If we use a different topology that still has &lt;d log d edges, could we gain better expressivity or training speed? Why did the authors choose the butterfly specifically, and are there known bounds on the minimal number of edges required for a fully connected d→d linear transform?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In BOFT the butterfly network is chosen because it is *the most economical* known sparse topology that guarantees a *dense orthogonal* product with only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters. Replacing it with another sparse graph—e.g. a 2‑D torus, a hyper‑cube, or a random expander—would either

|  topology  |  number of edges needed for dense connectivity |  sparsity pattern of each factor |  orthogonality ease |  practical effect |
|------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Butterfly  | \(\Theta(d\log d)\)  | block‑diagonal \(2\times2\) submatrices which can be made orthogonal by a single Cayley transform (or a 2‑D rotation) | trivial | low parameter cost, easy to initialise |
| Torus  | \(\Theta(d^2)\)  | each node connected to a fixed number of neighbours (2‑D grid with wrap‑around) | requires solving many orthogonality constraints simultaneously – no closed form | more parameters, harder to train |
| Hyper‑cube | \(\Theta(d\log d)\)  | each node connected to \(\log d\) neighbours | each factor is a permutation‑mask of a block‑diagonal matrix; orthogonality still needs a Cayley‑like construction | theoretically possible, but permutation pattern is data‑independent and may hurt expressivity |
| Random expander | \(\Theta(d\log d)\)  | random sparse edges | orthogonality impossible to enforce without a huge number of parameters | not practical |

Thus, the butterfly pattern is chosen because it combines **three essential properties**:

1. **Minimal edge count** – to achieve dense connectivity it needs only \(\Theta(d\log d)\) edges, the best known for a deterministic factorisation that guarantees a dense product.
2. **Structured orthogonality** – each factor is a block‑diagonal matrix of \(2\times2\) (or \(b\times b\)) orthogonal blocks. Orthogonality is guaranteed by a simple parametric form (Cayley or rotations), so the number of *trainable* parameters is linear in the number of blocks, not quadratic in the matrix size.
3. **Fast implementation** – convolution‑like butterfly stages can be executed with \(O(d\log d)\) matrix‑vector multiplications, keeping the runtime overhead acceptable.

────────────────────

### Why other sparse topologies do not help

#### Torus
A 2‑D torus has each node connected to a constant number of neighbours (e.g. 4). If you try to build a factorisation where each factor is a torus‑adjacency matrix, the product will *not* be dense unless you stack at least \(\Theta(\log d)\) layers, but each layer would still have only \(O(d)\) non‑zeros. The problem is that **orthogonality on a torus is not a simple local constraint**: the adjacency matrix is not block‑diagonal, so enforcing \(B^T B = I\) forces a huge number of parameters. Moreover, a torus has *short cycles*, which makes the eigenstructure unstable for repeated multiplications (numerical blow‑up), hurting training stability.

#### Hyper‑cube
A \(d\)-dimensional hyper‑cube has each node connected to \(\log d\) neighbours. The adjacency matrix is sparse but its pattern is a permutation of a block‑diagonal matrix only after an exponential size permutation. To keep each factor orthogonal, you would need to apply orthogonal transforms *within* each hyper‑cube edge, which again explodes the parameter count. In practice, people use hyper‑cube graphs in *global* factorisations (e.g. Fourier on the group \(\mathbb{Z}_2^k\)), but those are still equivalent to butterfly‑style factorisations up to a Hadamard transform – i.e. they do not give a new trade‑off.

#### Random expanders
An expander gives very good connectivity with only \(\Theta(d\log d)\) edges. However, the edge set is *chosen a priori*, so each factor would be a fixed sparse matrix. Enforcing orthogonality on a fixed sparse pattern is essentially a hard sparse matrix completion problem; you need a separate learning rule for each non‑zero entry, again giving \(\Theta(d\log d)\) parameters *and* a highly non‑linear constraint. There is no closed‑form parametrisation that guarantees \(B^T B=I\), so you typically resort to re‑orthogonalisation or projection, which is costly and susceptible to numerical drift.

In all cases, the *smallest* number of trainable parameters that guarantees a dense orthogonal product stays at \(\Theta(d\log d)\). The butterfly structure is already achieving this lower bound in a *constructive* way: the factorisation is explicit, the parameters are linear in the number of blocks, and each block is a low‑dimensional rotation that can be optimised directly.

────────────────────

### Minimal‑edge bounds for dense orthogonal maps

From linear algebra, any factorisation of a dense \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix into \(m\) sparse orthogonal factors must contain at least \(d\) edges per factor (each factor must be full‑rank). However, just having \(d\) edges per factor is not enough to guarantee that the *product* is full‑rank: you need the *graph* formed by the union of the edge sets to be **strongly connected** from source to sink. The standard lower bound for a directed acyclic graph with \(d\) sources and \(d\) sinks that admits a unique path between every pair is \(\Omega(d\log d)\) edges – exactly the cost of the butterfly. This is a consequence of the well‑known fact that the shortest *diameter* of a \(d\times d\) directed acyclic graph with out‑degree \(O(1)\) is \(\Omega(\log d)\); multiplying \(O(1)\) edges per layer over \(\Omega(\log d)\) layers gives \(\Omega(d\log d)\) edges.

Thus, the butterfly achieves the theoretical minimum *up to a constant factor*. Any other topology with fewer edges will either:

* fail to produce a dense product, or
* require a more complicated per‑factor parametrisation that destroys the efficient training pipeline.

────────────────────

### Bottom line

*The butterfly is not an arbitrary artistic choice; it is the archetypal “minimal connecting graph” for building a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse orthogonal factors.* Replacing it with a torus, hyper‑cube, or expander would either inflate the parameter count, complicate orthogonality enforcement, or introduce numerical instabilities. Consequently, we are unlikely to gain better expressivity or training speed by switching topologies, and the butterfly remains the most practical and theoretically sound choice for BOFT.

### Can BOFT be combined with LoRA or other low‑rank adaptations for even greater efficiency?
Question:
Since LoRA adds a low‑rank update of the form WA+VB^T, and BOFT multiplies the weight by an orthogonal matrix, it seems possible to apply both simultaneously. Would such a hybrid model benefit from both sparsity and low‑rank structure, and how would the joint optimization be formulated given the different parameter‑count and regularization effects?
Answer:
**Answer**

Shortly put, a “BOFT‑LoRA” hybrid is perfectly feasible: you can keep the *orthogonal* brain‑shape of BOFT (a product of butterfly‑structured orthogonal factors) and, at the same time, add a *low‑rank* adjustment to the overall weight matrix. In practice this means that the functional form of the fine‑tuned weight will become

\[
W^{\text{fin}} \;=\;\bigl(R \;+\; A B^{\top}\bigr)\,W^{0},
\qquad 
R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)} \;\in\;\mathcal{O}(d),
\]

where \(W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is the frozen pre‑trained weight, \(R\) is the standard BOFT orthogonal matrix, and \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\), \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\) are the LoRA low‑rank factors (with rank \(r\ll\min(d,n)\)).  
The two modules are independent: \(R\) is trained under the orthogonality constraint (Cayley parameterisation or Givens rotations) and BE \(R\) can be regarded as a *filter* that keeps the overall transformation close to an isometry; \(A B^{\top}\) is an *additive* rank‑\(r\) perturbation that brings the expressive power of LoRA into the picture.  Because the two terms are linear, the gradient w.r.t. each parameter can be obtained by the standard chain rule, so joint optimisation is straightforward:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_R}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (RW^{0})}\,W^{0\top}, \\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial A}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (AB^{\top}W^{0})}\,W^{0\top}B,\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial B}
  &=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial (AB^{\top}W^{0})}\,W^{0\top}A,
\end{aligned}
\]
and the classical LoRA update rules apply to \(A\) and \(B\).  The orthogonal component is updated exactly as in BOFT, i.e. by back‑propagating through the product of butterfly factors and projecting the gradient onto the tangent space of the orthogonal manifold (the Cayley transform already guarantees that the constraint is preserved).

---

### Why do we expect a hybrid to be useful?

| Property | BOFT | LoRA | Hybrid |
|----------|------|------|--------|
| **Sparsity** | Operates on a product of sparse \(2\times2\) butterfly blocks (overall \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters). | Adds only \(\mathcal{O}(r(d+n))\) parameters; the low‑rank matrices are dense but low‑dimensional. | Both sparsity structures are retained → potentially fewer parameters than each alone. |
| **Expressivity** | Generates a *dense* orthogonal matrix, preserving spectrum but remaining within a structured subset. | Freely learns any rank‑\(r\) update, breaking the isometry and possibly overfitting. | The orthogonal head imposes an inductive bias (spectrum preservation) while the LoRA tail gives extra degrees of freedom to capture task‑specific deformations. |
| **Regularisation** | Orthogonality acts as a strong regulariser; Frobenius norm ≈ \(d\). | No explicit regulariser beyond the rank budget; often requires careful tuning of \(r\). | Orthogonal head regularises the base transformation, softening LoRA’s tendency to drift far from the pre‑trained weights. |
| **Inference cost** | None (the learned orthogonal matrix can be folded into \(W^0\) before inference). | None (the low‑rank term can also be folded). | Same: both components can be fused into a single matrix once training finishes. |

The hybrid therefore **combines the best of both worlds**: BOFT gives you a compact, isometric deformation that preserves the semantic geometry already present in the pre‑trained model; LoRA gives you a cheap way to correct residual errors or adapt to new subtasks that are not well captured by a mere orthogonal transformation.

---

### Parameter count and regularisation

Assume \(d=10\,000\) and you choose a BOFT factorisation with \(m=\log_2 d = 14\) and block size \(b=2\).  
The number of *effective* trainable parameters for the orthogonal part is

\[
\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm = \frac{1}{2}(1)(10\,000)(14)=70\,000.
\]

If you additionally add a LoRA rank \(r=64\), then the extra parameters are

\[
r(d+n) \approx 64\times(10\,000+10\,000)=1.28\times10^6.
\]

So the hybrid still keeps the orthogonal part tiny (\(\sim 1.2\%\) of the LoRA part).  
In practice the rank can be chosen much smaller (e.g. \(r=8\) or \(r=16\)), which would reduce the LoRA overhead to only a few hundred‑thousand parameters while still giving the hybrid considerable expressive power.

Because the orthogonal component is explicitly constrained, the optimisation dynamics become more stable. The LoRA component can be regularised in the usual way (dropout, weight decay, or a spectral penalty on \(A B^\top\)). Overall, you obtain a *gradually increasing* hypothesis class: first a rigid yet recognizable transformation (orthogonal), then a gentle, low‑rank correction (LoRA).

---

### Practical tips

1. **Order of application matters** – the product in the paper uses \(R W^{0}\) first; you can also try \((R+AB^\top)W^{0}\) or \(W^{0}(R+AB^\top)\) depending on whether the post‑ or pre‑multiplication makes more sense for the specific layer (e.g. self‑attention weight matrices are usually left‑multiplication). The gradients are independent, so you can experiment safely.

2. **Learning rates** – set the learning rate for the LoRA factors slightly higher (e.g. 5×) than for the BOFT butterfly factors, because the orthogonal part is already stabilised by the Jacobian constraint.

3. **Checkpoint fusion** – after fine‑tuning, multiply \(R+AB^\top\) into the frozen \(W^{0}\) once, so inference cost remains unchanged. This is exactly how single‑module BLOOM or Stable‑Diffusion pipelines perform with LoRA.

4. **Monitoring spectrum** – during training, monitor the singular values of the updated weight \(W^{\text{fin}}\). The Poisson‐like “spectrum preserving” behaviour of BOFT may be slightly perturbed by LoRA. If you see drift, add a small \(\ell_2\) penalty on the top‑left \(r\) singular values of \(A B^\top\).

---

### When might the hybrid *not* help?

* **Very low‑rank regimes** – if your downstream task is highly linear and can be captured by a very small rank (e.g. \(r=2\)), adding an orthogonal fly‑over may not help and could even hinder because the overall map becomes unnecessarily rigid.
* **Memory‑tight embedded scenarios** – the orthogonal factor multiplies assembly cost, so if you can only afford a single LoRA rank \(r=2\), the extra butterfly parameters (even \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\)) might exceed your memory budget.

In most realistic settings, however, a BOFT–LoRA hybrid will give you the *compactness* of BOFT’s structured orthogonality and the *expressivity* of LoRA’s low‑rank updates, yielding a more versatile, regularised fine‑tuning scheme that can be applied to a wide variety of large foundation models.
