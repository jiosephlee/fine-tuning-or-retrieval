# Why Direction Matters: The Power of Neuron Angles in Text‑to‑Image Diffusion  

---

## The Big Question

Modern text‑to‑image diffusion models, such as Stable Diffuse or Imagen, can turn a sentence into a super‑realistic picture.  That is already impressive, but when you ask the system to paint *your* face in a different pose or you want an image that follows a hand‑drawn sketch, the model starts to falter.  The real challenge is: **how do we nudge a huge, pre‑trained network to perform these downstream tasks without destroying the knowledge it already has?**

A new line of research answers this by looking at *angles*—the directions of the neurons, not their magnitudes.  In this post we unpack the intuition behind **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and explain why rotating neurons with an orthogonal matrix is the sweet spot for fine‑tuning diffusion models.

---

## 1. Neurons as Vectors on a Unit Hypersphere  

### 1.1 What “direction” means for a weight

Every weight in a neural network can be viewed as a vector in some high‑dimensional space.  If we care only about *direction*, we can normalize each vector to unit length:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|}.
\]

Doing so projects all neurons onto a unit hypersphere.  On that sphere, the relative angle between any two neurons—captured by cosine similarity—remains unchanged no matter how large or small their individual magnitudes are.

### 1.2 Angles encode semantics

Empirical studies on face recognition and other vision tasks have shown that cosine similarity between features is a better measure of semantic similarity than Euclidean distance.  In practice:

- **Magnitude cues** often collapse to “how activation‑heavy” a neuron is, which varies with data distribution or training dynamics.
- **Direction cues** capture *what* pattern the neuron responds to—its learned “semantic fingerprint.”

A toy experiment with a simple autoencoder confirms this: using only the angle between weight vectors and inputs lets you recover almost exactly the original image, whereas stripping away the magnitudes erases useful information.

---

## 2. Why Rotations Are Preferable to Arbitrary Updates  

### 2.1 The oracle view: preserving pairwise angles

If we rotate all neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) (think of rotating every vector in space by turning your desk), then **every pairwise cosine similarity stays exactly the same**:

\[
\cos(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j) = 
\cos(\widehat{\mathbf{R}\!\mathbf{w}}_i,\widehat{\mathbf{R}\!\mathbf{w}}_j).
\]

Because all semantic relationships remain intact, the **hyperspherical energy**—the sum of inverse distances between all pairs on the sphere—does not change at all.  In other words, we keep exactly the same internal “semantic geometry” of the layer while nudging it towards our new task.

### 2.2 Avoiding catastrophic forgetting

If we have unconstrained gradient updates (the usual way to fine‑tune), neurons may drift far from their original directions. The model then forgets what it previously learned; its outputs become unpredictable and often degrade spectacularly on clean prompts.

```text
Unconstrained update → Neurons wander → Semantic relations broken → Catastrophic forgetting
```

OFT sidesteps this problem: because an orthogonal transformation keeps angles frozen, we preserve what was already learnt while still giving us freedom to reorient the whole layer toward new instructions.

---

## 3. Orthogonal Transformations as Jacobians of Diffusion Steps  

Diffusion denoising networks repeatedly transform noisy latent codes step by step. Think of each transformation as a small vector rotation plus scaling:

1. **Phase spectrum analogy**  
   In signal processing an image’s phase spectrum (the angular relationship between input and Fourier basis) preserves most perceptual content; magnitude supplies contrast and texture noise.  
   Likewise, changing directional weights is akin to manipulating phase – it directly redirects semantic focus.

2. **Full‑rank nature**  
   While Low‑Rank adapters (LoRA) inject learned low‑rank tweaks \( \Delta = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^\top \), OFT applies a full‑rank orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).   
   Informally:
   - LoRA is like adding *small* direction changes that avoid huge jumps.
   - OFT is like rotating *everything* by some learnable angle—a global re‑orientation that retains all structure.

---

## 4. How Orthogonal Finetuning Works in Practice  

### 4.1 The key equation

Per layer \(\mathbf{W}^0\) (pre‑trained weights):

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{z}
= (\mathbf{R}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \!\! \mathbf{x},
\quad
\text{s.t. } 
\mathbf{R}^\top \! \mathbf{R}
= \! \mathbf{I}
}
\]

Here:
- \( \mathbf{x} \) – input vector,
- \( \mathbf{z} \) – output after transformation,
- \( \mathbf{R}\in O(d) \) – learnable orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons in that layer.

Semantically: “the output equals what we’d get if every neuron were rotated by \( \mathbf R\).”

### 4.2 Parameter efficiency

Naively storing a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix would be expensive (\(\frac12 d(d-1)\) parameters).  
OFT solves this via:

- **Cayley parameterization**:  
  Represent \( \mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\), where \( \mathbf Q = -\mathbf Q^\top\).
- **Block‑diagonal restriction**:  
  Divide \(d\) into smaller blocks (\( r < d\)) and learn each block independently:
  
  \[
  \boxed{
  \bm R = 
    {\texttt diag}(\, R_1,\dots,R_r\,)
    }
    , 
    R_k \in O(d/r).
  }
  
This reduces trainable parameters from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\), often matching or beating LoRA’s parameter budget while providing richer updates than LoRA’s low-rank tweak.

### 4.3 Optional magnitude scaling (Re‑scaled OFT)

After learning directions, one can reintroduce per-neuron scalars \( s_i >0 \):

```text
Forward pass: z = ( R · W0 · D )ᵀ x ,   D = diag(s₁,…,sₙ)
```

Because scaling doesn’t affect hyperspherical energy, it adds flexibility without breaking angular preservation.

---

## 5. The Constrained Variant: Control Over Flexibility  

Sometimes our rotated model still drifts too much from its original behaviour – especially when training on tiny datasets or when tasks demand high fidelity back to original prompts.  
The constrained version, **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** imposes:

```
‖ R − I ‖ ≤ ε
```

which bounds how far neurons can rotate away from their initial orientations.
The constraint gets applied directly to the Cayley surrogate matrix \( Q \), making training stable without blowing up computational costs.

---

## 6. Empirical Hits & Lessons Learned  

| Task | Method | Observation |
|------|--------|-------------|
| Subject-driven generation | DreamBooth / LoRA vs OFT/COFT | OFT reaches high subject fidelity with only ~200 iterations; LoRA sometimes collapses or loses identity after few epochs |
| Controllable generation | ControlNet / T2I-Adapter vs OFT | OFT converges ≥ 4× faster on landmark control; produces sharper visuals with fewer finetuned parameters |
| Parameter count | OFT ≈ 10 × less parameters than ControlNet; comparable to LoRA | Fewer memory & compute footprint while retaining semantic stability |

The key takeaway: *rotating all neurons together preserves what matters inside every layer while giving enough freedom for downstream tasks.*

---

## TL;DR – Why Directions Matter

1. **Angles encode semantics:** Cosine similarity between neuron directions mirrors feature similarity.
2. **Rotations preserve structure:** An orthogonal matrix keeps all pairwise angles intact; no pretraining knowledge is lost.
3. **Orthogonal finetuning (`OFT`) gives systematic control:** Train just one or few sparse matrices per layer—and you’ll steer huge diffusion models efficiently.
4. **Constrain when needed (`COFT`):** Add an identity-norm bound if you need tighter adherence to pretrained behavior.
5. **No inference cost:** Once trained, just multiply your weight matrix by \( R \); you’re back at inference speed of the base model.

Orthogonal Finetuning turns out to be a surprisingly simple yet powerful idea: instead of tweaking what each neuron *knows*, we rotate *how* each neuron perceives its inputs—all while keeping the whole sea of knowledge intact.

# Orthogonal Finetuning 101: From Theory to Practical Implementation

**TL;DR:**  
Fine‑tuning a large text‑to‑image diffusion model can easily corrupt the model’s generative quality because the weights drift far from the pre‑trained point. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) tackles this by learning a *shared orthogonal matrix* for each layer, which leaves every neuron’s direction – and therefore the pairwise angles that encode semantic structure – untouched. A small tweak called Constrained OFT (COFT) caps how far the new weights can move, giving extra stability without hurting performance.

---

## 1. Why Angles Matter in Deep Nets

When a network is trained for classification, a common observation is that *normalizing all neurons to the unit hypersphere* often preserves or even improves accuracy.  
Why? Because semantic information lives mostly in the **direction** of a neuron rather than its magnitude:

* In an autoencoder experiment on flowers, the angular component alone was able to reconstruct images almost perfectly, while raw norms contained no useful data.  
* The directional similarity between any two neurons can be quantified by their cosine similarity; “uniform” angles among neurons are called *hyperspherical uniformity*.

The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) formalizes this idea as

\[
\text{HE}(W) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{i,j\\ i\neq j}} \lVert \hat w_i - \hat w_j \rVert^{-1},
\quad 
\hat w_i = \frac{w_i}{\lVert w_i\rVert},
\]

where \(W=[w_1,\dots,w_n]\) is a layer’s weight matrix.  
A low HE means neurons are spread out uniformly on the sphere; a high HE means many are clustered together.

---

## 2. The Core Idea of Orthogonal Finetuning

Suppose we have a pre‑trained layer \(W^0\). We want to adapt it to a downstream prompt or control signal while keeping its *semantic shape*.  

**Observation:** If we multiply all neuron vectors by *the same orthogonal matrix* \(R\) (i.e., \(W = R W^0\)), then each neuron's direction changes, but **every pairwise angle stays exactly the same**:

\[
\langle R x , R y \rangle = \langle x , y\rangle , \qquad R^\top R = I .
\]

Thus the hyperspherical energy is preserved: \(\text{HE}(W)=\text{HE}(W^0)\).  
This is the key invariant OFT exploits.

### 2.1 How OFT Is Formulated

For every trainable layer,

```text
z = (R · W⁰)ᵀ · x
```

with \(R \in O(d)\).  
We initialize \(R=I\) so training starts exactly at the pre‑trained point and then let the optimizer learn an orthogonal rotation that nudges the model into better performance on the downstream task.

### 2.2 Why This Is Safer Than Naïve Fine‑Tuning

Typical fine‑tuning uses a tiny learning rate hoping that staying close in Euclidean terms will preserve semantics. That never guarantees angle preservation; tiny rotational drifts can flip entire categories of features.

By contrast, OFT ensures that **all pairwise angles** (hence evolutionary semantics of all neurons) stay fixed unless carefully rotated together through \(R\). This structural preservation is what gives OFT its hindsight claims of *faster convergence and higher stability* over techniques like LoRA or naive freezing.

---

## 3. Building an Efficient Orthogonal Parameterizer

Learning a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix would introduce \(O(d^2)\) trainable parameters — impractical for deep models where \(d\) may be thousands.

### 3.1 Cayley Transform

A classic parametrization maps any skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q^{\,T} = -Q\) to an orthogonal matrix:

\[
R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}.
\]

Since \(Q^\top = -Q\), \(R^\top R=I\) automatically holds. The Cayley transform is differentiable and easy to embed into SGD: we simply update \(Q\), then recompute \(R\).

The drawback: it only covers orthogonal matrices with determinant + 1 (the special orthogonal group). Empirically, this restriction has negligible effect on image generation quality.

### 3.2 Block–Diagonal Trick

To reduce parameter count further, we restrict \(R\) to be block–diagonal with \(r>1\) blocks:

```text
R = diag(R₁, R₂, … , Rᵣ),   each R_k ∈ O(d/r).
```

Now each block shares its own skew‑symmetric matrix under Cayley transform. The number of free parameters drops from
\(d(d-1)/2 \approx O(d^2)\)
to
\(r·(d/r)(d/r-1)/2 \approx O(d^2/r)\).

If even fewer parameters are desired we can share blocks across different layers or use a single shared block for all layers — at the cost of some flexibility but gaining great efficiency.

---

## 4. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

While OFT already keeps angles fixed, it still allows large rotations that may drift too far from the pre‑trained solution because rotations themselves can be substantial in Euclidean space.

COFT augments OFT with an explicit *radius constraint*:

```text
‖ R - I ‖ ≤ ε,
```

where ε is typically set between 0 and 0.5.
In practice this is enforced by constraining the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix:
if
\(R ≈ I + 2Q + O(Q²)\),
then simply project Q onto a ball of radius ε′:
\(‖Q‖ ≤ ε′.\)

The effect: COFT learns rotations that are both angle‑preserving **and** stay close to the identity, yielding even smoother optimization curves and preventing occasional wandering into inferior basins.

---

## 5. Putting It All Together

The pipeline for applying OFT/COFT to a diffusion model looks like:

1. **Identify target layers** (e.g., attention weight matrices in Stable Diffusion).
2. **Replace each weight matrix W⁰** with \(R·W⁰,\ R=I + O(Q)\).
3. **Initialize Q = 0** so that training starts at pre‑trained weights.
4. **Train only Q** using standard Adam/SGD; all other parameters stay frozen.
5. (Optional for COFT) Apply projection after each gradient step to keep ‖Q‖ below ε′.

At inference time there’s no extra overhead: you simply multiply every target weight by its learned orthogonal matrix once and use the standard diffusion sampling pipeline.

---

## 6. Practical Take‑aways

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **What if I want more flexibility?** | Increase block size or share fewer blocks; you’ll trade off parameter count against expressive power. |
| **Can COFT still be very fast?** | Yes—because COFT just adds one projection step per update, which costs negligible time compared to forward/backward passes in diffusion models. |
| **Does this work on any architecture?** | In principle yes; it applies to fully connected layers, convolutional kernels (with block‐diagonal interpretation), and attention matrices alike as demonstrated in experiments on Stable Diffusion v1.5.*

---

### Toy Example: Rotating Two Neurons

Imagine two neurons in one layer with pre‑trained unit vectors `w₁⁰` and `w₂⁰`. Their dot product encodes class‐discriminating angle θ₀:

```
cosθ₀ = w₁⁰ᵀ w₂⁰
```

After learning an orthogonal `R`, we obtain new neurons `w₁ = R w₁⁰`, `w₂ = R w₂⁰`.  
Because rotations preserve dot products:

```
cosθ_new = w₁ᵀ w₂ = w₁⁰ᵀ w₂⁰ = cosθ₀,
```

so θ stays unchanged! The difference you get in task performance comes from *how* these rotated directions align with downstream constraints (e.g., subject identity or edge maps), not from untethering angular relationships.

---

## 7. Final Thoughts

Orthogonal Finetuning offers a principled route to adapt huge text‑to‑image diffusion models without sacrificing their rich generative prior:

* Hyperspherical energy preservation protects semantic fidelity.
* A shared orthogonal transform keeps the optimization manifold smooth.
* Block‐diagonal Cayley parametrization makes training practical.
* COFT’s radius constraint adds safety without introducing extra inference cost.

Experimentally, both OFT and COFT have shown superior results across subject‐driven generation and controllable generation tasks compared to prior finetuning algorithms such as LoRA or ControlNet, especially when data or compute budgets are tight.

If you’re building a personalized diffusion system—whether you want your model to output images of yourself under varied prompts or guide generation along custom edge maps—give OFT/COFT a try: it’s simple to plug into most existing pipelines and often yields more stable, faster convergence than conventional fine‑tuning baselines.

# Putting a Model on a Checkered Path: Subject‑Driven Generation Made Easy

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models can now turn a simple sentence into a photorealistic image.  Still, when we want the model to remember *a specific person* from only a handful of photographs, the problem quickly turns into a finetuning puzzle: how to tuck a new subject into the pretrained network while keeping the rest of its creative power intact?  

In this post we walk through how **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** tackles that puzzle, why it outperforms the status‑quo methods (classic vanilla finetuning and LoRA) in stability, convergence speed and visual fidelity, and what that means for practical subject‑driven generation.

---

## 1. The DreamBooth Setting: Remembering Once, Generating Everywhere

DreamBooth is the de facto template for subject‑driven image synthesis today.  
The recipe is simple:

1. **Collect** 3–5 photos of the target subject (e.g., a dog or a person).  
2. **Finetune** a powerful latent diffusion model (Stable Diffusion v1.5 in most real‑world pipelines) so that it can generate images of *that exact subject* in any context you describe with text.

In other words, DreamBooth teaches the model "this face looks like X" while still letting you say “X in a sunny park wearing sunglasses”.  Achieving that duality is tricky:

- Too little change → the model keeps forgetting the subject.  
- Too much change → the model loses its global knowledge and produces blurry or collapsed images.

The prevailing strategy in DreamBooth is to nudge all weights with an infinitesimally small learning rate so that the updated network stays close to its pre‑training state. This implicit “stay close” policy is fragile: it requires verbose hyper‑parameter tuning and usually stalls after only a few hundred iterations.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Finetuning: Rotate, Don’t Rewrite

### 2.1 The Core Idea

Instead of tweaking every weight by an unrestricted small step, OFT **rotates neurons** at every layer:

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},\qquad
\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
\]

Here:
- \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is the original weight matrix.
- \(\mathbf{R}\) is an **orthogonal matrix** learned during finetuning.
- The neuron directions (their *angles*) change, but their *pairwise cosine similarities* stay exactly the same as before.

Because hyperspherical energy – the sum of inverse distances between all normalized neurons – is unchanged by any orthogonal transform, OFT guarantees that the *geometric fabric* of each layer’s activations remains identical to the pre‑trained model.

### 2.2 Why Angles Matter

A toy experiment with an autoencoder shows that reconstructions only survive if we preserve neuron directions; magnitudes carry almost no semantic signal.  In diffusion models, neuron angles encode high‑level concepts such as “dog”, “sunset”, or “smooth skin”.  Rotating these directions is precisely what you need to make a new subject appear without tearing apart the rest of the network’s knowledge.

### 2.3 Constrained OFT (COFT)

Even with orthogonality, rotations can go too far for certain tasks. COFT sandwiches OFT’s transform inside an \(\epsilon\)-ball around identity:

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2 \leq \epsilon.
\]

In practice this explicit bound stabilises training even more – you control how much intuition from DreamBooth can drift away from geometry learned on huge datasets.

---

## 3. Empirical Battle: OFT vs Classic Finetuning vs LoRA

| Method | Parameters | Convergence (epochs) | Stability        | Subject Fidelity |
|--------|------------|---------------------|------------------|------------------|
| Vanilla finetuning | Full model | **>300** (often fails beyond ~200) | Highly unstable – easily collapses | Poor |
| LoRA | ≈ 1 % of full parameters | ~\(2000^*\) | Stable but information leakage takes time   | Moderate |
| **OFT/COFT** | ~ 0–1 % via block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices | **≈ 8–12** | Immediate stable convergence             | Excellent |

\(^{*}\) LoRA often needs many epochs to recover useful control signals.

### 3.1 Faster Convergence

In head‑to‑head experiments on CelebA-HQ (landmark→face), COFT reaches human‑rated visual quality after just **8 epochs**, while LoRA trails behind until about **20 epochs**. The speed gain comes from two facts:

1. **Angular updates** directly target semantic components instead of diffusing errors across all weights.
2. Because gradients flow over an orthogonally constrained manifold, optimisation lives on a smoother landscape.

### 3.2 Robust Stability

Classic fine‑tuning usually produces mode collapse when you push for too many iterations or tweak learning rates accidentally—images become abstract blobs or repeat copyright traces from training data. LoRA preserves stability but needs careful balancing between rank and learning rate; otherwise it either blurs identities or loses overall generative fluency.

OFT’s rotation constraint makes *every step* respect hyperspherical energy invariance; consequently even aggressive learning rates do not tear apart semantic structure.

### 3.3 Visual Fidelity and Identity Retention

Quantitative metrics borrowed from DreamBooth benchmarks—DINO similarity, CLIP Image-score, LPIPS diversity—show OFT scores quite often surpassing both Vanilla and LoRA by measurable margins:

- DINO: +6–12 % relative improvement.
- CLIP Image-score: +4–8 % relative improvement.
- Diversity: comparable or slightly better.

Qualitative samples illustrate OFT’s uncanny ability to preserve subtle facial features (eye shape, hair color) while still following evocative prompts (“in a summer raincoat”). LoRA tends to drift toward the author’s training data style; vanilla finetuning frequently loses facial landmarks altogether.

---

## 4. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Feels Right

- **Preservation of Hyperspherical Energy** solves an old problem: what structural aspect of weights must stay intact?  
- Orthogonality keeps pairwise cosine similarities *rigid*, ensuring that downstream layers still see well‑distributed activations they were optimised for in pretraining.
- Mathematically clean: no extra regulariser terms; just one group constraint enforced by Cayley parameterisation.
- Computationally lightweight: using block-diagonal slices reduces parameter counts from \(O(d^2)\) down to roughly \(O(d)\).

---

## 5. Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **If you only have a handful of photos** and want high-fidelity imagery that stays true to those shots while remaining creatively flexible, pick OFT/COFT straight out of the box.
2. Reduce finetuning time drastically without tinkering with learning rate schedules—just hit *8 epochs*, set \(\epsilon=0.05\), run.
3. No extra inference cost: once your orthogonal matrices are trained, simply pre-multiply them with your latent encoder’s weight tables and deploy as usual.

---

## 6. Looking Ahead

OFT opens doors beyond single-subject memorisation:

- **Composable style blocks**: multiply several orthogonal transforms learned from different subjects; theory says their product remains orthogonal – promising for multi‑style synthesis without interference.
- **Efficient block designs** could further reduce bias introduced by rigid block sharing or expandability into larger hidden dimensions without hurting performance.
- Parallel training across multiple GPUs becomes trivial because each layer learns its own independent rotation matrix.

As diffusion models get bigger and training costs soar, orthogonally shaping neuron landscapes gives us an elegant knob that balances creativity against memory retention—a knob we rarely had before.

---

### Bottom Line

Orthogonal Finetuning turns what used to be a painstaking balancing act into a principled rotation trick on each layer’s weights. The result? A tiny set of parameters that keep your favoured subject soberly rendered while letting your imagination roam free—without ever needing extra inference steps or sacrificing quality.

Happy generating!



# “Control” with Confidence: From Canny Edges to Real‑World Images via COFT  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have made it possible to generate photorealistic images from natural language.  Yet the same passion that drives us toward wider creativity also exposes a weakness: telling a diffusion model *exactly* where and what you want often requires extra supervision, such as edge sketches, segmentation maps or facial key‑points.  Recent systems like ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter attempt to bridge this gap by fine‑tuning a pre‑trained model on the new control signal.  They work, but their training dynamics are noisy, convergence is slow and the final models can still drift away from the original prompt.  

Enter **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** – a lightweight finetuning scheme that keeps the great generality of a pre‑trained diffusion backbone while letting you steer it in a very stable fashion.  Below we walk through the main ideas behind COFT, introduce a principled **control‑consistency metric**, and explain why this approach delivers smoother curves and higher data efficiency than earlier methods.

---

## 1. Why angling neurons matters for controllable generation

### The geometry of a neuron’s weight

A Diffusion UNet layer can be seen as a matrix multiplication \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\).  If every column of \(\mathbf{W}\) is normalised to unit length, we can look at the set of vectors \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\}_i\) on the *unit hypersphere*.  The pairwise **cosine similarity** between any two columns captures how “similar” their receptive fields are.  The sum of all these similarities – called **hyperspherical energy** – measures how evenly spread the neurons are.

Research has shown that much of an image’s semantic content lives in *direction*, not magnitude: rotating or reflecting all neuron directions keeps hyperspherical energy unchanged but can dramatically alter what the network produces.  Orthogonal transformations are perfect for this because they preserve every pairwise angle.  

### From theory to practice

In COFT, we replace each weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\) (the pre‑trained one) by \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\), where \(\mathbf{R}\) is an **orthogonal matrix** shared within each layer.  
The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{\ \mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\ }
\]

and \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\!\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) merely reorients all weights simultaneously, we never touch the magnitude information that encodes generic vision knowledge; we only tweak direction to honour the new control signal.

---

## 2. Constrained orthogonality ― why adding a tiny radius matters

Orthogonal matrices alone already stabilise training – they keep neuron norms constant and preserve angles.  Yet pure orthogonal finetuning still has *degrees of freedom*: for large rotations \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\) can become sizeable and inadvertently harm language guidance or generic style.

COFT tackles this by imposing an **ε‑deviation constraint**:

\[
\boxed{\ \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\leqslant \varepsilon \ }
\]

The parameter ε controls how much twist we allow relative to the base model.  
- **Large ε** → COFT behaves like OFT; we get generous flexibility but risk drifting.  
- **Small ε** → COFT stays very close to its pre‑trained state; stability dominates.

In practice a modest ε (e.g., \(10^{-1}\)) gives the best trade‑off: robust convergence without sacrificing control fidelity.

---

## 3. A control‑consistency metric that really counts

Evaluating controllability has always been tricky: most papers simply show examples or report overall FID/CLIP scores, both of which ignore whether *the output actually follows* the input scaffold.

COFT comes with its own metric: **Control Consistency (CC)**.  
It works in two steps:

1. **Extract** the control signal from a produced image using an off‑the‑shelf detector (edge detector, segmentation network or key‑point detector).  
2. **Compare** this reconstructed signal with the original user supplied one using a task‑specific distance:
   - Canny edges: Intersection over Union (IoU) + F1 score.
   - Segmentation maps: Mean IoU + overall accuracy.
   - Facial landmarks: L2 distance between predicted vs original points.

Since CC measures how well *the same modality* aligns after generation, it isolates controllability from aesthetic quality and gives a clean signal for training hyperparameter search.

---

## 4. Data efficiency – less is more

Table 1 (in our internal notes) shows how many labelled images are needed for each approach:

| Task | Control Signal | Min Images |
|------|----------------|------------|
| Canny → Photos | Edge map | \(30~(COFT)\) |
| Segmentation → Photos | Seg map | \(120~(COFT)\) |
| Landmarks → Faces | Landmark set | \(40~(COFT)\) |

Contrast that with ControlNet’s requirement of 1 k images per task when training from scratch on Stable Diffusion v1.x and T2I‑Adapter’s even higher appetite due to its adapter layers.

With COFT we see **5–10× fewer samples** per task while still matching or beating the downstream quality of other methods – especially under low‑sample conditions where overfitting would normally dominate.

---

## 5. Smooth learning curves that obey physics

Because every update is an *orthogonal* rotation plus an optional small adjustment bounded by ε, gradients flowing back naturally keep weights on a curved manifold rather than wandering off into singular values or exploding norms.  In practice:

- The loss dips steadily over each epoch.
- Validation metrics (CC, CLIP‑prompt alignment) rise monotonically.
- Unlike ControlNet which often exhibits sudden jumps after ~8 epochs (due to a hard bottleneck resolver), COFT’s trajectory is almost linear from start to finish.

## 6. Practical tips for flipping your diffusion model into Cinderella's mirror

| ✅ Step | What to do? | Why? |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Load pre‑trained Stable Diffusion (v1.x/v2.x). | Your starting point must already understand language and vision well. |
| Generate an initial few images with your control map + prompt; inspect if baseline UNet honors semantics but not exactly lines/segmentation too well. | Establish ground truth “before” so you know how far you’re improving. |

## 8. Future directions

* **Composable controls:** Because orthogonal matrices multiply into another orthogonal matrix, it becomes feasible to combine several downstream tasks in one pass—think “draw eyes then outline face” without retraining each time.

# Beyond Image Generation: Composability and Future Directions for Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has proven to be a lightweight, stable, and high‑quality way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation and controllable generation. By learning a layer‑shared orthogonal transformation that preserves hyperspherical energy, OFT keeps the pretrained model’s semantic knowledge intact while still giving it the flexibility to learn new skills. The next frontier for OFT research, however, is going to stretch its applicability far beyond single‑task finetuning. In this post we tease out three particularly promising directions: dealing with the computational bottleneck of matrix inverses, exploiting the composability of orthogonal transforms for multi‑task learning, and striking a better balance between parameter efficiency and model flexibility.

---

## 1. Scaling the Cayley Transform

The core of OFT is an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) that rotates the weight matrix of a layer \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) at finetuning time so that  
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!T}\mathbf{x},
\]
with an implicit orthogonality constraint \( \mathbf{R}^T \! = \! \mathbf{R}^{-1}\).  
A convenient way to parameterise \(\mathbf{R}\) is the Cayley transform
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q}^T = -\mathbf{Q}\)). This guarantees that \( \mathbf{R} \in O(d)\) without having to run expensive Gram–Schmidt or QR routines.

### 1.1 Why matrix inverses become a bottleneck

The inverse \((\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) costs \(O(d^{3})\) per forward/backward pass when implemented naively. For modern diffusion models:

- The UNet backbone contains millions of parameters; many layers have very large projection dimensions (e.g., \(d=3200\) in a key‑value head).
- Training on high‑resolution latent grids doubles the effective batch size, further escalating memory pressure.

These factors push the inverse operation toward being one of the most time‑consuming parts of a single training step.

### 1.2 What we can do now

1. **Block‑diagonal Cayley** – The paper’s authors already proposed partitioning \( \mathbf{R} \) into smaller blocks so that each block’s inverse is cheap:  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}= {\rm diag}(\,\underbrace{\dots}_{r {\rm \ blocks}}\dots)
   \].  
   With \(r=8\), each block is only \(d/r\times d/r\), dropping complexity from \(O(d^{3})\) to roughly \(O((d/r)^{3})r = O(d^{3}/r^{2})\).

2. **Approximate inverses** – For small skew matrices \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|<1/2\), we can expand
   \[
   (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
      = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \! \mathbf{Q}^{k},
   \]
   truncating at low order \(K=2$ or $3$. This eliminates explicit inversion altogether.

3. **Implicit solutions** – Solving linear systems with conjugate gradient (CG) or MINRES on GPU can be far cheaper than inverting dense matrices, especially if the system is well conditioned (which it is almost always for small perturbations around identity).

Open research challenge: building a “differentiable CG” module that can fit inside PyTorch or JAX autograd graphs without losing performance.

---

## 2. Orthogonality as a Composable Module

One of OFT’s most exciting properties is that **orthogonal matrices form a group** under multiplication: if you finish training a diffusion head with an orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}_{1}\), then train another head with a different orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}_{2}\), their product
\[ 
   \tilde{\mathbf R}=  \mathbf R_{2}  \!\cdot\!  \mathbf R_{1}
\]
is *still* an orthogonal rotation that simultaneously fulfils both constraints. This raises several tantalising questions:

| Question | Why it matters | Possible Approach |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Can we learn one global transform per task and compose them lazily? | Enables multi‑task inference without retraining. | Reuse the product of per‑task matrices at inference time; equivalent to running sequential finite diffs on the same Latent space. |
| Do multiple tasks “interfere” when matrices are multiplied? | Could lead to loss of semantics if one rotate erases another’s structure. | Empirically evaluate ranking signals (e.g., CLIP similarity) after cumulative composition; design regularizers that penalize large angular drift between tasks' eigenvectors. |
| Could we train a *shared* subspace matrix that balances all tasks simultaneously? | Provides parameter sharing across tasks while preserving rich representations. | A joint objective:
  $$
     L = L_{\text{text}} + L_{\text{subj}} + L_{\text{control}} 
          + \lambda_{\text{dir}}\!\sum_{\ell}\!{\Vert}
           {\rm cotan}(  {\bf R}^{(\ell)}_{\text{text}}
                    {\bf R}^{(\ell)}_{\text{subj}}{}^{-1})
              {\Vert}^{2}
  $$
  where final term encourages alignment among all transforms.

Experimental direction: start with two tasks (subject vs control), train them sequentially but enforce an *orthogonality alignment loss* so that their final matrices are close in angle space but still optimised for each task separately.

Open problem: proving when such compositions preserve desired properties (e.g., generating crisp edges or keeping identity unchanged). An elegant geometric analysis could provide theoretical guarantees.

---

## 3. Parameter Efficiency vs Flexibility

In OFT, we observe a remarkable drop in trainable parameters compared to LoRA because only the orthogonal matrix needs updating:

- A full dense orthogonal matrix requires \( O(d^2)\) parameters.
- With block-diagonal decomposition and shared blocks we reduce this roughly to \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

However, constraints also introduce bias:

- **Block size choice** balances expressivity against overfitting: larger blocks allow more global rotation but reduce regularisation.
- **Fixed determinant** (Cayley transforms only produce matrices with det=+1). Although empirically benign, some use cases might benefit from allowing reflections (det=-1).

Future work should explore *hybrid* parameterisations:

| Strategy | Pros | Cons |
|----------|------|------|
| Low-rank perturbation on top of identity | Adds fine-grained control while keeping overhead small | Might not fully preserve hyperspherical energy |
| Sparse random projections | Fast multiplication and inference | Harder to guarantee strict orthogonality |
| Structured deep networks (e.g., equivariant layers) | Very compact representation | Needs domain-specific design |

A rigorous study measuring *semantic retention* (via CLIP or DINO similarity) as block size varies will answer whether the current trade-off is optimal or if new structures can push boundaries further.

---

## TL;DR

- **Scalability bottleneck:** Replace expensive Cayley inversions with block-oriented approximations or iterative solvers.
- **Composability:** Orthogonal transforms compose nicely; exploring multi-task pipelines could eliminate separate finetunes.
- **Efficiency–flexibility continuum:** Block size and parametrisation choices dictate how much freedom our matrices have versus preserving pretrained knowledge.

Orthogonal Finetuning already feels like a natural next step after LoRA for diffusion models—its geometric foundations give us guarantees that simple learning rate tweaking never has. As researchers grapple with these open questions, we’ll see whether OFT becomes a standard plug‑and‑play adapter for any vision–language system, unlocking even richer downstream capabilities while staying light on both computation and data.*
