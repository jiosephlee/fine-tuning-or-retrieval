\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Putting a Model on a Checkered Path: Subject‑Driven Generation Made Easy

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models can now turn a simple sentence into a photorealistic image.  Still, when we want the model to remember *a specific person* from only a handful of photographs, the problem quickly turns into a finetuning puzzle: how to tuck a new subject into the pretrained network while keeping the rest of its creative power intact?  

In this post we walk through how **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** tackles that puzzle, why it outperforms the status‑quo methods (classic vanilla finetuning and LoRA) in stability, convergence speed and visual fidelity, and what that means for practical subject‑driven generation.

---

## 1. The DreamBooth Setting: Remembering Once, Generating Everywhere

DreamBooth is the de facto template for subject‑driven image synthesis today.  
The recipe is simple:

1. **Collect** 3–5 photos of the target subject (e.g., a dog or a person).  
2. **Finetune** a powerful latent diffusion model (Stable Diffusion v1.5 in most real‑world pipelines) so that it can generate images of *that exact subject* in any context you describe with text.

In other words, DreamBooth teaches the model "this face looks like X" while still letting you say “X in a sunny park wearing sunglasses”.  Achieving that duality is tricky:

- Too little change → the model keeps forgetting the subject.  
- Too much change → the model loses its global knowledge and produces blurry or collapsed images.

The prevailing strategy in DreamBooth is to nudge all weights with an infinitesimally small learning rate so that the updated network stays close to its pre‑training state. This implicit “stay close” policy is fragile: it requires verbose hyper‑parameter tuning and usually stalls after only a few hundred iterations.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Finetuning: Rotate, Don’t Rewrite

### 2.1 The Core Idea

Instead of tweaking every weight by an unrestricted small step, OFT **rotates neurons** at every layer:

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},\qquad
\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}.
\]

Here:
- \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is the original weight matrix.
- \(\mathbf{R}\) is an **orthogonal matrix** learned during finetuning.
- The neuron directions (their *angles*) change, but their *pairwise cosine similarities* stay exactly the same as before.

Because hyperspherical energy – the sum of inverse distances between all normalized neurons – is unchanged by any orthogonal transform, OFT guarantees that the *geometric fabric* of each layer’s activations remains identical to the pre‑trained model.

### 2.2 Why Angles Matter

A toy experiment with an autoencoder shows that reconstructions only survive if we preserve neuron directions; magnitudes carry almost no semantic signal.  In diffusion models, neuron angles encode high‑level concepts such as “dog”, “sunset”, or “smooth skin”.  Rotating these directions is precisely what you need to make a new subject appear without tearing apart the rest of the network’s knowledge.

### 2.3 Constrained OFT (COFT)

Even with orthogonality, rotations can go too far for certain tasks. COFT sandwiches OFT’s transform inside an \(\epsilon\)-ball around identity:

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_2 \leq \epsilon.
\]

In practice this explicit bound stabilises training even more – you control how much intuition from DreamBooth can drift away from geometry learned on huge datasets.

## 3. Empirical Battle: OFT vs Classic Finetuning vs LoRA

| Method | Parameters | Convergence (epochs) | Stability        | Subject Fidelity |
|--------|------------|---------------------|------------------|------------------|
| Vanilla finetuning | Full model | **>300** (often fails beyond ~200) | Highly unstable – easily collapses | Poor |
| LoRA | ≈ 1 % of full parameters | ~\(2000^*\) | Stable but information leakage takes time   | Moderate |
| **OFT/COFT** | ~ 0–1 % via block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices | **≈ 8–12** | Immediate stable convergence             | Excellent |

\(^{*}\) LoRA often needs many epochs to recover useful control signals.

### 3.1 Faster Convergence

In head‑to‑head experiments on CelebA-HQ (landmark→face), COFT reaches human‑rated visual quality after just **8 epochs**, while LoRA trails behind until about **20 epochs**. The speed gain comes from two facts:

1. **Angular updates** directly target semantic components instead of diffusing errors across all weights.
2. Because gradients flow over an orthogonally constrained manifold, optimisation lives on a smoother landscape.

### 3.2 Robust Stability

Classic fine‑tuning usually produces mode collapse when you push for too many iterations or tweak learning rates accidentally—images become abstract blobs or repeat copyright traces from training data. LoRA preserves stability but needs careful balancing between rank and learning rate; otherwise it either blurs identities or loses overall generative fluency.

OFT’s rotation constraint makes *every step* respect hyperspherical energy invariance; consequently even aggressive learning rates do not tear apart semantic structure.

### 3.3 Visual Fidelity and Identity Retention

Quantitative metrics borrowed from DreamBooth benchmarks—DINO similarity, CLIP Image-score, LPIPS diversity—show OFT scores quite often surpassing both Vanilla and LoRA by measurable margins:

- DINO: +6–12 % relative improvement.
- CLIP Image-score: +4–8 % relative improvement.
- Diversity: comparable or slightly better.

Qualitative samples illustrate OFT’s uncanny ability to preserve subtle facial features (eye shape, hair color) while still following evocative prompts (“in a summer raincoat”). LoRA tends to drift toward the author’s training data style; vanilla finetuning frequently loses facial landmarks altogether.

---

## 4. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Feels Right

- **Preservation of Hyperspherical Energy** solves an old problem: what structural aspect of weights must stay intact?  
- Orthogonality keeps pairwise cosine similarities *rigid*, ensuring that downstream layers still see well‑distributed activations they were optimised for in pretraining.
- Mathematically clean: no extra regulariser terms; just one group constraint enforced by Cayley parameterisation.
- Computationally lightweight: using block-diagonal slices reduces parameter counts from \(O(d^2)\) down to roughly \(O(d)\).

---

## 5. Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **If you only have a handful of photos** and want high-fidelity imagery that stays true to those shots while remaining creatively flexible, pick OFT/COFT straight out of the box.
2. Reduce finetuning time drastically without tinkering with learning rate schedules—just hit *8 epochs*, set \(\epsilon=0.05\), run.
3. No extra inference cost: once your orthogonal matrices are trained, simply pre-multiply them with your latent encoder’s weight tables and deploy as usual.

---

## 6. Looking Ahead

OFT opens doors beyond single-subject memorisation:

- **Composable style blocks**: multiply several orthogonal transforms learned from different subjects; theory says their product remains orthogonal – promising for multi‑style synthesis without interference.
- **Efficient block designs** could further reduce bias introduced by rigid block sharing or expandability into larger hidden dimensions without hurting performance.
- Parallel training across multiple GPUs becomes trivial because each layer learns its own independent rotation matrix.

As diffusion models get bigger and training costs soar, orthogonally shaping neuron landscapes gives us an elegant knob that balances creativity against memory retention—a knob we rarely had before.

---

### Bottom Line

Orthogonal Finetuning turns what used to be a painstaking balancing act into a principled rotation trick on each layer’s weights. The result? A tiny set of parameters that keep your favoured subject soberly rendered while letting your imagination roam free—without ever needing extra inference steps or sacrificing quality.

Happy generating!
