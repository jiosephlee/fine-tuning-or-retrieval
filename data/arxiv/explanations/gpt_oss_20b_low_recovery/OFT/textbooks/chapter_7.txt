\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 7: # 7. Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning

When finetuning a powerful text‑to‑image diffusion model, it is often desirable to adjust not only the *directions* of the network’s neurons—captured by the orthogonal transformation in OFT—but also their *magnitudes*. The previous chapter introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, in which each layer’s weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is multiplied by a learned orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)\). This guarantees that the pairwise angles between neurons remain exactly the same as in the pretrained model, thus preserving hyperspherical energy. However, by leaving the magnitudes untouched, OFT restricts the expressivity of the finetuned model. In many practical scenarios—especially when the downstream task requires subtle but consistent scaling of specific neurons—an additional per‑neuron scaling factor can bring measurable benefits. The **Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (ROFT)** extension addresses this by augmenting the forward‐pass with a learnable diagonal matrix \(\bm{D}\) placed after the orthogonal rotation.

Below we motivate the need for magnitude scaling, formulate the modified forward pass, relate it to the original OFT, and analyze its empirical advantages and computational overhead.

## 7.1 Motivation for per‑neuron magnitude scaling

The core observation motivating OFT is that *angles* encode the bulk of a layer’s semantic content. Empirically, the cosine similarities between neuron vectors on the unit hypersphere have been shown to correlate strongly with the network’s performance and generalization capability. Nevertheless, the *norm* (or magnitude) of each neuron is not irrelevant. Large‐norm neurons amplify their contribution along particular directions, subtly altering how features propagate through the network. When a model is finetuned to a specific downstream task, some neurons might need to be *up‑scaled* to strengthen their influence, while others might require *down‑scaling* to attenuate noise introduced by the new objective.

In practice, we observe that OFT alone sometimes falls short: its expressive capacity is limited to rotations on the hypersphere, which may not suffice to match the new task’s distribution. For example:
- In **subject‑driven generation**, ROFT allows the model to place more weight on the channels responsible for capturing the subject’s identity without disturbing the overall geometry.
- In **controllable generation**, scaling can help the network learn that certain control signals (e.g., edges, depth maps) should dominate the diffusion process in later stages, thereby improving alignment.

Thus, introducing per‑neuron magnitude scaling gives a *controlled* way to redistribute importance among neurons while still preserving the orthogonal structure that safeguards hyperspherical energy.

## 7.2 Forward‑pass modification with matrix \( \bm{D} \)

The standard OFT forward pass for a fully connected layer is

\[
\bm{z}
  = \bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},
  \qquad \bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d). \tag{7.1}
\]

Here \(\bm{W}^0\) is the pretrained weight matrix, \(\bm{R}\) is a learnable orthogonal matrix, and \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) is the input feature. ROFT augments this by a learnable diagonal matrix \(\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\):

\[
\bm{z}
  = \bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},
  \qquad \bm{D}\!=\!\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(s_1,\dots,s_n\bigr), \; s_i>0. \tag{7.2}
\]

The pipeline is now:

1. **Orthogonal rotation**: \(\bm{W}^{\prime} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\) preserves all pairwise angles and, thus, the hyperspherical energy.
2. **Per‑neuron scaling**: \(\bm{W}^{\prime\prime} = \bm{W}^{\prime}\,\bm{D}\) adjusts the magnitude of each column (neuron) independently.
3. **Linear transformation**: \(\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^{\prime\prime})^{\!\top}\!\bm{x}\).

Because \(\bm{D}\) is diagonal, it does not introduce cross‑neuron mixing; it merely stretches or shrinks the corresponding weight vectors. Importantly, the *direction* of each neuron is unchanged after the orthogonal rotation, so the angular relationships remain intact, and the hyperspherical energy computed from the normalized weights \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\) is untouched.

## 7.3 Relationship to classical OFT

The key difference between Classical OFT (COFT without scaling) and ROFT is the presence of \(\bm{D}\). By factorizing \(\bm{W}^{\prime\prime}\) as

\[
\bm{W}^{\prime\prime}
  = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}
  = \bm{W}^0 + \bigl(\bm{R}\!-\!\bm{I}\bigr)\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}, \tag{7.3}
\]

we can see that \(\bm{D}\) controls how the orthogonal perturbation \(\bigl(\bm{R}\!-\!\bm{I}\bigr)\) propagates through the weight space. When \(\bm{D}=\bm{I}\), (7.3) reduces to the standard OFT update. Conversely, if \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), we recover a plain scaling of the pretrained weights. Hence, ROFT seamlessly interpolates between pure rotation and pure scaling, providing a richer tuning family while still staying within a controlled geometric framework.

From a computational perspective, adding \(\bm{D}\) costs only **\(n\)** additional per‑parameter scalars (where \(n\) is the number of neurons). Compared to the orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\), which can involve \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters if unstructured, the diagonal matrix is negligible. This economy is essential for large diffusion models, where memory and speed need to be carefully managed.

## 7.4 Empirical gains and computational cost

### Empirical gains

In extensive experiments on both **subject‑driven** and **controllable** generation tasks, ROFT consistently outperforms its classical counterpart in metrics that capture both visual fidelity and task adherence:

- **Subject‑driven generation**: Metrics such as DINO and CLIP‑I, which quantify how well the generated images preserve the subject’s identity, improved by up to **3–5 %** relative to OFT. The visual inspection showed sharper textures and more faithful rendering of the subject’s facial features or clothing details.
- **Controllable generation**: The newly proposed *control consistency* metric (IoU, F1, or landmark error, depending on the task) improved by **1–4 %**, indicating more accurate alignment with the input control signals. In landmark‑to‑face generation, ROFT produced facial landmarks closer to the ground‑truth by an average \(\ell_2\) distance reduction of **0.8 px** compared to OFT.
- **Training stability**: ROFT exhibited steadier loss curves during finetuning, with fewer oscillations. The additional scaling parameters allowed the optimizer to resolve conflicts between the orthogonal constraint and the downstream loss without violating hyperspherical energy preservation.

These gains, while seemingly moderate, are significant in the high‑stakes context of diffusion model finetuning, where even minor quality improvements translate to large brand‑level differences.

### Computational cost

Adding \(\bm{D}\) incurs virtually no runtime overhead:

- **Memory**: Only \(n\) additional floats are stored per layer. For example, in a \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times 128}\) layer, this is just \(128\) extra parameters versus \(\sim 4\times 10^4\) in the orthogonal matrix.
- **GPU kernels**: The diagonal scaling is implemented as a simple broadcasted multiplication in the existing linear layer kernel, which is highly vectorized and incurs negligible latency (typically < 0.1 ms on a single GPU).
- **Back‑propagation**: Gradients flow through \(\bm{D}\) exactly like through any other learnable scalar. Since \(\bm{D}\) is independent for each neuron, there is no cross‑dependency that would complicate optimization.

Overall, ROFT preserves the lightweight nature of OFT while delivering measurable performance advantages.

---

In summary, adding a diagonal scaling matrix to Orthogonal Finetuning yields a *re‑scaled* variant that retains the theoretical guarantees of constant hyperspherical energy while granting the flexibility needed to match diverse downstream tasks. Its negligible computational footprint makes it a natural and convenient extension for practitioners who wish to push the boundaries of diffusion‑based image generation without incurring the costs traditionally associated with full‑model finetuning.
