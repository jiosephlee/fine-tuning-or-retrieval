Chapter 10: ## Chapter 10: Experimental Evaluation – Controllable Generation

### 10.1 Task Definitions and Datasets

Controllable generation is the task of steering a generative diffusion model to produce images that obey an explicit auxiliary signal—such as a set of edges, a segmentation map, or a landmark configuration—while also respecting a textual prompt. The auxiliary signals serve as side constraints that shape spatial layout, object geometry, or local attributes. In this chapter we evaluate Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on three canonical controllable generation benchmarks that cover the full spectrum from low‑level edge cues to high‑level semantic maps:

| Task | Control Signal | Dataset | Key Characteristics |
|------|----------------|---------|----------------------|
| Canny Edge‑to‑Image (C2I) | Sparse edge map | COCO (80k validation images) | Low‑level structural guidance; signals are binary and highly corrupted in regions of texture |
| Segmentation‑to‑Image (S2I) | Class‑wise mask per pixel | ADE20K (25k images of urban and indoor scenes) | Dense, multi‑class semantic layout; requires correct rendering of colors and textures |
| Landmark‑to‑Face (L2F) | 68 facial landmarks | CelebA‑HQ (30k high‑resolution faces) | Fine‑grained pose and expression control; densely distributed 2‑D coordinates |

For each task, we adopt the official training and validation splits as specified in the original dataset documentation, ensuring comparable statistical properties across the three benchmarks. All images are resized to a resolution of $512\times512$ for C2I and S2I, and $1024\times1024$ for L2F, matching the inference resolution used by Stable Diffusion v1.5.

### 10.2 Training Protocol and Hyper‑Parameter Choices

All experiments are performed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. The base model is Stable Diffusion v1.5, which comprises an encoder, a diffusion UNet (composed of self‑attention, cross‑attention, and convolution blocks), a VAE (decoder) and a pretrained CLIP text encoder. We finetune the UNet only, as it is the core component that conditions on both the input prompt and the control signal.

#### 10.2.1 Finetuning Setup

| Component | Finetuned Param. | Grid Search | Sampled Value |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|---------------|
| Orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ | shared per layer | $r = \{8, 16, 32\}$ (block size) | $r=16$ |
| Longitudinal weight $\bm{D}$ (re‑scaled OFT) | – | – | – |
| Learning rate | AdamW | $\{1\times10^{-5}, 5\times10^{-5}, 1\times10^{-4}\}$ | $5\times10^{-5}$ |
| Batch size | 1–4 per GPU | – | 4 |
| Training epochs | 20 | – | 20 |
| Scheduler | Cosine annealing | – | – |
| Warm‑up steps | 500 | – | 500 |

For COFT we additionally tune the radius constraint $\epsilon$ on a logarithmic scale: $\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}$, selecting $\epsilon = 0.01$ as the best performing value across all tasks.

All baselines – ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter, and LoRA – use the same training protocol except for the specific learnable modules they attach (e.g. ControlNet adds a small cross‑attention module). To ensure a fair comparison, we match the number of trainable parameters by fixing the block size in OFT/COFT to approximate the parameter count of the LoRA adapters.

#### 10.2.2 Loss Function

The loss function is the standard denoising score matching objective used in Stable Diffusion, augmented with a reconstruction loss between the predicted image $\hat{\bm{y}}$ and the ground truth $\bm{y}$:

\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\_{t,\hat{\bm{y}}\sim p_{t}}\left[\|\epsilon_{\theta}(\hat{\bm{y}}, t, \text{prompt}, \text{control}) - \epsilon_{\theta^0}(\bm{y}, t, \text{prompt}, \text{control})\|_{2}^{2}\right].
\]

The control signal is integrated into the conditioning through a dedicated **cross‑attention** mechanism that concatenates the control embedding to the text embedding before passing to the UNet. This mirrors the implementation in ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter, ensuring that the only difference between methods lies in the finetuning strategy.

### 10.3 Convergence Analysis and Landmark Error Curves

Convergence is quantitatively monitored by measuring the error between the predicted control output and the original control signal. For the L2F task, we compute the mean Euclidean distance between the 68 landmark coordinates extracted from the generated image (using a pretrained facial landmark detector) and the ground‑truth landmarks supplied as input. Figure 10.1 plots the average landmark error as a function of training epochs for all four methods. Key observations:

- **Fast Initial Rise**: OFT and COFT reach a plateau within 8 epochs, whereas LoRA requires roughly 16 epochs to converge to a comparable error, and ControlNet shows a delayed rise after 12 epochs.
- **Stabilization**: After the plateau, both OFT and COFT maintain low variance across epochs, whereas LoRA’s error oscillates due to the underlying low‑rank updates. ControlNet’s error spikes near the end of training, indicating instability in the added control module.
- **Parameter Efficiency**: Although OFT and COFT use fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet (≈70 k vs 300 k), they match or surpass ControlNet’s final error, hinting at a more effective use of each parameter.

The same trend holds for C2I and S2I tasks, where the error metric is either the pixel‑wise $\ell_2$ distance or the IoU for segmentation maps, respectively. In all three tasks, OFT/COFT demonstrate a smoother trajectory and faster convergence, requiring fewer epochs to achieve near‑optimal performance.

### 10.4 Control Consistency Metrics

To objectively quantify how faithfully a model respects the control signal, we introduce a set of *control consistency* metrics that mirror the evaluation methodology used for the downstream task itself. These metrics are defined per task:

| Task | Ground‑Truth Control | Predicted Control | Metric |
|------|----------------------|-------------------|--------|
| C2I | Canny edge map | Edge map extracted from generated image via skeletized Sobel filter | IoU, F1 |
| S2I | Pixel‑wise segmentation | Segmentation map produced by a pretrained UNet‑Seg on the generated image | Mean IoU, Pixel Accuracy, Overall Accuracy |
| L2F | 68 landmarks | Landmarks detected on generated face | Mean $\ell_2$ distance (in pixels) |

**IoU** and **F1** for edges measure the overlap between predicted and ground‑truth edge structures. For segmentation, **Mean IoU** averages the per‑class intersection‑over‑union, while **Pixel Accuracy** and **Overall Accuracy** assess the fraction of correctly labeled pixels. For landmarks, the Euclidean distance captures pose fidelity: lower values indicate more accurate reproduction of the input landmarks.

During evaluation, we generate 10,000 images per method per task, sampling random prompts and control signals from the validation set. All control extraction procedures are performed using off‑the‑shelf, pretrained models (e.g. DeepLabv3+ for segmentation, MTCNN for landmark detection) to prevent bias from the finetuned diffusion model.

### 10.7 Ablation Studies

#### 10.7.1 Effect of Training Epochs

Figure 10.3 shows control consistency metrics plotted against training epochs for all methods. OFT/COFT consistently reach near‑plateaus after 6–8 epochs, confirming the headline “fast convergence” claim. ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter require the full 20 epochs to approach comparable performance, indicating that the added control modules induce a longer inner optimization loop.

#### 10.7.2 Data Fraction Variations

We subsample varying fractions of the training dataset (5 %, 10 %, 20 %, 50 %, 100 %). Table 10.7 reports the mean ℓ₂ error for the L2F task. Even with only 5 % of the data, OFT/COFT achieve an error within 15 % of full‑data performance, whereas LoRA and ControlNet degrade by 40 % relative to full data. This confirms the *data‑efficiency* advantage of orthogonal finetuning.

| Data % | LoRA Error | ControlNet Error | OFT Error | COFT Error |
|--------|------------|------------------|-----------|------------|
| 5 | 41.7 | 53.2 | 19.5 | 18.7 |
| 10 | 34.2 | 44.8 | 15.6 | 14.9 |
| 20 | 30.1 | 38.9 | 13.7 | 12.8 |
| 50 | 22.8 | 31.5 | 11.3 | 10.5 |
| 100 | 18.1 | 27.8 | 11.4 | 10.9 |

#### 10.7.3 Block Parameter Size

We vary the block size $r$ of the orthogonal matrix. Smaller $r$ reduces parameter count but limits expressivity. Figure 10.4 plots control consistency versus $r$ for the S2I task. As $r$ increases from 8 to 32, the IoU rises from 0.69 to 0.73, while the parameter count grows from 60 k to 112 k. A middle ground ($r=16$) offers a good trade‑off between performance and resource usage.

### 10.8 Summary

The experimental results demonstrate that **Orthogonal Finetuning** and **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** provide a principled, efficient, and highly stable means of adapting large diffusion models to controllable generation tasks. Compared to legacy finetuning methods (LoRA, ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter), OFT/COFT exhibit:

1. **Superior Control Fidelity** across all three canonical tasks, as evidenced by higher IoU/F1/Accuracy and lower landmark error.
2. **Rapid Convergence** with a single‑digit number of epochs, reducing training costs and mitigating over‑fitting.
3. **Parameter Efficiency** without sacrificing quality; the orthogonal update incurs minimal overhead.
4. **Zero Inference Overhead**; the final model is equivalent to the warm‑started pretrained UNet, requiring no extra modules at inference time.

These advantages align directly with the desiderata laid out in the introduction: *training efficiency* and *generalizability preservation*. The orthogonal transformation ensures that pairwise neural relationships on the unit hypersphere remain unchanged, which the authors argue is critical for maintaining the semantic richness learned during large‑scale pretraining. In short, OFT/COFT transform the weight matrix by a learned rotation (or reflection), thereby re‑orienting neuron directions while preserving all internal geometry, leading to robust control without collateral degeneration of the base diffusion model.
