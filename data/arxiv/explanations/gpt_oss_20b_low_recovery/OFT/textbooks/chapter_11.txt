\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 11: ## 11. Comparative Discussion

### 11.1 Parameter Efficiency versus Generalisation

One of the central motivations for developing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) is to reconcile two seemingly opposing goals: **parameter efficiency** and **generalisability of pre‑trained knowledge**.  Traditional fine‑tuning approaches such as DreamBooth or full‑model regression tend to increase the number of trainable parameters dramatically, especially when the target task deviates significantly from the pre‑training distribution.  In contrast, the work by LoRA demonstrates that a low‑rank additive update can thinly attach a small number of trainable parameters to a massive backbone: if \(W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is the pre‑trained weight matrix, LoRA replaces it with
\[
W=W^{0}+A\,B^\intercal,
\]
where \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}\) and \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'}\) with a low rank \(r'\).  The parameter count scales as \(\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))\), which can be much smaller than the full matrix, but still permits significant model reshaping.

OFT takes a different tack.  It retains the full weight matrix \(W\) but restricts the transformation applied to it to an **orthogonal matrix** \(R\), applied multiplicatively:
\[
W=R\,W^{0},
\]
with \(R^\top R=I\).  Because an orthogonal matrix can be parameterised with only \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom, the number of trainable parameters becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) in the worst case.  In practice, **block‑diagonal parameterisation** shrinks this further to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\), where \(r\) is the number of blocks.  Empirically, this is typically an order of magnitude less than LoRA’s parameter budget when matching the rank \(r\) to the block size, as illustrated in the experimental reports.

In terms of *generalisation*, CFOT augments OFT by adding a hard radius constraint \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\).  This guarantees that each trainable weight stays close to its pre‑trained value while respecting the hyperspherical structure, thus preventing the model from drifting into regions that compromise the *semantic knowledge* encoded in the pre‑trained network.  The experiments in the original paper clearly show that OFT and COFT preserve subject identity in DreamBooth‑style tasks while avoiding the generation collapse seen in standard fine‑tuning.  The constrained variant further narrows the search space, leading to **more stable convergence** and **fewer required training iterations**, which directly translates to lower computational cost during training.

### 11.2 Orthogonality vs. Low‑rank Updates: LoRA

At first glance, OFT and LoRA look similar—they both keep the pre‑trained model as a reference and introduce a small, structured, trainable component.  However, **the nature of the transformation is fundamentally different**.

- **LoRA**: Adds a *low‑rank* matrix to the pre‑trained weights:
  \[
  W = W^{0} + (A B^\intercal).
  \]
  This construction is *additive* and allows the update to lie in a subspace of lower dimensionality.  The expected effect is that the model can learn new, task‑specific patterns while largely retaining the pre‑trained basis due to the small magnitude of the additive term.

- **OFT**: Applies a *multiplicative* orthogonal transformation:
  \[
  W = R W^{0},
  \]
  with \(R\) orthogonal.  Because orthogonal transformations preserve norms and angles, **pair‑wise neuron similarity** remains untouched, thereby maintaining the *hyperspherical energy* of the pre‑trained layers.  This invariance is crucial: hypotheses in the paper suggest that the angles between neurons encode high‑level semantics, and preserving those angles ensures that the latent manifold learned during pre‑training is not corrupted.

Moreover, LoRA’s additive update can disturb the delicate balance of directional relationships among neurons, potentially leading to **information loss**.  In practice, this is observed as a decline in subject fidelity and an increase in training instability.  OFT learns an *orthogonal* \(R\) that can rotate the entire neuron space but keeps the inherent geometry intact.  Coupled with the optional scaling factor in re‑scaled OFT, the method gains a touch of flexibility while still guarding the pre‑trained hyperspherical structure.

### 11.3 Inference Overhead: OFT vs. ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter

A significant practical advantage of OFT and COFT stems from the **absence of additional inference overhead**.  Since the fine‑tuned weights are explicitly computed as \(W=R\,W^{0}\) before inference, downstream processes need not modify the pipeline: one can replace the original weight matrix in the pre‑trained model with the product of the learned orthogonal matrix and the original weights.  In other words, inference occurs exactly as if the model had been trained once from scratch with the new weights—no extra attention layers, no re‑insertion of control branches, no runtime overhead.

In contrast, methods such as **ControlNet** or **T2I‑Adapter** append a *control‑specific* subnetwork or adapter modules to the pre‑trained backbone.  During inference, the control signal must be fed through these additional modules and then merged (e.g., via attention or gating) back into the main diffusion process.  This extra computation increases memory usage and inference latency.  Furthermore, ControlNet requires a separate control‑only forward pass for each control condition, whereas OFT merely requires the multiplication of the orthogonal matrix that has already been applied during training.

The paper emphasises that since OFT maintains a *single* set of adapted weights, it can be deployed exactly like the original model, achieving the same end‑to‑end speed as the baseline diffusion model—a decisive benefit for production deployments where latency is critical.

### 11.4 Convergence Smoothness and Ease of Hyper‑Parameter Tuning

In the experiments, the authors demonstrate that both OFT and COFT exhibit markedly **smooth convergence curves**.  Unlike LoRA or standard fine‑tuning, which can show abrupt changes in loss and sudden divergences, OFT’s train‑time dynamics are governed by rotations on a hypersphere.  Because the orthogonal constraint keeps the weights on a *continuous manifold*, gradient updates move the weights along a smooth geodesic path, thereby preventing the model from collapse or boundary‑crossing problems that typically happen with unconstrained updates.

The **constrained variant COFT** further stabilises training by enforcing \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\).  This hard radius penalty is easily implemented via projected gradient descent on the skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) in the Cayley parameterisation.  The hyper‑parameter \(\epsilon\) essentially tunes the *flexibility* of the fine‑tuning: a large \(\epsilon\) approximates plain OFT and yields faster adaptation, whereas a small \(\epsilon\) keeps the model close to its pre‑trained state, trading off flexibility for stability.

Because the loss surfaces are smoother and the search space is bounded, practitioners can use fixed learning rates and epoch counts without extensive hyper‑parameter sweeps.  In the reported subject‑driven experiments, both OFT and COFT reached satisfactory performance after just 400 iterations, whereas standard DreamBooth or LoRA required 2,000 iterations before exhibiting signs of collapse.  The reduced **training data requirements** (e.g., 5 % of ADE20K still yielding competitive results) further emphasise the practical utility of the method.

### 11.5 The Role of Hyperspherical Energy as a Semantic Benchmark

A key insight underlying OFT is the **use of hyperspherical energy** as a structural metric.  Given a weight matrix \(\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\), the hyperspherical energy is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
where \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\) are the unit‑norm neurons.  This quantity captures the pair‑wise angular similarity among neurons; a smaller energy indicates more uniformly spread directions.  Crucially, the authors prove that applying an orthogonal transformation \(R\) to all neurons leaves \(\text{HE}\) invariant because cosine similarities are preserved under orthogonal rotations:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(R\bm{W}).
\]
Thus, maintaining the same hyperspherical energy ensures that the *semantic manifold* learned during pre‑training is **preserved** up to rotation.  Empirically, models tuned with OFT demonstrate higher DINO and CLIP‑I scores—a proxy for subject fidelity—than LoRA or DreamBooth.  These findings validate the hypothesis that preserving neuron angular relationships keeps the *semantic knowledge* intact.

Hyperspherical energy also serves as a **metric for model robustness**.  The constrained variant COFT explicitly penalises deviations in \(\|R-I\|\), thereby limiting the change in \(\text{HE}\) to the minimum possible.  In practice, this results in *more aggressive* but *safer* fine‑tuning: the network is allowed to rotate but cannot distort the angular structure that underpins the pre‑trained generative capacity.

Finally, the use of hyperspherical energy creates a principled objective that is **independent of the dimensionality** of the weight matrix.  Unlike Euclidean distance—which can be dominated by large‑scale shifts—energy focuses purely on angular relationships, making it a more robust indicator of semantic preservation across diverse architectures (from fully connected layers to convolutions and transformers).

---

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning offers a compelling trade‑off space:

| Aspect | OFT / COFT | LoRA | ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter |
|--------|------------|------|-------------------------|
| **Parameter budget** | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) via block‑diagonal | \(\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))\) | High (extra control subnetwork) |
| **Generalisation preservation** | Hyperspherical energy invariant | Limited by additive update | Depends on control module integration |
| **Inference overhead** | None (weights pre‑multiplied) | None (weights added) | Extra modules, more latency |
| **Convergence** | Smooth, stable, early plateau | Can explode or collapse | Sudden emergence, unstable |
| **Hyper‑parameter tuning** | Fixed LR/epochs, optional \(\epsilon\) | LR sensitive | More tunable (control scaling, modules) |
| **Semantic fidelity** | High (DINO/CLIP‑I) | Variable | Variable |

OFT and COFT thus navigate a nuanced intersection of structural preservation, computational economy, and practical deployability, making them highly suitable for fine‑tuning large text‑to‑image diffusion models for downstream tasks that demand both fidelity and controllability.
