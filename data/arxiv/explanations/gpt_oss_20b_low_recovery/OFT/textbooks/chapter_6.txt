\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 6: # Efficient Orthogonal Parameterisation

Large‑scale text‑to‑image diffusion models contain millions of parameters and operate on high‑dimensional tensors. When applying **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, each trainable layer is replaced by a *shared orthogonal matrix* that rotates the pretrained weights. While conceptually straightforward, naively implementing such orthogonal constraints on every layer can become computationally intractable. This chapter dissects the practical hurdles and presents a sequence of mathematical tricks that make OFT both **efficient** and **scalable**. We first show why the most obvious strategy—Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation—fails in practice (Section 5.1). We then introduce the **Cayley transform**, a parameter‑free mapping from a free skew‑symmetric matrix to an orthogonal matrix, and explain why it is a computational win (Section 5.2). The subtle restriction to the **special orthogonal group** (determinant \(+1\)) and its innocuous impact on performance are analysed (Section 5.3). Next, we describe a **block‑diagonal scheme** that reduces the number of parameters dramatically while keeping the transformation fully orthogonal (Section 5.4). We also explore an optional **block‑sharing** strategy, pushing the parameter count even lower at the cost of some flexibility (Section 5.5). Finally, we juxtapose OFT’s parameter‑efficiency with the low‑rank style of **LoRA** (Section 5.6), highlighting their complementary strengths.

---

## 5.1 Why Simple Gram–Schmidt Is Infeasible

The most direct way to enforce orthogonality is to perform Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation on a matrix’s columns (or rows). For a weight matrix \(\bm{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), one would explicitly normalise each column and subtract the projection onto the span of its predecessors. While mathematically exact, this approach suffers from several practical drawbacks:

1. **Complexity in Large‐Dimensional Settings**: In diffusion models, \(d\) (the neuron dimension) often scales to several thousand (e.g., the hidden dimension of a transformer block is typically \(d=1024\) or larger). Gram–Schmidt requires a quadratic pass over the matrix to compute projections, leading to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2n)\) operations per update. When every layer needs to be orthogonalised, the total training cost skyrockets.

2. **Numerical Instability**: Repeated orthogonalisation can cause drift due to finite‐precision arithmetic. When the matrix is updated frequently (as in stochastic gradient descent), floating‑point errors accumulate, potentially breaking the orthogonality constraint at the end of training.

3. **Gradient Flow Complications**: Implementing orthogonalisation inside the computational graph requires differentiating through the Gram–Schmidt process. While differentiable, this imposes extra overhead and can hinder GPU acceleration because of non‑amenable control flow.

Given these impediments, a more efficient *parameterisation* of orthogonal matrices is essential. We next describe such a parameterisation.

---

## 5.2 Cayley Transform and Its Computational Advantage

Instead of forcing orthogonality via constrained optimisation, we can *parameterise* an orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) with unconstrained variables that automatically satisfy \(\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}\). The **Cayley transform** offers a beautiful and efficient mapping from a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}\) to a special orthogonal matrix:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^{\top}.
\]

Key points:

- **Skew‑Symmetry** ensures that \(\bm{Q}\) has only \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) independent parameters, matching the dimension of the orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(d)\).

- **Explicit Formula** renders \(\bm{R}\) orthogonal by construction; we never need to normalise or re‑orthogonalise.

- **Computational Efficiency**: Computing \((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) is the only expensive operation. In practice, since \(\bm{Q}\) is small (high‑dimensional but sparse in terms of degrees of freedom) and often well‑conditioned, solving linear systems \((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\bm{x}=\bm{y}\) is cheap relative to standard neural network forward passes. Moreover, on GPUs, matrix inversion or solve can be batched and reused across layers.

- **Differentiable**: All operations are differentiable, so gradients propagate cleanly back through \(\bm{Q}\).

The Cayley transform thus provides a vehicle for introducing orthogonality without expensive projection steps. However, it inherently maps onto the **special orthogonal group** \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\), with determinant \(+1\). Next we investigate this limitation.

---

## 5.3 Limitations: Special Orthogonal Group (Determinant +1)

Definitionally, the Cayley transform only yields matrices with **determinant equal to +1**, i.e. rotations, but not reflections (which would have determinant \(-1\)). This raises the following questions:

- **Do Reflections Play a Critical Role?** In the context of finetuning diffusion models, empirical evidence shows that restricting to pure rotations does not hurt performance. The pre‑trained weights are already high‑dimensional random matrices; multiplying by a reflection is equivalent to a rotation followed by a coordinate flip—something that the model can absorb during training.

- **Is the Loss of Reflections a Drawback?** For many engineered networks, the sign pattern of weights can be crucial (e.g., for binary classification). However, in diffusion models that use **standardised activations** and **attention mechanisms**, the sign flips are effectively absorbed by downstream linear layers. Empirically, we observed *no* degradation when we constrained training to \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\).

- **Computational Simplicity**: Avoiding reflections simplifies the parameterisation: we remove the need for handling reflections explicitly or for normalising the determinant to \(\pm1\). Thus the Cayley transform’s computational advantage outweighs any theoretical loss of expressivity.

Hence, operating on the special orthogonal group is an acceptable compromise in this setting.

## 5.4 Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Formulation and Parameter Counting

Even with the Cayley transform, a full orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)\) requires \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) parameters—still a significant overhead for very high‑dimensional layers. A common trick to **control parameter size** is to restrict \(\bm{R}\) to a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\bm{R}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{R}_1 & & \\
 & \ddots & \\
 & & \bm{R}_r
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\bm{R}_i \in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right), \; i=1,\dots,r.
\]

- The entire matrix is **orthogonal** because each block is orthogonal, and blocks acting on disjoint subspaces do not interfere.

- **Parameter Count**: Each block \(\bm{R}_i\) has \(\frac{(d/r)((d/r)-1)}{2}\) free parameters. With \(r\) blocks, the total is
  \[
  r \times \frac{(d/r)\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}
  \;=\;
  \frac{d}{2}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)
  = \frac{d^2}{2r} - \frac{d}{2}.
  \]
  Thus, the complexity scales as \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^2}{r}\bigr)\), a quadratic reduction whenever \(r>1\).

- **Trade‑Off**: Increasing \(r\) (more blocks, i.e., smaller blocks) reduces parameters but also **tightens** the allowed rotations—each block only rotates a sub‑subspace. Conversely, fewer blocks give more expressive power at the expense of parameters.

- **Practical Settings**: A typical choice is to set \(r\) equal to the number of input channels or to a small constant (e.g., \(r=8\)). For a \(2048\times 2048\) layer, \(r=8\) reduces parameters from \(\sim 2\times10^6\) to \(\sim 2.5\times10^5\), a savings of 10×.

Each block can be parameterised independently with its own Cayley transform (i.e., each block has its own skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}_i\)). This modularity also simplifies debugging and allows per‑block learning rates.

---

## 5.5 Optional Block Sharing for Extreme Parameter Reduction

Beyond partitioning \(\bm{R}\) into independent blocks, we can **share** the same orthogonal matrix across all blocks:

\[
\bm{R}_1 = \bm{R}_2 = \dots = \bm{R}_r \;\triangleq\; \bm{R}_s.
\]

Now the entire orthogonal matrix is simply a **replicated** block \(\bm{R}_s\) applied to each subspace. The implications are:

- **Parameter Count**: Only one block is learned, so the number of trainable parameters becomes
  \[
  \frac{d}{2}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr),
  \]
  which is roughly \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\bigr)\) relative to a full matrix—an extra factor of \(r\) reduction compared to block‑diagonal independent blocks.

- **Expressivity Loss**: Sharing forces all groups of neurons to undergo the *same* rotation. This reduces the model's ability to fine‑tune distinct groups differently, potentially hurting tasks that require highly specialised transformations.

- **Practical Use‑Case**: In scenarios where parameter budgets are extremely tight (e.g., mobile deployment), sharing can be an acceptable trade‑off. In most diffusion finetuning experiments, a moderate number of blocks without sharing yields the best balance.

The choice between independent blocks and block sharing can be tuned via a validation set, as the optimal setting depends on the downstream task’s complexity.

---

## 5.6 Comparison with LoRA’s Low‑Rank Approach

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and LoRA share a common motivation: *reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving the pretrained knowledge*. However, their mechanisms differ fundamentally.

| Aspect | LoRA | OFT |
|--------|------|-----|
| **Parameterisation** | Adds a low‑rank update: \( \Delta \bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}^\top \), where \( \bm{A}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r'} \), \( \bm{B}\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r'} \). | Multiplies pretrained weights by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix: \( \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \). |
| **Parameter Count** | \( r'(d + n) \) (linear in \(d\) and \(n\)). | With \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r\): \( \frac{d^2}{2r} - \frac{d}{2} \) (quadratic in \(d\) but divided by \(r\)). |
| **Orthogonality** | No explicit orthogonality; the update is rank‑restricted, but the overall weight matrix can drift arbitrarily. | Transformation is always orthogonal, preserving pairwise angles between neurons. |
| **Effect on Hyperspherical Energy** | Uncontrolled: the low‑rank update can drastically modify neuron directions, leading to a *different* energy profile. | Explicitly preserves hyperspherical energy by the orthogonal constraint. |
| **Stability** | Sensitive to learning rate; can collapse if the rank \(r'\) is too small/large. | More robust: orthogonal updates maintain the manifold geometry, yielding smoother optimisation. |
| **Memory Footprint** | Low, as only \(r'\) is usually \(<5\%\) of the weight dimension. | Depends on block size; can be tuned to similar budget by increasing \(r\). |

In practice, *OFT* tends to exhibit **faster convergence** and **better preservation** of the pretrained model’s semantics, thanks to the geometric guarantees afforded by orthogonality. *LoRA*, meanwhile, shines when the downstream task can be represented by a small linear perturbation—an assumption that breaks down for more intricate control tasks.

---

**Take‑away**: Efficient orthogonal parameterisation is pivotal for making Orthogonal Finetuning viable in large text‑to‑image diffusion models. By replacing expensive orthogonalisation with a skew‑symmetric Cayley mapping, restricting to a block‑diagonal architecture, and optionally sharing blocks, we can drastically reduce trainable parameters while retaining the full expressivity needed to remodel complex generative networks. This careful balance of *mathematical structure* and *practical efficiency* underlies OFT’s empirical success across a range of downstream generation tasks.
