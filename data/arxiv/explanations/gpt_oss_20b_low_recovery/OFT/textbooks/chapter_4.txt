\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 4: # 4. General Finetuning Framework  

In the previous chapter we introduced the core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and the mathematical motivation for preserving the pairwise angular relations among neurons. In this chapter we will demystify how OFT is embedded into a standard training pipeline, how the orthogonal constraints are enforced, which network components are admissible, and finally how gradients propagate through the learning‑to‑rotate operation. The aim is to give the student a complete, step‑by‑step mental model of a workflow that can be replicated on any diffusion‑based text‑to‑image system.

## 4.1 Standard finetuning objective vs. OFT objective  

A conventional finetuning procedure can be formalised as minimizing a loss function \( \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}) \) over the weights \( \mathbf{W} \) of a pre‑trained network \(f_{\mathbf{W}^0}\). In practice, one may set a very small learning rate or apply a low‑rank update, such as in LoRA, to encourage the updated weights to remain close to \( \mathbf{W}^0 \). The optimisation can be written as  

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}) \quad \text{s.t.}\;\; \lVert \mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^0\rVert_{F} \leq \delta .
\]

This Euclidean proximity constraint is the only explicit regularisation in many “naïve’’ finetuning pipelines. OFT, in contrast, replaces the Euclidean distance with a *structural* constraint that preserves the *hyperspherical energy* of the weight matrix. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) ( \( n \) neurons of dimension \( d \) ), the hyperspherical energy is  

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\, \bigl\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\rVert^{-1},
\quad\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\lVert\mathbf{w}_{i}\rVert_2}.
\]

The OFT objective seeks to make the finetuned matrix \( \mathbf{W} \) have the same hyperspherical energy as the pre‑trained matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \):

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\; \bigl| \, \text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \, \bigr|
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\min_{\mathbf{R}} \;\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}-\mathbf{W}\rVert_{F},
\]

under the orthogonality constraint \( \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} \). Notice that if \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal, then
\( \text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})=\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \) by invariance of pairwise cosine similarity under orthogonal transforms. Therefore, it suffices to learn only the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) rather than the full weight matrix, yielding a very compact optimisation problem.  

In other words, **OFT moves the optimisation onto a smooth \(d\)-dimensional unit hypersphere**, preserving all inter‑neuron angles while allowing the network to adjust its representation to the downstream task.

## 4.2 Equation of OFT with layer‑shared orthogonal matrices  

Let us formalise the forward pass for a generic linear layer under OFT. Denote by \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) the frozen pre‑trained weights, by \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) the learnable orthogonal transform, and by \( \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} \) the input activation. The output of the layer is

\[
\mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\;\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Equation (4.1) states that the fouth column of \( \mathbf{W} \) is replaced by a linear combination of the original columns after rotating the column space by \( \mathbf{R} \). The transformation is *multiplicative* and *global*, i.e., the same \( \mathbf{R} \) is applied to every neuron in that layer. 

When multiple layers are finetuned, each layer \( \ell \) receives its own orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)} \). We therefore write a layer‑shared notation:

\[
\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{W}^{(\ell),0}, 
\quad
\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}= \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(\ell-1)}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The constraint \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{I} \) is enforced *explicitly* during training by restricting the parameterisation of each \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)} \) (see §4.5).  

OFT is *fully compatible* with any linear operator that can be expressed as a matrix multiplication, including:
- classic fully‑connected layers (used in the cross‑attention, feed‑forward, and time‑embedding modules of a diffusion UNet);
- attention weight matrices (query–key‑value linear projections);
- depth‑wise convolution kernels (when unfolded into a linear map over the channel dimension).

The only modification is the introduction of a single extra matrix multiplication by \( \mathbf{R} \) before the usual matrix multiplication by the weight tensor.

## 4.3 How to initialise the orthogonal matrix (identity)  

Because orthogonal matrices form a *Lie group* (the orthogonal group \( O(d) \)), the natural “zero” element is the identity matrix \( \mathbf{I}_d \). Setting \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_d \) makes the finetuned network identical to the pre‑trained network at the start of training:

\[
\mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{I}_d \mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x} 
            = \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

This initialization guarantees that the training loss is initially the same as the pre‑trained loss, and that the gradient will initially point in a direction that respects the orthogonality constraint. 

Practically, the identity matrix is represented implicitly by a parameter‑efficient *skew‑symmetric* matrix \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) (see §4.5). One sets \( \mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0} \) at the start, and the Cayley transform
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
\]
then yields \( \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\). This parameterisation assures that the optimisation trajectory stays on the orthogonal manifold starting from the identity.

## 4.4 Scope of application: fully‑connected layers, attention weights, optionally convolutional layers  

The **orthogonal construction** can be applied wherever a weight matrix is multiplied on the left or right of an activation vector. 

### 4.4.1 Fully‑connected layers  
In a standard dense layer, neurons are columns of \( \mathbf{W} \). By multiplying a common orthogonal matrix from the left, every neuron is rotated in *feature space*, preserving pairwise angles. Since all neuron vectors lie on the same sphere, their uniform distribution on that sphere is unchanged.

### 4.4.2 Attention weight matrices  
Each self‑attention module features three linear projections  
\( \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h},\;
   \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h},\;
   \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{h} \).
OFT can be applied independently to each of \( \mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{W}_v \). The usual practice of finetuning only a few of these weight matrices (e.g. only \( \mathbf{W}_q \)) still benefits from OFT’s stable manifold, because the orthogonal transform is applied globally over all attention heads.

### 4.4.3 Convolutional layers (optional)  
Convolutions can be reshaped into a matrix multiplication over flattened feature maps. However, when the weight tensor has multiple input channels, a *block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrix can be applied where each block operates on a single channel. This is effectively a learned depth‑wise rotation: each channel undergoes a rotation independent of the others. Sharing the same block across channels can also be done, yielding a single rotation that acts on every spatial location identically. While this is not the default choice in OFT, it is a natural extension that preserves the same pairwise angular relations across channels.

In practice, we recommend starting with the *attention‑weight‑only* finetuning strategy, which requires only a handful of extra parameters (on the order of \( 10^5 \) for a full Stable Diffusion model) and already delivers strong results for both subject‑driven and controllable generation.

## 4.5 Back‑propagation and gradient flows through the orthogonal matrix  

Because the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) is not an arbitrary real matrix but a *constrained* one, gradients must be projected onto the tangent space of \( O(d) \) before updating the parameters. This guarantees that \( \mathbf{R} \) stays on the manifold throughout training.

### 4.5.1 Cayley parameterisation  
We parameterise \( \mathbf{R} \) via a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top} \):

\[
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{Q}) = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

This mapping is smooth and differentiable everywhere except where \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) is singular. For the small values of \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) that we optimise in practice, the inverse is well‑conditioned. The gradient of the loss \( \mathcal{L} \) with respect to \( \mathbf{Q} \) is computed via the chain rule:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{R}}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{R}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}.
\]

The Jacobian \( \partial \mathbf{R}/\partial \mathbf{Q} \) can be derived analytically or numerically using automatic differentiation. Since \( \mathbf{Q} \) is constrained to be skew‑symmetric, we project the full \( d\times d \) gradient onto the skew‑symmetric subspace:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
\;\gets\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
          - \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(
             \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
           + \bigl(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}\bigr)^{\!\top}\Bigr).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Equation (4.4) enforces the skew‑symmetry of \( \mathbf{Q} \) *after* back‑propagation.

### 4.5.2 Block‑diagonal structure  
When a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix is used, each block \( \mathbf{R}_b \in O(d_b) \) is parameterised independently via its own skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q}_b \). The same equations (4.3)–(4.4) apply to each block; the gradients are simply stitched together into a sparse block‑diagonal matrix. This structure reduces the number of learnable parameters from \( O(d^2) \) to \( O(d^2 / r) \) where \( r \) is the number of blocks, while maintaining the exact orthogonality of the full \( \mathbf{R} \).

### 4.5.3 Implicit gradients in the forward pass  
During the forward pass the weight matrix used in a linear layer is \( \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\). The gradient with respect to \( \mathbf{W} \) is obtained via the usual back‑propagation through the underlying linear operation. Then the gradient is back‑propagated *through* the multiplication by \( \mathbf{R}\):

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{R}}
  = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}.
\]

This derivative is then folded into the chain rule with (4.3) to produce the update to \( \mathbf{Q} \).

The beauty of this scheme is that the *only* trainable parameters live in \( \mathbf{Q} \), and all other gradients are absorbed implicitly. Consequently, the cost of back‑propagation is almost identical to that of a standard trainable weight matrix, yet the optimisation is much more stable because the parameter set is constrained to a smooth manifold.

## 4.6 Summary of the training pipeline  

The full OFT finetuning process for a text‑to‑image diffusion model proceeds as follows:

1. **Select target layers**: choose attention weight matrices (or other linear operators) to be finetuned.  
2. **Introduce an orthogonal block** \(\mathbf{R}\) for each chosen layer, initialise \(\mathbf{Q}=0\) (hence \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)).  
3. **Forward pass**: for each linear operator, replace the pre‑trained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
4. **Compute loss**: use the same downstream loss as the baseline method (e.g. reconstruction + prior preservation for DreamBooth).  
5. **Backward pass**: compute gradients w.r.t. \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\), back‑propagate through \(\mathbf{R}\) using (4.3) and (4.4).  
6. **Optimise**: update \(\mathbf{Q}\) with a standard optimiser (Adam, SGD, …).  
7. **Optional constraints**: if Constrained OFT (COFT) is used, clip \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) to stay within the acceptable radius \( \epsilon \).  

The entire pipeline is a drop‑in replacement for any method that finetunes a diffusion model, yet it incorporates a principled geometric constraint that dramatically improves stability and performance.  

In the following chapter we will present empirical results that confirm the theoretical advantages described above, showing that OFT converges faster, requires fewer training images, and produces images with higher semantic fidelity compared to existing finetuning strategies.
