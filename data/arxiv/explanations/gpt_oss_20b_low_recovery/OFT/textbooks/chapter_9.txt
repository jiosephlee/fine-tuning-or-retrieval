\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 9: # 9 Experimental Evaluation – Subject‑Driven Generation

In this chapter we present a comprehensive experimental study of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on the task of subject‑driven image generation.  We compare these methods to two widely‑used baselines: DreamBooth, which employs a lightweight fine‑tuning of the diffusion model through reconstruction loss and a class‑specific prior‑preservation regularizer, and LoRA, which augments the model with low‑rank weight adapters.  Our goal is to demonstrate that OFT/COFT can deliver faster convergence, greater stability, higher fidelity of the subject, and better sample diversity, while training only a modest number of additional parameters.

> **Key take‑aways**  
> • OFT/COFT converge quickly (within a few hundred iterations) and remain stable even after thousands of updates.  
> • Both approaches preserve the intrinsic semantic structure of the pre‑trained diffusion model, as shown by the preservation of hyperspherical energy.  
> • Quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, LPIPS) confirm that OFT/COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity and diversity.  
> • Human studies corroborate the quantitative findings, with participants favoring OFT/COFT generated images.  

Below, we describe the experimental protocol, elaborate on the quantitative evaluation, present the results in tables and figures, and discuss additional ablation experiments that probe key design choices of OFT/COFT.

---

## 8.1 Experimental setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5, dataset, training curves

### Pre‑trained model

All experiments are conducted on the publicly available Stable Diffusion v1.5 model, which consists of a U‑Net backbone trained on a 2.1 M‑image subset of LAION‑400M.  We use the officially released weights without modification, leveraging the text encoder (CLIP‑ViT‑B/32) and the diffusion scheduler (DDIM) as supplied in the open‑source repo.

### Subject‑driven dataset

We adopt the same subject dataset used in the DreamBooth baseline: a set of 30 real‑world subjects, each represented by 5–20 high‑resolution photographs.  The subjects span diverse categories (people, pets, objects), capturing a broad range of poses, lighting, and background conditions.  For each subject we reserve 10 images for training and the remaining images for evaluation.  Training examples are constructed by encoding the subject images into the diffusion model’s latent space and pairing them with a subject-specific prompt that includes a unique token (e.g., “\<dog\>”), as described in the DreamBooth paper.

### Fine‑tuning protocol

- **Learning rate**: All methods use a cosine‑annealed learning rate starting at $2\times10^{-4}$, decayed to $1\times10^{-6}$ over the course of training.  
- **Batch size**: 2 latent samples per GPU (effective batch size 8 on a 4‑GPU cluster).  
- **Optimiser**: AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$.  
- **Epochs**: We evaluate training curves up to 4000 iterations (≈ 20 epochs on the full 10‑image training set).  

For OFT/COFT we initialise the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as the identity matrix and then learn a block‑diagonal orthogonal transformation shared across all U‑Net layers (except the first convolution).  For COFT we set $\epsilon=0.01$ unless otherwise noted.  DreamBooth adopts the published settings: a reconstruction loss weight of 1.0 and a prior‑preservation weight of 0.05.  LoRA follows the recommended rank‑8 configuration, resulting in ~ 400 K trainable parameters.

Figure 9.1 displays the training loss curves for all four methods.  OFT and COFT exhibit a rapid drop in loss within the first few hundred iterations, whereas DreamBooth and LoRA need several thousand updates to reach a similar loss plateau.  Notably, after around 3000 iterations, the loss of DreamBooth starts to oscillate, hinting at instability; this instability is absent in OFT/COFT.

## 8.2 Finetuning stability and convergence speed

We assess stability by monitoring the quality of a fixed set of test images generated at each training epoch.  Specifically, after every 100 iterations we synthesize five images per subject using the same prompt and compare them to the target images with a simple parsimony metric: the cosine similarity between CLIP‑I embeddings.  The resulting curves (Figure 9.2) reveal that:

- **OFT/COFT** reach cosine similarity > 0.75 in under 100 iterations and maintain plateaued similarity thereafter.  
- **DreamBooth** attains a comparable similarity only after ~ 2500 iterations, and later deteriorates due to **model collapse** (images become overly stylised or lose subject identity).  
- **LoRA** performs best between 500–2000 iterations but then drops dramatically, indicating a fragile balance between adapting to the subject and preserving prior knowledge.

Parallel qualitative inspection (Figure 9.3) confirms the numerical trends.  DreamBooth images start corrupting the subject’s outline whereas OFT/COFT faithfully preserve facial features and object contours even after 4000 iterations.

## 8.3 Quantitative metrics: DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, LPIPS

To quantify subject fidelity, prompt fidelity and diversity, we adopt the same suite of metrics used in the DreamBooth benchmark:

| Metric | Meaning | Computation |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **DINO** | Average pairwise cosine similarity of DINO‑ViT‑S/16 embeddings between generated images and real subject images. | $\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i<j}\cos(\mathbf{d}_i,\mathbf{d}_j)$ |
| **CLIP‑I** | Average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP‑ViT‑B/32 image embeddings between generated images and real subject images. | Similar to DINO but using CLIP embeddings. |
| **CLIP‑T** | Average cosine similarity between CLIP image embeddings of generated images and CLIP text embeddings of the prompt. | $\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\cos(\mathbf{c}_m,\mathbf{t})$ |
| **LPIPS** | Average perceptual similarity between generated images of the same subject under the same prompt; lower values imply higher diversity. | Mean L2 distance in LPIPS space. |

For each subject we generate **100** images at the final iteration and compute these metrics.  Table 9.1 reports the mean ± std over all subjects.

| Method | DINO | CLIP‑I | CLIP‑T | LPIPS |
|--------|------|--------|--------|-------|
| DreamBooth | 0.842 ± 0.015 | 0.792 ± 0.020 | 0.615 ± 0.030 | 0.428 ± 0.012 |
| LoRA | 0.813 ± 0.022 | 0.762 ± 0.025 | 0.578 ± 0.035 | 0.451 ± 0.015 |
| OFT | 0.882 ± 0.013 | 0.811 ± 0.018 | 0.599 ± 0.025 | 0.415 ± 0.010 |
| COFT | 0.885 ± 0.012 | 0.814 ± 0.017 | 0.602 ± 0.024 | 0.412 ± 0.009 |

Observations:

1. **Subject fidelity** (DINO, CLIP‑I) is consistently higher for OFT/COFT, indicating better preservation of the subject’s visual identity.  
2. **Prompt fidelity** (CLIP‑T) shows no significant difference, suggesting that all methods equally honour the textual prompt.  
3. **Diversity** (LPIPS) is lowest for OFT/COFT, meaning the generated images exhibit finer variation while remaining faithful to the subject.

## 8.4 Comparison tables and discussion

### Parameter count and computational overhead

| Method | Trainable Params | Inference Overhead |
|--------|------------------|--------------------|
| DreamBooth | ~ 42 M (full model fine‑tuning) | None (full‑weight update) |
| LoRA | 400 K | None (low‑rank adapters) |
| OFT | 960 K (block‑diagonal $r=8$) | None (matrix multiplication at inference) |
| COFT | 1.02 M (same as OFT + $\epsilon$ norm) | None |

OFT/COFT add only a few megabytes to the model, yet achieve superior results.  Importantly, both methods incur **no runtime overhead** during inference: the learned orthogonal matrix is multiplied once to the pre‑trained weight and the resulting weight matrix can be cached.

### Sample efficiency

We performed a step‑wise ablation of training data: we trained OFT/COFT on 5 % and 10 % of the ADE20K dataset for the segmentation‑to‑image task.  Figure 9.4 shows that OFT/COFT converge to comparable performance with only 5 % data, illustrating highly sample‑efficient adaptation.

## 8.5 Human evaluation study

To complement the objective metrics, we ran a human preference survey using 10 participants who are familiar with text‑to‑image generation.  Each participant was shown a random pair of images per subject: one generated by DreamBooth, one by OFT (or COFT), without knowing the source.  Participants selected the image that best preserved the subject while responding to the textual prompt.  

**Results** (Figure 9.5):  
- 78 % of participants preferred OFT over DreamBooth.  
- 85 % preferred COFT (a slight advantage over OFT).  
- 12 % found them indistinguishable.

The high preference rates confirm that the quantitative advantages translate to perceptual quality.

## 8.6 Ablation studies: block size, ε, number of trainable parameters

### Impact of block size

We varied the number of blocks $r$ in the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix:

| $r$ | Trainable Params | DINO | LPIPS |
|-----|------------------|------|-------|
| 1 (full orthogonal) | 6.41 M | 0.862 | 0.437 |
| 4 | 1.61 M | 0.877 | 0.424 |
| 8 | 0.96 M | 0.882 | 0.415 |
| 16 | 0.62 M | 0.875 | 0.422 |

Increasing $r$ reduces parameter count but slightly harms subject fidelity, indicating that more expressive orthogonal transformations better preserve semantics.

### Effect of $\epsilon$ in COFT

We swept $\epsilon$ from 0.001 to 0.05:

| $\epsilon$ | DINO | CLIP‑I | LPIPS |
|------------|------|--------|-------|
| 0.001 | 0.877 | 0.805 | 0.423 |
| 0.01 | 0.885 | 0.814 | 0.412 |
| 0.05 | 0.871 | 0.798 | 0.425 |

A small $\epsilon$ yields stronger stability (lower variance), while too large values degrade fidelity.

### Number of trainable parameters

We plotted Subject-Fidelity vs. Params in Figure 9.6.  OFT/COFT trace a steep curve: doubling parameters yields a modest increase in fidelity, whereas DreamBooth’s curve saturates quickly.  This confirms that OFT/COFT achieve a better trade‑off between model capacity and preservation.

---

### Summary

Across all evaluations—training curves, quantitative metrics, human judgements, and ablations—OFT and COFT consistently outperform the baseline methods.  Their orthogonal transformation framework preserves the angular relationships that encode semantic knowledge while allowing efficient downstream adaptation.  These findings establish OFT/COFT as a compelling approach for subject‑driven image generation in large diffusion models.
