\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Theory

Large diffusion models learn a rich internal representation of visual concepts and language. Yet, when one seeks to specialize such a model—for example, to keep a particular subject’s identity in a new context or to enforce a structural constraint like an edge map—naïve weight updates quickly erode the pretrained knowledge. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposes a principled way to modify a pre‑trained diffusion model while guaranteeing that two key properties are retained: the *semantic content* encoded by the angles between neurons, and the *numerical proximity* to the original weights. The following exposition explains why neuron orientations are the primary carriers of semantic information, demonstrates the effect of perturbing only the direction of neurons, and derives the theoretical guarantee that orthogonal transformations preserve the so‑called *hyperspherical energy*—a scalar that quantifies pairwise angular relationships.

---

## 2.1 The role of neuron direction in semantic encoding

Every layer in a neural network can be viewed as a linear map followed by an activation. Consider a fully connected layer \( \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\); its columns \( \mathbf{w}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{w}_n\in\mathbb{R}^d\) are the **neurons**. When a data vector \( \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) passes through this layer, the i‑th pre‑activation is \( z_i = \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}\). If we normalise each neuron, \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i/\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\), the pre‑activation can be written as
\[
z_i = \lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\,(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}).
\]
Thus the **magnitude** \(\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\) scales the contribution of that neuron, whereas the **direction** \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\) determines how that contribution aligns with the inputs. Empirical studies of deep networks suggest that *direction*—the vertex of the unit sphere on which the neuron lies—carries the bulk of semantic meaning. This intuition is formalised in SphereNet, where normalising all neurons to the unit sphere does not impede but even improves generalisation. In such a setting, two neurons are said to align if the angle between them is small (high cosine similarity) and to repel if the angle is large. The ensemble of angles encodes a geometric structure that reflects the relationships between features represented across the network. Consequently, perturbations to the direction of the neurons can significantly shift the semantic output of the network, while magnitude changes have a more limited effect.

---

## 2.2 Toy experiments illustrating angular importance

To make the above intuition concrete, the authors conducted a simple autoencoder experiment on a small flower dataset. The encoder (\(\mathbf{W}\)) maps a \(4\times4\) input patch \(\mathbf{x}\) to a 10‑dimensional latent vector. Two decoding schemes were evaluated:

1. **Inner product decoding** – reconstructs the image using the same weight \( \mathbf{W}\) as training,
2. **Magnitude decoding** – uses the magnitudes \(\lVert\mathbf{w}_i\rVert\) (discarding direction),
3. **Angle decoding** – uses only the directions \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\).

The output image reconstructed from the *angles* was almost indistinguishable from the original; the *magnitudes* produced a meaningless chunk of grey. This experiment demonstrates that, in a realistic network, the directional component of each neuron is the primary reservoir of visual information. Because the encoder’s weights were learned with the standard inner‑product, the training objective did not explicitly constrain magnitudes, yet post‑hoc reconstruction based purely on angles preserved the image. This behaviour generalises to diffusion models, where each denoising step involves linear transformations followed by non‑linearities: if the angles shift, the denoising trajectory changes dramatically, thus altering the final image.

## 2.3 Orthogonal transformation as the simplest rotational finetuning

Suppose we wish to *rotate* all neurons of a layer simultaneously so that their angles with each other remain the same. Let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\)). Multiplying all neurons by \(\mathbf{R}\) yields new neurons \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\). Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products,
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j
= \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j
= \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j,
\]
the *cosine similarity* (i.e. the angle) between any pair of neurons remains unchanged. Therefore, an orthogonal matrix can re‑express a layer in a different coordinate basis while leaving the entire *pairwise geometry* intact. This is exactly the property we desire for a finetuning strategy: we want to move the weight vector in a way that keeps the semantic relationships (angles) constant.

In contrast, arbitrary weight updates may distort these angles, creating an unpredictable cascade of semantic drift. The orthogonal approach also guarantees **numerical stability**: small orthogonal perturbations keep the layer’s spectrum well‑behaved, preventing exploding or vanishing activations. Consequently, orthogonal finetuning emerges as a minimalist yet powerful way to modify a diffusion model at the neuron level.

## 2.4 Formal proof: global minimisation of hyperspherical energy via orthogonal maps

Let \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) be a pretrained weight matrix and \(\mathbf{W}\) the finetuned one. Consider the *hyperspherical energy*,
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) 
= \sum_{i\neq j}\lVert\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\rVert^{-1},
\]
where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i/\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\). This scalar sums the inverse distances between all neuron pairs on the unit hypersphere, effectively measuring how *uniform* the angles are; a lower energy indicates a more spread‑out configuration.

**Lemma**: For any orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\), \(\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)=\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\).

*Proof*: Since \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves inner products, the dot product \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\) is invariant. Consequently, the Euclidean distance \(\lVert\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\rVert\), which depends only on this dot product, is unchanged. Therefore each summand in \(\operatorname{HE}\) remains identical, and so does the sum. ∎

Now we can pose the finetuning objective
\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\bigl\lvert \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\bigr\rvert.
\]
The value zero is achievable *iff* \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) for some orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Hence every orthogonal transform of the pretrained layer satisfies the optimality condition. This proves that orthogonal finetuning **globally minimises** the hyperspherical energy difference, i.e., it preserves the full pairwise relation structure of the pretrained neurons.

## 2.5 Connection to 2‑D Fourier phase and image semantics

An instructive analogy comes from the two‑dimensional Fourier transform. Any real image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum (the absolute values of Fourier coefficients) and a phase spectrum (the angles of those complex coefficients). Empirical studies have shown that the phase spectrum alone suffices to reconstruct the image’s structure and semantics; altering the magnitude spectrum while preserving the phase leads to a visually coherent image. In our setting, the *direction* of a neuron (its angle on the hypersphere) plays a role analogous to *phase*: it encodes the high‑level semantic configuration. Conversely, the magnitude behaves like the Fourier *magnitude*: changing it without affecting direction (or phase) does not disturb the overall shape or meaning. This analogy further justifies focusing on angular adjustments when finetuning.

---

## 2.6 Why preserving angles safeguards pre‑training knowledge

Suppose we finetune a diffusion model without any constraints. The optimizer may find a set of weights that achieves the downstream task but at the cost of drastically altering the neural geometry—neurons may cluster together, repel excessively, or become orthogonal to the input subspace. Such a re‑organisation can undermine the delicate balance that the pretrained model established to match the joint text–image distribution. Consequently, the model may lose the ability to generate diverse, high‑fidelity images.

Orthogonal finetuning sidesteps this danger because it restricts the adaptation to a *pure rotation* (or reflection) of the weight space. Every pairwise cosine similarity stays exactly as in the original model. Hence the *semantic manifold* that the network has learned—encoded by the angles—remains intact. Moreover, the rank‑preserving nature of orthogonal matrices guarantees that the expressive capacity of the layer is unchanged. The remaining degrees of freedom—choosing which rotation to apply—allow the model to align better with the new task (e.g., matching a few subject images or adhering to an edge mask) while keeping the pre‑training knowledge live and usable.

In practice, this stability manifests as faster convergence, fewer collapsed samples, and the ability to finish training with a modest number of iterations and data samples. Thus, the *preservation of angles* is central to the success of orthogonal finetuning.
