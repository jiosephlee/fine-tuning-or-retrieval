\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does an orthogonal transformation in OFT avoid destroying pre‑training knowledge?
Question:
Equation (5) states that applying an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to the pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\) preserves the hyperspherical energy because \(\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \widehat{\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}}_i\). Intuitively, I see that rotations preserve dot‑products, but why is that sufficient to guarantee that the model’s pre‑trained representation power is retained? Is there a formal argument that shows the distance between the transformed weights and the original weights is solely due to rotation, and why this does not impact the diffusion process?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Orthogonal finetuning only rotates (or reflects) every neuron inside a layer.  
Because a rotation keeps all pairwise dot‑products (hence all angles) unchanged, the *hyperspherical energy* – the sum of reciprocals of Euclidean distances between the *unit* neurons – stays exactly the same as in the frozen model.  In other words, the whole “inter‑neuron geometry” of the layer is preserved.  The representation power of the layer is therefore not altered, apart from a rigid re‑labelling of the coordinate system.  The Euclidean distance from the old weights to the new ones is simply the amount of rotation applied, but this does **not** change any of the dot‑products that the diffusion process uses, so the diffusion forward dynamics stay on the same hypersphere trajectory and the diffusion probability (which depends only on the dot‑products) is untouched.

Below is a more detailed, formal walk‑through that ties the geometry of orthogonal matrices to the objectives of OFT.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Orthogonal matrices preserve neuron angles  

Let  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}=[\mathbf{w}_1^0,\dots ,\mathbf{w}_n^0]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\;\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}_d .
\]

Define the finetuned matrix as  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}
      =[\mathbf{w}_1,\dots ,\mathbf{w}_n],\qquad
\mathbf{w}_i=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i .
\]

For any two neurons we have  

\[
\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{w}_j
   =(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_i)^\top(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_j)
   =\mathbf{w}^{0\top}_i\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}^0_j
   =\mathbf{w}^{0\top}_i\mathbf{w}^0_j .
\]

Thus every inner product – and consequently every cosine similarity and Euclidean
distance between the **unit‑norm** neurons – is identical to the frozen model:

\[
\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{w}_j}
     {\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\|}
 =\frac{\mathbf{w}_i^{0\top}\mathbf{w}_j^0}
       {\|\mathbf{w}_i^0\|\,\|\mathbf{w}_j^0\|}.
\]

Because the *hyperspherical energy* is defined as  

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})
   =\sum_{i\neq j}\!\Big\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\Big\|^{-1},
   \qquad
   \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i=\frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},
\]

it is entirely determined by these pairwise distances.  
Hence

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W})=\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}),
\]

exactly the invariance stated in Eq. (5) of the paper.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Why preserving hyperspherical energy guarantees semantic stability  

Diffusion models process an internal state \(\mathbf{x}\) with linear layers of the form \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{x}\).
The output of a layer, and therefore the entire forward pass, depends only on the *direction* of the neuron weights, not on their magnitude (the magnitude is usually absorbed by the subsequent layer or normalisation).  
Because the angles between neurons are the same after an orthogonal transformation, the *function* implemented by the layer is an isometry: the mapping \(\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{x}\) is rotated rigidly in the space of hidden activations.  
Consequently the diffusion transition kernel (which is constructed from these activations) is unchanged, except for a global change of basis.  In a probabilistic sense the law of the diffusion process, which depends on dot–products of the activations, remains identical.

If one imagines the layer’s weight vectors as points on the surface of the unit hypersphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\), then rotation simply re‑labels those points while keeping all pairwise spherical distances intact.  From an optimisation view, orthogonal finite‑tuning moves the model **exactly on the manifold** of all weight matrices that realise the same hyperspherical geometry.  There is no distortion of the “semantic field” that the original model had learned; only the coordinate axes used to express that field are rotated.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Distance between transformed and original weights

It is sometimes useful to quantify how far a rotated matrix lies from the original one.  
If \(\mathbf{R}\) is close to \(\mathbf{I}\), one can expand

\[
\|\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}
   =\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}-\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}
   =\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}
   \;\le\;\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{2}\;\|\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{F}.
\]

Thus the Frobenius distance is bounded by the operator norm of the rotation matrix.  In OFT the learning rate applied to \(\mathbf{Q}\) (the Cayley‑parameterised skew‑symmetric matrix) controls how large \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\) can become, and COFT explicitly caps it with the hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\).  This guarantees that the weights do not stray far from their initial values, while still allowing a *global* angular re‑orientation that keeps the hyperspherical energy intact.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Why the diffusion process is untouched

During a diffusion step the denoising network computes

\[
\mathbf{h}_{t+1} = \mathbf{h}_{t}
                    + f_{\text{diff}}( \mathbf{h}_{t} ; \theta ),
\]

where \(f_{\text{diff}}\) is a composition of linear layers, nonlinearities and attention.  
Each linear layer only depends on inner products between its weight vectors and the current activation.  Because both the weight vectors and the activations are simultaneously rotated (the activations are rotated by the prior linear layer), the *relative* geometry of the entire network remains the same.  In other words, the diffusion score at every timestep – which ultimately determines the probability density of the sampled image – is **invariant under a global orthogonal transformation of the weights**.  

Hence the diffusion dynamics are untouched; only the *coordinate system* inside each layer is changed.  The network therefore retains its original generative capacity, and any fine‑tuning will only affect how the newly introduced task (e.g., subject edition or edge control) is aligned with this existing semantic manifold.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Take‑away

- **Orthogonal finetuning rotates every neuron in a layer.**  
  Rotation keeps all pairwise dot‑products exactly the same.
- **Hyperspherical energy depends only on those dot‑products.**  
  Therefore the energy is preserved *exactly*, fulfilling Eq. (5).
- **The diffusion model’s forward dynamics are a function of the inner
  products, not of the absolute weight vectors.**  
  A global rotation therefore produces an *equivalent* diffusion model,
  merely expressed in a different basis.
- **Constrained OFT (COFT) guarantees the rotation is small,
  limiting how far the new weights can deviate from the original ones
  while still preserving all angles.**

In short, OFT’s orthogonal transformation preserves the full relational
geometry of each layer: the model keeps the same “semantic map” it learnt
during pre‑training, and the diffusion process itself is left entirely
intact.  Only the coordinate system inside each frozen layer is re‑oriented,
which allows the added task to be learned efficiently without sacrificing
the pretrained generative quality.
