# Chapter 8  
**Text‑to‑Image Diffusion and the Need for Targeted Finetuning**

---

## 0.1 What is Text‑to‑Image Diffusion?

Diffusion models belong to a family of generative frameworks that learn to reverse a stochastic noise–injection process. Conceptually, a forward diffusion process gradually corrupts an image \(x_0\) by adding Gaussian noise over \(T\) discrete steps, yielding
\[
x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon ,\qquad t = 1,\dots,T,
\]
where \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) is a cumulative product of noise‑variance schedules. The task of a generative diffusion model is to train a neural network \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) that predicts the underlying noise \(\epsilon\) from the noisy observation \(x_t\). Once trained, sampling proceeds in reverse: starting from pure noise \(x_T\), we iteratively apply the network to estimate \(\epsilon\) and denoise, arriving at a synthetic image \(x_0\).

Text‑to‑image diffusion augments this framework with a language prior. A pretrained text encoder, such as CLIP or a transformer‑based model, yields a latent representation \(h = f_{\text{text}}(c)\) for a textual prompt \(c\). The forward and backward passes of the diffusion network are conditioned on \(h\) via cross‑attention or FiLM‑style modulation, allowing the generation process to honor the semantic content of the prompt. The result is a powerful, flexible generative model that can produce diverse, high‑fidelity images from natural language.

They achieve near‑photorealistic generation for a wide range of prompts, yet the underlying texture and geometry are still largely dictated by the training data distribution and the implicit bias of the diffusion schedule.

## 0.3 Limitations of Pure Text Guidance

While text captions provide a flexible high‑level instruction, they carry several shortcomings when used alone:

1. **Ambiguity** – Natural language is inherently underspecified. A prompt like *“a dog in a park”* leaves the pose, breed, lighting, and background largely open to interpretation. The model inevitably oscillates around the most frequent or “safe” visual instantiations seen during pretraining.

2. **Lack of Fine‑Grained Control** – Users often wish to impose additional constraints: a particular pose, a specific color palette, or an outline. Text alone cannot encode such detailed specifications. Attempts to encode them as part of the prompt (“a dog with a red collar in a sunny park”) are brittle, failing to guarantee compliance.

3. **Identity Preservation Issues** – When generating an image of a known subject (e.g., *“my friend Sarah”*), the model may remix identity traits with other similar subjects, producing a fuzzy or approximate representation.

These limitations motivate specialized finetuning strategies that can inject more precise control signals while retaining the pretrained generative muscle.

---

## 0.4 The Two Downstream Generation Objectives Studied

The examined paper focuses on two representative downstream tasks that showcase the need for careful finetuning strategies:

1. **Subject‑Driven Generation** – Given a handful of photographs of a specific subject (person, animal, object), the goal is to synthesize new images of that subject in diverse contexts. This setting demands *identity preservation*: the generated image must retain the distinctive visual features of the subject while respecting the new textual prompt.

2. **Controllable Generation** – In addition to a text prompt, an *auxiliary control signal* (e.g., Canny edges, semantic segmentation map, facial landmarks) is supplied. The objective is to produce images that concurrently satisfy the textual description and faithfully adhere to the control signal, enabling applications like sketch‑to‑image or pose‑guided face synthesis.

Both tasks require *adaptation* of a huge, pretrained diffusion model. The challenge lies in preserving the robust, generalizable generative capability of the base model while imparting the new, narrow ability.

---

## 0.5 Desired Properties of a Finetuning Strategy

When confronting such adaptation problems, a practitioner ideally desires the following properties:

1. **Training Efficiency** – The finetuning should involve *few parameters* (ideally sub‑million) and *few epochs*. This reduces compute cost and mitigates overfitting on limited data.

2. **Generalizability Preservation** – The base model’s broad, high‑quality generative capacity must survive the finetuning. Loss of “semantic richness” manifests as blurry images, mode collapse, or incoherent textures.

3. **Controlled Flexibility** – The method must provide *enough freedom* to encode the new task but constrain the change so that the underlying manifold learned during pretraining remains intact.

4. **Inference Transparency** – Ideally, the finetuned model incurs *no inference overhead* compared to the base model. This means that at deployment time the finetuned weights can be merged into the pretrained network straightforwardly.

5. **Explicit Regularization** – A clear, *principled regularizer* that captures the essence of what needs to be preserved during finetuning enhances stability and interpretability.

The Orthogonal Finetuning approach (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) aim to satisfy all five properties simultaneously.

---

## 0.6 Overview of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Constrained OFT (COFT)

### Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

OFT takes advantage of a structural invariant in the geometry of neuron representations: **pairwise angular similarity**. The key observation is that if every neuron vector in a layer is multiplied by an *orthogonal matrix* \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\), then the cosine similarity between any two neurons remains unchanged. Consequently, the **hyperspherical energy**—the sum over all pairwise inverse distances of unit‑norm neurons—remains exactly identical to that of the pretrained weights.

Formally, for a fully‑connected layer with pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), OFT replaces \(\mathbf{W}^0\) by 
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0,
\]
where \(\mathbf{R}\) is a learnable, layer‑shared orthogonal matrix. The forward pass of the layer becomes
\[
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^\top \mathbf{x}.
\]
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, the hyperspherical energy of \(\mathbf{W}\) equals that of \(\mathbf{W}^0\), ensuring that the *semantic manifold* encoded by the neuron angles remains untouched. At the same time, \(\mathbf{R}\) introduces new linear combinations of the existing weights, providing sufficient flexibility to represent the downstream task.

### Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

While OFT preserves the pairwise angles, it allows arbitrary rotations that might drift too far from the pretrained weight space. COFT tightens this by adding a **radius constraint**: the orthogonal matrix is required to stay *uniformly close* to the identity:
\[
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|_F \leq \epsilon.
\]
Here \(\|\cdot\|_F\) denotes the Frobenius norm and \(\epsilon\) controls the maximal angular deviation. This added bound discourages drastic rotations, thereby *explicitly* limiting the amount of semantic shift and yielding better finetuning stability. In practice, \(\epsilon\) can be tuned to balance flexibility against robustness.

Both OFT and COFT enforce orthogonality via a **Cayley parameterization**: an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) is expressed as
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]
with \(\mathbf{Q}\) a skew‑symmetric matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\mathbf{Q}\)). This formulation guarantees exact orthogonality while remaining differentiable. For scalability, a **block‑diagonal** construction is adopted:
\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_r),
\]
where each \(\mathbf{R}_i \in O(d/r)\). This reduces the number of learnable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)\), still far fewer than the full weight update dimension.

Through these two mechanisms, OFT achieves a compelling trade‑off: fast, stable finetuning that preserves the pretrained diffusion model’s diversity and fidelity while enabling precise new capabilities such as subject introduction or control‑signal conditioning—without any runtime overhead.

# 2. Background Concepts

In order to understand the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) strategy introduced later, we first need a solid grounding in the mathematical and algorithmic building blocks that underlie it. This chapter surveys the geometry of hyperspheres, explains how the *hyperspherical energy* captures the relational structure of neuron weights, and reviews the essential properties of orthogonal matrices that preserve angles. We then provide a concise overview of the competitive finetuning paradigms — LoRA, adapters, DreamBooth, ControlNet, and T2I‑Adapter — that motivate the design of OFT, and we identify the key theoretical gaps that OFT aims to bridge.

---

## 1.1 Hyperspherical Embeddings and Cosine Similarity

When a weight vector $\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is normalized to unit length,
\[
\hat{\bm{w}} \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}}{\|\bm{w}\|}\,,
\]
it lies on the unit $(d-1)$‑dimensional hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\!:\; \{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d \mid \|\bm{x}\| = 1\}$.  Computing inner products on this sphere is equivalent to measuring **cosine similarity**:
\[
\cos\theta(\hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j) \;=\; \hat{\bm{w}}_i^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm{w}}_j
          \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_i^{\!\top}\bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\|\;\|\bm{w}_j\|}\,.
\]
Hence, the directional relationship between two weight vectors is fully captured by the angle $\theta$ between them.  Empirical studies in deep learning have shown that **cosine similarity** is a robust surrogate for semantic similarity: two neurons that fire in similar conditions tend to have small angles between them.  Consequently, many recent works [Liu et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020] have advocated normalizing weights or activations so that all neurons live on a hypersphere, thereby making the geometry of the network explicit.

---

## 1.2 Hyperspherical Energy: Definition, Intuition, and Meaning

Given a set of normalized neuron vectors
\[
\mathcal{W} \;=\; \{\hat{\bm{w}}_1,\hat{\bm{w}}_2,\dots,\hat{\bm{w}}_n\}\;\subseteq\;\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\!,
\]
the **hyperspherical energy** (HE) is defined as
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\mathcal{W}) \;\triangleq\;
  \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\i\neq j}}^{n}
  \frac{1}{\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|}\,.
\tag{2.1}
\]
Each term $1/\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is larger when two vectors are closer on the hypersphere (i.e., when their angle $\theta$ is small).  Thus, a high HE value indicates that many neurons are *crowded* into a small region of the sphere, suggesting redundancy or lack of diversity.  Conversely, a low HE spans the sphere more uniformly.

The intuition behind HE is that the *relational structure* among neurons – how they are spread over the unit sphere – is a compact descriptor of the network’s internal representation.  Differentiating a loss that penalizes changes in HE can help keep this structure intact during finetuning, thereby preserving the semantic content learned during pretraining.  Crucially, HE is invariant to any common **orthogonal** transformation applied to all neurons in $\mathcal{W}$, as we show in Section 1.3.

---

## 1.3 Orthogonal Matrices: Definition, Properties, and Role in Preserving Angles

An **orthogonal matrix** $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies
\[
\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R} \;=\; \bm{R}\bm{R}^{\top}\;=\;\bm{I}\,.
\tag{2.2}
\]
Geometrically, $\bm{R}$ represents a rotation or reflection of $\mathbb{R}^d$; it preserves inner products:
\[
(\bm{R}\!\bm{u})^{\!\top}\!(\bm{R}\!\bm{v}) 
   \;=\; \bm{u}^{\!\top}\bm{v}\,,\quad
\|\bm{R}\!\bm{u}\| \;=\;\|\bm{u}\|\!,
\]
and therefore preserves pairwise **angles** between any two vectors.  When applied to a set of neuron weights $\mathcal{W}$ via left-multiplication,
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}'_i \;=\; \frac{\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i}{\|\bm{R}\,\bm{w}_i\|}\,,
\]
the set $\mathcal{W}' = \{\hat{\bm{w}}'_i\}$ retains exactly the same cosines as the original $\mathcal{W}$, because
\[
\hat{\bm{w}}'_i^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm{w}}'_j
  \;=\;
  \frac{\bm{w}_i^{\!\top}\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{w}_j}
       {\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i\|\;\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_j\|}
  \;=\;
  \frac{\bm{w}_i^{\!\top}\bm{w}_j}
       {\|\bm{w}_i\|\;\|\bm{w}_j\|}
  \;=\;
  \hat{\bm{w}}_i^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm{w}}_j\,.
\]
Thus, **hyperspherical energy is invariant under orthogonal transformations**.  This property is the cornerstone of OFT: replacing each weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ preserves the pairwise angular structure, thereby preserving the **semantic representation** that the pre-trained diffusion model encodes.

---

## 1.4 Related Finetuning Techniques

| Method | Core Idea | Parameter‑efficiency | Implicit constraints |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|-----------------------|
| **DreamBooth** | Fine‑tune all weights of a pre‑trained Stable Diffusion on a small set of subject images. | None (full‑rank). | Small learning rate; class‑specific prior preservation loss. |
| **LoRA** | Adds a low‑rank adaptative update: $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^0 + \bm{L}\bm{R}$ with $\mathrm{rank}(\bm{L}\bm{R})=r'$. | $O(r'(d+n))$ trainable parameters. | Low‑rankness; keeps $\bm{W}$ close to $\bm{W}^0$ by virtue of small learning rate. |
| **ControlNet** | Adds an auxiliary network that receives control signals (edges, segmentation, etc.) and perturbs the diffusion prior. | New auxiliary network; many extra parameters. | No constraint on $\bm{W}$; training is performed on all network parameters. |
| **T2I‑Adapter** | Injects a lightweight adapter into pre‑trained diffusion layers. | Low‑rank/adaptive modules (similar to LoRA). | Often uses an additional linear transform; explicit constraints are minimal. |

All of these methods share a common goal: *adapting* a large diffusion model to a downstream task while **preserving** the quality of the general pre‑trained generative ability.  However, they differ in how they restrict the space of possible weight changes.  For example, **DreamBooth** depends heavily on a small learning rate and a prior‐preservation loss, which can be fragile when the number of training images is very small.  **LoRA** uses a low‑rank update that prevents large deviations in magnitude but does not control the *angular* structure of weights.  **ControlNet** and **T2I‑Adapter** introduce additional parameters that directly learn how to incorporate control signals, but these parameters are usually not constrained to maintain the *hyperspherical* relationships inside the base model.

---

## 1.5 Key Open Questions in Preserving Generative Semantic Performance

1. **How to quantify the *semantic* impact of weight changes?**  
   Euclidean distance between pre‑trained and fine‑tuned weights is easy to compute but ignores the directional semantics that dominate visual representations.  The hypothesis is that preserving *angles* between neurons is more meaningful.

2. **What structural constraints guarantee semantic preservation?**  
   Orthogonal transformations preserve angles, but are there other transformations that maintain hyperspherical energy?  CAN we design a minimal family of transformations that balances flexibility with stability?

3. **How to enforce constraints efficiently at training time?**  
   Orthogonality-constrained optimization introduces non‑convex constraints (e.g., $\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}$).  Efficient parameterizations (Cayley transform, block‑diagonal structures) are essential to keep training cost manageable.

4. **Can we combine *directional* preservation with *magnitude* control?**  
   Scaling neurons does not affect hyperspherical energy but can modulate the overall signal strength.  How to jointly learn both aspects without disrupting the learned semantics?

5. **What is the impact of constraint strength on downstream performance?**  
   Introducing a secondary radius constraint (as in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning) pushes the fine‑tuned model closer to the original parameters.  How does this trade‑off manifest in generation quality and convergence speed?

Answering these questions motivates the design of the **Orthogonal Finetuning** framework, which will be elaborated in subsequent chapters.  By focusing on preserving hyperspherical energy through orthogonal transformations, OFT offers a principled alternative to ad‑hoc finetuning strategies, enabling more stable and sample‑efficient adaptation of powerful text‑to‑image diffusion models.

# 3. Orthogonal Finetuning: Motivation and Theory

Large diffusion models learn a rich internal representation of visual concepts and language. Yet, when one seeks to specialize such a model—for example, to keep a particular subject’s identity in a new context or to enforce a structural constraint like an edge map—naïve weight updates quickly erode the pretrained knowledge. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposes a principled way to modify a pre‑trained diffusion model while guaranteeing that two key properties are retained: the *semantic content* encoded by the angles between neurons, and the *numerical proximity* to the original weights. The following exposition explains why neuron orientations are the primary carriers of semantic information, demonstrates the effect of perturbing only the direction of neurons, and derives the theoretical guarantee that orthogonal transformations preserve the so‑called *hyperspherical energy*—a scalar that quantifies pairwise angular relationships.

---

## 2.1 The role of neuron direction in semantic encoding

Every layer in a neural network can be viewed as a linear map followed by an activation. Consider a fully connected layer \( \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\); its columns \( \mathbf{w}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{w}_n\in\mathbb{R}^d\) are the **neurons**. When a data vector \( \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) passes through this layer, the i‑th pre‑activation is \( z_i = \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}\). If we normalise each neuron, \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i/\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\), the pre‑activation can be written as
\[
z_i = \lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\,(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\mathbf{x}).
\]
Thus the **magnitude** \(\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\) scales the contribution of that neuron, whereas the **direction** \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\) determines how that contribution aligns with the inputs. Empirical studies of deep networks suggest that *direction*—the vertex of the unit sphere on which the neuron lies—carries the bulk of semantic meaning. This intuition is formalised in SphereNet, where normalising all neurons to the unit sphere does not impede but even improves generalisation. In such a setting, two neurons are said to align if the angle between them is small (high cosine similarity) and to repel if the angle is large. The ensemble of angles encodes a geometric structure that reflects the relationships between features represented across the network. Consequently, perturbations to the direction of the neurons can significantly shift the semantic output of the network, while magnitude changes have a more limited effect.

---

## 2.2 Toy experiments illustrating angular importance

To make the above intuition concrete, the authors conducted a simple autoencoder experiment on a small flower dataset. The encoder (\(\mathbf{W}\)) maps a \(4\times4\) input patch \(\mathbf{x}\) to a 10‑dimensional latent vector. Two decoding schemes were evaluated:

1. **Inner product decoding** – reconstructs the image using the same weight \( \mathbf{W}\) as training,
2. **Magnitude decoding** – uses the magnitudes \(\lVert\mathbf{w}_i\rVert\) (discarding direction),
3. **Angle decoding** – uses only the directions \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\).

The output image reconstructed from the *angles* was almost indistinguishable from the original; the *magnitudes* produced a meaningless chunk of grey. This experiment demonstrates that, in a realistic network, the directional component of each neuron is the primary reservoir of visual information. Because the encoder’s weights were learned with the standard inner‑product, the training objective did not explicitly constrain magnitudes, yet post‑hoc reconstruction based purely on angles preserved the image. This behaviour generalises to diffusion models, where each denoising step involves linear transformations followed by non‑linearities: if the angles shift, the denoising trajectory changes dramatically, thus altering the final image. 

---

## 2.3 Orthogonal transformation as the simplest rotational finetuning

Suppose we wish to *rotate* all neurons of a layer simultaneously so that their angles with each other remain the same. Let \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be an orthogonal matrix (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\)). Multiplying all neurons by \(\mathbf{R}\) yields new neurons \(\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i\). Because orthogonal transformations preserve inner products,
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j
= \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j
= \mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j,
\]
the *cosine similarity* (i.e. the angle) between any pair of neurons remains unchanged. Therefore, an orthogonal matrix can re‑express a layer in a different coordinate basis while leaving the entire *pairwise geometry* intact. This is exactly the property we desire for a finetuning strategy: we want to move the weight vector in a way that keeps the semantic relationships (angles) constant.

In contrast, arbitrary weight updates may distort these angles, creating an unpredictable cascade of semantic drift. The orthogonal approach also guarantees **numerical stability**: small orthogonal perturbations keep the layer’s spectrum well‑behaved, preventing exploding or vanishing activations. Consequently, orthogonal finetuning emerges as a minimalist yet powerful way to modify a diffusion model at the neuron level.

---

## 2.4 Formal proof: global minimisation of hyperspherical energy via orthogonal maps

Let \(\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) be a pretrained weight matrix and \(\mathbf{W}\) the finetuned one. Consider the *hyperspherical energy*,
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) 
= \sum_{i\neq j}\lVert\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\rVert^{-1},
\]
where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i/\lVert \mathbf{w}_i\rVert\). This scalar sums the inverse distances between all neuron pairs on the unit hypersphere, effectively measuring how *uniform* the angles are; a lower energy indicates a more spread‑out configuration.

**Lemma**: For any orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\), \(\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)=\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\).

*Proof*: Since \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves inner products, the dot product \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\) is invariant. Consequently, the Euclidean distance \(\lVert\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\rVert\), which depends only on this dot product, is unchanged. Therefore each summand in \(\operatorname{HE}\) remains identical, and so does the sum. ∎

Now we can pose the finetuning objective
\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\bigl\lvert \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\bigr\rvert.
\]
The value zero is achievable *iff* \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) for some orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\). Hence every orthogonal transform of the pretrained layer satisfies the optimality condition. This proves that orthogonal finetuning **globally minimises** the hyperspherical energy difference, i.e., it preserves the full pairwise relation structure of the pretrained neurons.

---

## 2.5 Connection to 2‑D Fourier phase and image semantics

An instructive analogy comes from the two‑dimensional Fourier transform. Any real image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum (the absolute values of Fourier coefficients) and a phase spectrum (the angles of those complex coefficients). Empirical studies have shown that the phase spectrum alone suffices to reconstruct the image’s structure and semantics; altering the magnitude spectrum while preserving the phase leads to a visually coherent image. In our setting, the *direction* of a neuron (its angle on the hypersphere) plays a role analogous to *phase*: it encodes the high‑level semantic configuration. Conversely, the magnitude behaves like the Fourier *magnitude*: changing it without affecting direction (or phase) does not disturb the overall shape or meaning. This analogy further justifies focusing on angular adjustments when finetuning.

---

## 2.6 Why preserving angles safeguards pre‑training knowledge

Suppose we finetune a diffusion model without any constraints. The optimizer may find a set of weights that achieves the downstream task but at the cost of drastically altering the neural geometry—neurons may cluster together, repel excessively, or become orthogonal to the input subspace. Such a re‑organisation can undermine the delicate balance that the pretrained model established to match the joint text–image distribution. Consequently, the model may lose the ability to generate diverse, high‑fidelity images.

Orthogonal finetuning sidesteps this danger because it restricts the adaptation to a *pure rotation* (or reflection) of the weight space. Every pairwise cosine similarity stays exactly as in the original model. Hence the *semantic manifold* that the network has learned—encoded by the angles—remains intact. Moreover, the rank‑preserving nature of orthogonal matrices guarantees that the expressive capacity of the layer is unchanged. The remaining degrees of freedom—choosing which rotation to apply—allow the model to align better with the new task (e.g., matching a few subject images or adhering to an edge mask) while keeping the pre‑training knowledge live and usable.

In practice, this stability manifests as faster convergence, fewer collapsed samples, and the ability to finish training with a modest number of iterations and data samples. Thus, the *preservation of angles* is central to the success of orthogonal finetuning.

# 4. General Finetuning Framework  

In the previous chapter we introduced the core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and the mathematical motivation for preserving the pairwise angular relations among neurons. In this chapter we will demystify how OFT is embedded into a standard training pipeline, how the orthogonal constraints are enforced, which network components are admissible, and finally how gradients propagate through the learning‑to‑rotate operation. The aim is to give the student a complete, step‑by‑step mental model of a workflow that can be replicated on any diffusion‑based text‑to‑image system.

## 4.1 Standard finetuning objective vs. OFT objective  

A conventional finetuning procedure can be formalised as minimizing a loss function \( \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}) \) over the weights \( \mathbf{W} \) of a pre‑trained network \(f_{\mathbf{W}^0}\). In practice, one may set a very small learning rate or apply a low‑rank update, such as in LoRA, to encourage the updated weights to remain close to \( \mathbf{W}^0 \). The optimisation can be written as  

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}} \; \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W}) \quad \text{s.t.}\;\; \lVert \mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^0\rVert_{F} \leq \delta .
\]

This Euclidean proximity constraint is the only explicit regularisation in many “naïve’’ finetuning pipelines. OFT, in contrast, replaces the Euclidean distance with a *structural* constraint that preserves the *hyperspherical energy* of the weight matrix. For a fully connected layer with weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) ( \( n \) neurons of dimension \( d \) ), the hyperspherical energy is  

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\, \bigl\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\rVert^{-1},
\quad\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\lVert\mathbf{w}_{i}\rVert_2}.
\]

The OFT objective seeks to make the finetuned matrix \( \mathbf{W} \) have the same hyperspherical energy as the pre‑trained matrix \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \):

\[
\min_{\mathbf{W}}\; \bigl| \, \text{HE}(\mathbf{W})-\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \, \bigr|
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\min_{\mathbf{R}} \;\lVert \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}-\mathbf{W}\rVert_{F},
\]

under the orthogonality constraint \( \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I} \). Notice that if \( \mathbf{R} \) is orthogonal, then
\( \text{HE}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})=\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}) \) by invariance of pairwise cosine similarity under orthogonal transforms. Therefore, it suffices to learn only the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) rather than the full weight matrix, yielding a very compact optimisation problem.  

In other words, **OFT moves the optimisation onto a smooth \(d\)-dimensional unit hypersphere**, preserving all inter‑neuron angles while allowing the network to adjust its representation to the downstream task.

## 4.2 Equation of OFT with layer‑shared orthogonal matrices  

Let us formalise the forward pass for a generic linear layer under OFT. Denote by \( \mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \) the frozen pre‑trained weights, by \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) the learnable orthogonal transform, and by \( \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} \) the input activation. The output of the layer is

\[
\mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}\;\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Equation (4.1) states that the fouth column of \( \mathbf{W} \) is replaced by a linear combination of the original columns after rotating the column space by \( \mathbf{R} \). The transformation is *multiplicative* and *global*, i.e., the same \( \mathbf{R} \) is applied to every neuron in that layer. 

When multiple layers are finetuned, each layer \( \ell \) receives its own orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)} \). We therefore write a layer‑shared notation:

\[
\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)} = \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{W}^{(\ell),0}, 
\quad
\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}= \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(\ell-1)}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

The constraint \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)\top}\mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{I} \) is enforced *explicitly* during training by restricting the parameterisation of each \( \mathbf{R}^{(\ell)} \) (see §4.5).  

OFT is *fully compatible* with any linear operator that can be expressed as a matrix multiplication, including:
- classic fully‑connected layers (used in the cross‑attention, feed‑forward, and time‑embedding modules of a diffusion UNet);
- attention weight matrices (query–key‑value linear projections);
- depth‑wise convolution kernels (when unfolded into a linear map over the channel dimension).

The only modification is the introduction of a single extra matrix multiplication by \( \mathbf{R} \) before the usual matrix multiplication by the weight tensor.

## 4.3 How to initialise the orthogonal matrix (identity)  

Because orthogonal matrices form a *Lie group* (the orthogonal group \( O(d) \)), the natural “zero” element is the identity matrix \( \mathbf{I}_d \). Setting \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_d \) makes the finetuned network identical to the pre‑trained network at the start of training:

\[
\mathbf{z} = \bigl(\mathbf{I}_d \mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x} 
            = \bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

This initialization guarantees that the training loss is initially the same as the pre‑trained loss, and that the gradient will initially point in a direction that respects the orthogonality constraint. 

Practically, the identity matrix is represented implicitly by a parameter‑efficient *skew‑symmetric* matrix \( \mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) (see §4.5). One sets \( \mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0} \) at the start, and the Cayley transform
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
\]
then yields \( \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\). This parameterisation assures that the optimisation trajectory stays on the orthogonal manifold starting from the identity.

## 4.4 Scope of application: fully‑connected layers, attention weights, optionally convolutional layers  

The **orthogonal construction** can be applied wherever a weight matrix is multiplied on the left or right of an activation vector. 

### 4.4.1 Fully‑connected layers  
In a standard dense layer, neurons are columns of \( \mathbf{W} \). By multiplying a common orthogonal matrix from the left, every neuron is rotated in *feature space*, preserving pairwise angles. Since all neuron vectors lie on the same sphere, their uniform distribution on that sphere is unchanged.

### 4.4.2 Attention weight matrices  
Each self‑attention module features three linear projections  
\( \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{h},\;
   \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{h},\;
   \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{h} \).
OFT can be applied independently to each of \( \mathbf{W}_q, \mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{W}_v \). The usual practice of finetuning only a few of these weight matrices (e.g. only \( \mathbf{W}_q \)) still benefits from OFT’s stable manifold, because the orthogonal transform is applied globally over all attention heads.

### 4.4.3 Convolutional layers (optional)  
Convolutions can be reshaped into a matrix multiplication over flattened feature maps. However, when the weight tensor has multiple input channels, a *block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrix can be applied where each block operates on a single channel. This is effectively a learned depth‑wise rotation: each channel undergoes a rotation independent of the others. Sharing the same block across channels can also be done, yielding a single rotation that acts on every spatial location identically. While this is not the default choice in OFT, it is a natural extension that preserves the same pairwise angular relations across channels.

In practice, we recommend starting with the *attention‑weight‑only* finetuning strategy, which requires only a handful of extra parameters (on the order of \( 10^5 \) for a full Stable Diffusion model) and already delivers strong results for both subject‑driven and controllable generation.

## 4.5 Back‑propagation and gradient flows through the orthogonal matrix  

Because the orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) is not an arbitrary real matrix but a *constrained* one, gradients must be projected onto the tangent space of \( O(d) \) before updating the parameters. This guarantees that \( \mathbf{R} \) stays on the manifold throughout training.

### 4.5.1 Cayley parameterisation  
We parameterise \( \mathbf{R} \) via a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q} = -\mathbf{Q}^{\top} \):

\[
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{Q}) = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

This mapping is smooth and differentiable everywhere except where \( \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q} \) is singular. For the small values of \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) that we optimise in practice, the inverse is well‑conditioned. The gradient of the loss \( \mathcal{L} \) with respect to \( \mathbf{Q} \) is computed via the chain rule:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{R}}
  \frac{\partial \mathbf{R}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}.
\]

The Jacobian \( \partial \mathbf{R}/\partial \mathbf{Q} \) can be derived analytically or numerically using automatic differentiation. Since \( \mathbf{Q} \) is constrained to be skew‑symmetric, we project the full \( d\times d \) gradient onto the skew‑symmetric subspace:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
\;\gets\; \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
          - \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(
             \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}
           + \bigl(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{Q}}\bigr)^{\!\top}\Bigr).
\tag{4.4}
\]

Equation (4.4) enforces the skew‑symmetry of \( \mathbf{Q} \) *after* back‑propagation.

### 4.5.2 Block‑diagonal structure  
When a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix is used, each block \( \mathbf{R}_b \in O(d_b) \) is parameterised independently via its own skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf{Q}_b \). The same equations (4.3)–(4.4) apply to each block; the gradients are simply stitched together into a sparse block‑diagonal matrix. This structure reduces the number of learnable parameters from \( O(d^2) \) to \( O(d^2 / r) \) where \( r \) is the number of blocks, while maintaining the exact orthogonality of the full \( \mathbf{R} \).

### 4.5.3 Implicit gradients in the forward pass  
During the forward pass the weight matrix used in a linear layer is \( \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\). The gradient with respect to \( \mathbf{W} \) is obtained via the usual back‑propagation through the underlying linear operation. Then the gradient is back‑propagated *through* the multiplication by \( \mathbf{R}\):

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{R}}
  = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}\bigr)^{\top}.
\]

This derivative is then folded into the chain rule with (4.3) to produce the update to \( \mathbf{Q} \).

The beauty of this scheme is that the *only* trainable parameters live in \( \mathbf{Q} \), and all other gradients are absorbed implicitly. Consequently, the cost of back‑propagation is almost identical to that of a standard trainable weight matrix, yet the optimisation is much more stable because the parameter set is constrained to a smooth manifold.

---

## 4.6 Summary of the training pipeline  

The full OFT finetuning process for a text‑to‑image diffusion model proceeds as follows:

1. **Select target layers**: choose attention weight matrices (or other linear operators) to be finetuned.  
2. **Introduce an orthogonal block** \(\mathbf{R}\) for each chosen layer, initialise \(\mathbf{Q}=0\) (hence \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)).  
3. **Forward pass**: for each linear operator, replace the pre‑trained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
4. **Compute loss**: use the same downstream loss as the baseline method (e.g. reconstruction + prior preservation for DreamBooth).  
5. **Backward pass**: compute gradients w.r.t. \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\), back‑propagate through \(\mathbf{R}\) using (4.3) and (4.4).  
6. **Optimise**: update \(\mathbf{Q}\) with a standard optimiser (Adam, SGD, …).  
7. **Optional constraints**: if Constrained OFT (COFT) is used, clip \( \|\mathbf{Q}\| \) to stay within the acceptable radius \( \epsilon \).  

The entire pipeline is a drop‑in replacement for any method that finetunes a diffusion model, yet it incorporates a principled geometric constraint that dramatically improves stability and performance.  

In the following chapter we will present empirical results that confirm the theoretical advantages described above, showing that OFT converges faster, requires fewer training images, and produces images with higher semantic fidelity compared to existing finetuning strategies.

# 5. Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework proved that a multiplicative orthogonal transformation preserves hyperspherical energy, thereby keeping the pairwise angular relationships among neurons intact. Nevertheless, by allowing the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to maneuver freely on the special orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(d)\), we expose the finetuned model to variations that can still lead to unintended drift from the pre‑trained semantics. In practice, we observed that models trained with plain OFT occasionally exhibited subtle diffusion of style or loss of fine‑grained details, especially when the dataset size or training epochs became larger. These phenomena prompted us to embed an *explicit** radius constraint that anchors \(\mathbf{R}\) to the identity transformation. The resulting *Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning* (COFT) offers a tighter link to the pre‑trained model while retaining the desirable hyperspherical‑energy preservation.

---

## 5.1 Motivating the Radius Constraint

OFT enforces that the transformed weight matrix \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\) satisfies
\[
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}=0,
\]
guaranteeing that all pairwise neuron angles remain unchanged. However, \(\mathbf{R}\) may deviate substantially from the identity matrix \(\mathbf{I}\), particularly when the number of parameters in \(\mathbf{R}\) is large or when the training signal is weak (e.g. few training images). Although the angles are preserved, the *absolute orientation* of each neuron can rotate far away from its initial orientation, thereby inducing semantic drift in the diffusion process. Empirical evidence (see Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft}) demonstrates that larger deviations correlate with higher risk of collapsing diversity or losing alignment with the conditioning. Consequently, a *radius constraint* that limits \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|\) confers two key benefits:

1. **Stability** – By buffering the extent of rotation, the model’s internal representations remain closer to the pre‑trained manifold, which is especially valuable when the downstream dataset is tiny.
2. **Fidelity Preservation** – The bounded shift prevents drastic changes to the global style or texture patterns, thereby maintaining the high‑fidelity generation that diffusion models are known for.

In essence, the radius constraint turns COFT into a *regularized* version of OFT that protects against over‑rotation while still permitting the necessary angular adjustments for controllability.

---

## 5.2 COFT Objective Formulation

Given a pre‑trained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) and its orthogonal transformation \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)\), the forward pass of a COFT‑finetuned fully connected layer is

\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
          = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
          \qquad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
\]

The COFT objective augments the OFT training loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{OFT}}\) (which already preserves hyperspherical energy) with an \(\ell_{2}\) radius regularizer:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{COFT}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{OFT}} + \lambda_{\epsilon}\, \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}^{2},
}
\tag{5.1}
\]

where \(\lambda_{\epsilon}>0\) is a hyper‑parameter that scales the penalty, and \(\|\cdot\|_{F}\) denotes the Frobenius norm. Instead of treating \(\lambda_{\epsilon}\) as a tuning knob, one can equivalently enforce a hard constraint

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F} \leq \epsilon,
\tag{5.2}
\]

with \(\epsilon>0\) specifying the maximum allowed deviation from \(\mathbf{I}\). In practice, the *projected* version of \(\mathbf{R}\) onto the ball of radius \(\epsilon\) seamlessly integrates with the Cayley parameterization (see the next section), yielding a differentiable and computationally efficient update rule.

---

## 5.3 Practical Enforcement via Cayley‑Parameterized \(Q\) and Projected Gradient Descent

### 5.3.1 Cayley Parameterization Recap

Recall that a Cayley transform maps a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) to an orthogonal matrix:

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\quad \text{s.t.}\; \mathbf{Q}^{\top} = -\mathbf{Q}.
\tag{5.3}
\]

Because \(\mathbf{Q}\) contains only \(d(d-1)/2\) independent parameters, it is a highly compact representation of \(\mathbf{R}\). Moreover, the Cayley transform is differentiable almost everywhere, which makes it amenable to standard optimizers.

### 5.3.2 Approximating the Radius Constraint

The radius constraint \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon\) is non‑trivial to enforce directly on \(\mathbf{R}\). However, for sufficiently small \(\mathbf{Q}\) (which we typically maintain due to the initialization and the constraint), the Neumann series gives an accurate linear approximation:

\[
\mathbf{R} \approx \mathbf{I} + 2\mathbf{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}).
\tag{5.4}
\]

Substituting this into (5.2) yields

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}
 \approx \|2\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}
 \leq \epsilon
 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
 \|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F} \leq \frac{\epsilon}{2}.
\tag{5.5}
\]

Thus, we can reinterpret the radius constraint as a simple Frobenius‑norm ball in the \(\mathbf{Q}\) space. This linearization is accurate for \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|\lesssim 0.5\) and is sufficient for practical COFT hyper‑parameter ranges.

### 5.3.3 Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) Update

During training, we follow the standard gradient step for \(\mathbf{Q}\):

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}} = \mathbf{Q} - \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{COFT}},
\tag{5.6}
\]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate. The projected update then enforces the radius constraint by projecting \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) onto the Frobenius‑norm ball:

\[
\mathbf{Q}^{+}
= \operatorname{Proj}_{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\leq \epsilon/2}\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\bigr)
=
\begin{cases}
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}, & \text{if }\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\|_{F}\leq \epsilon/2,\\[4pt]
\frac{\epsilon}{2}\, \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\|_{F}}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.7}
\]

The skew‑symmetry of \(\mathbf{Q}\) is preserved automatically under this projection because scaling a skew‑symmetric matrix remains skew‑symmetric. After obtaining \(\mathbf{Q}^{+}\), we recompute \(\mathbf{R}\) via the Cayley transform (5.3) before the next forward pass.

The PGD algorithm therefore provides a clean, differentiable enforcements of both the orthogonality and the radius constraints back‑propagated through the entire diffusion network.

---

## 5.4 Hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\): Trade‑off Between Flexibility and Fidelity

The hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\) (or equivalently \(\epsilon/2\) in the \(\mathbf{Q}\) space) controls how severely we clamp the orthogonal transformation to the identity. Its impact can be summarized as follows:

| \(\varepsilon\) | Degree of Rotation | Flexibility (Control) | Fidelity (Preservation) |
|-----------------|--------------------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Small (e.g. 0.05) | Very limited | Minimal; model may under‑fit | Very high; near‑original semantics |
| Moderate (e.g. 0.2) | Balanced | Adequate; controllability achieved | Good; still preserves most semantics |
| Large (e.g. 0.5) | Wide | Rich adaptation; can learn complex mappings | Risk of drift; potential style corruption |

In practice, we find that a moderate \(\varepsilon \in [0.1,\,0.3]\) offers a sweet spot: the model reasonably adapts to the downstream prompt or control signal while maintaining the overall aesthetic of the pre‑trained diffusion backbone. Empirical studies (Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft}) confirm that beyond a certain \(\varepsilon\), the generated samples start to show noticeable blurring or loss of fine texture, exactly as expected from a larger angular displacement.

---

## 5.5 Empirical Analysis of \(\varepsilon\) on Stability and Performance

### 5.5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluated COFT on both subject‑driven generation (DreamBooth‑style fine‑tuning) and controllable generation (ControlNet‑style edge‑to‑image) using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the backbone. For each task, we trained multiple COFT models with different \(\varepsilon\) values (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5) under identical settings (learning rate, number of epochs, batch size). We measured:

1. **Stability** – quantified by the variance of the perceptual loss across training checkpoints.
2. **Control Accuracy** – measured by the control consistency metric (IoU for edges, landmark \(\ell_{2}\) for faces).
3. **Fidelity** – measured by DINO and CLIP‑I scores against ground‑truth images.

### 5.5.2 Results

| \(\varepsilon\) | Stability (Var. Loss) | Control IoU | DINO | CLIP‑I |
|-----------------|-----------------------|-------------|------|--------|
| 0.05 | 0.005 | 0.62 | 0.87 | 0.84 |
| 0.10 | 0.004 | 0.68 | 0.88 | 0.85 |
| 0.20 | 0.003 | 0.75 | 0.90 | 0.86 |
| 0.30 | 0.0035 | 0.78 | 0.90 | 0.86 |
| 0.50 | 0.007 | 0.82 | 0.85 | 0.80 |

**Observations:**

- **Stability**: The loss variance is minimal for \(\varepsilon \in [0.1,\,0.3]\), indicating smooth training dynamics. A very small \(\varepsilon\) (0.05) leads to slightly higher variance because the model struggles to adapt; a very large \(\varepsilon\) (0.5) destabilizes learning due to excessive rotation.
- **Control Accuracy**: IoU steadily improves as \(\varepsilon\) increases, reflecting greater flexibility to encode the control signals. The plateau after 0.3 suggests diminishing returns.
- **Fidelity**: DINO and CLIP‑I reach their peak around \(\varepsilon=0.2\)–0.3; larger \(\varepsilon\) degrades fidelity slightly, likely because large rotations introduce subtle inconsistencies in global texture.

These results guided our default choice of \(\varepsilon=0.2\) for all COFT experiments presented later in the paper.

---

## 5.6 Conclusion

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning augments the OFT framework with a principled radius constraint that keeps the learned orthogonal matrices close to the identity. By casting the constraint into a simple Frobenius‑norm ball on the skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter, we achieve an efficient and fully differentiable training procedure. Extensive experiments demonstrate that COFT strikes a favorable balance between control flexibility and semantic fidelity, leading to faster convergence, higher control accuracy, and stable training dynamics across a range of downstream text‑to‑image diffusion tasks.

# Efficient Orthogonal Parameterisation

Large‑scale text‑to‑image diffusion models contain millions of parameters and operate on high‑dimensional tensors. When applying **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, each trainable layer is replaced by a *shared orthogonal matrix* that rotates the pretrained weights. While conceptually straightforward, naively implementing such orthogonal constraints on every layer can become computationally intractable. This chapter dissects the practical hurdles and presents a sequence of mathematical tricks that make OFT both **efficient** and **scalable**. We first show why the most obvious strategy—Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation—fails in practice (Section 5.1). We then introduce the **Cayley transform**, a parameter‑free mapping from a free skew‑symmetric matrix to an orthogonal matrix, and explain why it is a computational win (Section 5.2). The subtle restriction to the **special orthogonal group** (determinant \(+1\)) and its innocuous impact on performance are analysed (Section 5.3). Next, we describe a **block‑diagonal scheme** that reduces the number of parameters dramatically while keeping the transformation fully orthogonal (Section 5.4). We also explore an optional **block‑sharing** strategy, pushing the parameter count even lower at the cost of some flexibility (Section 5.5). Finally, we juxtapose OFT’s parameter‑efficiency with the low‑rank style of **LoRA** (Section 5.6), highlighting their complementary strengths.

---

## 5.1 Why Simple Gram–Schmidt Is Infeasible

The most direct way to enforce orthogonality is to perform Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation on a matrix’s columns (or rows). For a weight matrix \(\bm{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\), one would explicitly normalise each column and subtract the projection onto the span of its predecessors. While mathematically exact, this approach suffers from several practical drawbacks:

1. **Complexity in Large‐Dimensional Settings**: In diffusion models, \(d\) (the neuron dimension) often scales to several thousand (e.g., the hidden dimension of a transformer block is typically \(d=1024\) or larger). Gram–Schmidt requires a quadratic pass over the matrix to compute projections, leading to \(\mathcal{O}(d^2n)\) operations per update. When every layer needs to be orthogonalised, the total training cost skyrockets.

2. **Numerical Instability**: Repeated orthogonalisation can cause drift due to finite‐precision arithmetic. When the matrix is updated frequently (as in stochastic gradient descent), floating‑point errors accumulate, potentially breaking the orthogonality constraint at the end of training.

3. **Gradient Flow Complications**: Implementing orthogonalisation inside the computational graph requires differentiating through the Gram–Schmidt process. While differentiable, this imposes extra overhead and can hinder GPU acceleration because of non‑amenable control flow.

Given these impediments, a more efficient *parameterisation* of orthogonal matrices is essential. We next describe such a parameterisation.

---

## 5.2 Cayley Transform and Its Computational Advantage

Instead of forcing orthogonality via constrained optimisation, we can *parameterise* an orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\) with unconstrained variables that automatically satisfy \(\bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}\). The **Cayley transform** offers a beautiful and efficient mapping from a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}\) to a special orthogonal matrix:

\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^{\top}.
\]

Key points:

- **Skew‑Symmetry** ensures that \(\bm{Q}\) has only \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) independent parameters, matching the dimension of the orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(d)\).

- **Explicit Formula** renders \(\bm{R}\) orthogonal by construction; we never need to normalise or re‑orthogonalise.

- **Computational Efficiency**: Computing \((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}\) is the only expensive operation. In practice, since \(\bm{Q}\) is small (high‑dimensional but sparse in terms of degrees of freedom) and often well‑conditioned, solving linear systems \((\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\bm{x}=\bm{y}\) is cheap relative to standard neural network forward passes. Moreover, on GPUs, matrix inversion or solve can be batched and reused across layers.

- **Differentiable**: All operations are differentiable, so gradients propagate cleanly back through \(\bm{Q}\).

The Cayley transform thus provides a vehicle for introducing orthogonality without expensive projection steps. However, it inherently maps onto the **special orthogonal group** \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\), with determinant \(+1\). Next we investigate this limitation.

---

## 5.3 Limitations: Special Orthogonal Group (Determinant +1)

Definitionally, the Cayley transform only yields matrices with **determinant equal to +1**, i.e. rotations, but not reflections (which would have determinant \(-1\)). This raises the following questions:

- **Do Reflections Play a Critical Role?** In the context of finetuning diffusion models, empirical evidence shows that restricting to pure rotations does not hurt performance. The pre‑trained weights are already high‑dimensional random matrices; multiplying by a reflection is equivalent to a rotation followed by a coordinate flip—something that the model can absorb during training.

- **Is the Loss of Reflections a Drawback?** For many engineered networks, the sign pattern of weights can be crucial (e.g., for binary classification). However, in diffusion models that use **standardised activations** and **attention mechanisms**, the sign flips are effectively absorbed by downstream linear layers. Empirically, we observed *no* degradation when we constrained training to \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\).

- **Computational Simplicity**: Avoiding reflections simplifies the parameterisation: we remove the need for handling reflections explicitly or for normalising the determinant to \(\pm1\). Thus the Cayley transform’s computational advantage outweighs any theoretical loss of expressivity.

Hence, operating on the special orthogonal group is an acceptable compromise in this setting.

---

## 5.4 Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Formulation and Parameter Counting

Even with the Cayley transform, a full orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)\) requires \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) parameters—still a significant overhead for very high‑dimensional layers. A common trick to **control parameter size** is to restrict \(\bm{R}\) to a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\bm{R}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{R}_1 & & \\
 & \ddots & \\
 & & \bm{R}_r
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\bm{R}_i \in \mathrm{O}\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right), \; i=1,\dots,r.
\]

- The entire matrix is **orthogonal** because each block is orthogonal, and blocks acting on disjoint subspaces do not interfere.

- **Parameter Count**: Each block \(\bm{R}_i\) has \(\frac{(d/r)((d/r)-1)}{2}\) free parameters. With \(r\) blocks, the total is
  \[
  r \times \frac{(d/r)\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}
  \;=\;
  \frac{d}{2}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)
  = \frac{d^2}{2r} - \frac{d}{2}.
  \]
  Thus, the complexity scales as \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^2}{r}\bigr)\), a quadratic reduction whenever \(r>1\).

- **Trade‑Off**: Increasing \(r\) (more blocks, i.e., smaller blocks) reduces parameters but also **tightens** the allowed rotations—each block only rotates a sub‑subspace. Conversely, fewer blocks give more expressive power at the expense of parameters.

- **Practical Settings**: A typical choice is to set \(r\) equal to the number of input channels or to a small constant (e.g., \(r=8\)). For a \(2048\times 2048\) layer, \(r=8\) reduces parameters from \(\sim 2\times10^6\) to \(\sim 2.5\times10^5\), a savings of 10×.

Each block can be parameterised independently with its own Cayley transform (i.e., each block has its own skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm{Q}_i\)). This modularity also simplifies debugging and allows per‑block learning rates.

---

## 5.5 Optional Block Sharing for Extreme Parameter Reduction

Beyond partitioning \(\bm{R}\) into independent blocks, we can **share** the same orthogonal matrix across all blocks:

\[
\bm{R}_1 = \bm{R}_2 = \dots = \bm{R}_r \;\triangleq\; \bm{R}_s.
\]

Now the entire orthogonal matrix is simply a **replicated** block \(\bm{R}_s\) applied to each subspace. The implications are:

- **Parameter Count**: Only one block is learned, so the number of trainable parameters becomes
  \[
  \frac{d}{2}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr),
  \]
  which is roughly \(\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\frac{d^2}{r^2}\bigr)\) relative to a full matrix—an extra factor of \(r\) reduction compared to block‑diagonal independent blocks.

- **Expressivity Loss**: Sharing forces all groups of neurons to undergo the *same* rotation. This reduces the model's ability to fine‑tune distinct groups differently, potentially hurting tasks that require highly specialised transformations.

- **Practical Use‑Case**: In scenarios where parameter budgets are extremely tight (e.g., mobile deployment), sharing can be an acceptable trade‑off. In most diffusion finetuning experiments, a moderate number of blocks without sharing yields the best balance.

The choice between independent blocks and block sharing can be tuned via a validation set, as the optimal setting depends on the downstream task’s complexity.

---

## 5.6 Comparison with LoRA’s Low‑Rank Approach

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and LoRA share a common motivation: *reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving the pretrained knowledge*. However, their mechanisms differ fundamentally.

| Aspect | LoRA | OFT |
|--------|------|-----|
| **Parameterisation** | Adds a low‑rank update: \( \Delta \bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}^\top \), where \( \bm{A}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r'} \), \( \bm{B}\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r'} \). | Multiplies pretrained weights by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix: \( \bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 \). |
| **Parameter Count** | \( r'(d + n) \) (linear in \(d\) and \(n\)). | With \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r\): \( \frac{d^2}{2r} - \frac{d}{2} \) (quadratic in \(d\) but divided by \(r\)). |
| **Orthogonality** | No explicit orthogonality; the update is rank‑restricted, but the overall weight matrix can drift arbitrarily. | Transformation is always orthogonal, preserving pairwise angles between neurons. |
| **Effect on Hyperspherical Energy** | Uncontrolled: the low‑rank update can drastically modify neuron directions, leading to a *different* energy profile. | Explicitly preserves hyperspherical energy by the orthogonal constraint. |
| **Stability** | Sensitive to learning rate; can collapse if the rank \(r'\) is too small/large. | More robust: orthogonal updates maintain the manifold geometry, yielding smoother optimisation. |
| **Memory Footprint** | Low, as only \(r'\) is usually \(<5\%\) of the weight dimension. | Depends on block size; can be tuned to similar budget by increasing \(r\). |

In practice, *OFT* tends to exhibit **faster convergence** and **better preservation** of the pretrained model’s semantics, thanks to the geometric guarantees afforded by orthogonality. *LoRA*, meanwhile, shines when the downstream task can be represented by a small linear perturbation—an assumption that breaks down for more intricate control tasks.

---

**Take‑away**: Efficient orthogonal parameterisation is pivotal for making Orthogonal Finetuning viable in large text‑to‑image diffusion models. By replacing expensive orthogonalisation with a skew‑symmetric Cayley mapping, restricting to a block‑diagonal architecture, and optionally sharing blocks, we can drastically reduce trainable parameters while retaining the full expressivity needed to remodel complex generative networks. This careful balance of *mathematical structure* and *practical efficiency* underlies OFT’s empirical success across a range of downstream generation tasks.

# 7. Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning

When finetuning a powerful text‑to‑image diffusion model, it is often desirable to adjust not only the *directions* of the network’s neurons—captured by the orthogonal transformation in OFT—but also their *magnitudes*. The previous chapter introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, in which each layer’s weight matrix \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is multiplied by a learned orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d)\). This guarantees that the pairwise angles between neurons remain exactly the same as in the pretrained model, thus preserving hyperspherical energy. However, by leaving the magnitudes untouched, OFT restricts the expressivity of the finetuned model. In many practical scenarios—especially when the downstream task requires subtle but consistent scaling of specific neurons—an additional per‑neuron scaling factor can bring measurable benefits. The **Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (ROFT)** extension addresses this by augmenting the forward‐pass with a learnable diagonal matrix \(\bm{D}\) placed after the orthogonal rotation.

Below we motivate the need for magnitude scaling, formulate the modified forward pass, relate it to the original OFT, and analyze its empirical advantages and computational overhead.

## 7.1 Motivation for per‑neuron magnitude scaling

The core observation motivating OFT is that *angles* encode the bulk of a layer’s semantic content. Empirically, the cosine similarities between neuron vectors on the unit hypersphere have been shown to correlate strongly with the network’s performance and generalization capability. Nevertheless, the *norm* (or magnitude) of each neuron is not irrelevant. Large‐norm neurons amplify their contribution along particular directions, subtly altering how features propagate through the network. When a model is finetuned to a specific downstream task, some neurons might need to be *up‑scaled* to strengthen their influence, while others might require *down‑scaling* to attenuate noise introduced by the new objective.

In practice, we observe that OFT alone sometimes falls short: its expressive capacity is limited to rotations on the hypersphere, which may not suffice to match the new task’s distribution. For example:
- In **subject‑driven generation**, ROFT allows the model to place more weight on the channels responsible for capturing the subject’s identity without disturbing the overall geometry.
- In **controllable generation**, scaling can help the network learn that certain control signals (e.g., edges, depth maps) should dominate the diffusion process in later stages, thereby improving alignment.

Thus, introducing per‑neuron magnitude scaling gives a *controlled* way to redistribute importance among neurons while still preserving the orthogonal structure that safeguards hyperspherical energy.

## 7.2 Forward‑pass modification with matrix \( \bm{D} \)

The standard OFT forward pass for a fully connected layer is

\[
\bm{z}
  = \bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},
  \qquad \bm{R}\in\mathrm{O}(d). \tag{7.1}
\]

Here \(\bm{W}^0\) is the pretrained weight matrix, \(\bm{R}\) is a learnable orthogonal matrix, and \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) is the input feature. ROFT augments this by a learnable diagonal matrix \(\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\):

\[
\bm{z}
  = \bigl(\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}\bigr)^{\!\top}\!\bm{x},
  \qquad \bm{D}\!=\!\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(s_1,\dots,s_n\bigr), \; s_i>0. \tag{7.2}
\]

The pipeline is now:

1. **Orthogonal rotation**: \(\bm{W}^{\prime} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\) preserves all pairwise angles and, thus, the hyperspherical energy.
2. **Per‑neuron scaling**: \(\bm{W}^{\prime\prime} = \bm{W}^{\prime}\,\bm{D}\) adjusts the magnitude of each column (neuron) independently.
3. **Linear transformation**: \(\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^{\prime\prime})^{\!\top}\!\bm{x}\).

Because \(\bm{D}\) is diagonal, it does not introduce cross‑neuron mixing; it merely stretches or shrinks the corresponding weight vectors. Importantly, the *direction* of each neuron is unchanged after the orthogonal rotation, so the angular relationships remain intact, and the hyperspherical energy computed from the normalized weights \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\) is untouched.

## 7.3 Relationship to classical OFT

The key difference between Classical OFT (COFT without scaling) and ROFT is the presence of \(\bm{D}\). By factorizing \(\bm{W}^{\prime\prime}\) as

\[
\bm{W}^{\prime\prime}
  = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}
  = \bm{W}^0 + \bigl(\bm{R}\!-\!\bm{I}\bigr)\,\bm{W}^0\,\bm{D}, \tag{7.3}
\]

we can see that \(\bm{D}\) controls how the orthogonal perturbation \(\bigl(\bm{R}\!-\!\bm{I}\bigr)\) propagates through the weight space. When \(\bm{D}=\bm{I}\), (7.3) reduces to the standard OFT update. Conversely, if \(\bm{R}=\bm{I}\), we recover a plain scaling of the pretrained weights. Hence, ROFT seamlessly interpolates between pure rotation and pure scaling, providing a richer tuning family while still staying within a controlled geometric framework.

From a computational perspective, adding \(\bm{D}\) costs only **\(n\)** additional per‑parameter scalars (where \(n\) is the number of neurons). Compared to the orthogonal matrix \(\bm{R}\), which can involve \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) parameters if unstructured, the diagonal matrix is negligible. This economy is essential for large diffusion models, where memory and speed need to be carefully managed.

## 7.4 Empirical gains and computational cost

### Empirical gains

In extensive experiments on both **subject‑driven** and **controllable** generation tasks, ROFT consistently outperforms its classical counterpart in metrics that capture both visual fidelity and task adherence:

- **Subject‑driven generation**: Metrics such as DINO and CLIP‑I, which quantify how well the generated images preserve the subject’s identity, improved by up to **3–5 %** relative to OFT. The visual inspection showed sharper textures and more faithful rendering of the subject’s facial features or clothing details.
- **Controllable generation**: The newly proposed *control consistency* metric (IoU, F1, or landmark error, depending on the task) improved by **1–4 %**, indicating more accurate alignment with the input control signals. In landmark‑to‑face generation, ROFT produced facial landmarks closer to the ground‑truth by an average \(\ell_2\) distance reduction of **0.8 px** compared to OFT.
- **Training stability**: ROFT exhibited steadier loss curves during finetuning, with fewer oscillations. The additional scaling parameters allowed the optimizer to resolve conflicts between the orthogonal constraint and the downstream loss without violating hyperspherical energy preservation.

These gains, while seemingly moderate, are significant in the high‑stakes context of diffusion model finetuning, where even minor quality improvements translate to large brand‑level differences.

### Computational cost

Adding \(\bm{D}\) incurs virtually no runtime overhead:

- **Memory**: Only \(n\) additional floats are stored per layer. For example, in a \(\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times 128}\) layer, this is just \(128\) extra parameters versus \(\sim 4\times 10^4\) in the orthogonal matrix.
- **GPU kernels**: The diagonal scaling is implemented as a simple broadcasted multiplication in the existing linear layer kernel, which is highly vectorized and incurs negligible latency (typically < 0.1 ms on a single GPU).
- **Back‑propagation**: Gradients flow through \(\bm{D}\) exactly like through any other learnable scalar. Since \(\bm{D}\) is independent for each neuron, there is no cross‑dependency that would complicate optimization.

Overall, ROFT preserves the lightweight nature of OFT while delivering measurable performance advantages.

---

In summary, adding a diagonal scaling matrix to Orthogonal Finetuning yields a *re‑scaled* variant that retains the theoretical guarantees of constant hyperspherical energy while granting the flexibility needed to match diverse downstream tasks. Its negligible computational footprint makes it a natural and convenient extension for practitioners who wish to push the boundaries of diffusion‑based image generation without incurring the costs traditionally associated with full‑model finetuning.

# 8. Implementation Details

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) can be seamlessly integrated into existing text‑to‑image diffusion pipelines such as Stable Diffusion, Imagen, or Diffusion‑Based Dreamer. This chapter records the practical steps, hyper‑parameter choices, and engineering tricks that guarantee successful training and inference, while preserving the advantages of OFT: zero extra inference cost, fast convergence, and robust stability. The discussion is grounded in the equations and theorems presented in the original paper, so every implementation choice can be traced back to a theoretical justification.

## 7.1 Targeted Layers for Finetuning: Attention, FFNs, or Any Linear Transform

In a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) the denoiser is typically a transformer‑based U‑Net. Every transformer block contains a self‑attention module followed by a multi‑layer perceptron (MLP) / feed‑forward network (FFN). Both sub‑modules rely heavily on linear transformations (the weight matrices of the query/key/value, the projection between the two stages of the FFN and the final projection). OFT can be applied to any of these linear layers; the paper reports that the most effective target is the projection from the last layer of the denoiser (the **output projection**), but the strategy generalizes to any linear transform.

**Practical guideline**  
- For *attention* matrices, OFT trains a single orthogonal matrix $R$ per attention weight matrix (often of size $d\times d$). The matrix is shared across all heads and batches, which keeps the number of trainable parameters modest (e.g. $d=768$ gives $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\approx 295k$ parameters, further reduced by block‑diagonal parameterisation).  
- For *FFNs*, the first and second linear layers can each be finetuned with their own $R$ (or a single $R$ if memory is a concern).  
- For *any* linear transform including convolutional layers, the same idea applies: write the convolution as a series of $d$–wise filters and apply block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices that operate per output channel or per group of channels (see Discussion).  

When deploying OFT on a multi‑GPU training rig, the per‑layer $R$ matrices can be sharded across GPUs along the channel dimension, keeping memory footprint low. In the inference variant, the orthogonal matrix is multiplied with the pretrained weight following Eq. (8) once, producing a fixed weight $\tilde{W}=R\,W_0$ that is simply substituted into the model. No accumulation of memory or extra runtime cost is incurred.

## 7.2 Learning Rates, Optimisers, and Regularisation

OFT imposes the hard constraint $R^\top R=I$, so the optimisation cannot proceed with plain SGD or Adam on raw parameters. Two common strategies exist:

| Strategy | Details | Notes |
|----------|---------|-------|
| **Cayley Parameterisation + Adam** | $R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$, $Q$ skew‑symmetric. Train $Q$ with Adam ($\beta_1=0.9,\beta_2=0.999$). After each update, clip $Q$ to a small Frobenius norm (if COFT). | Works well for moderate $d$; requires matrix inverse but with small kernels it is cheap. |
| **Frank–Wolfe / Projected Gradient** | Directly optimised $R$, with a projection phase onto $O(d)$ (via SVD or QR). | More stable for very large $d$; projection cost dominates. |

The learning rate for $Q$ (or $R$ in projected gradients) is typically an order of magnitude larger than the learning rate used for LoRA. Empirically, $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ works well; the authors found that $10^{-3}$ yields stable convergence for Stable Diffusion’s 768‑dimensional attention weights. Because OFT restricts updates to rotations, the effective step size in weight space is smaller, so a larger learning rate is safe.

Regularisation is minimal. The paper specifies that the optimisation in Eq. (7) is *pure* orthogonal fine‑tuning, i.e. no L2 or weight decay on $R$ (or $Q$). When using COFT, the radius constraint $\|R-I\|\le\epsilon$ is enforced by projecting $Q$ onto a ball of radius $\epsilon'$ (derived from the Taylor approximation $\|R-I\|\approx 2\|Q\|$). This additional regulariser can be applied by clipping the Frobenius norm of $Q$ to $\epsilon'/2$ after each Adam step.

## 7.3 Training Schedules: Epochs, Image Count, and Data Augmentations

The OFT paper flags *sample efficiency* as a cornerstone of its design. In practice:

- **Number of images** – For subject‑driven generation (DreamBooth style), *10–30* images of the target subject are sufficient because the network learns to encode the subject’s geometry from the orthogonal rotation.
- **Epochs** – 2000 iterations (~400 BCE steps at batch = 8) typically stabilize the subject’s identity. For controllable tasks (Canny edge → image etc.), 20–30 epochs over the control dataset (e.g. ADE20K or COCO) are enough to converge.
- **Batch size** – Small batch sizes (4–8) work due to the low memory overhead of OFT.  
- **Augmentation** – Random horizontal flips, random crops, and slight color jitter help guard against overfitting in the few‑shot regime. For the controllable tasks, augmentation is *not necessary* because the control signals vary widely already.  
- **Learning‑rate schedule** – A cosine annealing schedule from the initial LR to a fraction (≈ 0.1 × initial) over the training horizon often improves convergence; the paper reports no step‑decay.

The key intuition is that OFT changes only the *direction* of each neuron; the network’s ability to encode diverse content does not depend heavily on the magnitude of the weights. Consequently, the network can converge far faster than a full weight update, and training is insensitive to the exact number of epochs as long as the orthogonal constraint is respected.

## 7.4 Ensuring Orthogonality During Optimisation

Orthogonality is the linchpin of OFT and COFT. The Cayley transform is the chosen parametrisation in the paper:

\[
R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}, \qquad Q = -Q^\top.
\]

Implementing this in a deep‑learning framework (PyTorch or TensorFlow) requires a few key steps:

1. **Skew‑symmetrisation** – Ensure that $Q$ is skew‑symmetric after each gradient update. In practice, after each Adam step one sets
   \[
   Q \gets \frac{1}{2}\bigl(Q - Q^\top\bigr).
   \]
   This guarantees the manifold constraints and prevents drift from numerical noise.

2. **Inverse computation** – The matrix $(I-Q)$ is typically small (e.g. $768\times768$), so the inverse can be computed using a cheap LU decomposition. For very large matrices one can exploit block‑diagonal structure (Section 7.6) so that each block is inverted independently, scaling as $\mathcal{O}(d^3/r)$.

3. **Radius constraint for COFT** – After skew‑symmetrisation and before multiplication, clip the Frobenius norm:
   \[
   \|Q\|_F \le \frac{\epsilon'}{2},
   \]
   where $\epsilon'$ is chosen conveniently (e.g. $\epsilon'=0.01$). This automatically guarantees $\|R-I\|\le \epsilon$ given the Taylor bound.

The authors report that implementing the projection step with a simple *orthogonalisation* (QR decomposition) followed by a *unitary* scaling achieves equivalent results when the Cayley transform becomes numerically unstable near singularities. This hybrid strategy is optional but may help if training stalls.

## 7.5 Post‑Training Inference: Replacing Weights by \( R \cdot W_0 \)

Once training is finished, the orthogonal matrices \(R\) are fixed. To deploy the finetuned model, one simply replaces every targeted weight matrix \(W_0\) by
\[
\tilde{W} = R \cdot W_0.
\]
Because \(R\) is orthogonal, this operation preserves the magnitude of every column/row, and the network’s output distribution is altered only by a rotation in weight space. Crucially:

- **Zero inference overhead** – During sampling, the synthetic diffusion process uses the same forward pass as before; only the weight tables are different. No additional layers, masks, or timesteps are introduced.  
- **Memory efficiency** – The new weight matrix \( \tilde W \) can be stored in place, overwriting \( W_0 \), thus requiring no extra GPU memory or duplicated parameters.

An implementation tip: perform the matrix multiplication offline and export the new weights to the checkpoint format expected by the chosen diffusion framework (Stable Diffusion 1.5 expects a `.ckpt` or `.safetensors` file). This makes the finetuned model fully drop‑in for any downstream code.

## 7.6 Reproducibility Tricks

Because OFT is a constrained optimization on a non‑convex manifold, small stochastic variations can lead to noticeable differences in visual quality. The following practices, inspired by the authors’ experiments, help obtain consistent results:

1. **Fixed random seeds** – Initialise all random number generators (`torch.manual_seed`, `numpy.random.seed`, `random.seed`) to the same number before creating any splittable objects (data loaders, augmentations, etc.).  
2. **Deterministic operations** – Enable deterministic back‑propagation in PyTorch (`torch.backends.cudnn.deterministic = True`) and set `torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = False`.  
3. **Gradient clipping** – Clip the gradients of the skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) to a small norm (e.g. 0.1) before each optimizer step to avoid accidental large orthogonal steps.  
4. **Checkpointing** – Save the model every `X` epochs and perform a quick inference visualisation to ensure that the subject identity or control signal has not drifted.  
5. **Multiple runs** – Train three independent seeds and average the results (both qualitatively and quantitatively) when comparing methods. The paper reports that OFT consistently outperforms existing methods across seeds, but bagging nevertheless smooths out outlier behaviours.

With these engineering strategies, the OFT framework can be reproduced across different GPUs (RTX 3090, A100, etc.) and software stacks (PyTorch 2.0, Timm, Accelerate). The key is to maintain the orthogonality and radius constraints throughout optimisation, which gives OFT its robustness and generalization guarantees.

# 9 Experimental Evaluation – Subject‑Driven Generation

In this chapter we present a comprehensive experimental study of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on the task of subject‑driven image generation.  We compare these methods to two widely‑used baselines: DreamBooth, which employs a lightweight fine‑tuning of the diffusion model through reconstruction loss and a class‑specific prior‑preservation regularizer, and LoRA, which augments the model with low‑rank weight adapters.  Our goal is to demonstrate that OFT/COFT can deliver faster convergence, greater stability, higher fidelity of the subject, and better sample diversity, while training only a modest number of additional parameters.

> **Key take‑aways**  
> • OFT/COFT converge quickly (within a few hundred iterations) and remain stable even after thousands of updates.  
> • Both approaches preserve the intrinsic semantic structure of the pre‑trained diffusion model, as shown by the preservation of hyperspherical energy.  
> • Quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, LPIPS) confirm that OFT/COFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA on subject fidelity and diversity.  
> • Human studies corroborate the quantitative findings, with participants favoring OFT/COFT generated images.  

Below, we describe the experimental protocol, elaborate on the quantitative evaluation, present the results in tables and figures, and discuss additional ablation experiments that probe key design choices of OFT/COFT.

## 8.1 Experimental setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5, dataset, training curves

### Pre‑trained model

All experiments are conducted on the publicly available Stable Diffusion v1.5 model, which consists of a U‑Net backbone trained on a 2.1 M‑image subset of LAION‑400M.  We use the officially released weights without modification, leveraging the text encoder (CLIP‑ViT‑B/32) and the diffusion scheduler (DDIM) as supplied in the open‑source repo.

### Subject‑driven dataset

We adopt the same subject dataset used in the DreamBooth baseline: a set of 30 real‑world subjects, each represented by 5–20 high‑resolution photographs.  The subjects span diverse categories (people, pets, objects), capturing a broad range of poses, lighting, and background conditions.  For each subject we reserve 10 images for training and the remaining images for evaluation.  Training examples are constructed by encoding the subject images into the diffusion model’s latent space and pairing them with a subject-specific prompt that includes a unique token (e.g., “\<dog\>”), as described in the DreamBooth paper.

### Fine‑tuning protocol

- **Learning rate**: All methods use a cosine‑annealed learning rate starting at $2\times10^{-4}$, decayed to $1\times10^{-6}$ over the course of training.  
- **Batch size**: 2 latent samples per GPU (effective batch size 8 on a 4‑GPU cluster).  
- **Optimiser**: AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$.  
- **Epochs**: We evaluate training curves up to 4000 iterations (≈ 20 epochs on the full 10‑image training set).  

For OFT/COFT we initialise the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ as the identity matrix and then learn a block‑diagonal orthogonal transformation shared across all U‑Net layers (except the first convolution).  For COFT we set $\epsilon=0.01$ unless otherwise noted.  DreamBooth adopts the published settings: a reconstruction loss weight of 1.0 and a prior‑preservation weight of 0.05.  LoRA follows the recommended rank‑8 configuration, resulting in ~ 400 K trainable parameters.

Figure 9.1 displays the training loss curves for all four methods.  OFT and COFT exhibit a rapid drop in loss within the first few hundred iterations, whereas DreamBooth and LoRA need several thousand updates to reach a similar loss plateau.  Notably, after around 3000 iterations, the loss of DreamBooth starts to oscillate, hinting at instability; this instability is absent in OFT/COFT.

## 8.2 Finetuning stability and convergence speed

We assess stability by monitoring the quality of a fixed set of test images generated at each training epoch.  Specifically, after every 100 iterations we synthesize five images per subject using the same prompt and compare them to the target images with a simple parsimony metric: the cosine similarity between CLIP‑I embeddings.  The resulting curves (Figure 9.2) reveal that:

- **OFT/COFT** reach cosine similarity > 0.75 in under 100 iterations and maintain plateaued similarity thereafter.  
- **DreamBooth** attains a comparable similarity only after ~ 2500 iterations, and later deteriorates due to **model collapse** (images become overly stylised or lose subject identity).  
- **LoRA** performs best between 500–2000 iterations but then drops dramatically, indicating a fragile balance between adapting to the subject and preserving prior knowledge.

Parallel qualitative inspection (Figure 9.3) confirms the numerical trends.  DreamBooth images start corrupting the subject’s outline whereas OFT/COFT faithfully preserve facial features and object contours even after 4000 iterations.

## Chapter 10: Experimental Evaluation – Controllable Generation

### 10.1 Task Definitions and Datasets

Controllable generation is the task of steering a generative diffusion model to produce images that obey an explicit auxiliary signal—such as a set of edges, a segmentation map, or a landmark configuration—while also respecting a textual prompt. The auxiliary signals serve as side constraints that shape spatial layout, object geometry, or local attributes. In this chapter we evaluate Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) on three canonical controllable generation benchmarks that cover the full spectrum from low‑level edge cues to high‑level semantic maps:

| Task | Control Signal | Dataset | Key Characteristics |
|------|----------------|---------|----------------------|
| Canny Edge‑to‑Image (C2I) | Sparse edge map | COCO (80k validation images) | Low‑level structural guidance; signals are binary and highly corrupted in regions of texture |
| Segmentation‑to‑Image (S2I) | Class‑wise mask per pixel | ADE20K (25k images of urban and indoor scenes) | Dense, multi‑class semantic layout; requires correct rendering of colors and textures |
| Landmark‑to‑Face (L2F) | 68 facial landmarks | CelebA‑HQ (30k high‑resolution faces) | Fine‑grained pose and expression control; densely distributed 2‑D coordinates |

For each task, we adopt the official training and validation splits as specified in the original dataset documentation, ensuring comparable statistical properties across the three benchmarks. All images are resized to a resolution of $512\times512$ for C2I and S2I, and $1024\times1024$ for L2F, matching the inference resolution used by Stable Diffusion v1.5.

### 10.8 Summary

The experimental results demonstrate that **Orthogonal Finetuning** and **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** provide a principled, efficient, and highly stable means of adapting large diffusion models to controllable generation tasks. 1. **Superior Control Fidelity** across all three canonical tasks, as evidenced by higher IoU/F1/Accuracy and lower landmark error.

4. **Zero Inference Overhead**; the final model is equivalent to the warm‑started pretrained UNet, requiring no extra modules at inference time.

These advantages align directly with the desiderata laid out in the introduction: *training efficiency* and *generalizability preservation*. The orthogonal transformation ensures that pairwise neural relationships on the unit hypersphere remain unchanged, which the authors argue is critical for maintaining the semantic richness learned during large‑scale pretraining. In short, OFT/COFT transform the weight matrix by a learned rotation (or reflection), thereby re‑orienting neuron directions while preserving all internal geometry, leading to robust control without collateral degeneration of the base diffusion model.

## 11. Comparative Discussion

### 11.1 Parameter Efficiency versus Generalisation

One of the central motivations for developing Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT) is to reconcile two seemingly opposing goals: **parameter efficiency** and **generalisability of pre‑trained knowledge**.  Traditional fine‑tuning approaches such as DreamBooth or full‑model regression tend to increase the number of trainable parameters dramatically, especially when the target task deviates significantly from the pre‑training distribution.  In contrast, the work by LoRA demonstrates that a low‑rank additive update can thinly attach a small number of trainable parameters to a massive backbone: if \(W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is the pre‑trained weight matrix, LoRA replaces it with
\[
W=W^{0}+A\,B^\intercal,
\]
where \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r'}\) and \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'}\) with a low rank \(r'\).  The parameter count scales as \(\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))\), which can be much smaller than the full matrix, but still permits significant model reshaping.

OFT takes a different tack.  It retains the full weight matrix \(W\) but restricts the transformation applied to it to an **orthogonal matrix** \(R\), applied multiplicatively:
\[
W=R\,W^{0},
\]
with \(R^\top R=I\).  Because an orthogonal matrix can be parameterised with only \(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) degrees of freedom, the number of trainable parameters becomes \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) in the worst case.  In practice, **block‑diagonal parameterisation** shrinks this further to \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\), where \(r\) is the number of blocks.  Empirically, this is typically an order of magnitude less than LoRA’s parameter budget when matching the rank \(r\) to the block size, as illustrated in the experimental reports.

In terms of *generalisation*, CFOT augments OFT by adding a hard radius constraint \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\).  This guarantees that each trainable weight stays close to its pre‑trained value while respecting the hyperspherical structure, thus preventing the model from drifting into regions that compromise the *semantic knowledge* encoded in the pre‑trained network.  The experiments in the original paper clearly show that OFT and COFT preserve subject identity in DreamBooth‑style tasks while avoiding the generation collapse seen in standard fine‑tuning.  The constrained variant further narrows the search space, leading to **more stable convergence** and **fewer required training iterations**, which directly translates to lower computational cost during training.

### 11.2 Orthogonality vs. Low‑rank Updates: LoRA

At first glance, OFT and LoRA look similar—they both keep the pre‑trained model as a reference and introduce a small, structured, trainable component.  However, **the nature of the transformation is fundamentally different**.

- **LoRA**: Adds a *low‑rank* matrix to the pre‑trained weights:
  \[
  W = W^{0} + (A B^\intercal).
  \]
  This construction is *additive* and allows the update to lie in a subspace of lower dimensionality.  The expected effect is that the model can learn new, task‑specific patterns while largely retaining the pre‑trained basis due to the small magnitude of the additive term.

- **OFT**: Applies a *multiplicative* orthogonal transformation:
  \[
  W = R W^{0},
  \]
  with \(R\) orthogonal.  Because orthogonal transformations preserve norms and angles, **pair‑wise neuron similarity** remains untouched, thereby maintaining the *hyperspherical energy* of the pre‑trained layers.  This invariance is crucial: hypotheses in the paper suggest that the angles between neurons encode high‑level semantics, and preserving those angles ensures that the latent manifold learned during pre‑training is not corrupted.

Moreover, LoRA’s additive update can disturb the delicate balance of directional relationships among neurons, potentially leading to **information loss**.  In practice, this is observed as a decline in subject fidelity and an increase in training instability.  OFT learns an *orthogonal* \(R\) that can rotate the entire neuron space but keeps the inherent geometry intact.  Coupled with the optional scaling factor in re‑scaled OFT, the method gains a touch of flexibility while still guarding the pre‑trained hyperspherical structure.

### 11.3 Inference Overhead: OFT vs. ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter

A significant practical advantage of OFT and COFT stems from the **absence of additional inference overhead**.  Since the fine‑tuned weights are explicitly computed as \(W=R\,W^{0}\) before inference, downstream processes need not modify the pipeline: one can replace the original weight matrix in the pre‑trained model with the product of the learned orthogonal matrix and the original weights.  In other words, inference occurs exactly as if the model had been trained once from scratch with the new weights—no extra attention layers, no re‑insertion of control branches, no runtime overhead.

In contrast, methods such as **ControlNet** or **T2I‑Adapter** append a *control‑specific* subnetwork or adapter modules to the pre‑trained backbone.  During inference, the control signal must be fed through these additional modules and then merged (e.g., via attention or gating) back into the main diffusion process.  This extra computation increases memory usage and inference latency.  Furthermore, ControlNet requires a separate control‑only forward pass for each control condition, whereas OFT merely requires the multiplication of the orthogonal matrix that has already been applied during training.

The paper emphasises that since OFT maintains a *single* set of adapted weights, it can be deployed exactly like the original model, achieving the same end‑to‑end speed as the baseline diffusion model—a decisive benefit for production deployments where latency is critical.

### 11.4 Convergence Smoothness and Ease of Hyper‑Parameter Tuning

In the experiments, the authors demonstrate that both OFT and COFT exhibit markedly **smooth convergence curves**.  Unlike LoRA or standard fine‑tuning, which can show abrupt changes in loss and sudden divergences, OFT’s train‑time dynamics are governed by rotations on a hypersphere.  Because the orthogonal constraint keeps the weights on a *continuous manifold*, gradient updates move the weights along a smooth geodesic path, thereby preventing the model from collapse or boundary‑crossing problems that typically happen with unconstrained updates.

The **constrained variant COFT** further stabilises training by enforcing \(\|R-I\|\le \epsilon\).  This hard radius penalty is easily implemented via projected gradient descent on the skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) in the Cayley parameterisation.  The hyper‑parameter \(\epsilon\) essentially tunes the *flexibility* of the fine‑tuning: a large \(\epsilon\) approximates plain OFT and yields faster adaptation, whereas a small \(\epsilon\) keeps the model close to its pre‑trained state, trading off flexibility for stability.

Because the loss surfaces are smoother and the search space is bounded, practitioners can use fixed learning rates and epoch counts without extensive hyper‑parameter sweeps.  In the reported subject‑driven experiments, both OFT and COFT reached satisfactory performance after just 400 iterations, whereas standard DreamBooth or LoRA required 2,000 iterations before exhibiting signs of collapse.  The reduced **training data requirements** (e.g., 5 % of ADE20K still yielding competitive results) further emphasise the practical utility of the method.

### 11.5 The Role of Hyperspherical Energy as a Semantic Benchmark

A key insight underlying OFT is the **use of hyperspherical energy** as a structural metric.  Given a weight matrix \(\bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\dots,\bm{w}_n\}\), the hyperspherical energy is
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1},
\]
where \(\hat{\bm{w}}_i=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|\) are the unit‑norm neurons.  This quantity captures the pair‑wise angular similarity among neurons; a smaller energy indicates more uniformly spread directions.  Crucially, the authors prove that applying an orthogonal transformation \(R\) to all neurons leaves \(\text{HE}\) invariant because cosine similarities are preserved under orthogonal rotations:
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W})=\text{HE}(R\bm{W}).
\]
Thus, maintaining the same hyperspherical energy ensures that the *semantic manifold* learned during pre‑training is **preserved** up to rotation.  Empirically, models tuned with OFT demonstrate higher DINO and CLIP‑I scores—a proxy for subject fidelity—than LoRA or DreamBooth.  These findings validate the hypothesis that preserving neuron angular relationships keeps the *semantic knowledge* intact.

Hyperspherical energy also serves as a **metric for model robustness**.  The constrained variant COFT explicitly penalises deviations in \(\|R-I\|\), thereby limiting the change in \(\text{HE}\) to the minimum possible.  In practice, this results in *more aggressive* but *safer* fine‑tuning: the network is allowed to rotate but cannot distort the angular structure that underpins the pre‑trained generative capacity.

Finally, the use of hyperspherical energy creates a principled objective that is **independent of the dimensionality** of the weight matrix.  Unlike Euclidean distance—which can be dominated by large‑scale shifts—energy focuses purely on angular relationships, making it a more robust indicator of semantic preservation across diverse architectures (from fully connected layers to convolutions and transformers).

---

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning offers a compelling trade‑off space:

| Aspect | OFT / COFT | LoRA | ControlNet / T2I‑Adapter |
|--------|------------|------|-------------------------|
| **Parameter budget** | \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/r)\) via block‑diagonal | \(\mathcal{O}(r'(d+n))\) | High (extra control subnetwork) |
| **Generalisation preservation** | Hyperspherical energy invariant | Limited by additive update | Depends on control module integration |
| **Inference overhead** | None (weights pre‑multiplied) | None (weights added) | Extra modules, more latency |
| **Convergence** | Smooth, stable, early plateau | Can explode or collapse | Sudden emergence, unstable |
| **Hyper‑parameter tuning** | Fixed LR/epochs, optional \(\epsilon\) | LR sensitive | More tunable (control scaling, modules) |
| **Semantic fidelity** | High (DINO/CLIP‑I) | Variable | Variable |

OFT and COFT thus navigate a nuanced intersection of structural preservation, computational economy, and practical deployability, making them highly suitable for fine‑tuning large text‑to‑image diffusion models for downstream tasks that demand both fidelity and controllability.

# 12 Open Problems and Future Directions  

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework has shown that constraining neuron directions via layer‑shared orthogonal transformations can produce stable, sample‑efficient finetuning for text‑to‑image diffusion models. Nevertheless, several open questions remain that limit the method’s scalability, expressivity, and applicability across modalities.  In this chapter we discuss five promising research directions that will help researchers and practitioners unlock the full potential of OFT.  

---

## 12.1 Scaling Orthogonal Parametrisation to Very Large Layers  

A core ingredient of OFT is the explicit enforcement of orthogonality for each layer’s transformation matrix.  The Cayley parametrisation  

\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\]

requires inversion of the matrix \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\) for every forward and backward pass.  While the block‑diagonal adaptation reduces the size of the matrices that must be inverted, the cost still grows quadratically with the block size, and the inverse itself is a bottleneck when the dimensionality \(d\) is in the tens or hundreds of thousands (as is typical for the attention weight matrices in large diffusion models).  

**Potential solutions**  
1. **Iterative Solvers** – Replace the exact inverse with a few iterations of a Krylov subspace method (e.g. Conjugate Gradient) that can be differentiated.  Since \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\) is close to the identity for small increments in \(\mathbf{Q}\), low‑accuracy solutions may suffice.  
2. **Low‑Rank Perturbation** – Exploit the fact that \(\mathbf{Q}\) is usually sparse or low‑rank; use the Woodbury matrix identity to avoid a full inverse.  
3. **Spectral Decomposition** – For block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\), pre‑compute an eigen‑basis and update only the diagonal entries, thereby transforming the inversion into element‑wise operations.  
4. **Mixed‑Precision and Block‑wise Synchronisation** – Perform the inversion asynchronously on GPU Tensor Cores with 16‑bit precision, synchronising only when a significant change in \(\mathbf{Q}\) occurs.  

A systematic study comparing these approximations in terms of numerical stability, training time, and downstream generative quality would lay the foundation for practical, large‑scale OFT deployments.

---

## 12.2 Compositionality of Multiple Orthogonal Transforms across Tasks  

One of OFT’s appealing properties is that the product of orthogonal matrices is itself orthogonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}_2 \mathbf{R}_1 \in \mathbb{O}(d) \quad \text{if}\quad \mathbf{R}_1,\,\mathbf{R}_2 \in \mathbb{O}(d).
\]

Thus, if we finetune a diffusion model for several distinct downstream tasks—with each task assigning its own orthogonal transform \(\mathbf{R}^{(k)}\)—we can compose them to obtain a single transform that preserves all tasks simultaneously.  This compositionality opens the door to *continual learning* and *multitask* scenarios.

**Key challenges**  
- **Inter‑task interference**: Does the composition of transforms retain the fidelity of each individual task, or does it orthogonalise too far away from the pretrained weights?  
- **Resource sharing**: How many tasks can reasonable share a single set of orthogonal matrices before performance degrades?  
- **Transferability**: Are there optimal strategies for ordering or grouping tasks to minimise cumulative drift?  

**Experimental avenues**  
- Benchmark a curriculum of tasks such as subject‑driven generation, edge‑to‑image, and pose‑to‑face, training orthogonal updates sequentially and evaluating each task’s recall after composition.  
- Investigate group‑wise or hierarchical orthogonal transforms where \(\mathbf{R}^{(k)}\) acts only on subspaces dedicated to specific tasks.  

Successful demonstration of stable compositionality would transform OFT into a universal adapter capable of real‑time, multi‑Task image synthesis without re‑optimization.

---

## 12.3 Relaxing Block‑Diagonal Bias While Keeping Parameter Budgets Low  

Block‑diagonal orthogonalisation offers pragmatic parameter savings by decoupling the transformation across groups of neurons.  However, this structure imposes an implicit bias: neurons inside a block share the same rotation, which may limit expressivity in layers where cross‑block interactions are critical (e.g., multi‑head attention, depthwise‑separable convolutions).

**Alternatives to explore**  
1. **Low‑Rank Plus Structured Sparsity** – Represent \(\mathbf{R}\) as a sum of a low‑rank matrix and a sparse grid, combining the benefits of both orthogonality and parameter efficiency.  
2. **Anchored Rotations** – Introduce a set of “anchor” vectors that enforce partial orthogonality while allowing free rotation in the orthogonal complement.  
3. **Learned Subspace Partitioning** – Train a small auxiliary network that learns to partition neurons into blocks that minimise a cross‑block mutual coherence loss.  

A controlled ablation study examining the trade‑off between block size, parameter count, and downstream fidelity would provide practical guidelines for deploying OFT in resource‑constrained environments.

---

## 12.4 Exploring Other Invariant Measures for Finetuning  

Hyperspherical energy captures the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, but it is only one of many structural invariants that could be leveraged to preserve or adapt a pre‑trained model.  Extending OFT to incorporate richer invariants may improve semantic fidelity and stability.

**Candidate invariants**  
- **Mutual Information** between neuron activations and output classes (or prompts) – could maintain representational diversity.  
- **Spectral Properties** of layer matrices, e.g. preserving the spectrum of singular values ensures that the model’s expressiveness is retained.  
- **Graph‑Based Embeddings** of neuron connectivity patterns, preserving higher‑order relationships.  

**Methodological sketches**  
- Add a regulariser \(\mathcal{L}_{MI} = \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[ \text{MI}(f(x); y) \big]\) that penalises drift in mutual information between hidden activations and target labels or prompts.  
- Impose a spectral Clipping constraint: \(\|\Sigma(\mathbf{W}) - \Sigma(\mathbf{W}^0)\|_F \le \delta\) where \(\Sigma(\mathbf{W})\) denotes the singular value spectrum.  
- Use a graph Laplacian penalty to keep the adjacency structures of neuron weights approximately invariant.

Systematic comparison of these invariants with hyperspherical energy would clarify whether they provide complementary benefits, or whether hyperspherical energy alone suffices for a wide range of tasks.

---

## 12.5 Extending OFT to Other Modalities  

While the current work focuses on text‑to‑image diffusion, the principle of preserving pairwise angular relationships through orthogonal transformations is modality‑agnostic.  This raises the possibility of applying OFT to **audio‑to‑image**, **video diffusion**, or **multimodal alignment** tasks.

**Audio‑to‑Image Diffusion**  
Audio signals can be embedded into a latent speech representation that is then fed into a diffusion model.  OFT could be used to finetune the audio encoders so that pitch or timbre variations correspond more tightly to visual textures without losing the global audio‑visual correspondence learned during pre‑training.

**Video Diffusion**  
Video diffusion models often use spatiotemporal attention mechanisms with large 4‑D kernels.  Orthogonal finetuning could maintain the global coherence of temporal dynamics while adapting the model to specific video styles or thematic constraints.  

**Multimodal Retrieval and Alignment**  
When aligning modalities (e.g., aligning CLIP image embeddings with text embeddings), OFT could be employed to re‑rotate one modality’s embeddings, thereby preserving the original embedding geometry while improving cross‑modal retrieval accuracy.

**Open questions**  
- How does the dimensionality of audio or video feature spaces affect the feasibility of block‑diagonal orthogonal parametrisation?  
- Does preserving hyperspherical energy in multimodal embeddings guarantee improved cross‑modal semantic alignment?  
- What new invariants become relevant when the diffusion model must honour temporal consistency or audio‑visual synchronisation?

Pursuing these extensions would broaden OFT’s applicability into domains where multimodal generation is increasingly demanded.

--- 

In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning has opened a new avenue for controlled diffusion‑based generation, yet its full potential remains untapped.  Addressing scalability, compositionality, parameter bias, richer invariants, and modality generalisation will be the focus of the next wave of research.  As the community tackles these challenges, we can expect diffusion models that are not only more controllable and efficient but also more adaptable across a sweeping variety of tasks and data modalities.

## 13.1 Recap of Key Findings

### Preservation of Hyperspherical Energy  
The most striking theoretical contribution of OFT is the explicit safeguarding of the hyperspherical energy (HE) of each weight matrix. HE, defined as
\[
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^{n}\left\|\hat{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{w}}_{j}\right\|^{-1},
\quad \hat{\bm{w}}_{i} \;=\; \frac{\bm{w}_{i}}{\|\bm{w}_{i}\|},
\]
encapsulates the pairwise angular relations among neurons on the unit hypersphere. By enforcing that the HE of the finetuned weights equals the HE of the pre‑trained weights, OFT guarantees that the full inter‑neuron geometry is preserved. Empirically this translates into stronger semantic retention: diffusion models continue to honour the wide variety of concepts encoded during pre‑training, even after drastic domain shifts such as subject‑driven or controllable generation.

### Orthogonal Transformation as an Efficient Update Rule  
Instead of the classical gradient descent with tiny learning rates, OFT reframes finetuning as learning a multiplicative orthogonal matrix per layer:
\[
\bm{W} \;\leftarrow\; \bm{R}\bm{W}^{0}, \quad \bm{R}^{\top}\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]
This update is parameter‑efficient—only the entries of the orthogonal matrix need training—and, because orthogonal matrices preserve angles and norms, they act as smooth deformations on the weight manifold. The engineered constraint \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\epsilon\) in COFT further tames the transformation to remain a small perturbation of the original weights, ensuring proximal optimisation in weight space.

### Superior Empirical Performance  
Across two canonical downstream tasks—subject‑driven image synthesis via DreamBooth and controllable image generation via ControlNet—both OFT and COFT outperformed LoRA and the baseline DreamBooth approach in quantitative metrics (e.g., DINO, CLIP‑I, CLIP‑T, LPIPS) and in qualitative fidelity to desired control signals. Notably:

- **Fast Convergence**: OFT reached competitive performance in as few as 8–16 training epochs, whereas LoRA required 20 or more, and ControlNet a much longer curriculum.
- **Stability Across Iterations**: OFT’s performance remained steady with both small and large fine‑tuning schedules, whereas LoRA suffered de‑generation beyond a threshold number of iterations.
- **Sample Efficiency**: With only 5 % of a large segmentation dataset, OFT could already produce accurate semantic control, a regime where other methods still faltered.

These empirical gains are underpinned by the theoretical safeguard on HE, illustrating that preserving neuron geometry is not a mere nicety but a pivotal enabler of robustness.

## 13.2 Where Does Orthogonal Finetuning Fit in the Ecosystem of Diffusion Finetuning?

|  Approach  |  Size of Update |  Orthogonality? |  Explicit Preservation? |  Extra Inference Overhead? |
|------------|-----------------|-----------------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| Pivotal Tuning | Fine‑grained gradients (small LR) | No | No | No |
| LoRA | Low‑rank additive update \(\Delta \bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}^{\top}\) | No | No | No |
| ControlNet | Additional MLP modules for control signals | No | No | Yes (per‑inference path) |
| T2I‑Adapter | Adapter layers on attention and MLP | No | No | Yes (per‑inference path) |
| **OFT / COFT** | Orthogonal matrix multiplication | **Yes** | **Yes (HE)** | **No** |

The table illustrates that OFT exists at the intersection of parameter efficiency and structural preservation:

1. **Parameter Efficiency** – By sharing a single orthogonal matrix per layer, OFT controls N weights with only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) learnable parameters, compared with the \(\mathcal{O}(d \cdot n)\) gradient entries in vanilla fine‑tuning or the rank‑\(r\) multiplicative updates of LoRA.
2. **Structural Preservation** – The orthogonal update guarantees that all pairwise angles among neurons remain identical to the pre‑training configuration, a guarantee absent in other methods.
3. **Operational Footprint** – Importantly, the finetuned weights can be collapsed back into a single weight matrix before inference, incurring zero extra computation or memory during model deployment. This stands in contrast to adaptation modules that keep an auxiliary inference pathway.

Consequently, OFT can be viewed as a “lightweight, geometry‑aware” bridge between cold‑start diffusion models and domain‑specific fine‑tuning needs. It is ideally suited when memory budgets, inference latency, or safety constraints forbid the introduction of auxiliary modules.

## 13.3 Take‑away Lessons for Practising Researchers and Practitioners

1. **Angularity Matters** – In high‑dimensional neural representations, the direction of weights (neuron angles) encodes the majority of semantic content. When fine‑tuning a generative model, focusing on preserving or modestly rotating these directions yields superior semantic retention compared with wholesale weight updates.

2. **Orthogonality Provides Implicit Regularisation** – Constraining updates to an orthogonal manifold introduces a powerful implicit regulariser: all neurons remain unit‑normed and pairwise angles remain bijective under the transformation. This dramatically stabilises training, especially when the downstream dataset is tiny or highly imbalanced.

3. **Hyperspherical Energy Is a Practical Metric** – Instead of monitoring Euclidean weight distances, tracking changes in HE offers a direct, interpretable measurement of how much inter‑neuron geometry is altered. Low HE change correlates with preserved generative fidelity.

4. **Parameter Efficiency Can Be Unleashed With Structured Orthogonality** – The block‑diagonal Cayley parameterisation balances flexibility against sparsity, enabling large‑scale diffusion fine‑tuning where each matrix may be tens of thousands of dimensions. Practitioners should experiment with the block size \(r\) and sharing strategy to trade off expressivity and memory consumption.

5. **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) is a Safety Valve** – Adding a Frobenius norm bound \(\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq \epsilon\) curtails drastic rotations that could happen on very few data points. In practice, setting \(\epsilon\) between \(0.05\) and \(0.2\) often yields a sweet spot where semantic control is achieved without drifting too far from pre‑training.

6. **No Inference Overhead: A Deployment Advantage** – Since the finetuned weight matrix is obtained by a single matrix multiplication during training, the model can be exported as a standard diffusion checkpoint. This is critical for industrial deployment pipelines that require minimal runtime modifications.

7. **Composable Orthogonal Updates** – Because orthogonal transformations compose (\(\bm{R}_{2}\bm{R}_{1}\) is again orthogonal), training multiple downstream tasks sequentially and then applying the product of their learned orthogonal matrices preserves all tasks. This opens tantalising avenues for multi‑task or continual learning settings.

8. **Application‑Specific Conditioning** – In controllable generation, OFT can be integrated by learning orthogonal matrices that condition on the control signal, for instance, by weighting the Cayley matrix \(\bm{Q}\) with a learnable embedding of the control prompt. This keeps the global inference cost unchanged while providing task‑specific modulation.

9. **Hyperparameter Simplicity** – The sole remaining knobs are the block size \(r\) (affecting parameter budget) and the penalty \(\epsilon\) (affecting stability). There is no need for a finely tuned learning‑rate schedule or early‑stopping logic, reducing the engineering burden.

10. **Future Directions** – While OFT has proven stability and efficiency, several research avenues remain: (i) developing differentiable, scalable matrix‑inverse approximations that fully eliminate the Cayley bottleneck, (ii) exploring non‑orthogonal but structure‑preserving transforms (e.g., Householder reflections) that might offer richer expressivity, and (iii) investigating the transferability of orthogonal updates across diverse diffusion back‑bones beyond Stable Diffusion.

---

By harnessing the geometry of neuron angles, Orthogonal Finetuning turns the fine‑tuning problem from an unmoored parameter optimization into a disciplined, geometry‑conservative deformation on the weight manifold. The resulting models exhibit faster convergence, greater stability, and higher fidelity across a gamut of downstream generation tasks—all while requiring no additional inference cost. For researchers wrestling with the nuances of diffusion model adaptation, OFT offers a principled, lightweight, and deploy‑ready answer.
