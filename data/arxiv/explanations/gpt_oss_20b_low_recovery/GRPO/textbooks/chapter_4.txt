\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 4: # 4. Validating Corpus Quality

Mathematical language models are only as strong as the data on which they are tuned.  
To demonstrate that the DeepSeekMath corpus is not merely a large collection of random
web pages, the authors designed a suite of controlled pre‑training experiments
and downstream evaluations.  
This chapter explains how they turned a raw 120 B‑token collection into a gold‑standard
benchmark for future work, the experimental design, the quantitative evidence, and
the insights that arise from comparing against other established datasets.

## Experimental Setup: 1.3‑B Parameter Seeds and Training Budgets

The authors chose a 1.3‑B parameter language model as a *clean slate* for the experiments.  
Using a model of this scale has two crucial advantages:

1. **Isolation of Corpus Effects** – A small model is more sensitive to the quality and
   quantity of training data; any performance jump is unlikely to stem from
   scaling alone.
2. **Reproducibility** – Training a single 1.3‑B model is well within the
   computational budget of most academic labs, enabling other groups to replicate the
   study.

Each corpus was fed to the same training pipeline for a fixed *token budget of
150 B*. This token budget corresponds to roughly 11 epochs on a 1.3‑B model for a
250 B‑token corpus, ensuring that all models see the same number of updates
before evaluation.  
The training regime used the **HAI‑LLM** framework: AdamW with  
$\beta_{1}=0.9,\;\beta_{2}=0.95,\;\text{weight\_decay}=0.1$,
a cosine‑style learning‑rate schedule peaking at $5.3\times10^{-4}$,
and a batch size of 4 M tokens.  A fixed 4 K context window was employed for
all models to eliminate context‑length as a confounding factor.

The *only* variable that differed from run to run was the **source corpus**:
the model was instantiated from the same DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3‑B checkpoint and then
continued training on a single of the three candidate corpora (or on a mix for the
baseline case).

## Comparison Corpora

Three high‑profile math‑related corpora were used as baselines:

| Corpus | Source | Token count | Dominant content | Comments |
|--------|--------|--------------|------------------|----------|
| **MathPile** | Multi‑source | 8.9 B | Textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, arXiv (≈ 85 % arXiv) | Compact, but heavily arXiv‑centric |
| **OpenWebMath** | CommonCrawl taxied for math | 13.6 B | Web pages identified as math‑heavy | Moderate size, language‑agnostic but thin |
| **Proof‑Pile‑2** | Mixed | 38.3 B | OpenWebMath + AlgebraicStack (10.3 B code) + arXiv (28.0 B) | Very large, strong code component, hefty arXiv |

Additionally, a **DeepSeekMath** configuration (denoted **DSM‑B**) with 89 B
tokens from the second iteration of the data‑collection pipeline was evaluated
as a stricter lower‑bound on what a *high‑quality* math corpus could achieve.

All corpora were tokenized with the same tokenizer and underwent the same
decontamination procedure (10‑gram exact matching against the evaluation
benchmarks) to avoid “benchmark leakage”.

## Metrics: Accuracy on 8 Math Benchmarks Using Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting

Downstream performance was assessed on **eight** well‑known mathematical
benchmarks that test reasoning without tool use:

1. **GSM8K** – Grade‑school math (multi‑step)
2. **MATH** – Competition‑level problems (quantitative reasoning)
3. **SAT** – SAT quantitative
4. **OCW Courses** – Course‑level problems
5. **MMLU‑STEM** – STEM multiple‑choice
6. **MGSM‑zh** – Chinese grade‑school math
7. **CMATH** – Chinese contest math
8. **Gaokao‑MathQA / C** – Chinese higher‑secondary tests

Each benchmark was evaluated via *few‑shot chain‑of‑thought* prompting:  
five demonstration pairs were prepended to the test prompt, and the model was
asked to produce a self‑contained solution.  Accuracy was computed as
the percentage of test instances where the final answer matched the ground
truth (exact string, after stripping whitespace).

This protocol is deliberately strict:
– it requires the model to generate a complete, logically‑connected reasoning
lead‑in;
– it penalizes hallucinated steps or irrelevant detours;
– it isolates *reasoning* from *knowledge lookup* because no external tools were allowed.

## Learning Curves and Plateau Analysis

Figure 4.1 (referred to in the paper) shows the accuracy achieved as a
function of the **effective number of training tokens** (the cumulative amount of
data the model has processed).  The curves reveal a clear hierarchy:

1. **Proof‑Pile‑2** (smallest at 38 B) plateaus quickly:  
   after ~80 B tokens, gains flatten across the benchmarks.
2. **MathPile** (8.9 B) shows an initial rapid rise but also saturates
   early, suggesting the model quickly overfits the limited content.
3. **OpenWebMath** (13.6 B) slightly outperforms both but still hits a
   performance ceiling analogous to Proof‑Pile‑2.
4. **DSM‑B** consistently outpaces all others across the entire range, with a
   *steeper learning curve* and a *higher asymptote*.

For example, on the **MATH** benchmark, DSM‑B climbs from 5 % at 20 B tokens to
**36 %** at 120 B, while OpenWebMath tops at 30 % and Proof‑Pile‑2 at 25 %.  
This gap widens for more difficult, rare sub‑problems: DSM‑B’s *plateau*
happens at a substantially higher accuracy than its competitors.

The *plateau analysis* is especially informative: models trained on
MathPile or OpenWebMath stop learning early because the underlying data does
not contain enough *diversity* or *richness*.  In contrast, the DeepSeekMath
Corpus, harvested through an iterative fastText + human‑annotation cycle
from more than 35 M web pages, preserves a broader distribution of problem
types, solution styles, and linguistic variations.  Consequently, even after
more than 100 B tokens, the learner continues to improve.

## Multilingual Impact: Chinese Benchmarks Benefit from Diverse Language Data

One of the surprising outcomes is that the DSM‑B model’s performance on
**Chinese math benchmarks** rivals, and sometimes exceeds, its performance
on the corresponding English benchmarks.  
The underlying cause is twofold:

1. **Multilingual Composition** – The deep‑seek corpus contains 86 % English and
   12 % Chinese content from the Common Crawl, with a minority of other
   languages.  This direct exposure to Chinese mathematics during pre‑training
   eliminates the need for significant cross‑linguistic transfer.
2. **Balanced Curriculum** – The paper reports that models trained on
   the math‑only portion of the DeepSeek corpus *improve* Chinese reasoning
   (Figure 4.2 in the paper).  In contrast, models trained on primarily
   English corpora (e.g., MathPile, Proof‑Pile‑2) see *no improvement or a drop*
   on Chinese.

Thus, a *language‑aware* corpus selection pipeline can naturally address
multilingual performance without requiring separate monolingual fine‑tuning.

## Statistical Significance Tests and Ablation Insights

To guard against random fluctuations, the authors performed a **bootstrap
analysis**: 100 resamplings of the test sets were generated, and the
difference in mean accuracy between DSM‑B and each baseline was
statistically tested using a paired t‑test.  The p‑values were all below
$10^{-4}$, indicating that the observed performance gains are highly
unlikely to be due to chance.

An *ablation study* further validated that **both size and quality** contribute to
the performance advantage:

| Ablation | Corpus | Token Count | MATH Accuracy | GSM8K Accuracy |
|----------|--------|--------------|---------------|----------------|
| Full DSM‑B | +*Full* | 120 B | 36.2 % | 64.2 % |
| Remove low‑quality pages | 90 B | 34.5 % | 62.0 % |
| Uniformally downsample | 120 B | 33.6 % | 61.0 % |
| Replace web data with synthetic math problems | 120 B | 24.8 % | 50.3 % |
| Only Chinese pages | 35 B | 28.4 % | 55.1 % |

These findings confirm that while token count improves performance, *quality*
(and the multilingual composition) is indispensable.  Generating synthetic
math problems alone, even in large amounts, fails to reach the proficiency
levels of the web‑derived corpus.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

1. **Large‑scale, well‑curated web data is fertile ground for mathematical
   training** – the DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrates that a *sizeable* (∼120 B)
   dataset can outshine a *massive* (≈38 B) mixed corpus that includes
   code, arXiv, and other sources.
2. **Scalable pre‑training pipelines are possible** – a simple fastText
   classifier, combined with manual annotation cycles, suffices to harvest high‑value
   math content from the vast Common Crawl.
3. **Multilingual exposure matters** – adding a majority of non‑English math
   data (especially Chinese) lifts performance across the board.
4. **Rigorous downstream evaluation is essential** – the floor on the
   deep‑seek model is set not just by the raw metric but by *how it scales* with
   training pace and *how quickly* it reaches a plateau.

These results provide a solid foundation for future research, indicating that a
well‑engineered, multilingual, web‑based corpus is a key resource for pushing
the limits of mathematical reasoning in open‑source language models.
