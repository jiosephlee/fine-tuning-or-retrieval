\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 10: # 10. Unified Paradigm for Sequential Training Methods

Mathematical reasoning is one of the most demanding “structured” tasks that large language models (LLMs) must master.  The community has explored many ways of refining a base model after pre‑training—supervised fine‑tuning (SFT), reward‑guided methods such as rejection sampling fine‑tuning (RFT), direct preference optimization (DPO), and reinforcement learning (RL) approaches like proximal policy optimization (PPO) and its variant Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).  These methods differ in data flow, reward signal, and optimization algorithm, yet they all share the same underlying learning objective: steering the conditional distribution of the model toward responses that are judged preferable by some external signal.

In this chapter we formalize a single **gradient‑coefficient abstraction** that captures every method in Table 1 of the paper.  We then use it to compare empirical trends (offline vs online sampling, outcome vs process supervision, iterative RL) and to explain why Group Relative Policy Optimization delivers the largest gains for mathematical reasoning, while giving researchers a unified lens to propose future improvements.

---

## 10.1 The Component Triplet

Every training pipeline can be decomposed into three mutually independent components:

| Symbol | Meaning | Typical instantiation in the methods discussed |
|--------|---------|-----------------------------------------------|
| \( \mathcal{D} \) | **Data source** – the set of question–response pairs the model sees during an update | *Offline*: SFT dataset; *Online*: outputs sampled from the current policy |
| \( r_{\varphi} \) | **Reward function** – a scalar that tells how good a given answer is | *Rule‑based*: correctness oracle; *Model‑based*: learned pairwise preference or scalar reward |
| \( \mathcal{A} \) | **Algorithm** – the way data and reward are turned into a parameter gradient | SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO, etc. |

With this triplet we can rewrite every method uniformly as

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\!\mathcal{J}(\theta)\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\!\Big[
\, \tfrac{1}{|o|}\!\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}\!
GC_{\mathcal{A}}\!\bigl(q,o,t,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t \mid q,o_{<t})\,\Big].
\tag{10.1}
\]

*Equation 10.1* is simply a “policy gradient” formula where the only ingredient that can vary across methods is the **gradient coefficient** \(GC_{\mathcal{A}}\).  Everything that tells us which events to favor, which to discourage, and by how much, is encoded in this coefficient.

---

## 10.2 Gradient Coefficient Formalism

The gradient coefficient \(GC_{\mathcal{A}}\) encapsulates **both** the reward signal and the algorithmic mechanism that transforms it into updates.  In the paper we see three distinct forms:

1. **SFT** – *unweighted* gradient coefficient  
   \[
   GC_{\text{SFT}} \;=\; 1.
   \]
   Every token is treated with equal importance; no reward is required.

2. **RFT** – *binary* coefficient based on a correctness oracle  
   \[
   GC_{\text{RFT}}
   \;=\;
   \mathbf{1}\!\bigl\{ r_{\varphi}(q,o)\!>\!0 \bigr\},
   \]
   where the reward is +1 if the answer is correct, 0 otherwise.  The model is trained only on output examples that survive the filter.

3. **DPO** – *pairwise* coefficient that compares two sampled responses
   \[
   GC_{\text{DPO}}
   \;=\;
   \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\bigl( -\!\bigl(r_{\varphi}(q,o^+)-r_{\varphi}(q,o^-)\bigr)\bigr)}.
   \]
   Here the reward model scores two responses \(o^+,o^-\) for the same question, and the gradient pushes the probability mass toward the higher‑rewarded one.

4. **PPO/GRPO** – *unbiased RL* coefficients  
   \[
   GC_{\text{PPO}}
   \;=\;
   \min\!\Bigl[
          \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}\! \hat A_t,\,
          \text{clip}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}},\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\!\hat A_t
        \Bigr]
   \]
   where \(\hat A_t\) is the advantage computed by Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). GRPO replaces the advantage estimator with a *group‑relative* one (Section 10.4) and removes the auxiliary value network.

The **definition** of \(\hat A_t\) is what drives the *strength* and *direction* of learning.  For RFT the advantage is simply \(\hat A_t = r_{\varphi}(q,o)\), whereas for GRPO it is the *relative* difference between the reward of the current sample and the average reward of the group that shares the same question.  Thus the sign and magnitude of the coefficient directly encode *why* a token should become more (or less) probable.

## 10.3 Offline vs Online Data Sampling

### 10.3.1 Offline Sampling

In offline settings (SFT, RFT, DPO), the data source \(\mathcal{D}\) is a pre‑existing labeled or filtered dataset.  The policy is frozen during data generation; all updates use the same static string of text.  Benefit: **stable**, **cheap** to sample.  Drawback: the training signal may rapidly saturate because the policy distribution cannot change the data it sees.

### 10.3.2 Online Sampling

In online settings (PPO, GRPO, Online RFT), \(\mathcal{D}\) is produced by *sampling from the current policy*.  Consequently, the data stream reflects the model’s evolving capabilities.  Empirically, as shown in the paper’s Figure 10 (online vs offline RFT), online RFT eventually surpasses offline RFT, indicating that exploration of the policy’s own behavior brings richer, more informative examples as training progresses.

*Why does online sampling help in math?*  
Mathematical tasks are highly compositional.  The early policy produces mostly incorrect, short, or unstructured answers.  Once the policy learns to output steps that obtain partial credit, the difference between correct and incorrect samples becomes sharper.  This signals a stronger reward gradient for correct completions, which offline sampling can’t provide once the training data has saturated.

## 10.4 Online versus Offline Reinforcement Learning

In reinforcement learning, the policy is **updated** on the very data that it *generated*.  The paper compares two styles:

1. **Offline RL** (e.g. RFT, DPO) – policy never sees its own outputs during data collection, so the state–action pairs in the hypothetical trajectory are not influenced by the evolving policy.  
2. **Online RL** (PPO, GRPO) – policy samples new outputs every training step; the batch of generated trajectories becomes the next training data.

Key empirical finding (Figure 10 in the paper): **online RL, when combined with a group‑relative reward, yields larger gains**—particularly on the *out‑of‑distribution* tasks (e.g., CMATH, MGSM‑zh).  The intuition is that online RL stresses *sampling diversity*; the model is continually encouraged to avoid the local optimum of repeated, high‑reward samples that are already learned, whereas offline RL can quickly plateau.

---

## 10.5 Process vs Outcome Supervision

The reward model may provide a *single scalar* per outcome (e.g., the correctness of the final answer) or a *sequence of scalar rewards* aligning with each reasoning step (process rewards).

- **Outcome Supervision** assigns the same reward to every token of an output.  
  \[
  \hat A_{i,t}\;=\;\tilde r_i.
  \]
  While simple, it ignores the *temporal* structure of solving a problem.

- **Process Supervision** (used by GRPO+PS) assigns a reward at the end of each sub‑step (e.g., after each derived equation).  
  \[
  \hat A_{i,t}\;=\;\sum_{k\ge t}\tilde r_i^{\text{step}(k)}.
  \]
  This gives *fine‑grained signals* to the model, encouraging the generation of correct intermediate steps, not just the final answer.

Empirical evidence (Section 10.4 of the text) shows **process supervision + online RL** achieves the *highest* MATH scores (51.7 %) on the 7B model, compared to outcome‑only (46.8 %).  For many mathematical problems, a single bad intermediate step can dead‑end the entire reasoning chain; process supervision explicitly penalizes that, steering the policy toward *valid reasoning sequences*.

## 10.6 Iterative RL and Replay Buffers

During long RL runs a *reward model* trained on early samples quickly becomes stale.  The paper’s **iterative GRPO** recycles the policy’s own outputs to continually refresh the reward model:

1. Sample a batch of \(G\) outputs from the current policy on each question.  
2. Compute rewards with the current reward model.  
3. Add the newly labeled examples to a *replay buffer* that holds a mixture of the latest 10 % samples and a fixed 10 % of historical data.  
4. After the policy update, the reward model is re‑trained (or fine‑tuned) on this buffer.

This two‑phase loop guarantees two things:  
- The **reward signal stays aligned** with the current policy distribution.  
- The **model is protected** from catastrophic forgetting of earlier (possibly simpler) patterns.

Indeed, the paper reports that iterative GRPO improves the 7B model’s performance by about 5 % relative, confirming the value of recurrent reward fine‑tuning.

## 10.7 Lessons from Rule‑↔︎ Model‑Based Reward

The reward function can be generated by an explicit *rule* (e.g., correctness oracle) or by a *neural* *model* (trained on pairwise human preferences).  Several observations arise:

1. **Rule‑based rewards** are cheap, deterministic, and *orthogonal*: they only check whether the final answer is correct.  They are *poor* at describing *why* a solution is wrong; the policy can only learn to avoid doomed answer patterns, not to improve intermediate reasoning.

2. **Model‑based rewards** can capture *relative quality*—not just binary correctness.  A trained reward network can reward *better* steps even if the final answer is still incorrect, providing a *gradient* within the space of partially correct solutions.  This is precisely what GRPO exploits: by comparing a group’s outputs, it uses the *relative* reward to drive the policy toward the *best* among the group.

The paper shows that on the same data the *model‑based* reward combined with GRPO outperforms rule‑based RFT by over 5 % on the MATH benchmark.  This demonstrates how *soft* preferences better align with the complex structure of a mathematical solution than hard binary correctness checks.

## 10.8 Application: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO sits cleanly in the unified framework:

- **Data source**: *online* – a group of \(G\) responses drawn from the current policy.  
- **Reward**: *model‑based* – a learned scalar comparison model evaluated on each output.  
- **Algorithm**: *online RL*, with a **group‑relative advantage**  
  \[
  \hat A_{i,t}\;=\;\frac{r_i-\bar r_{\mathcal{G}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}},
  \]
  where \(\bar r_{\mathcal{G}}\), \(\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}\) are the mean and std. of rewards in the group \(\mathcal{G}\).

Because reward is *normalised within a group*, GRPO scales naturally with the size of the reward model’s output distribution and **eliminates the need for a costly value network** (the critic in PPO).  The memory and compute savings are significant: on a 7B model training for 500 B tokens, GRPO uses ~3 × less GPU memory than PPO.  Functionally, the advantages arise from:

1. **Relative comparison** gives the model a *direct signal* of how much better or worse its answer is compared to peers, even when the absolute reward is noisy.  
2. **Group‑based baselines** reduce variance without extra learnable modules.  
3. **KL regularisation** against the reference policy (the SFT model) enforces *conservatism*—the policy cannot deviate too far from the base distribution while still receiving a large advantage.

The result is a 4 % absolute boost on GSM8K and a 5 % boost on MATH for a 7B model—the largest improvement reported in the paper across all methods.

## 10.9 Practical Take‑aways

| Aspect | Recommendation | Why |
|--------|----------------|-----|
| Data Source | Prefer *online* sampling for RL on complex tasks. | Maximises diversity and relevance of the data with respect to the current policy. |
| Reward | Use a *model‑based* reward that captures relative quality. | Enables fine‑grained feedback beyond binary correctness. |
| Algorithm | Adopt *group‑relative* advantage estimation (GRPO) or similar offline baselines. | Eliminates critic overhead and stabilises learning. |
| Reward Signal Granularity | Employ *process supervision* when step‑wise correctness matters. | Directly rewards correct intermediate reasoning, essential for math. |
| Iteration | Iterate the reward model with a replay buffer. | Keeps the reward aligned with the shifting policy distribution. |
| Scale | Keep the reference model static during RL. | Prevents catastrophic drift and enables accountability. |

For researchers wishing to **reproduce** or **extend** the methods, the paper’s codebase (released publicly under a permissive license) includes full implementations of code‑and‑math pre‑training pipelines, the GRPO algorithm, and the data‑collection pipeline for the DeepSeekMath Corpus.  Researchers are encouraged to apply the unified paradigm to *other domains* (e.g., legal reasoning, physics) by simply swapping the reward module while keeping the data‑sampling block unchanged.

---

## 10.10 Concluding Thoughts

The abstraction introduced in equation (10.1) unifies a spectrum of seemingly disparate training regimes under a single lens: **gradient‑coefficient**.  By explicitly modeling how *data*, *rewards*, and *algorithms* interact, we can:

- Diagnose why some methods stall (e.g., offline RFT can’t leverage policy change).  
- Systematically design new variants (e.g., adding a “temperature” factor to the group‑relative advantage).  
- Transfer insights across tasks (e.g., using process supervision for theorem proving).  

The DeepSeekMath work demonstrates that *engineering data* (the 120B‑token math corpus) and *engineering algorithms* (GRPO) are both critical.  The unified paradigm clarifies their roles and offers a roadmap for future advances in mathematical reasoning for LLMs, and indeed for any structured generation task.
