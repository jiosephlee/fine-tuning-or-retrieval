\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 11: # 11. Limitations, Ethical Considerations, and Open Questions

## 11.1 Overview

DeepSeekMath has demonstrated remarkable progress in mathematical reasoning for open‑source large language models (LLMs). Yet, like every complex system built on noisy data and approximate optimisation, it inherits a spectrum of limitations. Understanding these shortcomings is essential for researchers who wish to use, extend, or build upon DeepSeekMath, and for stakeholders who rely on it for sensitive mathematical tasks. This chapter dissects the most salient weaknesses of DeepSeekMath—cognitive, data‑driven, procedural, and safety‑related—while reflecting on the ethical implications that arise from them. Finally, we outline concrete research directions that may bridge existing gaps, encourage principled open‑source advancement, and ensure responsible deployment.

---

## 11.2 Geometry and Theorem‑Proof Weaknesses

### 11.2.1 Empirical Evidence

Where competition‑level benchmarks such as **MATH** and **GSM8K** show substantial gains, geometry‑heavy problems (e.g., involving Euclidean proofs, trigonometric identities with multiple angles, or Euclidean plane geometry) remain a pain point. In dry‑runs conducted by the authors, questions concerning triangles, circles, ellipses, and geometric transformations often led to incorrect or incoherent derivations. Similarly, the miniature formal–proving benchmark **miniF2F** indicates that despite solid *informal‑to‑formal* performance, many symbolic proof sketches raised gaps that could not be bridged by automated provers such as Sledgehammer.

### 11.2.2 Why Does Geometry Fail?

1. **Sparse Geometric Content in the Pre‑Training Corpus**  
   The DeepSeekMath Corpus, despite its size (120 B tokens), derives largely from Common Crawl. The proportion of geometry‑rich text—particularly formal textbooks, problem sets with constructions, or proof‑intensive discussions—appears below the threshold of the model’s learning capacity. Since geometric reasoning relies heavily on spatial intuition and multistep theorem chaining, insufficient exposure erodes the model’s internal representations.

2. **Ambiguity of Symbolic Geometric Descriptions**  
   Geometric statements in natural language often use imprecise language (“in a triangle with angle A > B”), or the phrasing varies wildly across cultures. The model struggles to disambiguate such descriptions without explicit figure references or formalised axioms. Consequently, it may hallucinate or misinterpret the given circles or angles, leading to chain‑of‑thought failures.

3. **Limited Formal Reasoning in the Training Pipeline**  
   Although the paper introduces **offline process‑supervised RL** and uses a reward model that compares outputs, the training data for RL contains only a handful of geometric reasoning problems. The reward model, trained primarily on general correctness, may not penalise subtle geometric errors effectively (e.g., a misapplied proportionality rule in a triangle). Moreover, RL conflicts with the **rule‑based** evaluation of the MATH benchmark, which emphasises the correctness of the final answer rather than intermediate geometric relationships.

4. **Normalization Bias in Tool‑Integrated Reasoning**  
   For problems that benefit from Python or symbolic engines (e.g., *sympy*), the model can compute many geometric quantities numerically. However, the **learning signal** generated from the reward model is round‑off‑aware: a near‑correct numerical result may still be deemed “incorrect” under the stringent exact‑match oracle. As a result, the model may avoid relying on tools for geometry, preferring to output an incomplete or wrong derivation.

### 11.2.3 Potential Remedies

- **Augment the Corpus with Geometry‑Centric Collections**  
  Curate dedicated geometry datasets such as Euclid‑in‑Action, AoPS Geometric Problem Sets, or geometry Olympiad archives; leverage existing processors that transform diagram descriptions into formal invariants.
- **Incorporate Figure‑Based Data**  
  Integrate multimodal training that pairs textual descriptions with diagram images, enabling the model to learn visual‑symbolic correspondences. This could be realised through Vision‑Language pre‑training on geometry textbooks augmented with diagrams.
- **Refine Formalization Workflows**  
  Extend the **informal‑to‑formal** pipeline to thicker probability distributions over secondary axioms. Even a slightly increased success rate in formulating Isabelle proofs could provide richer supervision for geometry reasoning.
- **Design Geometry‑Specific Reward Models**  
  Construct a process reward model that explicitly scores intermediate geometric facts (e.g., angle equality, similarity, proportion) rather than only final answer correctness.

## 11.3 Benchmark Contamination Subtlety

### 11.3.1 Background

The DeepSeekMath authors acknowledge a meticulous **contamination filtering** step: any 10‑gram segment matching an evaluation benchmark fragment (e.g., GSM8K or MATH) is removed from the training data. However, the *granularity* of this filter raises concerns.

### 11.3.2 Hidden Contamination Pathways

1. **Cross‑Document Synonyms**  
   Many Google‑search‑indexed pages paraphrase benchmark problems verbatim, but with synonyms or re‑ordered clauses. A purely 10‑gram match may miss these variant phrasings, allowing accidental leakage.

2. **Sub‑Benchmark Overlap**  
   The authors filter **“questions or answers from English mathematical benchmarks”** individually. Yet, set‑theoretic overlap can arise: a problem from the MATH dataset might also appear in GSM8K or vice versa. If the filter blocks the specific interrogative text but leaves derivations or explanatory text, the model could still learn high‑fidelity steps.

3. **Non‑Exact Match Data Augmentation**  
   The training pipeline may reconstruct a problem by *randomly sampling* sentences from varied sources. Even if the exact wording is absent, the model can *interpolate* the hidden solution pattern from unrelated but structurally similar passages.

4. **Historical Data Batching**  
   The authors mention a *human‑annotated* refinement step. However, page‑level de‑duplication may retain near‑duplicate content (e.g., forum threads) that contain largely identical solution histories, thereby indirectly exposing the model to benchmark‑like reasoning sequences.

### 11.3.3 Diagnosing Contamination

- **Long‑term Repeat Frequency (LRF) Analysis**: For each training token, count how often it appears in the benchmark; high‑frequency tokens are suspect.
- **Cross‑Entropy Evaluation**: Evaluate the model's likelihood on benchmark test instances before and after filtering to detect over‑fitting spikes attributable to leakage.
- **Synthetic K‑Shards**: Partition the training data into k shards, train on k−1 shards, test on the left‑out shard and the benchmark; a significant accuracy gap may hint at contamination.

### 11.3.4 Mitigation Strategies

- **Stronger De‑duplication**: Use *sentence‑level* **SimHash** or **CLIP‑text** embeddings to detect near‑duplicate passages even with paraphrases.
- **Adversarial Filtering**: Train a model to detect *semantic* overlap between training and benchmark sentences, then prune the high‑overlap ones.
- **Open‑Source Benchmark Release**: Encourage the community to release hidden test sets that are known to be absent from the training corpus, thereby allowing external evaluation of contamination.

## 11.4 Scale Constraints: Few‑Shot Performance and Parameter Gaps

### 11.4.1 Few‑Shot Asymmetry

While DeepSeekMath achieves **48–50 %** accuracy on the competition‑level MATH benchmark (single‑shot), **few‑shot** experiments reveal minimal gains. The model’s ability to generalise from a handful of example explanations is impeded by its relatively shallow **parameter count (7 B)** and the lack of *meta‑learning* signals during pre‑training. By contrast, GPT‑4’s few‑shot amplification stems from its gigantic 175 B parameter space, which has a higher capacity to memorise pattern‑rich exemplar passages.

### 11.4.2 Parameter‑to‑Performance Trade‑Off

DeepSeekMath’s **7 B** parameter space is significantly smaller than the 540 B of **Minerva** and 70 B of **WizardMath**. Although the paper demonstrates that **less is sometimes more**—particularly when training data is of higher quality—the inherent capacity limits affect:

- **Depth of Hierarchical Reasoning**: Multi‑step chain‑of‑thoughts with many tokens are more likely to diverge or hallucinate in a smaller model.
- **Multi‑Lang Fluency**: The Chinese curriculum shows poorer results (notably on geometry) because the filter may have under‑represented Chinese mathematical discourse.
- **Tool‑Embedded Reasoning**: Handling code and symbolic libraries requires richer internal computational modules, a challenge in a 7 B model.

### 11.4.3 Open‑Source Trajectory

Scaling up inference‑friendly architectures (e.g., *prefix‑tuning*, *efficient transformer* variants) while retaining a 7–10 B parameter space could narrow the performance gap. Alternatively, generating *synthetic* high‑quality training data at a cheaper cost (e.g., via active learning with a smaller seed model) could compensate for the limited parameters.

## 11.5 Data Bias in Source Selection – English Domination

### 11.5.1 Origin of Bias

The DeepSeekMath Corpus, despite being multilingual, contains a dominant proportion of English text (~70 %). The remaining slice—Chinese (≈25 %)—is obtained via keyword search in Common Crawl and manual inclusion. As a consequence, learned representations encode more English mathematical jargon and 12‑point reasoning patterns.

### 11.5.2 Manifestations

- **Language‑United Benchmarks**: The model performs slightly better on **Gaokao‑Math** than **MGSM‑zh**; the disparity grows when moving to geometry‑heavy Chinese test sets.
- **Cross‑Domain Transfer**: In Arabic or Hindi numeracy problems (not directly evaluated in the paper but relevant), performance falls below chance—an indication that the model cannot generalise to non‑English syntax or representation.
- **Bias in Tool Availability**: The tool‑integrated prompts are largely defined in English (e.g., *math*, *sympy*). Thus, the model may struggle to map Chinese problem statements to appropriate library calls.

### 11.5.3 Mitigation

- **Balanced Dataset Collection**: Use language‑conditioned crawlers to collect equal volume of high‑quality math passages from native languages (e.g., Japanese Math StackExchange, Russian mathematics blogs).
- **Synthetic Alignment**: Translate existing high‑quality English datasets into other languages via neural machine translation trained on mathematics‑specific bilingual corpora, then fine‑tune.
- **In‑Domain Diversity Injection**: Include *language‑agnostic* mathematical notation (e.g., LaTeX kernels) to allow the model to recognise and translate equations regardless of surrounding text.

## 11.6 Amplification of Misalignment through RL

### 11.6.1 The Genesis of Misalignment

Reinforcement Learning (RL) with GRPO relies on a **reward model** trained to score outputs. The reward model itself is trained on *human preference* or *rule‑based correctness* data. If the reward model contains systematic biases—e.g., over‑valuing concise but incomplete answers—the subsequent GRPO training will *amplify* these biases, pushing the policy to produce those sub‑optimal yet high‑reward outputs more frequently.

### 11.6.2 Empirical Signals

- **Pass@K vs. Maj@K**: The paper demonstrates that RL improves the **majority** accuracy of Top‑K outputs (Maj@K) but not the **first‑rank** success (Pass@K). This behaviour indicates a *distribution drift* toward a more “robust” but not necessarily *correct* peak.
- **Tool‑Integrated Bias**: RL might encourage more frequent use of tools that produce clean numeric results. When a problem can be solved by a trivial computation, the model may default to this route rather than exploring deeper reasoning.

### 11.6.3 Safeguards

- **Reward Uncertainty Modelling**: Introduce Bayesian or ensemble uncertainty estimates into the reward model; penalise predictions with high epistemic uncertainty.
- **Cost‑Sensitive RL**: Weight the RL objective by a calibrated *correctness penalty* that explicitly lowers the reward for a solution that fails a stringent verification step.
- **Human‑in‑the‑Loop Auditing**: Periodically sample RL‑generated outputs across multiple prompts, annotate correctness, and feed the corrections back into the reward model.

## 11.7 Human‑in‑the‑Loop Safety and Auditing

### 11.7.1 Safety Concerns

- **Hallucinations**: Even subtle hallucinations—like mislabeling a number as a mathematic constant or assuming the presence of a directed graph—can lead to downstream errors in scientific contexts.
- **Privacy Leakage**: Data curation from Common Crawl may inadvertently contain private or copyrighted content. Even with de‑duplication, the model could reproduce entire passages if it memorised them.
- **Regulatory Compliance**: The model’s outputs, if used in grading or financial modelling, must comply with local educational standards.

### 11.7.2 Auditing Framework

1. **Content‑Level Filters**  
   Employ a pre‑generation filter that checks for *plagiarism score* (e.g., Perplexity‑based N‑gram overlap) and *coherence* (via a secondary language model).
2. **Post‑Generation Fact‑Checking**  
   For arithmetic or formulaic outputs, trigger an *automatic verifier*, such as a symbolic solver, to confirm the correctness of each equation or derivation step.
3. **Human Review Loops**  
   For high‑stakes applications, route automatic responses to a domain expert (e.g., a math teacher) before official release. Capture feedback in a *human‑audit log* to serve as future training data.

## 11.8 Future Open‑Source Trajectories

### 11.8.1 Modular RL Pipelines

- **Publicly Available RL Checkpoints**: Release trained reward models and policy checkpoints separately, allowing the community to experiment with alternative RL triplets (e.g., DPO, RFT).
- **Open Exploration Datasets**: Make raw RL sampling logs (queries, multiple outputs, rewards) downloadable so researchers can benchmark new RL algorithms on identical data.

### 11.8.2 Data‑Sharing Initiatives

- **Mathematics Corpus Consortium**: Coordinate a shared repository of high‑quality, multilingual math datasets, heavily curated for style, tokenization, and zero‑bias.
- **Benchmark‑Accompanying Datasets**: Publish the exact mathematical web pages used to train the model, with anonymisation, to facilitate contamination audits.

### 11.8.3 Robustness Benchmarks

- **Geometric Robustness Benchmark**: Introduce a dedicated subset of geometry problems that filter out trivial numeric edge cases, emphasizing diagram reasoning and theorem inference.
- **Tool‑Safety Benchmark**: Evaluate the model’s repeated use of external libraries for solving problems, tracking **adversarial mis‑call** (example: invoking an unsafe `eval` function).
- **Bias‑Audit Benchmark**: Create a taxonomy of bias‑propagating prompts (e.g., stereotypical named‑entity reasoning) and test for over‑representation in math contexts.

## 11.9 Conclusion

The Open‑Source community now has access to a **mathematical LLM** that waivers the performance frontier with 7 B parameters alone. Yet, its limitations are instructive:

- Geometry and theorem‑proof reasoning remain fragile, tied to data scarcity and training signals.
- Benchmark contamination, though mitigated, poses subtle risks that demand rigorous detection mechanisms.
- Scale imposes constraints on few‑shot flexibility, and language dominance invites biases that curb global applicability.
- RL amplification risks misalignment, particularly when reward signals lack uncertainty estimates or comprehensive coverage.
- Human oversight and safety auditing remain non‑trivial obligations, especially for high‑impact product deployments.

To address these cracks, the field should prioritize: **diverse, geometry‑rich data**; **robust contamination detection**; **parameter‑efficiency research**; **trust‑worthy reward modeling**; and **transparent safety auditing**. Only by weaving these strands into a cohesive research fabric can we ensure that mathematical LLMs become dependable partners—rather than mere predictors—across global scientific, educational, and industrial landscapes.
