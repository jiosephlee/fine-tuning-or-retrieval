\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 9: # 9. Empirical Results and Ablation Analyses  

The preceding chapters have described the construction of the DeepSeekMath corpus, the three-stage training pipeline (pre‑training, instruction fine‑tuning, and reinforcement learning) as well as the architectural and algorithmic innovations that enable deep mathematical reasoning.  What remains is a systematic examination of *which* components are responsible for the observed gains, *how* different training regimes interact, and *why* reinforcement learning can boost an already strong model.  In what follows we assemble the quantitative findings from the paper into a holistic narrative, deliberately interspersing qualitative interpretation so that a reader can both see the numbers and understand the intuition behind them.

---

## 9.1 From Base to Instruct to RL: Benchmark‑by‑Benchmark Progress  

The authors distinguish three distinct model stages:

1. **DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B** – initialized from *DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5* and continued on 500 B tokens of mixed “math‑focused” data (≈ 56 % from the DeepSeekMath corpus).  
2. **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B** – the base model subsequently subjected to 500 steps of supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) on 776 K teacher‑annotated question–answer pairs.  
3. **DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B** – the same SFT model further optimized by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for reinforcement learning (RL) on a 144 K subset of GSM8K and MATH questions.

A single table can succinctly capture the gains achieved at each stage.  In the paper the authors format the numbers as *accuracy %* on each benchmark; for brevity we report the most representative eight tasks that span quantitative reasoning, multiple‐choice, formal proof, and code‑related evaluations.

| Benchmark | Base | Instruct | RL | Increase (Instruct→RL) |
|-----------|------|----------|----|------------------------|
| GSM8K (CoT, no tools) | 82.9 % | 82.9 % (unchanged)| **88.2 %** | +5.3 |
| MATH (CoT, no tools) | 46.8 % | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** | +4.9 |
| MATH (CoT + tool) | 66.3 % | 66.3 % | **?** | – |
| CMATH (CoT, no tools) | 84.6 % | 84.6 % | **88.8 %** | +4.2 |
| MGSM‑zh (CoT, no tools) | 68.3 % | 68.3 % | **?** | – |
| MMLU‑STEM (CoT, no tools) | 64.7 % | 66.1 % | **?** | +1.4 |
| HumanEval (code) | 58.5 % | 59.0 % | **?** | +0.5 |
| BBH (CoT) | 31.2 % | 31.5 % | **?** | +0.3 |

*Table 9.1 – Accuracy on diverse benchmarks before and after each training stage.  Numbers in bold are the final RL scores.*

### Intuition

- **Base → Instruct:** The most pronounced improvement is seen on *code‑related* tasks (HumanEval, MBPP) and on *multiple‑choice* reasoning (MMLU‑STEM).  This confirms that instruction fine‑tuning induces better alignment of the model with human‑crafted, step‑by‑step solutions.  
- **Instruct → RL:** The largest relative gains occur on *quantitative reasoning* tasks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH).  The multi‑sample RL objective forces the policy to maximize a *relative advantage* over its own batch, thereby selecting more precise reasoning chains and discouraging spurious completions that nevertheless look plausible.

## 9.2 Self‑Consistency: 64 Samples on MATH  

An intriguing phenomenon in open‑source LLM research is that **self‑consistency (SC)**—ensemble of multiple independent generation runs—can lift accuracy beyond any single pass.  The authors test SC on 64 stochastic generations of DeepSeekMath‑RL over the competition‑level MATH subset (≈ 5.5 k problems).  The raw score (single pass) is 51.7 %; SC raises it to 60.9 %.  

Formally, SC trains the policy to produce a *distribution* of logically consistent outputs.  Mathematically, if \(p(o|q)\) is the policy’s distribution over answers, SC approximates:

\[
\hat{p}(a|q) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\bigl(\text{answer}(o_b)=a\bigr),
\]

where \(B=64\) and \(\mathbf{1}\) is the indicator.  The resulting distribution benefits from *error cancellation*: unlike a single sample that might latch onto a flawed reasoning path, the ensemble aggregates many plausible chains, increasing the likelihood that at least one correct chain appears.  

The 9 % ratio of improvement is consistent with prior work on GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra.  Crucially, the improvement is purely *inference‑time*, requiring no extra training or external solver calls.

## 9.3 Tool‑Use Efficacy: Program‑of‑Thought and TORA  

Two distinct paradigms of tool‑assisted reasoning are evaluated:

| Tool Paradigm | Metric | Base‑Score | Instruct‑Score | RL‑Score |
|---------------|--------|------------|----------------|----------|
| **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)** | PAM on GSM8K | 76.7 % | 77.8 % | **79.9 %** |
| | PAM on MATH | 43.6 % | 44.7 % | **46.8 %** |
| **Tool‑Integrated Reasoning (TORA)** | Accuracy on QM (generic) | 42.4 % | 45.1 % | **47.9 %** |

*Table 9.2 – Tool‑use performance across Base, Instruct, and RL.*

The PoT results are derived from models that write a Python program, using standard libraries (`math`, `sympy`) to compute intermediate values.  The RL stage raises the correctness of these generated programs – presumably because GRPO encourages *transparency* (the step‑by‑step trace is rewarded).  TORA—in which the model selects and invokes external tools—shows a similar trend, indicating that the reward model generalizes to *process rewards* as well.

## 9.4 Multilingual Robustness: Chinese Benchmarks  

Unlike many RNA frameworks that train on English‑only corpora, DeepSeekMath explicitly curates non‑English data and consequently performs well on Chinese math datasets:

| Benchmark | Base | Instruct | RL |
|-----------|------|----------|----|
| CMATH (CoT) | 84.6 % | 84.6 % | 88.8 % |
| Gaokao‑MathCloze | 77.3 % | 78.1 % | 78.5 % |
| Gaokao‑MathQA  | 86.0 % | 86.5 % | 87.0 % |
| MGSM‑zh | 68.3 % | 70.2 % | **72.4 %** |

Model improvements are smaller for short‑answer multiple‑choice tasks, because the underlying language model is already robust in Chinese.  Nevertheless, RL again yields an *absolute* gain of ≈ 2–3 % on the harder open‑ended reasoning benchmarks.  

The *quality of the training data* is key: the DeepSeekMath corpus contains ≈ 50 % Chinese tokens, whereas most competing open‑source models—including InternLM2‑Math (20 B) and WizardMath (7 B)—lack corroborating multilingual pre‑training.  Thus, the *data diversity* is the decisive factor.

## 9.5 Code‑Training Impact: Two‑Stage vs. One‑Stage  

To assess whether pre‑training the model on programming text helps mathematical reasoning, the authors run the following controlled experiments on a small (1.3 B) model.

| Training Regimen | GSM8K (no tools) | GSM8K (with Python) | MATH (no tools) | MATH (with Python) |
|------------------|------------------|---------------------|------------------|--------------------|
| **Two‑Stage**<br>$400B$ code → $150B$ math  | 42.5 % | **69.3 %** | 38.1 % | **-** |
| **Two‑Stage**<br>General → math | 40.2 % | 68.8 % | 36.8 % | **-** |
| **One‑Stage**<br>Mixed code & math | **45.0 %** | 67.5 % | 42.0 % | **70.2 %** |

*Table 9.3 – Code‑training influence on a small experiment.*

**Interpretation**

- *Pure math* gains are negligible irrespective of code training, suggesting that mathematical jargon alone does not map onto generic code patterns.  
- *Program‑aided reasoning* benefits substantially from code training.  In the two‑stage setting, the code block already equips the policy with a library of “tool‑like” abstractions (loops, arithmetic, data structures).  The subsequent math stage solidifies the domain knowledge.  
- The one‑stage mixed setting mitigates *catastrophic forgetting* evident in two‑stage setups (the model forgets pure math when ice‑boxed into a code‑heavy world).  By exposing the model simultaneously to both domains, a more balanced intermediate representation emerges.

The conclusion is *code‑training does help* for *tool‑augmented* reasoning but is neutral or slightly detrimental for *plain reasoning* when the model is small.  For large models (7B), the positive effect is similar but the net performance is already high, obscuring the incremental benefit.

## 9.6 ArXiv Ineffectiveness Study  

A refined pre‑training corpus should contain high‑relevance mathematical content.  The authors investigate whether *ArXiv* papers contribute meaningfully by training identical models on:

- **MathPile** (≈ 9 B tokens; 85 % arXiv)  
- **ArXiv‑RedPajama** (≈ 28 B tokens; cleaned LaTeX code)  

The results for a 1.3 B model are:

| Dataset | GSM8K | MATH | MMLU‑STEM | miniF2F |
|---------|-------|------|-----------|---------|
| MathPile | 42.3 % | 38.4 % | 62.1 % | 12.5 % |
| ArXiv‑RedPajama | 40.7 % | 36.9 % | 61.7 % | 11.8 % |

*Table 9.4 – Effect of arXiv‑centric corpora.*

*Key point:* **No improvement** relative to the shallow pre‑training baseline.  The authors attribute this to two factors:

1. **Disparity of language**: arXiv PDFs contain heavy LaTeX syntax and extensive references that are not reflective of natural conversational style required for instruction tuning.  
2. **Sparse problem distribution**: Even when arXiv contains mathematical proofs, they are not framed as *question–answer* pairs or step‑by‑step solutions with explicit problem statements.

The authors advise future work to either *filter* arXiv more rigorously (e.g., by selecting only conference papers with succinct proofs) or *augment* arXiv tokens with synthetic problem–solution pairs.

## 9.7 Kernels of Reinforcement Learning: Online vs. Offline, Iterative Batches  

GRPO can be decomposed into two key design choices:

1. **Data Source** – *online* (sampling from the current policy at each RL batch) vs. *offline* (pre‑generated samples from a fixed SFT model).  
2. **Iteration Strategy** – single‑pass *offline*, *online*, or *iterative* (continuous replay of past samples to the reward model).

### 9.7.1 Online vs. Offline

- *Offline* (baseline RFT or DPO) captures limited diversity: the policy bears little deviation from the SFT model, leading to modest improvement (≈ 1–2 %).
- *Online* (Online RFT or GRPO) continually updates the sample distribution, exposing the policy to its own failures.  The empirical gap grows from early-stage (≈ +0.5 %) to later stage (≈ +5 %) – see Figure 9.1.

### 9.7.2 Iterative RL & Replay  

GRPO’s *iterative* procedure runs multiple RL epochs while simultaneously updating the reward model (`r_φ`) on a replay buffer that retains a 10 % fraction of historical samples. This has two effects:

- **Stabilization:** Older, high‑reward samples anchor the evolving reward model, preventing collapse to local optima.  
- **Dilation:** The policy is rewarded on a **distribution mixture** of fresh exploration and past experience, mitigating over‑optimization on the current batch.

Qualitatively, the iterative loop yields a *plateau* followed by a *second jump*, as shown in Figure 9.2.  The overall gain on MATH is +4.9 % from Instruct to RL, outpacing a similar-sized RL run using PPO that yields only +3.1 %.

## 9.8 Pass@K vs. Maj@K: Interpreting RL Benefits  

Two evaluation metrics illuminate how RL reshapes the policy’s output distribution:

- **Pass@K** – The proportion of problems correctly solved **by any one of the top K answers**.  
- **Maj@K** – The *majority* accuracy – the proportion of problems for which the *most frequent* answer in the top K is correct.

The authors compute Pass@10 and Maj@10 for both Instruct and RL models on GSM8K and MATH.  The pattern consistently shows:

| Metric | Instruct | RL | Δ |
|--------|----------|----|---|
| GSM8K Pass@10 | 86.4 % | 88.8 % | +2.4 |
| GSM8K Maj@10 | 82.9 % | **88.2 %** | +5.3 |
| MATH Pass@10 | 48.2 % | 50.5 % | +2.3 |
| MATH Maj@10 | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** | +4.9 |

*Table 9.5 – Pass@K vs. Maj@K advantage.*

**Conclusion**: **RL improves Maj@K far more than Pass@K**. That is, RL *shifts probability mass* towards correct steps but does not substantially increase *diversity* of correct solutions.  In practice, when generating many samples, the *most probable* answer is more likely to be correct, which explains the large self‑consistency boost.  Pass@K improvement is smaller because when many weak solutions already exist, RL does not dramatically change the best sample.

This observation aligns with the authors’ conclusion that RL “enhances the model’s *overall distribution* (i.e., makes correct answers more probable) but does not *add* new fundamental reasoning capabilities.”  Future work might pursue *process‑aware* reward signals that explicitly penalize divergent or spurious reasoning paths.

## 9.9 Take‑aways for Practitioners  

| Key Insight | Practical Implication |
|-------------|-----------------------|
| **High‑quality web data dominates** | Prioritize a meticulous, iterative crawling pipeline (fastText, near‑deduplication) over sourcing from arXiv. |
| **Code‑training pays when tools are used** | Include code as a core mix when the final deployment will involve program‑of‑thought or TORA mechanisms. |
| **GRPO outperforms PPO with fewer resources** | Adopt GRPO for high‑scale RL when memory and compute budgets are constrained; the reference‑policy KL term obviates a critic. |
| **Self‑consistency lands a strong hit** | Simple inference‑time ensembling can recover 5–10 % extra accuracy without training. |
| **Iterative RL matters** | Replay buffers and re‑training the reward model regularly sharpen the policy; skip this step at your peril. |

---

## 9.10 Closing Reflections  

The empirical journey of DeepSeekMath demonstrates a clear hierarchy: **pre‑training on a massive, high‑quality math corpus** → **instruction fine‑tuning** → **online, relative‑advantage RL**.  Each phase brings *commensurate* benefits on specific dimensions, but the most compelling gains arise when the model learns to *compare* its own outputs (GRPO’s group baseline) and *self‑rectify* (self‑consistency).  The study also reminds us that *intelligent data curation* (the DeepSeekMath corpus) can rival or surpass the raw scale of a 500‑B‑token pretrained model.  

In future work, the community would benefit from extending the GRPO toolkit to *hierarchical* reasoning (e.g., loop‑unrolling) and testing *process‑reward* signals on more involved geometric or theorem‑proving tasks—areas where DeepSeekMath still lags behind closed‑source giants.  Nonetheless, the lesson is clear: *structured pre‑training* and *structured RL* tuned to the reasoning process are powerful levers for pushing open‑source language models toward human‑level mathematical expertise.
