\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: # Building the DeepSeekMath Corpus

The success of a language model geared toward mathematical reasoning hinges on the quality of the data it sees during pre‑training.  In this chapter we dissect the iterative pipeline that transforms raw Common Crawl (CC) into the 120 B‑token DeepSeekMath Corpus, the backbone of DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B.  We walk through each engineering choice—from the selection of the seed corpus to the multilingual contingency plans and contamination safeguards—so that students will understand both the intuition behind and the practicalities of large‑scale data curation for mathematics.

## Seed Corpus Selection: OpenWebMath and Positive/Negative Examples

The first ingredient is a high‑precision seed of mathematical text.  The developers chose **OpenWebMath** \citep{openwebmath} because it contains carefully curated, web‑style excerpts that already exhibit the token patterns (mathematical notation, code snippets, LaTeX fragments) we want to reproduce in the downstream model.  However, OpenWebMath is too small to bootstrap a robust classifier.  The remedy is to augment it with an equal number of negative examples—typical web pages that are *not* mathematical.

### Positive Examples
Randomly sample \(N_{+}=500\,000\) documents from OpenWebMath.  Every such document is tagged as “math” and becomes a positive training instance.  Importantly, we keep a sanity‑check set that is withheld for later validation, ensuring the classifier does not overfit to the seed.

### Negative Examples
To cover the vast diversity of CC, we randomly harvest \(N_{-}=500\,000\) documents from a fresh, URL‑pruned crawl of CC.  This set is guaranteed to have no overlap with OpenWebMath and is labeled “non‑math”.  These negatives will help the learner reach a clear decision boundary between heavily mathematical sections and generic web content.

## fastText Classifier Training and Hyper‑parameter Choices

With \(N_{+}\) and \(N_{-}\) prepared, we instantiate a **fastText** binary classifier \citep{joulin2016fasttext}.  Its lightweight architecture (single‐layer softmax over sub‑word n‑grams) is suited to the churned data: it can cheaply train millions of documents while capturing character‑level patterns that appear in mathematical notation.

| Hyper‑parameter | Value | Rationale |
|-----------------|-------|-----------|
| **Vector Dimension** | 256 | Sufficiently expressive to encode word/character n‑grams, yet small enough for fast training on commodity GPU. |
| **Learning Rate** | 0.1 | Fast convergence is critical because we train over many epochs of noisy web data. |
| **Word n‑gram Max Length** | 3 | Matches the granularity of LaTeX commands (`\int`, `\frac`) and short tokens. |
| **Minimum Word Occurrence** | 3 | Filters out rare tokens that would otherwise swamp the vocabulary. |
| **Epochs** | 3 | Empirically balances training time and over‑fitting on the noisy negatives. |

After training, the model assigns each web page a probability \(p_{\text{math}}\) that it belongs to the math class.  Because CC data is heavily imbalanced toward non‑math pages, a conservative threshold (e.g. \(p_{\text{math}}>0.85\)) is used to filter candidates.

## Web Page Deduplication and Near‑Deduplication

The raw CC dump contains billions of HTML files, many of which are near‑duplicates (e.g. mirror sites, paginated content).  Running the classifier on every page would be wasteful and would bias the corpus toward repeating the same material.  Therefore, the pipeline first applies **URL‑based deduplication** to remove exact URL repeats.  Next, a **Near‑Deduplication** step uses the Content Addressable Filesystem (CAFS) hashing technique: the body of each page is hashed with a perceptual hash, and pages whose hashes differ by fewer than a Hamming distance threshold are collapsed into a single representative.  The result is a reduced set of roughly 40 B HTML pages, which still contains the majority of unique mathematical content.

## Iterative Retrieval Cycles and Domain‑Level Enrichment

The fastText classifier is initially weak because its positive examples are too narrow (only OpenWebMath).  Consequently, after the first round of retrieval we analyze the **domain distribution** of the retrieved pages—that is, grouping pages by their base URL (e.g. `mathoverflow.net`).  For each domain we compute the fraction of pages that were retrieved.  Domains where more than 10 % of pages survive the filter are labeled **math‑heavy** (e.g. `mathoverflow.net`, `arxiv.org`, `stackexchange.com`).

We then manually annotate *content‑type* URL fragments within these domains (e.g. `mathoverflow.net/questions`).  All pages *linked* from those fragments, but not yet retrieved, are added to the seed set.  Re‑train fastText on the enlarged seed, and run the pipeline again.  This iterative cycle helps the classifier learn new lexical cues (e.g. "proof", "lemma", "conjecture") and adapt to novel domains.

After four cycles, the process converges: the set of domains that survive is saturated, and the 120 B‑token corpus contains 35.5 M pages.  At this point we stop—the extra iterations would only recover a negligible fraction of new material.

## Scoring and Ranking of Candidate Pages

Every candidate page receives a **MathScore** equal to the classifier’s posterior probability.  Sorting pages by MathScore allows us to inspect the quality spectrum.  In practice, the top 40 B tokens (the “retention budget” in the first round) yield a validation accuracy of similar magnitude to the top 80 B or 120 B, suggesting diminishing returns beyond the first 40 B.  Therefore, we time‑slice the corpus: we experiment with 40 B, 80 B, 120 B, and 160 B token subsets to gauge *token‑budget* trade‑offs.  Each subset is processed into clean plaintext, segmented into tokens using the DeepSeek tokenizer, and stored in a Sharded Parquet format for efficient pre‑training ingest.

## De‑contamination Strategies for Benchmark Overlap

A critical hazard is *benchmark contamination*: if training data contains questions that appear in evaluation sets, performance estimates become inflated.  To prevent this, DeepSeekMath’s pipeline follows the de‑contamination guideline \citep{deepseek-coder}: for each evaluation benchmark (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.), we construct a 10‑gram substring database.  Any webpage whose text contains an exact match to any benchmark fragment longer than 10 tokens is discarded *entirely*.  For shorter fragments (3–9 tokens) we apply exact matching only if the fragment occurs in a single paragraph, thereby mitigating accidental over‑rejections.

The de‑contamination step is applied *after* the final retrieval cycle, ensuring that the 120 B corpus is free of contamination.  Nevertheless, we repeat a post‑training check with an up‑to‑date version of the benchmark data; any newly added problems are flagged, and the contaminated pages are pruned in a second pass.

## Quantifying Corpus Size vs. Prior Corpora (MathPile, Proof‑Pile‑2)

To contextualize the scale of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, we compare it against two widely‑used math corpora:

| Corpus | Source | Tokens (after filtering) | Notes |
|--------|--------|--------------------------|-------|
| **MathPile** | Textbook, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, arXiv | 8.9 B | ~85 % from arXiv, high arXiv fidelity but limited web width |
| **OpenWebMath** | CommonCrawl filtered with domain heuristics | 13.6 B | Smallest token budget among publicly available web‑based math corpora |
| **Proof‑Pile‑2** | OpenWebMath + AlgebraicStack (10.3 B) + arXiv (28.0 B) | 51.9 B | Mixed high‑quality math code + arXiv |
| **DeepSeekMath Corpus** | 120 B tokens from CC | 120 B | 9× OpenWebMath, 7× Proof‑Pile‑2,  _inter‑domain_ multilingual |

The sizes are plotted on a log‑scale: each successive corpus roughly triples the token budget, with DeepSeekMath comfortably occupying the upper right corner.  The sheer volume would overwhelm a naive pre‑training schedule unless carefully staged, which is why we employ a *token‑budget evaluation* scheme (description above) to vet each candidate corpus size.

## Multilingual Analysis: English vs. Chinese Coverage

An often overlooked design choice in math corpora is language diversity.  The DeepSeekMath Corpus is explicitly multilingual: during each retrieval cycle we preserve all non‑English pages that pass the MathScore filter.  The resulting token distribution is approximately:

- **English**: 55 %
- **Chinese**: 35 %
- **Other (Japanese, Korean, Russian, etc.)**: 10 %

The prevalence of Chinese is deliberate, informed by the observation that many open math sources (e.g. `mathtree.cn`, `jisheng.net`) contain substantial problem sets and solutions.  Empirical downstream results confirm that a bilingual corpus yields better Chinese benchmark performance (e.g., CMATH, Gaokao-MathQA) without sacrificing English scores (GSM8K, MATH).  Language‑aware tokenization (subword merging across alphabets) ensures that symbols (e.g. Greek letters) are handled consistently across scripts.

By contrast, prior corpora such as MathPile and Proof‑Pile‑2 are heavily skewed toward English.  This bias explains why models trained on them often under‑perform on Chinese benchmarks.  Consequently, the multilingual design of DeepSeekMath directly translates to a competitive advantage on cross‑lingual mathematical tests.

---

### Key Takeaways

1. **Seed quality matters**: A small but high‑accuracy seed (OpenWebMath) is amplified via fastText to harvest a vast, diverse web‑based math corpus.  
2. **Iterative refinement**: Domain‑level enrichment and manual annotation elevate the classifier’s reach beyond the initial seed, striking a balance between *precision* and *recall*.  
3. **Deduplication is essential**: Without URL and near‑dedup‑ filters, the corpus would be riddled with redundant content, de‑meaning the training signal and inflating memory usage.  
4. **Token‑budget calibration**: Experiments over 40–160 B tokens reveal the point of diminishing returns, guiding efficient use of compute resources.  
5. **Contamination safeguards**: Exact‑matching against benchmark 10‑grams preserves the integrity of evaluation metrics.  
6. **Multilingual coverage**: Integrating both English and Chinese improves performance on a breadth of benchmarks, demonstrating that mathematical reasoning does not privilege a single language.  

In the next chapter, we will explore how this curated corpus feeds into the two‑stage pre‑training of DeepSeekMath‑Base, highlighting the role of code pre‑training and the eventual instruction tuning that turns raw mathematical knowledge into fluent, trainable reasoning.
