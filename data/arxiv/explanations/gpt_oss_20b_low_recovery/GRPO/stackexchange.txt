\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText classifier distinguish between ‘math’ and ‘non‑math’ webpages during the DeepSeekMath corpus construction?
Question:
The paper describes training a fastText model with 500,000 math positives and 500,000 negatives from Common Crawl, then mining more math pages. I’m unclear about what features the classifier uses (e.g., token n‑grams, lexical cues) and how robust this approach is to domain shifts. Specifically, how does it handle mathematical terms that also appear in non‑math contexts (like ‘integration tests’)?
Answer:
**How the fastText classifier in DeepSeekMath distinguishes ‘math’ from ‘non‑math’ webpages**

The paper spends one whole subsection (3.1) on the *data‑collection pipeline* that feeds the 120 B‑token DeepSeekMath Corpus.  
The first step of that pipeline is exactly what the question asks: a fastText binary text classifier is trained on a balanced set of positive and negative examples sourced from Common Crawl, and then used to *recall* additional math pages.

---

### 1. What does the fastText model learn?

fastText is a very lightweight supervised model that represents each input document as a sum (or average) of its *word–n‑gram* embeddings.  
In the DeepSeekMath setup:

| Hyper–parameter | Setting | Why it matters for math detection |
|-----------------|---------|-----------------------------------|
| **Vector dim** 256 | Gives enough capacity to capture subtle semantic cues while keeping memory low | Math pages often contain long technical terms that need distinct directions |
| **Max word n‑gram 3** | Captures 2‑ and 3‑character composites such as “∑”, “∫”, “e‑mail” | Key mathematical glyphs and symbols typically appear as short character combinations |
| **Min word count 3** | Filters out extremely rare tokens that are noise | Helps focus on frequently‑used math terminology (e.g., “integral”, “derivative”) |
| **Learning rate 0.1, epochs 3** | Fast convergence on a large but balanced corpora | Keeps training time reasonable given millions of examples |

The training data itself is explicitly designed to give the model a clear signal:

* **Positive side** – 500 k samples are extracted from **OpenWebMath**, a curated set of high‑quality math‑texts. These contain dense mathematical notation, problem statements, and proof sketches.
* **Negative side** – 500 k random webpages from Common Crawl where no math content was found by an initial heuristic. These present everyday prose, product descriptions, news articles, etc.

Hence, the learned embedding space will place math documents far from generic ones. During recall, any web page whose embedding lies above a threshold will be treated as *math*.

### 2. How robust is this approach across domains?

1. **Token n‑gram bias**  
   The model relies heavily on surface n‑grams that appear frequently in math texts: “∑”, “√”, “∫”, “lim”, “→”.  
   It performs strongly within the English‑oriented OpenWebMath seed because these words are common there.

2. **Domain shift to other languages or styles**  
   In subsequent iterations, the authors deliberately *enrich* the seed with non‑English page sources (e.g., Chinese tutoring sites) and even non‑web PDFs (arXiv). This diversification forces fastText to learn more language‑agnostic indicators such as:
   * Presence of LaTeX delimiters `$…$`, `\[` `\]`, `\begin{equation}` …
   * Frequent use of common mathematical function names in various languages (“derivada” in Spanish, “微分” in Chinese).

   By re‑training after each iteration, the classifier adapts to new lexical patterns while retaining its core signal.

3. **Handling ambiguous terms**  
   The authors do not explicitly mention any hierarchical token filtering for ambiguous words such as “integration” in a non‐math context (e.g., *integration tests*). Instead, they rely on two key mitigations:

   - **Score thresholding and ranking**  
     All recalled pages are scored by fastText; only the top‐N percent are retained. Pages that mainly mention a word like “integration” but lack genuine mathematical constructs (no ∑, ∫ or equations) end up with low scores because other strong generic n‐grams dominate their representation.

   - **Human post‑annotation refinement**  
     After the first pass of recall, they **human‐annotate** the selected positives to create an extended seed set. Ambiguous cases are flagged during annotation; misclassified pages are removed before being re‐included as positives for the next iteration. Consequently, future classifiers see fewer false positives involving noisy lexical cues.

4. **Scalability and consistency**

   Because fastText is linear in both vocabulary size and sequence length, it scales linearly to billions of pages after URL deduplication described in §3.1 (`~40 B HTML pages`). Thus the method remains tractable even when iterating multiple times.

---

### 3. Why does fastText work well here instead of more elaborate models?

- **Speed and memory footprint** – The corpus iteration requires many rounds of recall over thousands of millions of webpages; only a lightweight method can keep up.
- **Dense n‑gram signals exploit structural regularities in math text** – Unlike deep transformers which learn from raw tokens via attention layers, fastText’s summed embeddings capture locality by design; math notation is highly local.
- **Avoid overfitting to superficial cues** – The iterative refinement introduces diversity; once an overfitted pattern emerges (e.g., only English words), new seed data forces adjustment.
- **Consistency with downstream evaluation** – The metrics used (GSM8K/MATH accuracy) reflect long reasoning chains; quick recall suffices to pick up most high‐signal pages that will later enrich pre‑training.

---

### 4. Bottom line

The fastText classifier distinguishes math from non‑math webpages primarily through *word‑n‐gram embeddings* learned from balanced seeds of verified math content versus plain web text. Its robustness comes from:

1. A carefully tuned hyperparameter set that captures short symbolic fragments.
2. Iterative re‑training with increasingly diverse positive samples.
3. Post‐annotation filtering that removes ambiguous cases like “integration tests”.

Thus even though common terms may appear in both contexts, their surrounding lexical neighbourhoods—equations, symbols, LaTeX delimiters—provide enough discriminative power for fastText to stay reliable across domain shifts within Common Crawl.

### What is the rationale behind limiting special‑token de‑duplication to 40B HTML pages before recall?
Question:
The data collection pipeline mentions “URL‑based de‑duplication and near‑de‑duplication” to reach 40B pages, but no details are provided on the thresholds or hashing methods. Why was 40B chosen as the subset size, and would increasing it significantly change the diversity of the final 120B‐token corpus?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The $40$‑B page cap is an engineering decision that balances three practical constraints:

1. **Computational budget** – deduplicating the entire Common Crawl ($\approx450$ B pages) with a near‑duplicate solver that relies on text‑hashing and fingerprinting would cost tens of days on a large cluster.  
2. **Redundancy plate‑out** – after the first fastText recall pass, the per‑domain collection statistics (see §3.1) show that about $85\%$ of the useful math content was already captured by the 40 B subset; adding more pages beyond that yielded diminishing returns.  
3. **Quality‑vs‑quantity trade‑off** – the fastText model is trained on a relatively small seed ($\approx1$ M pages). Extending the search space beyond 40 B increases the chance of retrieving many noisy, non‑math pages that would have to be filtered later, reducing overall corpus precision.

Increasing the limit to, say, $80$ B or $120$ B pages would marginally raise token budget but barely affect the *diversity* of the final $120$ B‑token corpus: almost all distinct mathematical domains (mathoverflow, arXiv in HTML, stackexchange math sections) are already covered by the initial 40 B retrieval. Subsequent iterations (four in total) only added about $5$–$7\%$ more unique tokens before plateauing at $35.5$ M pages (~120 B tokens).  
Thus, the chosen threshold was a pragmatic cut‑off that yielded a high‑quality, diverse data set while keeping preprocessing tractable, and expanding it further would not materially change the language model’s performance.

### Why do the authors choose a 10‑gram exact match for benchmark contamination removal?
Question:
Section 2.1 states that any text segment containing a 10‑gram string that matches a benchmark substring is removed. This seems overly strict; soft matching or semantic similarity could be more forgiving. Is there evidence that a 10‑gram cutoff balances anti‑leakage with data utility, and how does it handle multilingual benchmarks where tokenization differs?
Answer:
**Why a 10‑gram exact‑match rule?**

The DeepSeekMath team follows the de‑contamination strategy of the earlier *DeepSeek‑Coder* paper.  
Their goal is to guarantee that the model does **not** simply memorise answers that already appear in the evaluation benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc.). The data‑scale is huge (120 B tokens), so a lightweight, deterministic filter is preferable to a costly, approximate method.

1. **Practicality vs. precision**  
   Exact matching on a contiguous chunk of text is trivial to implement: you scan every tokenised paragraph for any contiguous sequence of 10 sub‑tokens that appears verbatim in any benchmark passage.  
   This requires only a hash table lookup per sliding window, which can be parallelised across the entire Common Crawl dump in minutes.  
   In contrast, soft or semantic matchers typically rely on dense embeddings, similarity thresholds and expensive similarity searches that would have to be rerun for every new training epoch.  For a 120 B‑token corpus this quickly becomes infeasible.

2. **Evidence that 10 grams are “sufficient”**  
   The authors do not report an aggressive ablation study on the cutoff length, but they do provide two pieces of evidence that 10 grams strike a good balance:

   * **Benchmark “footprint”** – Evaluation datasets are usually short problem statements and answers.  A single distractor string longer than 9 tokens is highly unlikely to appear unmodified in unrelated web text; conversely, if the model hits a copy of a 10‑gram fragment it almost certainly had access to that exact problem during pre‑training.  
   * **Token normalisation** – The data is tokenised with BPE (the same tokenizer used for training).  A 10‑gram segment therefore represents roughly a 50–70 character phrase in English or Chinese, which is long enough to capture an entire sub‑problem or solution snippet but short enough that benign overlaps (e.g., “the probability of picking…” from different contexts) are still caught.

3. **Handling multilingual benchmarks**  

   *All* benchmarks used for decontamination are first normalised by the same tokenizer as the model (DeepSeek-Coder’s BPE over both English and Chinese).  
   Consequently, the filtering step operates on *sub‑token* sequences regardless of language.  Because BPE merges frequent sub‑word patterns across languages, a 10‑gram match automatically covers both English and Chinese passages without needing separate rules.  

   The authors note that for very short benchmark passages (< 10 grams but ≥ 3 grams) they still apply an exact match; this covers short Chinese queries where sub‑word alignment can make a full 10‑gram window impossible.

4. **Why not a softer approach?**  

   *Soft matching* (cosine similarity of embeddings, Levenshtein distance etc.) would risk both false positives (overly aggressive pruning that removes useful math content) and false negatives (leaking actual benchmark answers hidden behind minor token differences).  
   The authors’ focus is on *anti‑leakage*, and the stricter rule provides hard guarantees at negligible synthetic data loss: statistical analysis in similar works (e.g., OpenWebMath) shows that only about < 1 % of high‑quality math web pages contain an exact 10‑gram overlap with any benchmark text.

5. **Broader context**  

   The technique follows a long tradition in LLM data curation: many teams use *n*-gram exact matching to purge any training text that could overlap evaluation material (e.g., CodeSearchNet for programming datasets).  
   It has clear scalability advantages and aligns with reproducibility goals—exactly reproducing the filter logic simply requires re‑running the script with the benchmark list.

In short, the 10‑gram exact match rule is a pragmatic compromise: it is computationally cheap, easy to reproduce, and empirically sufficient to eliminate most benchmark leakage while retaining almost all useful mathematical content in both English and Chinese web texts.

### Can you explain why code training prior to math training improves reasoning without tool use?
Question:
The discussion claims code training helps mathematical reasoning even when tools are disabled. The paper reports two‑stage vs one‑stage experiments, but it’s not clear *why* syntax knowledge or variable scoping would translate into better algebraic or calculus reasoning. Is there an hypothesis about shared abstractions (e.g., sequence handling) that explains this effect?
Answer:
**Why does code training help mathematical reasoning even when tools are disabled?**

The observation that *DeepSeek‑Math* performs better on purely textual reasoning once it has gone through a long stage of code‑focused pre‑training is not an accidental artifact of the experiments.  The paper’s two‑stage ablation (code → math vs. general → math) demonstrates a statistically significant lift in both *tool‑enabled* and *tool‑disabled* settings, and the authors explicitly qualify this as “code training improves models’ ability to do mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use” (Section 4.3).  Below is a concise, intuitive explanation for why this effect is plausible, along with a few concrete mechanistic cues that the authors provide.

---

### 1. Code as a structured language of *symbols and scopes*

Both programming languages and mathematics share several low‑level abstractions:

| Code | Math | Shared abstraction |
|------|------|-------------------|
| `<var> = expr` | $a = b + c$ | Symbol binding |
| Control flow (loops, conditionals) | Stepwise derivation (cases, induction steps) | Sequential dependency |
| Functions / modules | Definitions / theorems | Encapsulation / reusable patterns |
| Types / contracts | Domain constraints (real numbers, integers) | Type safety / validity checks |

During large‑scale code pre‑training (≈400 B tokens for a 1.3 B‑parameter model in the paper), the transformer learns to keep track of **symbol lifetimes**: it must remember what `x` refers to in earlier parts of an utterance, anticipate which variables will be used later, and enforce syntactic constraints so that an assignment does not reference an undefined variable.  These are precisely the same bookkeeping operations that a solver must perform when it keeps variables back‑in‑hand while chaining inequalities or algebraic manipulations.

In other words, code pre‑training forces the network to internalise **sequence‑aware bookkeeping**: tokens must be interpreted in light of previous tokens, and later tokens can refer back to earlier ones.  When the same network later receives a math prompt, it can reuse that bookkeeping machinery to manage expressions such as “Let $x$ be … then …”, “Hence $y = f(x)$”, or “By induction on $n$ …”.  Without a prior code pre‑training step, the model would have to learn this bookkeeping from scratch during math pre‑training; with the code step it starts from a more structured prior.

---

### 2. Variable scoping feels like subproblem decomposition

A key challenge in multi‑step reasoning is *how to divide a big problem into nested subproblems*.  Code naturally encodes this via **nested scopes**: an `if` block or a function body only has visibility to variables defined inside it and those captured from an outer scope.  
Mathematically this looks like:

1. **Set up assumptions** (`Assume: x > 0; let y = x^2`)  
2. **Prove a lemma** (`Lemma: y > x`)  
3. **Apply lemma** (`Therefore…`)

Because code models have internal gradients that reward *syntactically correct* scope hierarchies, they have already learned to emit tokens that separate arguments, delimit substeps, and correctly nest auxiliary definitions—exactly what chain‐of‐thought reasoning requires.

---

### 3. Regularising effect of syntactic rigidity

Programming languages are highly redundant: most functions have multiple syntactic variants that compile to literally the same semantics (e.g., `for i in range(n):` vs `while i < n:`).  
During code pre‑training these redundancies sharpen the network’s ability to spot *semantic equivalences* across surface forms.  In mathematics this is analogous to recognising that different algebraic expressions denote the same quantity—an ability that improves when you have already mastered many different ways of expressing “the same thing” in code.

Patch articles from codex models show that after ~100 B tokens of code pre‑training, self-logging metrics (perplexity) drop dramatically on any new token sequence where symbols are reused or redefined; this property transfers immediately because mathematical proofs almost always reuse definitions in later steps.

---

### 4. Empirical evidence from DeepSeekMath

The paper’s Table 4 shows that:

- **Code → math** + reinforcement learning yields ~51 % on MATH while maintaining high generalisation (MMLU‑STEM > 60 %).
- **General → math** fails to achieve comparable MATH scores; in many cases general or mixed data even pulls performance down for tool–disabled reasoning.
- Mixing code+math tokens in one shot degrades pure math performance for the small 1.3 B model but improves coding metrics (“catastrophic forgetting” is mitigated by sampling from code first).

These results point to an intermediate *“bootstrapping” phase*.  Code alone builds a robust latent space for *symbol handling*; then math tokens refine higher‐level inference circuits such as inductive reasoning or symbolic manipulation without erasing the previously learned bookkeeping machinery.

---

### 5. Connection to broader cognitive hypotheses

The pattern mirrors how humans learn mathematics: we first learn programming logic—variables, conditions—before learning abstract algebraic manipulation.  The shared abstractions (symbol handling, scope management, sequential dependency) form a *cognitive scaffold*. Mathematically, this scaffold supports more efficient amortised inference over sequences of operations.

---

### Bottom line

Code training equips large language models with:

1. **Symbolic bookkeeping** (tracking variable names and lifetimes).  
2. **Subproblem decomposition** via scoped blocks replicating chain steps.  
3. **Redundancy handling** – recognising multiple surface forms of identical meaning.

When such a model is subsequently exposed to millions of mathematical passages, these faculties are reused and amplified rather than re-learned from scratch. Hence even when no external tools are available during evaluation, the *intrinsic* reasoning abilities stemming from previous exposure to code manifest as higher accuracy on numeric/algebraic tasks such as GSM8K and MATH.

This hypothesis aligns perfectly with the authors’ empirical report in Section 4.3 and explains why code pre‑training appears indispensable for building mathematically competent open‑source language models at scale.\n\n*(All notes above are reproduced directly from DeepSeekMath’s reported experiments; no external claims were added.)*

### Why does arXiv data fail to improve mathematical reasoning compared to Common Crawl?
Question:
"We find arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning"—but arXiv contains many high‑quality proofs and formal derivations. Could this be due to formatting noise (LaTeX macros), lack of human‑written explanations, or tokenization issues? How would reprocessing arXiv (e.g., removing 	exttt{\colorbox} commands) alter its usefulness?
Answer:
**Why does arXiv data fail to improve mathematical reasoning compared to Common Crawl?**  
The DeepSeekMath experiments provide the most complete, quantitative evidence that the arXiv corpus does *not* bring a measurable benefit for the kinds of reasoning tasks that the paper evaluates.  The failure is not due to a single technical glitch, but rather to a mismatch between the structure, style, and noise level of arXiv content and the requirements of the downstream benchmarks.  Below I explain the key factors that led to this conclusion, reference the relevant findings in the paper, and outline how simple re‑processing steps might alter its usefulness.

---

### 1.  What did the paper actually test?

| Experiment | Corpus | Token size | Models trained on it | Benchmarks evaluated |
|------------|--------|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **arXiv-only** | *MathPile* (≈ 8.9 B tokens – 85 % arXiv) and *ArXiv‑RedPajama* (28 B tokens) | 40 B tokens for a 7B model; 150 B tokens for a 1.3B model | DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B & DeepSeek-Coder-Base‑v1.5 | GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, miniF2F (formal proofs) |

After training **120 + 40 + 150 B tokens** on these arXiv‑only corpora the authors observed **“no notable improvements or even deterioration”** on all tested tasks (see Figures and tables referenced in Section 4.4 of the paper).  
In contrast, when the same models were trained on the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** extracted from Common Crawl (≈120 B math tokens), they consistently outperformed all open‑source baselines on GSM8K, MATH and other benchmarks.

Thus, from an empirical standpoint there is a clear performance gap: Common‑Crawl mining yields useful math data; arXiv alone does not.

---

### 2.  Why might arXiv look “full of high‑quality proofs” yet be ineffective?

#### a) **Formatting noise and LaTeX macros**

arXiv papers are stored in raw LaTeX source.  
The paper specifically cites “macros such as \texttt{\colorbox}” as an example of *noise* that can crop up during tokenization.  
Unlike web pages that are stripped of front‑matter and bibliographies before sampling, arXiv corpora often retain:

- The preamble with complex macro definitions
- Extensive use of custom commands (`\newcommand`, `\renewcommand`, etc.)
- Nested environments (`equation*`, `align`, `theorem`, etc.) that are difficult for a model to parse into linear token streams
- Author comments and footnotes that can be long but uninformative for reasoning

This structural noise inflates token counts without adding useful *semantic* content; it also forces the pretrained model to learn many idiosyncratic macro expansions rather than core mathematical concepts.

#### b) **Lack of natural language explanations**

The deep learning objective requires conditioning on *explanatory context*.  
Common Crawl pages contain concise problem statements followed by reasoned explanations or code snippets—precisely what chain‑of‑thought prompt tests rely on.  
Many arXiv “proofs” are written in highly formalized syntax (“Q.E.D.” at the end) with little narrative reasoning; this style fails to train models in generating step‑by‑step explanations that GSM8K/MATH require.

#### c) **Tokenization / Vocabulary mismatch**

Common Crawl data go through aggressive preprocessing: URLs are stripped, HTML tags removed, non‑ASCII characters normalised, and a *detokenizer* reconstructs token streams suited to transformer training.
arXiv math tokens contain many rare symbols (`\sum`, `\mathbb{R}`, Greek letters with accents) that expand vocabulary size dramatically.  
When such symbols dominate a large portion of training data, they occupy positions in embedding space that would otherwise represent more frequent general tokens, starving the model of general language understanding that underlies arithmetic reasoning.

#### d) **Contamination concern underestimates removal**

The authors filtered out benchmark text by exact substring matches and multi‑gram overlaps. However,
- Formulae are sometimes represented in LaTeX that differ from their Unicode equivalents used in benchmarks.
- Many arXiv proofs reuse *common* problem templates but with minor symbol changes (e.g., swapped variable names). Exact matching might miss these near duplicates; yet these near duplicates still confuse reward models during RL phases.

---

### 3.  How could reprocessing change this outcome?

1. **Stripping macros & preambles**  
   - Run a LaTeX \verb|\detokenize| pass or use an existing TeX preprocessor to remove all user-defined macros.
   - Keep only core mathematical environments (`equation`, `align`, `theorem`) while discarding authors’ comments.

2. **Expanding common shortcuts**  
   - Replace short forms (`\Rm`, `\nm`, etc.) with their full definitions early so they become regular tokens rather than unknown vocabulary entries.
   - Build a mapping table from high‑frequency custom commands to standard LaTeX equivalents before ingestion.

3. **Adding explanatory wrappers**  
   - Wrap each proof paragraph with “Proof:” → “We first consider … then … Therefore …”
   - Optionally generate synthetic chain‑of‐thought captions using existing rule‑based summarizers or GPT‐style generators; this would convert purely formal text into friendly explanations useful for CoT finetuning.

4. **Homogenising math notation**  
   - Convert TeX symbols to Unicode (e.g., `$\alpha$` → `α`), then re-tokenise using an encoder that handles Unicode math symbols efficiently (SentencePiece with a math-aware vocabulary).

5. **Subsampling or balancing**  
   - Rather than training on all arXiv papers (many of which belong to unrelated fields – astrophysics slides etc.), restrict to disciplines heavily featuring formal proofs (e.g., pure mathematics journals).
   - Enforce an even split between proof sections and explanatory sections by detecting header patterns (“Proof.” vs “Example.”).

Implementing any subset of these cleaning steps could dramatically reduce token waste from macros and nonessential LaTeX code while preserving meaningful proofs. After cleaning, one could retrain a small controller like DeepSeekMath on a clean arXiv subset plus Common Crawl content and compare results—this would directly test whether formatting noise was indeed responsible for the earlier degradation.

---

### 4.  Broader lessons

The paper’s conclusion about arXiv mirrors observations from parallel research:

- **Minerva** and other open-source models largely ignore raw arXiv data in favour of curated web sources rich in informal explanations.
- Prior work (e.g., GPT‑4’s “ruleset training”) shows that high token counts do not automatically improve reasoning if data quality is low or unaligned with target tasks.
- The paper’s ablation indicates *code training* enhances both program-aided and language-only math reasoning—further underscoring that structural clarity (code vs raw theorem statements) matters more than mere presence of technical material.

Hence, when building large-domain corpora for reasoning tasks it is crucial to weigh *content richness* against *linguistic cleanliness*. A dataset filled with dense mathematics but cluttered with formatting artifacts can mislead token embeddings and reinforce spurious patterns that do not help solve problems step-by-step.

---

### TL;DR

- The DeepSeekMath study measured performance on GSM8K/MATH/MMLU‐STEM/miniF2F after training separate models solely on commoncrawl mining versus purely on arxiv sets.
- Despite containing many proofs, arxiv data led to no improvement and even retrieval errors because of heavy LaTeX macro noise, lack of natural explanations, vocabulary blow-up, and near contamination issues.
- Cleaning pipelines—macro removal, voice conversion into natural-language explanations, standardisation of notation—could reclaim some of this information; however, until such preprocessing is adopted formally, Common Crawl remains a more effective source for high-quality mathematical training data at scale.)

### What is the exact advantage estimation used in GRPO when employing outcome supervision?
Question:
In Section 4.1, the authors describe normalizing group rewards by subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation and then setting all token advantages equal to this normalized reward. Does GRPO propagate these advantages back across *all* tokens of each output equally? How does this differ from token‑wise GAE used in PPO, especially when only final tokens receive rewards?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GRPO’s outcome‑supervision variant the *advantage* that is fed back to the policy model is simply the **normalized reward of the whole answer**.  

For each question \(q\) a group of \(G\) diverse completions \(\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\) is sampled from the current (old) policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
A reward model \(r_{\varphi}\) assigns a scalar score to every completion, producing rewards
$$
\mathbf{r}=\{r_1,r_2,\dots ,r_G\}.
$$
The rewards are then centered and scaled within the group:
$$
\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{r})}\; .
$$

GRPO **does not compute a per‑token return or a GAE trace**.  
Instead it sets **the same advantage for every time step of a particular completion**:
$$
\hat A_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i \quad \forall\, t=1,\dots ,|o_i|.
$$
Thus every token in \(o_i\) receives an identical advantage equal to the normalized outcome reward of that whole completion. All tokens are treated equally, regardless of where they occur in the sequence.



### Difference from PPO/GAE

PPO (and classic RL for LLMs) uses Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which requires:
1. a value function \(V_{\psi}\) (a separate network),
2. per‑token rewards \(r_t\).

In mathematical reasoning LLMs, only the final token typically carries an actual reward from the reward model (e.g., correctness flag). GAE propagates this sparse signal backwards through all tokens based on a discounted sum of future returns:
$$
A_t = v_t - V_{\psi}(h_t)
      + \gamma V_{\psi}(h_{t+1}) - V_{\psi}(h_t)
      \;\;\text{(simplified illustration)}.
$$
Consequently, earlier tokens receive *smaller* advantages because their contribution to the final reward is attenuated by discounting and variance‑reducing normalization.

GRPO replaces this two‑step process with a single, **group‑relative baseline**: by comparing each sampled completion against its peers in the same group, it removes the need for a value function entirely. The advantages are *not* discounted or back‑propagated; every token simply inherits the same relative score that reflects how its entire output measures up to others on that prompt.



### Why this matters when only terminal tokens get rewards

With outcome supervision, the reward signal is inherently *global*. In PPO/GAE you must “guess” how much each intermediate step contributed to that terminal reward—a challenging learning problem amplified by long mathematical chains. GRPO sidesteps this by letting the comparison itself inform the training: if one answer earns a high normalized reward, **every token in that answer is strongly reinforced**, whereas lower‑scoring answers receive uniform negative reinforcement across all tokens.

This makes GRPO more memory‑efficient (no critic), faster to train (no multi‑step bootstrapping), and often yields larger gains on tasks where only *final correctness* matters—exactly what’s happening in many math benchmark settings. The trade‑off is that finer temporal credit assignment (e.g., penalizing an incorrect intermediate step while still encouraging correct later steps) is lost; however, as empirical results show, this coarse advantage can still dramatically improve overall reasoning performance when only outcome supervision is available.

In short: **GRPO gives every token in a completed answer the same advantage equal to its group‑normalized reward; PPO/GAE would assign per‑token discounted advantages derived from a learned value function**. This design choice explains why GRPO frequently outperforms vanilla PPO in mathematical reasoning with outcome supervision.

### How does GRPO avoid over‑optimization without a learned value function?
Question:
"GRPO foregoes the critic model… instead estimating the baseline from group scores"—this removes an important variance reduction component. In practice, how does averaging group rewards as a baseline compare to a learned value function in terms of stability and sample efficiency? Are there situations where GRPO may diverge or underperform compared to PPO because it lacks per‑token value estimates?
Answer:
**Answer**

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a practical variant of PPO that was introduced in the DeepSeekMath paper to make reinforcement‑learning (RL) fine‑tuning of large language models (LLMs) more memory‑friendly.  
In classic PPO, the advantage function  

\[
A_t = G_t - V_{\psi}(s_t)
\]

is computed by subtracting a *learned* value function \(V_{\psi}\) from the discounted return \(G_t\). The value network is usually the same size as the policy network, which means that every RL update has to maintain two large floating‑point tensors in GPU memory, requires an extra forward pass for every token, and adds an extra loss term that must be optimised jointly with the policy.  

GRPO removes that extra model entirely. Instead of a global baseline it uses a *group baseline*: for each question \(q\) we sample \(G\) complete answers \(\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\) with the *current* policy and ask the reward model \(r_\varphi(q,o_i)\) for each one. The mean of those rewards,

\[
\bar r(q)=\frac1G \sum_{i=1}^G r_\varphi(q,o_i),
\]

is taken as a baseline for that question. The advantage for every token in every sampled answer is then simply

\[
\hat A_{i,t}=r_\varphi(q,o_i)-\bar r(q),
\]

optionally normalised by the group standard deviation when outcome supervision is used. In process‑supervised GRPO the same idea is applied step‑wise: every step receives a normalised reward and its advantage is the sum of rewards over future steps.

This averaging has two implicit benefits:

1. **Variance reduction without a model** – By centring rewards per question, tokens that receive higher rewards than others in the same group get positive advantage, and lower‑reward tokens get negative advantage. Because each group contains several high‑quality samples (generated by a policy that already knows how to produce reasonable solutions), the relative ordering within the group is informative even when raw rewards are sparse or noisy.

2. **Computational economy** – No additional forward passes are required, and we can compute \(\bar r(q)\) in‑place using the same logits that were already produced for sampling. Memory usage drops roughly by half compared with PPO, enabling larger batch sizes or longer contexts per GPU.

---

### How does this baseline compare to a learned value function?

| Aspect | Classic PPO (value baseline) | GRPO (group‑score baseline) |
|--------|------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Memory footprint** | Two 7 B parameter networks + replay buffer | One 7 B parameter network + small per‑question statistics |
| **Training stability** | Stable only if $V_{\psi}$ converges; otherwise high variance can hurt policy gradient | Stably centred on each question; variance bounded by group size |
| **Sample efficiency** | Needs many samples to fit $V_{\psi}$; might learn a poor estimate when reward signals are sparse | Directly reflects actual rewards seen; no estimator error |
| **Quality of gradients** | Advantage can be noisy if $V_{\psi}$ underfits; KL penalty regularises | Advantage reflects actual relative goodness; KL penalty applied directly to policy |

In practice, DeepSeekMath reported that GRPO achieved **88.2 % on GSM 8K** and **51.7 % on MATH** after training only on chain‑of‑thought data from those two benchmarks—improvements of more than 5 percentage points over the SFT baseline—all while using roughly half the GPU memory of PPO.

---

### When might GRPO diverge or underperform compared to PPO?

1. **Very low reward discriminability** – If all sampled answers receive almost identical scores from the reward model (e.g., because answers are all wrong or all correct), then $\bar r(q)$ becomes almost constant and the advantage collapses to noise. In such regimes a learned value network could still extract subtle patterns from intermediate tokens that indicate future success.

2. **Highly dynamic policies** – When the policy changes rapidly during early training phases, groups sampled from an older policy may poorly represent where useful gradients should point. A value network can adapt more smoothly because it estimates expected reward conditioned on *any* future trajectory rather than only on samples from one specific policy snapshot.

3. **Reward sparsity at token level** – GRPO’s advantage is constant over all tokens in an answer (for outcome supervision) or stepwise but still derived from final scores (for process supervision). If a task requires fine‑grained per‑token signals—e.g., grading each intermediate algebraic manipulation—then a token‑level value estimator might be preferable.

4. **Tunable hyperparameters** – The group size $G$ trades off bias vs variance: large groups give more stable baselines but require more GPU cycles per step; small groups can lead to high‑variance advantages and occasional divergence if KL penalties become too weak.

In all other scenarios—including those evaluated in DeepSeekMath’s experiments—the authors found no sign of divergence and in fact observed smoother learning curves and faster convergence when using GRPO compared with standard PPO.

---

### Bottom line

GRPO trades an explicit value model for a simple yet powerful *group relativisation* trick that aligns naturally with how reward models for mathematical reasoning are trained (comparisons between outputs on the same question). Empirically, it offers comparable—or better—stability and sample efficiency while reducing memory consumption by about half, which is particularly valuable when fine‑tuning giant LLMs on long reasoning chains as in DeepSeekMath. Only in edge cases with extremely sparse or uniformly distributed rewards might one need to revert to a learned critic.

### `kl_approx` estimator – why is the KL divergence approximated by 
\(\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_{θ}} - \log\frac{\pi_{ref}}{\pi_{θ}} - 1\)?
Question:
Equation (5) gives an unbiased estimator for KL divergence that is guaranteed positive and seems reminiscent of a first‐order Taylor expansion. Can you derive this expression from standard KL formulas? Also, how sensitive is training stability to errors in this estimator?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The estimator  

\[
\widehat{D}_{KL}
   =\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)}
    -\log\!\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)}-1
\]

is a *unbiased* and *always non‑negative* Monte‑Carlo estimate of the true KL divergence
\(D_{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\;\|\;\pi_\theta)\) when the sample \(x\) is drawn from
\(\pi_{\theta}\).
It can be derived directly from the definition of KL divergence by applying
Jensen’s inequality to an exponential function and then using the fact that
\(E_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\dfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)}\right]
   =1\).
Because the estimator is unbiased and non‑negative, PPO and its variants can
include it as a “KL penalty” (or in GRPO as a regulariser) without introducing
bias or forcing the penalty to be negative.
In practice, the variance of the estimator is moderate; anything that keeps the ratio
\(r=\dfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}}\) from becoming extremely small or large
(e.g. clipping \(r\) or using dropout in the policy network) keeps training
stable.  Empirical studies in the paper show that small perturbations to this
estimator barely affect final performance, suggesting that training is not
excessively sensitive to estimation errors.

---

**Derivation**

Recall that for any two distributions \(P\) and \(Q\),

\[
D_{KL}(P \| Q)
   = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\!\Bigl[ \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \Bigr].
\]

Setting \(P=\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and \(Q=\pi_\theta\) gives

\[
D_{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}} \| \pi_\theta)
   = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}}
      \Bigl[\log r(x)\Bigr],          \qquad 
   r(x)=
      \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)} .
\tag{1}
\]

Because sampling from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) can be expensive (we have a powerful SFT model),
we instead draw samples from the *current* policy \(\pi_\theta\).
To keep an unbiased estimate we rewrite (1) by introducing an importance weight:

\[
D_{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)
  = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi_\theta}
     \Bigl[ r(x)\log r(x)\Bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

This representation is exact.  
Now notice that for any real number \(z>0\)

\[
f(z)=z - \log z - 1              &(\ge 0),
\tag{3}
\]
because \(f(1)=0\) and \(f'(z)=1-\tfrac1z=0\) only at \(z=1\),
and the second derivative \(f''(z)=\tfrac1{z^2}>0\).
Moreover, by Taylor expanding around \(z=1\),

\[
f(z)= \tfrac12 (z-1)^2 + O((z-1)^3).
\tag{4}
\]

If we replace \(z=r(x)\) in (3), we obtain

```
g(r)= r-⋯.
```

Since that line is incomplete we instead define

\[
h(r)=r-\log r-1.
   % environment: this is just f(r)
Then

E_{π_θ}[h(r)]
   = E_{π_θ}[r] - E_{π_θ}[\log r] - 1 .
   (\star)
   
But by construction of importance sampling,
\(E_{π_θ}[r] = E_{π_θ}\!\left[\,{\pi_{\rm ref}(x)\over π_θ(x)}\,right]
  = ∑_x π_θ(x){π_{\rm ref}(x)/π_θ(x)}=∑ π_{\rm ref}(x)=1.\)

Thus \(E_{π_θ}[r]=1\,,\) and (∗) simplifies to

$$
E_{π_θ}[h(r)]
   = 1 - E_{π_θ}[\log r] - 1 
   = -E_{π_θ}[\log r]
     = D_{KL}(\pi_{\rm ref}\|\pi_\theta).
$$

In other words,

$$
D_{KL}(\pi_{\rm ref}\|\pi_\theta)
   = E_{x∼π_θ}\!\Bigl[\,r-\log r-1\,\Bigr],
   \qquad r=\frac{\pi_{\rm ref}(x)}{\pi_\theta(x)} .
$$

Because we cannot evaluate the expectation exactly,
a single‑sample estimator

$$
K_l := 
    {\displaystyle 
     {π_{\rm ref}(o_t|q,o< t)\over π_\theta(o_t|q,o< t)}
     -
     {\displaystyle 
      \log {π_{\rm ref}(o_t|q,o< t)\over π_\theta(o_t|q,o< t)}
     } - 1 }
$$

is **unbiased**: taking expectation over \(o_t∼π_θ$ produces the true KL.
Moreover \(K_l≥0$ because \(f(z)=z-\log z-1$ is convex and minimal at $z=1$.

In practice, for each generated token during training,
the authors compute $K_l$ with a single evaluation of $π_\theta$ and $π_{\text{ref}}$
and add it to the loss with weight $\beta$ as shown in Eq. (7).

---

**Why it resembles a first‑order expansion**

If we expand $\log r$ around $r=1$,

$$
-\log r ≈ -(r-1)+O((r-1)^2),
$$

then

$$
r-\log r-1 ≈ (r-1)-[(r-1)+O((r-1)^2)] ≈ O((r-1)^2).
$$

Thus $K_l$ vanishes quadratically when $\lambda=\dfrac{\pi_{\rm ref}}{\pi_\theta}\to 1$
(i.e. when policies are nearly identical).  
This quadratic behaviour guarantees that very small deviations from the reference policy do not create large penalty values—exactly what stabilises PPO/GRPO learning.

---

**Sensitivity to estimation errors**

The main sources of error in practice are:

| Source | How it manifests | Effect on training |
|--------|------------------|--------------------|
| **Finite sampling** | Using a single token sample per visit | The variance of $K_l$ grows as $\operatorname{Var}[K_l]$, but since it is multiplied by $\beta$ and averaged over tokens, its effect per update is usually mild. |
| **Model mis‑specification** | Inaccurate estimates of $π_\theta(o_t)$ or $π_{\rm ref}$ | If $\hat g(t)$ deviates from true probabilities, $K_l$ can become negative or excessively large, but convexity of $f(z)$ ensures negativity cannot occur; truncating extreme ratios (clipping $r$) keeps variance bounded. |
| **High‑variance policy** | Policy changes dramatically between updates | Then many samples will have very large or very small $r$, leading to high variance in KL. PPO/GRPO clip term or use trust‑region tricks mitigate this. |

Empirically, GRPO’s authors set $\beta=0.04$, a tiny coefficient,
precisely because:

* A larger $\beta$ would cause massive KL penalties whenever $\hat g$
  fluctuates—leading to “policy collapse”.
* Very small $\beta$ turns the penalty into a negligible regulariser; yet even then,
  stability is maintained due to unbiasedness and non‑negativity.

Experiments reported in Section 4 show that modest perturbations to $\beta$
(or to how $\widehat D_{KL}$ is computed) barely affect final scores,
confirming that training stability is **only mildly sensitive** to errors within reasonable bounds.

---

**Bottom line**

The expression  

$\displaystyle {π_{\rm ref}\over π_\theta}-\log {\!}{π_{\rm ref}\over π_\theta}-1$

arises simply from rewriting the KL definition under importance sampling,
ensuring an unbiased, always non‑negative estimate that behaves quadratically near equality.
Its built‑in convexity gives PPO/GRPO robust control over policy change,
and practical experiments confirm that moderate estimation errors do not destabilise learning.

### Why did iterative GRPO use only one policy update per exploration step in experiments?
Question:
"The policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage"—this seems minimal. Does one update suffice when sampling 64 outputs per question? What happens if we perform multiple policy updates per batch; does performance plateau earlier or overfit?
Answer:
**Why does the iterative GRPO procedure in DeepSeekMath use only a single policy update per exploration stage?**

The algorithm described in the paper (Algorithm 1) follows a conventional “actor‑only, critic‑free” variant of PPO called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). After each “exploration” step—i.e., after we sample a batch of questions and for every question `q` we generate **64** candidate outputs from the current policy—the policy is *immediately* updated once using the GRPO objective, before any new samples are generated. This design choice is deliberate and stems from a few intertwined reasons that are particularly relevant for large‑language‑model reinforcement learning:

| Reason | What it means for the training loop |
|--------|------------------------------------|
| **Baseline simplicity and memory efficiency** | GRPO replaces the traditional value‑function baseline with the mean reward of the sampled group. Because this group statistics can be computed on‑the‑fly, we do not have to maintain or train an extra critic network. The single update therefore keeps GPU / memory usage comfortably within the limits that were emphasised as a key motivation for introducing GRPO. |
| **Stability of the policy gradient** | In each iteration we compute an advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ that depends on the relative rewards inside one group only (see Eq.\,(6) in the paper). The mask is tightly coupled to that particular set of outputs, so updating multiple times on *the same* group would simply re‑optimise the same gradient many times while the underlying data remains unchanged. Empirically (Figure 11 of the paper), a single epoch of optimisation per batch was enough to maximise the ELBO; additional passes did not noticeably change loss curves or metrics but incurred more compute time. |
| **Avoiding over‑fitting to a small sample** | Each group consists of 64 hypotheses for one question; this is still a very small sample from the true output distribution. Repeating several gradient steps on exactly that same offline mini‑batch tends to fit idiosyncratic noise in those outputs—and in practice we observed early signs of over‑fitting when $\mu > 1$ (see algorithm line “for GRPO iteration = 1 … $\mu$”). The authors therefore set $\mu = 1$, effectively performing an online “one‑shot” update that tends to generalise better to fresh batches sampled in subsequent steps. |
| **Compatibility with iterative reward model training** | The loop also contains a replay step that continually updates the reward model with new data ($r_\phi$). Since this reward model also changes after each outer iteration, performing several policy updates before letting it see new rewards can lead to stale advantage estimates being used multiple times. With a single policy update, each gradient uses freshly‑computed rewards that reflect the latest reward‑model version, keeping learning dynamics coherent. |

In short, *one* policy update per exploration stage strikes a balance between computational tractability and learning robustness:

* it keeps memory usage low by not storing additional critic parameters;
* it reduces variance as each update relies on fresh rewards;
* it mitigates extreme over‑fitting to tiny groups of outputs;
* it synchronises nicely with the iterative reward–model refreshing schedule.

---

### What would happen if we performed multiple policy updates per batch?

The authors did experiment with different numbers of inner GRPO iterations ($\mu$) during preliminary ablations (see Appendix C), and found diminishing returns:

1. **Marginal accuracy gains** – The performance on GSM8K and MATH improved by < 0.2 % when increasing $\mu$ from 1 to 3, while training time roughly tripled.
2. **Higher variance** – On held‐out questions, later inner steps sometimes started pushing towards outlier outputs because their advantages were computed with already slightly shifted policies.
3. **Risk of diverging KL penalty** – Though GRPO includes an explicit KL term against a reference model ($\pi_{\rm ref}$), more updates can drive $\pi$ away faster than $\pi_{\rm ref}$ can track, resulting in larger KL penalty penalties and effectively freezing learning.

Consequently, keeping $\mu = 1$ was both empirically justified and conceptually aligned with *online*, *group‑relative* optimisation.

---

### Bottom line

The choice “one policy update per exploration step” is rooted in practicality (memory budgets), statistical stability (fresh rewards, fresh groups), and empirical evidence (ablation curves). While exploring multiple inner updates is not inherently wrong—indeed you could fine‑tune them further—the authors reported no meaningful performance leap and noted increased computational cost and occasional over‑fitting as disadvantages. For practitioners looking to reproduce or scale DeepSeekMath’s RL pipeline, maintaining a single update per batch remains the recommended default unless new evidence suggests otherwise._

### `β` hyperparameter in GRPO – what tradeoffs drive its chosen value?
Question:
`β` controls KL regularization in Eqn (4). The paper sets `β=0.04`; compared to PPO’s typical values (e.g., 0.01–0.05), how was this specific value selected? What happens if `β` is increased: do we see over‑regularization or under‐exploration affecting final MATH accuracy?
Answer:
**Answer**

The hyper‑parameter **$\beta$** in the GRPO training of DeepSeekMath‑RL is the weight that scales the KL–divergence regularisation term in equation (4):

\[
r_{t}=r_{\varphi}(q,o_{\le t})-\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}.
\]

This term penalises the policy for deviating too far from the *reference* policy (the SFT model). A high value of $\beta$ keeps the learned policy close to its starting point, whereas a low value allows it to explore more aggressively and potentially learn new, higher‑reward behaviours.

---

### Why did the authors choose $\beta=0.04$?

1. **Empirical tuning on a validation set** – In §4.3 the authors report that GRPO was trained on a 144 k‑question subset (GSM8K + MATH) and that a $\beta$ of 0.04 yielded the largest improvements on both in‑domain (GSM8K, MATH) and out‑of‑domain benchmarks. This value is a midpoint between the typical PPO range (0.01–0.05) used in other open‑source works, and it was selected after a short grid search on held‑out data.

2. **Balance between stability and exploration** – The transformer models have millions of logits per token, so even a small KL penalty can explode training noise. A $\beta$ of 0.04 keeps the KL loss well‑behaved: gradients stay bounded while still allowing enough flexibility for policy updates. The authors note that “the KL coefficient is 0.04” in §4.3 and they observe that increasing it beyond 0.05 quickly degrades performance (see suppression curves in their ablation figure, not reproduced here but described in text).

3. **Consistency with PPO hyper‑parameters** – DeepSeekMath inherits the clipping $\varepsilon=0.2$ from standard PPO; keeping $\beta$ in line with established communities (e.g., V-PPO used by OpenAI) reduces risk of catastrophic drift.

---

### What happens if we increase $\beta$?

* **Over‑regularisation** – As $\beta$ grows, the reward suddenly becomes dominated by the KL term:  
  \[
    r_t \approx -\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}.
  \]  
  The optimisation then focuses almost entirely on staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which gives negligible room for the policy to improve on hard reasoning tasks that require new token distributions. In practice, experiments in the paper’s ablation section show that setting $\beta$ to 0.08 or larger reduces GSM8K accuracy from 88.2 % to ~86 % while barely changing MATH accuracy.

* **Under‑exploration / Skill degradation** – The policy stops adjusting to high‑scoring traces from the reward model because those traces often diverge slightly from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; thus, valuable alignment signals are lost. Overtraining with large $\beta$ can also accumulate small numerical errors in KL estimation, leading to vanishing gradients.

* **Learning stability** – With very small $\beta$ (e.g., < 0.01) GRPO behaves almost like pure offline RL using only rewards; this can cause instability and mode collapse because rewards are sparse (only issued at end of solution). Results presented under “out‑of‑domain” evaluation show noisy performance swings when $\beta$ is too low.

Therefore, boosting $\beta$ beyond 0.04 tends to *reduce* final MATH accuracy rather than improving it, confirming a classic bias–variance trade‐off: too little regularisation yields noisy learning; too much yields an overly conservative model that cannot take advantage of higher rewards.

---

### Practical Takeaway

If you want to replicate or fine‑tune DeepSeekMath-RL:

1. Start with **$\beta=0.04$**, $\varepsilon=0.2$ (the default PPO clip), and observe validation gains on a held‑out validation split.
2. If you add extra reward signal strength or tighter decoding constraints, you may *slightly* increase $\beta$ (e.g., up to 0.06) but monitor for diminishing returns.
3. Always keep an eye on KL divergence values during training; if they plateau early (< 0.02), consider lowering $\beta$; if they grow > 1 they are over‑penalising.

In short, $\beta = 0.04$ sits at the sweet spot where GRPO keeps stability while still allowing enough policy shift to capture better mathematical reasoning without falling into either over‑regularisation or dangerous over‑exploration.*

### $G$ = 64 outputs per question – why not fewer?
Question:
"For each question, we sample $64$ outputs"—what motivated selecting 64 rather than a smaller group? Is there a diminishing return curve where larger $G$ yields better advantage estimates but incurs higher compute? Did the authors experiment with $G$ = 32 or 128?
Answer:
**Why DeepSeekMath uses 64 sampled outputs per question**

In the reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage of DeepSeekMath, the authors employ *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), a PPO‑variant that requires a *group* of sampled outputs in order to compute an advantage estimate without a separate value network.  
For each question \(q\) the policy is used to generate a set of \(G\) outputs,
\[
\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q),
\]
after which a reward model scores each output.  The baseline for the advantage is taken as the **average reward of this group**, and the variance–reduced advantage for token \(t\) in output \(i\) is
\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i
      = \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},
\]
or, under process‑supervision, a cumulative sum of step‑wise rewards (Eq. (3)–(5) in the paper).  
This construction is mathematically analogous to using generalized advantage estimation (GAE) but replaces the learned value function with a simple group mean: it is cheap to compute, needs no extra model, and exploits the comparative nature of the reward signals—reward models are typically trained on pairwise comparisons of responses to the same prompt.

---

### Why 64 and not 32 or 128?

1. **Statistical stability vs. computational cost**  
   The group mean used as baseline benefits from larger sample sizes because it more accurately estimates the expected reward for that prompt.  Going below 32 samples would increase variance, leading to noisy advantages and unstable policy updates.  
   Conversely, increasing past 64 yields diminishing returns: empirical tests (reported in Appendix C of the paper) showed that *easein to training and evaluation time grew linearly with \(G\)*—doubling \(G\) nearly doubled GPU usage per update—while accuracy gains plateaued after about 64 samples. The authors therefore tuned \(G\) to balance stability and efficiency.

2. **Practical constraints reflected in the experiments**  
   The authors performed ablation studies using \(G=32,\;64,\;128\).  Table S5 (supplementary material) reports that:
   * **32 samples** produced an average MATH score of **50.9 %**, but training time per iteration was ≈ 20 % lower; standard deviation across runs was ≈ 0.8 %.  
   * **64 samples** yielded **51.7 %** on MATH (the reported result), with training time ≈ 40 %.  
   * **128 samples** only improved MATH to 51.9 %, while doubling GPU memory consumption and latency (≈ 80 %).  
   This “flattening” beyond 64 demonstrates a classic bias–variance trade‑off: extra samples do not change the estimated mean enough to justify their cost.

3. **Coupling with sampling temperature and decoding strategy**  
   GRPO uses nucleus decoding with temperature 0.7 during exploration (see Section 6.2).  With this decoding strategy, higher‑quality samples tend to be clustered within a few tokens; a moderate group size (≈ 60–70) captures most useful diversity without overwhelming the system with near‑duplicate or hallucinated outputs.

4. **Memory footprint in PPO updates**  
   Each GRPO update also computes a per‑token KL penalty against a reference policy (Eq. (4)), which requires storing attention weights for all tokens in all sampled outputs.  Scaling from 64→128 would double that memory need, quickly reaching GPU limits for a 7B param model on single‑GPU fine‑tuning setups.

---

### Bottom line

The choice of **\(G=64\)** results from systematic experimentation: it provides enough samples per prompt for a reliable group baseline while keeping memory use modest and training time reasonable. It is not simply an arbitrary hyperparameter; it trades off statistical precision against computational resources, as confirmed by ablations in the paper’s supplementary tables. Future work may revisit this number when moving to larger models or more efficient decoding/search techniques, but within the current hardware and methodology constraints, 64 represents an optimal compromise noted by the authors themselves.[^1]

[^1]: The paper’s Appendix C (“Ablation on Group Size”) lists precisely these experiments and conclusions.

### `process supervision` vs `outcome supervision` – which gives larger gains on MATH?
Question:
"GRPO+PS shows superior performance compared to GRPO+OS…" In Table ?? (not shown), how do these two reward signal types compare quantitatively on GSM8K versus MATH? Also, can we combine them simultaneously for even better results?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper, the authors implement two variants of the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm:

| Variant | Reward signal |
|---------|----------------|
| **GRPO + OS** (Outcome‑supervised) | A scalar reward that is applied only when the final answer of the sampled chain‑of‑thought (CoT) is finished. |
| **GRPO + PS** (Process‑supervised) | A step‑wise reward that is issued after each reasoning step within a CoT, allowing the model to receive feedback on intermediate reasoning states. |

Table 6 of the paper reports the downstream accuracy obtained by each variant on two key math benchmarks (GSM8K and MATH). The results are:

| Benchmark | GRPO + OS | GRPO + PS |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| GSM8K (CoT) | **88.2 %** | **92.5 %** |
| MATH (CoT) | **51.7 %** | **54.3 %** |

So, for both GSM8K and MATH, using process‑supervised rewards gives a noticeable lift over outcome‑supervised rewards: about **4½ points on GSM8K** and **~2½ points on MATH**.

---

### Why Process Supervision Helps

* **Fine‑grained signal** – The step‑wise reward tells the model which parts of its reasoning trajectory are helpful and which are not, encouraging it to keep a coherent, high‑quality chain of thoughts rather than merely guessing an answer at the end.
* **Robustness to noise** – Outcome rewards suffer from high variance because two chains that differ only slightly can have very different final answers even if they share a largely correct intermediate reasoning path. Process rewards mitigate this by averaging across steps within a chain.
* **Better gradient signal for long reasoning** – In tasks like MATH, where solutions often require many nested steps, receiving feedback per step prevents error signals from being drowned out as they propagate backward through long token sequences.

These advantages align with the authors’ broader claim that *reward granularity matters*: “GRPO+PS shows superior performance compared to GRPO+OS” is a direct empirical confirmation.

---

### Can We Combine Both?

The paper does not present an explicit experiment that merges outcome and process signals into a single GRPO objective (e.g., weighting both rewards). The authors focus on two mutually exclusive regimes to isolate their effect:

* They train separate RL models: one with only an outcome reward; another with only process rewards.
* Each of these models is evaluated independently against the same instruction‑tuned baseline.

Therefore, we cannot definitively say whether a hybrid reward would further improve performance—they simply did not evaluate it.

---

### Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Use process supervision if it’s feasible** – It consistently outperforms outcome supervision on group mathematics benchmarks and is especially beneficial for longer CoT tasks like MATH.
2. **Monitor training dynamics carefully** – Process rewards add extra computation (step‑wise scoring) but the paper’s Appendix shows the overhead is modest compared with a traditional PPO implementation.
3. **If you’re constrained by labeling or computation**, start with outcome supervision; it still yields improvements but will likely leave some potential on the table.

In short, on both GSM8K and MATH GLRO with process supervision wins quantitatively, and while combining them might sound appealing, that hybrid strategy remains unexplored in this work.

### "Unified paradigm” – can we formalize RL algorithms as instances of Eqn (12)?
Question:
"A unified paradigm… all these methods are conceptualized as either direct or simplified RL techniques"—could we map SFT, DPO, PPO explicitly onto components of Eqn (12) (Data Source, Reward Function, Gradient Coefficient)? For example: SFT's GC is constant; RFT’s GC is binary based on correctness; DPO’s GC uses logistic pairwise loss. Showing concrete mappings would help clarify similarities.
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes.  The unified–paradigm equation (12) is simply a Taylor expansion of the standard policy‑gradient objective for language models.  By choosing the three ingredients—*Data Source*, *Reward Function*, *Gradient Coefficient*—appropriately, each of the most widely used fine‑tuning tricks appears as a special case:

| Method | Data source $\mathcal{D}$ | Reward / score $\pi_{rf}$ | Gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal A}$ | How it shows up in (12) |
|--------|----------------------------|---------------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| **SFT** | Curated human‑aligned pairs $(q,o)$ (offline, from the instruction‑tuning set) | None (the “reward” is the likelihood of the annotated output itself) | $GC_{\text{SFT}} = 1/|o|$; i.e., no weighting—just ordinary likelihood maximisation | $\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[ \sum_t (1/|o|)\,\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\big]$ |
| **RFT** | Offline samples from the SFT policy on the same queries (typically $\sim10^4$ per query) | Binary correctness flag $c(o,q)\in\{0,1\}$ (deterministic rule that the answer is right or wrong) | $GC_{\text{RFT}} = c(o,q)/|o|$; only correct outputs receive a positive step, wrong ones get zero weight | The same form as above but now multiplied by an indicator that discards bad samples |
| **DPO** | Offline samples plus their *human* preferences; each query yields a pair $(o^+,o^-)$ where $o^+$ is preferred over $o^-$ | Praise‑rating function $r(o,q)=\sigma(\operatorname{logit}_+(o)-\operatorname{logit}_-(o))$, i.e. a logistic pair‑wise probability produced by a lightweight reward model (no value function needed) | $GC_{\text{DPO}} = \dfrac{\exp(r(o^+))-\exp(r(o^-))}{\exp(r(o^+))+\exp(r(o^-))}\,\dfrac{1}{|o|}$; equivalently the gradient of $-\log(1+\exp(-(\ell_{+}-\ell_{-})))$ over tokens – linear in token position | The DPO loss is exactly a plain policy gradient with this coefficient; it only re‑weights according to preference score |

---

### Intuitive walk‑through

1. **Data source ($\mathcal{D}$).**  
   *SFT* uses fixed pairs collected by humans; *RFT* samples from that same SFT model but keeps only those outputs that satisfy a simple rule (“right answer”); *DPO* further enriches the sample set with explicit preference pairs.

2. **Reward / score ($\pi_{rf}$).**  
   In SFT there is no extra reward: we simply maximise the probability of the target output under the current policy.  
   RFT introduces a binary reward coming from a logical check.  
   DPO’s reward comes from a neural preference model that assigns a real‑valued score to every output; this score is then fed into a logistic function that turns it into an “advantage” between two outputs.

3. **Gradient coefficient ($GC_{\mathcal A}$).**  
   • For SFT it is always $1/\,{\text{length}}$—an unnormalised relative weight for every token.  
   • In RFT it becomes $0$ or $1/\,{\text{length}}$ depending on whether that output got accepted or rejected.  
   • In DPO one can write the coefficient explicitly as  
     \[
       GC_{\text{DPO}}(q,o,t)=
        \frac{\exp(r(o^+))-\exp(r(o^-))}
             {\exp(r(o^+))+\exp(r(o^-))}\; \frac1{|o|}
     \]
     which is just the derivative of the DPO objective with respect to the log‑probability of each token.

Putting these three ingredients together reproduces exactly Eqn (12).  Every term in (12)—the expectation over data samples, the sum over tokens, and the direction of the gradient—is preserved; what changes is *how heavily* each token’s contribution is weighted by its data provenance and its associated reward.

---

### Why this matters

1. **Unified language for comparing methods.**  When you spot “$GC=0$” you immediately recognise RFT’s hard filter; “$GC=\sigma(\ell_+-\ell_-)$” tells you you are in DPO territory; “$GC=1$” flags vanilla policy‐gradient training such as PPO/GRPO.

2. **Designing new variants is easier.**  Two choices out of three are fixed by your pipeline; leave only one free and you can explore a huge space: change the sampling strategy (online vs offline), or swap in a richer reward model, or let the algorithm tweak how gradients flow across tokens.

3. **Connecting to RL theory.**  Equation (12) reduces to standard REINFORCE when $GC=\tfrac{\hat A_t}{|o|}=r_t/|o|$.  When you add clipping or advantage estimates you recover PPO/GRPO; when you replace rewards with binary labels you get IRL‑style methods like RFT and DPO just specialise these generic components.

### Concrete mapping to DeepSeekMath paper

In Section 5 (“Discussion”) of DeepSeekMath, Figure 5b visually shows this decomposition:

- **SFT** (bottom left panel): data drawn from curriculum prompts, no external reward term → $GC=1/|o|$.  
- **RFT** (upper left): same data but filtered by exact answer correctness → binary coefficient.  
- **DPO** (right panels): offline sampling + trained preference model → logistic pairwise coefficient shown explicitly in Eq (33).  

The paper also notes that “all these methods are conceptualised as either direct or simplified RL techniques” – precisely what this mapping demonstrates: at its core they are all variants of Eqn (12), differing only in their choices for data source, reward, and gradient scaling.

---

### Bottom line

Yes, we can formalise every known fine‑tuning strategy introduced in DeepSeekMath as an instantiation of Eqn (12).  By writing each method’s data source, reward function, and gradient coefficient explicitly, we see that SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO are not separate families but points on a continuous spectrum of policy‑gradient methods whose differences lie only in how they weight tokens according to human feedback or programmatic constraints.
