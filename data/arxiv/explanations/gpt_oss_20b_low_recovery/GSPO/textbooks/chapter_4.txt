\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: # 4. Motivation for a Sequence‑Level Approach

Training reinforcement learning (RL) agents that can generate long, high‑fidelity language responses demands a careful orchestration of data collection, sample reuse, and objective design. When a policy model is too large to generate every sample on demand, practitioners typically adopt a large rollout batch for computational efficiency. However, this engineering choice introduces an off‑policy learning setting that undermines the assumptions underpinning many RL algorithms. The section that follows unpacks how these practical necessities create a fertile ground for instability, why token‑level importance weights fail to satisfy the fundamental requirements of importance sampling, and how the accumulation of noise over long sequences can precipitate catastrophic model collapse. Finally, we synthesize these observations into a guiding principle: the unit of reward should align with the unit of optimization.

---

## Off‑policy learning in large batch RL

A core constraint when scaling language‑model RL is hardware utilization. Modern GPUs or tensor‑core accelerators perform most efficiently when batch sizes are on the order of several hundred thousand tokens per step. To reach this throughput, a common pipeline groups thousands of queries, each paired with several long responses, into one large *rollout batch*. Within this batch, the model—denoted \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\)—generates a set of responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) for each query \(x\). Because the policy to be updated, \(\pi_{\theta}\), has not yet seen these responses, we call this a *mismatch* between the behavior distribution and the target distribution.

Instead of recomputing new responses at every gradient step (which would be prohibitively expensive), practitioners divide the large batch into multiple *mini‑batches* and perform several gradient updates on the same data. This staggering of updates transforms a single on‑policy dataset into an *off‑policy* training regime: the mini‑batch samples are drawn from \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) while the updates push \(\pi_{\theta}\) toward a target that is gradually changing during training. The consequence is that each gradient step operates on data that no longer matches the distribution implicitly defined by the current parameters. Classical policy‑gradient methods, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its transformer‑specific variation GRPO, mitigate this mismatch by clipping importance ratios or using counter‑acting critics. Yet, as we shall illustrate, when the mismatch is compounded by very long sequences and many embedded queries, these mitigation strategies can become inadequate.

---

## Importance sampling and its requirements

Importance sampling (IS) is a statistical method for estimating expectations under a *target* distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). Its canonical form is

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\left[ f(z) \right]
 = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

Several assumptions underlie this identity:

1. **Sufficient overlap**: \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) and \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) must assign non‑zero probability to the same support. If \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) assigns weight to regions the behavior model never visited, the estimator becomes undefined.

2. **Multiple samples**: The replacement of a single sample by a weighted version only works when we aggregate a *large* number of i.i.d. samples from the behavior distribution. The law of large numbers guarantees that the weighted sum converges to the target expectation as the batch size grows.

3. **Weight stability**: The variance of the importance weight \(\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)/\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)\) must be controlled. Excessive variance inflates estimator noise and can dominate learning dynamics.

4. **Proper alignment**: The function \(f(z)\) to be integrated must be consistent with the level at which the weight is computed. If the weight is applied to token‑level events, then \(f(z)\) should also be token‑wise; if the weight is at sequence level, \(f(z)\) must be a sequence‑wise reward or loss.

In the RL context for language models, the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) defines the distribution of entire responses \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_{|y|})\) conditioned on a query \(x\). The reward function \(r(x,y)\) typically assigns a scalar score to the whole response (e.g., judged by a verifier or a skill metric). Consequently, the natural pairing between weights and rewards is *sequence‑level* rather than *token‑level*. When one inadvertently substitutes token‑level ratios for sequence‑level corrections, the IS assumptions are violated.

## Why token‑level weights violate importance sampling

The GRPO objective introduces a token‑wise importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\quad
i = 1,\dots,G,\; t = 1,\dots,|y_i|.
\]

Each token \(y_{i,t}\) is sampled once from the *next‑token* distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x, y_{i,<t})\). The ratio above therefore treats a single draw as if it could correct for the entire next‑token distribution—precisely the inverse of the usual IS setting, where many draws are available to estimate an expectation. As a result:

- The ratio provides no guarantee of proximity to the target probability because it is computed from a *single* observation. In other words, the expectation in the IS equation cannot be approximated by one sample; many samples are needed for a reliable estimate.

- The distribution of \(w_{i,t}\) becomes highly skewed; tokens that happen to be rare under the old policy can produce exceedingly large ratios, while common tokens yield ratios near one. This skew translates directly into high‑variance gradient estimates.

- Since each token in a long response contributes a distinct ratio, the final policy gradient is a weighted sum of many noisy terms, amplifying instability.

In contrast, a sequence‑wise weight

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\!,
\]

is naturally a ratio of two probabilities over the entire sequence. Although it may also exhibit variance, the variance is typically smoother because it aggregates over all tokens. More importantly, this ratio is theoretically justified by importance sampling applied at the correct granularity: one sequence sample replaces one target distribution evaluation, honoring the “multiple samples needed for variance reduction” principle.

---

## Accumulation of training noise in long sequences

Long‑distance dependencies inflate the risk of noise accumulation. In the token‑wise framework, consider a response of length \(L\). The gradient term for a single token is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\, \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}).
\]

Even if the advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) were stabilized, the amplification factor \(w_{i,t}\) is applied individually to each token. Suppose the expected magnitude of \(w_{i,t}\) deviates by a factor of \(1+\delta\) from unity (due to sampling variability). The cumulative effect across the sequence is a multiplicative drift that aggregates roughly like

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{L} (1+\delta_t) \;\approx\; \exp\!\left(\;\sum_{t=1}^{L} \delta_t\;\right),
\]

where \(\delta_t\) are zero‑mean but noisy fluctuations. Even if \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_t] = 0\), the variance of the sum grows as \(L\sigma^2\), leading to a log‑normal distribution with a long right‑tail. A few extreme tokens can dominate the overall weight, pushing it far out of the clipping band and yielding unreliable gradient updates.

Moreover, the *clipping* mechanism, designed to keep the ratio within \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\), counters this trend but does not eliminate the underlying high‑variance signal that comprises the bulk of the gradient. Consequently, over successive gradient steps, the model may be steered in erratic directions, especially when the sequence length grows to hundreds of tokens, as is typical in advanced reasoning tasks.

---

## Experiments showing model collapse

Empirical evidence of the fragility of token‑level importance weights emerged when training massive language models on tasks requiring elaborate reasoning. When the GRPO algorithm was applied with a moderate rollout batch, the training curve displayed erratic increases in reward followed by sudden drops to near‑zero, after which the model failed to recover. This phenomenon—termed *model collapse*—was irreversible even when the training process was halted, the model checkpoint was rolled back, and hyperparameters such as the clipping range were tuned.

A systematic ablation pinpoints the culprit: by replacing the token‑wise weight \(w_{i,t}\) with the sequence‑wise weight \(s_i(\theta)\) and recomputing the gradient accordingly (i.e., Group Sequence Policy Optimization), the model’s performance continued to climb steadily without any collapse. This experiment demonstrates that the instability is not inherent to the policy or the reward but stems from the misapplication of importance sampling at an inappropriate granularity.

---

## Principle: unit of reward ≈ unit of optimization

The discussion above leads to a clear guiding principle for designing RL objectives in large language models:

> **The granular unit of the objective function should match the granular unit of the reward signal.**

Rewards in language‑model RL are naturally defined over entire responses—complete and coherent texts. Therefore, the importance weighting, clipping, and gradient computation should operate at the sequence level. Token‑wise weighting introduces noise that violates the assumptions of importance sampling and aggregates in a manner that can destabilize the learning process. Sequence‑wise weighting aligns with the reward, respects the statistical foundations of IS, and mitigates variance accumulation.

Adhering to this principle transforms the training dynamics in several ways:

- **Stability**: The gradient estimates become less noisy, reducing the risk of catastrophic collapse.
- **Efficiency**: By avoiding unnecessary token‑wise computations that drown in variance, the loss surface is smoother and the model converges faster.
- **Simplicity**: Eliminating token‑level importance ratios eliminates the need for auxiliary mechanisms such as *Routing Replay* that were invented to rescue token‑level approaches in sparse MoE settings.

In sum, the root cause of RL instability in large‑scale language‑model training lies in treating length‑invariant rewards as if they were token‑wise. A sequence‑level perspective not only restores the theoretical integrity of importance sampling but also yields practical benefits that empower the next generation of RL‑enhanced language models.
