\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 6: # 6. Gradient Analysis and Stability Insights

In this chapter we unpack the mathematics behind the key stability guarantees of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). We first revisit the exact gradient derivations for GSPO and its predecessor GRPO, then analyze the differences in their weighting schemes. Finally, we show how the accumulation of unstable terms in GRPO’s per‑token weighting can cause catastrophic failure in long‑response settings, while GSPO’s sequence‑level weighting ameliorates this effect and yields a more robust training signal.

---

## 6.1 Gradient Derivation for GSPO

Recall that the GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Bigl(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i
\Bigr)
\right].
\tag{6.1}
\]

For the moment we suppress the clipping operation (it does not affect the analytical form of the gradient) and focus on the core expression

\[
\ell_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\,\{y_i\}}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\right],
\]
where the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) is defined as

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\;=\;
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\tag{6.2}
\]

The expectation is taken over the distribution of groups of responses generated by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Since \(\widehat{A}_i\) depends solely on the reward of the full response \(y_i\), the gradient can be pushed inside the expectation:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&=\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\right] \\
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

Using \(\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)\) and noting that

\[
\log s_i(\theta)
=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})-
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right] \\
&=\boxed{
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Equation (6.3) is precisely the gradient used in practice: each token in a response is weighted *identically* by the sequence‑level factor \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\). In other words, the gradient contribution of a given response is proportional to its likelihood ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)\), spread uniformly across all tokens.

## 6.2 Gradient Derivation for GRPO

For GRPO the objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i
\Bigr)
\right],
\tag{6.4}
\]
where the token‑wise importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\tag{6.5}
\]
and every token in a response shares the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\).

Again dropping clipping for clarity and pulling the gradient inside:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta} w_{i,t}(\theta)
\right] .
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is a ratio of two likelihoods, we obtain

\[
\nabla_{\theta} w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
because the denominator \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) is treated as constant with respect to \(\theta\). Substituting gives

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

Equation (6.6) reveals that GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its *token‑level* importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Unlike GSPO, the weighting factor is not the same across tokens in the same response: if a particular token becomes highly unlikely under the new policy relative to the old one, its contribution to the gradient will be severely down‑weighted or even clipped to zero by the outer clipping operator.

---

## 6.3 Comparison of Per‑Token Weighting

Both algorithms ultimately aim to correct for the off‑policy distributional shift between the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). However, the *unit* at which this correction is applied differs:

| Algorithm | Weighting Scheme | Unit of Weight | Effect |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|--------|
| GSPO | Sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) | Entire response | Per‑token contribution is uniformly scaled by \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\). |
| GRPO | Token‑level importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) | Token | Each token is weighted by its own ratio. |

Because GSPO’s weight is built from the *product* of all token ratios in a response, it is naturally more stable and less sensitive to the variance of any single token. In contrast, GRPO couples each token’s gradient to the potentially volatile ratio of that single token, leading to *heterogeneous* scaling across the same response.

The difference is most clearly illustrated if we rewrite the two weighting factors in terms of the underlying token log likelihoods:

- GSPO: \[
s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\bigr]\right).
\]

- GRPO: \[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
= \exp\!\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\bigr].
\]

GSPO’s average over the sequence smooths out any outlier terms, while GRPO’s per‑token expression preserves them, magnifying the impact of rare tokens.

---

## 6.4 Accumulation of Unstable Terms in GRPO

When generating longer responses, the product of token probabilities becomes exponentially small, and small perturbations in a token’s likelihood can cause large swings in the importance ratio. Since GRPO’s gradient for a response \(\{y_i\}\) is an *average* of token‑level contributions, a single token that spikes or drops can dominate the update for that response.

Formally, suppose token \(t^\ast\) in response \(i\) receives an anomalously high importance ratio \(w_{i,t^\ast}(\theta)\gg 1\). In the gradient (6.6) the corresponding term receives a disproportionate boost:

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\,w_{i,t^\ast}(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t^\ast}\mid \cdot).
\]

If \(t^\ast\) is also on the boundary of the clipping window, the gradient may be *sharply* modified, injecting high variance into the parameter update. Over many such tokens and many iterations this stochasticity accumulates, especially severe when the model is already near convergence and the policy does not change dramatically between updates. In the extreme, the token‑level ratios can grow unbounded, leading to exploding or vanishing gradients, precise the “model collapse” phenomenon described in the introduction.

Moreover, because the token‑level ratio is evaluated *after* the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) has been frozen, its denominator is fixed. If the policy changes the probability distribution enough to make the old token likelihood significantly larger (or smaller), \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can exceed the clipping range dramatically. The outer clipping then caps the contribution, but only after the potentially harmful token has already averted its influence on the gradient—yet the associated high variance remains in the averaging process and can provoke instability in subsequent steps.

---

## 6.5 Implications for Long‑Response Tasks

Long‑response tasks (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, code generation) exacerbate the issues highlighted above because:

1. **Sequence Length Amplifies Variance**: The likelihood of a long sequence is the product of many small probabilities, making the sequence likelihood highly sensitive to a few token changes. Thus, token‑level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) are naturally more volatile.

2. **Accumulation Over Time**: Each token’s contribution is added to the gradient sum. Even if the mean of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is close to one (as the policies are often similar), the *variance* can be large, leading to noisy updates on average.

3. **Clipping for Off‑Policy Corrections**: The clipping interval is typically chosen to be narrow for token‑level weights, otherwise the training would diverge. This means that many tokens are clipped to the boundary, effectively discarding their gradient contribution. When most tokens are clipped, the remaining few dominate the update, potentially in a non‑representative way.

In contrast, GSPO mitigates these issues in several ways:

- **Uniform Weighting per Response**: Since every token in a response receives the same factor \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\), a large deviation in any one token’s likelihood is averaged over the whole sequence, dramatically reducing variance.

- **Reduced Dependence on Token-By-Token Variations**: The gradient for a response is driven by the *overall* likelihood shift, which is a smoother function of the policy parameters because it aggregates across the entire sequence.

- **Effective Clipping at the Response Level**: Clipping the entire sequence likelihood ratio is much less likely to truncate many tokens simultaneously, preserving more gradient signal.

- **Length Normalization**: By taking the \(|y_i|\)-th root of the likelihood ratio, the magnitude of \(s_i(\theta)\) remains in a manageable range, preventing extreme scaling that could destabilize training.

Concrete numerical experiments (see Section 4.2 of the paper) show that GSPO reduces the variance of the policy gradient estimates by almost an order of magnitude compared to GRPO when evaluated on long‑response datasets, confirming the theoretical intuition.

## 6.6 Numerical Illustration of Variance Reduction

Consider a synthetic example with a response of length \(L=10\). Suppose the old policy assigns a uniform probability \(p_{\text{old}}=0.1\) to every token, while the new policy changes the probability of each token in denomination \(\pm 5\%\) around the old value. The per‑token weight for token \(t\) is

\[
w_{t}= \frac{p_{\text{new}}^t}{p_{\text{old}}} = 1 \pm 0.05.
\]

The variance of \(w_t\) is \(\sigma^2_w = 0.05^2 = 0.0025\). For GRPO, the gradient for the response is the average of \(L\) such terms, so the variance of the *average* is

\[
\sigma^2_{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{\sigma^2_w}{L} = \frac{0.0025}{10} = 0.00025.
\]

Now consider GSPO’s sequence‑level weight:

\[
s = \prod_{t=1}^{L} w_t^{1/L} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^L w_t\right)^{1/L}.
\]

Assuming independence, the variance of the product is

\[
\sigma^2_s \approx \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^L \log^2 w_t\right) - 1 \approx L \sigma^2_{\log w},
\]
where \(\sigma^2_{\log w}\) is the variance of \(\log w_t\), which is smaller than that of \(w_t\) itself because \(\log(1+x)\approx x\) for small \(x\). After taking the \(|y_i|\)-th root, the variance is further reduced:

\[
\sigma^2_{s^{1/L}} \approx \frac{\sigma^2_s}{L^2} \approx \frac{\sigma^2_{\log w}}{L} < \sigma^2_{\text{GRPO}}.
\]

Thus, even in this toy example, GSPO inherently produces a smaller variance estimate for the policy gradient than GRPO does. This variance advantage scales more strongly as the sequence length grows, explaining why GSPO is particularly effective for long‑response tasks.

---

## 6.7 Concluding Remarks

The derivations above demonstrate a clear mathematical reason for GSPO’s superior stability: its gradient is **sequence‑level** and **uniformly weighted**, whereas GRPO’s gradient is **token‑level** and **heterogeneously weighted**. By moving the importance ratio from the token domain to the sequence domain, GSPO reduces high‑variance contributions that can corrupt the learning signal, particularly when dealing with long responses or sparse MoE activations. This insight not only explains earlier empirical observations regarding model collapse in GRPO but also motivates the design of GSPO as a robust, scalable RL algorithm for large language models.
