\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 5: # 5. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Core Idea

Reinforcement learning (RL) for language models is notoriously fragile. The policy is an autoregressive neural network that generates tokens one by one, and the reward is typically **assigned to the entire response**. When the number of tokens grows or the model becomes very large (e.g., Mixture‑of‑Experts, MoE), the unstable training dynamics of previous algorithms such as GRPO can lead to catastrophic model collapse. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses the root cause of this instability by aligning the **unit of importance weighting** and **unit of reward**: both are defined at the sequence level. In the following sections we unpack the key components of GSPO, showing why they matter and how they are put together into a coherent, provably stable RL objective.

## Definition of sequence‑level importance ratio \( s_{k}(\theta) \)

A language model parameterized by \(\theta\) defines a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). For a query \(x\) and a response \(y\), the likelihood under the policy is

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t}),
\]

the product of token‑wise next‑token probabilities. In GRPO the importance weight is computed token by token:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})},
\]

and then multiplied into the gradient. This token‑level weighting breaks the principle of importance sampling because each token is weighted by a likelihood ratio that was computed **using only the single token sampled** from the old policy, not the full sequence. GSPO replaces this with a **sequence‑level** ratio that describes how much the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) diverges from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) over the whole response:

\[
s_{k}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_k|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_k|}.
\]

The exponent \(1/|y_k|\) normalizes the ratio to a per‑token scale and prevents extremely long responses from dominating the magnitude of \(s_{k}(\theta)\). The ratio can also be written as an average of log token‑level ratios:

\[
s_{k}(\theta)=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_k|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_k|}
              \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k,t}|x,y_{k,<t})}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{k,t}|x,y_{k,<t})}\right).
\]

Thus, \(s_{k}(\theta)\) reflects the **expected change in likelihood per token** when moving from the old policy to the current one. Because it is constructed from an average, it has low variance and is well‑suited for stable gradient estimation.

## Length normalization and variance control

The raw likelihood ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)\) grows or shrinks exponentially with the length of \(y\). Consequently, sequences of different lengths would produce importance ratios that differ by orders of magnitude, forcing the clipping range to depend on response length. By **length normalization**—raising the ratio to the reciprocal of the sequence length—we obtain a quantity that is roughly on the same scale regardless of how many tokens were generated. This mitigates two crucial problems:

1. **Variance of the importance weight**: Long sequences can yield ratios that are extremely large or tiny, increasing gradient variance. Normalization keeps the weight within a narrow window.
2. **Clipping calibration**: Since the clipped range must know the typical value of the importance ratio, a length‑normalized ratio allows a single, fixed clipping interval to work for all sequence lengths.

Empirically, GSPO uses left and right clipping thresholds on the order of \(10^{-4}\), a magnitude compatible with the normalized ratios. In contrast, GRPO employs thresholds around \(10^{-1}\) due to the un‑normalized token‑level ratios.

## Group‑based advantage estimation

In RL, the advantage function quantifies how much better a response is compared to a baseline. GSPO adopts the same **group‑based** advantage used in GRPO because it avoids the expensive value network. For a query \(x\) and its \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\), the advantage of response \(y_i\) is

\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\quad
\mu_x=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r(x,y_j),\;
\sigma_x^2=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}\bigl(r(x,y_j)-\mu_x\bigr)^2.
\]

Thus each response is centered and normalized by the mean and standard deviation of rewards within its group. This has several benefits:

- **Variance reduction**: By normalizing, the scale of advantages is kept to roughly one, easing learning.
- **Implicit baselining**: Responses that are worse than the group mean receive negative advantages and are discouraged; better responses are rewarded.
- **Differentiable with respect to the model**: Since the rewards \(r(x,y)\) are not differentiable, the normalization is performed on the *non‑differentiable* reward values, not on the policy parameters.

Because all tokens in a response receive the same advantage, the objective will later reward or punish the entire sequence coherently.

## GSPO objective re‑derivation

Putting the pieces together, the GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Biggl[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
          \text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
          \bigr)
\Biggr].
\tag{5.1}
\]

The structure of this objective is directly inherited from PPO:

1. **Importance weight** (\(s_i(\theta)\)) multiplies the **advantage**.
2. **Clipping** limits the weight to a proximity band around 1. If the weight is too large or too small, the clipped value is used instead to guarantee the update does not stray far from the old policy.
3. **Expectation** is taken over the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) and the rollouts sampled from the old policy.

Deriving the gradient (ignoring the clipping for brevity) follows the standard REINFORCE trick:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Notice that the gradient **does not** involve any token‑wise importance weights: every token in a response contributes equally through the \(1/|y_i|\) factor. This is in stark contrast to GRPO where tokens are multiplied by their own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), which may be substantially larger or smaller than 1, thereby biasing the gradient artificially. By uniformly weighting tokens, GSPO eliminates the primary source of high‑variance and instability in GRPO.

## Clipping at the sequence level vs. token level

In PPO and GRPO, **clipping is applied at the token level**. Roughly speaking, each token \(y_t\) in the response receives its own importance ratio \(w_t(\theta)\). Tokens with extreme ratios (i.e., far from the old policy) are clipped to avoid over‑aggressive updates. However, because the reward is attached to the whole sequence, this token‑wise clipping produces a mismatch: tokens that should be rewarded together are sometimes clipped independently, leading to confusing gradient signals.

GSPO’s **sequence‑level clipping** solves this mismatch elegantly:

- The entire response is either accepted within the clipping window or rejected as a whole.
- Tokens that collectively deviate too much from the old policy are removed from the gradient estimator, regardless of how individual tokens behave.
- The resulting clip threshold is tuned on the per‑sequence importance ratios \(s_i(\theta)\), ensuring a consistent sensitivity across all lengths.

Empirically, the fraction of tokens clipped under GSPO is *an order of magnitude higher* than under GRPO. Yet GSPO still demonstrates higher training efficiency. This counter‑intuitive observation underscores that the tokens eliminated due to clipping carry noisy gradient information that would otherwise destabilize learning. By discarding them at the sequence level, GSPO preserves a cleaner, more reliable learning signal.

## Choice of clipping ranges and their impact

The clipping hyperparameters \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}\) define a symmetric window \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) around the value 1. In practice, GSPO uses smaller \(\varepsilon\) (e.g., \(\varepsilon \approx 3\times10^{-4}\)) than GRPO because the importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) is a length‑normalized quantity that stays close to 1 even when the underlying policy changes significantly. A narrow clip ensures that any large deviation is penalized swiftly, preserving the proximal nature of the update.

If \(\varepsilon\) is set too **large**, the clipping mechanism becomes ineffective; the algorithm essentially becomes a standard policy gradient with importance weighting, re‑introducing the high‑variance problem that led to collapse. Conversely, if \(\varepsilon\) is too **small**, too many sequences will be clipped, starving the gradient estimator of useful samples and slowing convergence.

Fine‑tuning \(\varepsilon\) is therefore crucial but not overly delicate: the ratio’s length‑normalization reduces the sensitivity to the precise value, and standard grid‑search strategies on a held‑out validation set quickly reveal the stable region.

---

In summary, GSPO redefines the importance ratio at the correct abstraction level, normalizes it to control variance, and aligns the reward, advantage, and objective within a single coherent mathematical framework. By doing so, it eliminates the pathological noise that plagued GRPO, stabilizes policy updates even in massive MoE architectures, and ultimately unlocks the full potential of reinforcement learning for large‑scale language models.
