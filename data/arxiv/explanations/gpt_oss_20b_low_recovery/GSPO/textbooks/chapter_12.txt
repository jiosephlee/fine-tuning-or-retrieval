\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 12: # 12. Limitations, Future Work, and Open Questions

Reinforcement learning for large language models has, in the past few years, demonstrated a remarkable ability to amplify performance on a broad range of long‑term reasoning and zero‑shot tasks.  The Group Sequence Policy Optimisation (GSPO) algorithm is a cornerstone of that progress, but it is not a silver bullet.  Understanding where GSPO falls short, what stands in the way of further breakthroughs, and how the community might address those gaps is crucial for moving the field forward.  In this chapter we dissect the open limitations of GSPO, sketch plausible research directions, and outline concrete open problems that invite a deeper theoretical and empirical investigation.

---

## Situations where token‑level dynamics matter

GSPO was designed around the intuition that the reward is a property of the *entire* response, and that thereby the policy should be updated using sequence‑level importance ratios.  In practice, however, a language model does not always act as a monolithic generator.  Several scenarios emerge in which token‑level effects become non‑negligible:

| Scenario | Why token‑level control is relevant | How GSPO may be sub‑optimal |
|----------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Multi‑turn dialogue** | Each turn can depend on a *partial* context that changes step‑by‑step; the value of an early token may influence future tokens differently. | GSPO’s group‑based advantage, by averaging over the entire response, may dilute the nuanced impact of early tokens. |
| **Fine‑grained safety constraints** | Certain tokens can trigger policy violations or compliance checks instantaneously (e.g., profanity filters). | GSPO’s clipping operates on the whole sequence, possibly allowing a token that violates policy to survive because it is embedded in a high‑reward overall response. |
| **Sparse rewards or low‑frequency events** | Rewards that only materialise at a specific token (e.g., a correct answer within a longer passage). | GSPO’s sequence‑level advantage averages rewards across all tokens, potentially masking the reward signal at that token. |
| **Computational cost control** | Token‑wise computational budgeting (e.g., limiting token‑wise latency per query). | GSPO’s inability to directly tune the likelihood of individual tokens may hinder fine‑grained optimisation of such costs. |

When token‑level dynamics become crucial, the token‑level variant GSPO‑token may offer a provisional remedy.  GSPO‑token preserves the sequence‑level importance ratio while allowing per‑token advantages, thereby enabling granularity without abandoning the stabilising properties of a single importance ratio.  Nevertheless, the theoretical guarantees of GSPO‑token remain to be established, and the added computational burden (because of per‑token advantage estimation) can offset its benefits.  Future work could explore hybrid objectives that blend token‑level clipping with sequence‑level weighting, or introduce *hierarchical* importance ratios where tokens are grouped into logical units (e.g., sentences, entities) with their own ratios.

## Choice of group size \(G\) and its effect

GSPO’s central idea relies on grouping multiple responses \(\{y_i\}\) to a single query \(x\).  The size of this group, denoted \(G\), governs several trade‑offs:

1. **Variance of the advantage estimate**—The normalized advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is computed over the group.  A larger \(G\) reduces the variance of \(\widehat{A}_i\) (by more accurately estimating the mean and standard deviation), yet it also risks diluting individual signal extremes that could otherwise guide learning.

2. **Computational burden**—Sampling \(G\) responses per query multiplies the rollout cost linearly in \(G\).  In large‑scale deployments, \(G\) must be chosen carefully to balance wall‑clock time and data efficiency.

3. **Policy gap control**—More diverse responses in a larger group can facilitate the identification of under‑represented modes in the policy distribution.  However, if \(G\) is too large relative to the batch size, the reward distribution may become biased toward highly scored responses, leading to premature convergence (sometimes called *policy collapse*).

Empirical tuning of \(G\) in the original GSPO experiments was performed on a *coarse* scale (e.g., \(G=4\) or \(8\)).  A systematic exploration of \(G\) remains an open problem.  Candidate research avenues include:

- **Adaptive group sizing**: Dynamically adjusting \(G\) per query based on a metric such as *diversity* (e.g., entropy of token distributions) or *certainty* (e.g., variance of the generated sequences).
- **Curriculum‑style grouping**: Starting with small \(G\) during early training to encourage fast progress, then gradually increasing \(G\) as the policy stabilises.
- **Theoretical bounds**: Deriving upper and lower bounds on \(G\) that guarantee a desired level of variance for \(\widehat{A}_i\), or establishing convergence rates as a function of \(G\).

Future work could test these hypotheses on benchmark tasks spanning both short‑term and long‑term rewards, evaluating the impact on both the learning curve and final model quality.

## Alternatives to Gaussian advantage normalisation

GSPO adopts a *Gaussian* normalisation of the advantages within each group:

\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
\qquad \mu = \mathrm{mean}\bigl( \{r(x,y_j)\}\bigr), 
\quad \sigma = \mathrm{std}\bigl( \{r(x,y_j)\}\bigr).
\]

This choice is motivated by the desire to decorrelate the magnitude of \(\widehat{A}_i\) from the reward scale and to stabilize learning.  However, it also introduces statistical fragility when the reward distribution contains outliers or is multimodal.  Alternatives worth pursuing include:

1. **Robust standardisation**  
   Replace mean and standard deviation with robust statistics such as the median and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD).  This reduces sensitivity to outliers and heavy‑tailed reward distributions, potentially yielding more stable advantages when rewards are noisy or skewed.

2. **Reward‑based baseline subtraction**  
   Instead of normalising within each group, subtract a *global* baseline such as the overall mean reward of the current batch or the moving average over training steps.  This may keep advantage estimates less noisy when group samples are few, though it may not fully capture the *relative* advantage among responses to the same query.

3. **Learned advantage scalars**  
   Introduce a *learnable* scalar \(c_{\text{adv}}\) that scales the advantages:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_i = c_{\text{adv}} \, \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma}.
   \]
   The scalar could be optimized jointly with policy parameters, allowing the model to adapt its sensitivity to reward gradients dynamically.

4. **Non‑linear transformations**  
   Apply a non‑linear mapping (e.g., clipping, log‑transform) before normalisation to suppress extreme values.  This could be beneficial when rewards have a bounded range but are heavily skewed.

Future research could run comparative studies to determine which normalisation scheme yields the best trade‑off between *sample efficiency* and *training stability*, especially on tasks with sparse or highly variable rewards.  Moreover, understanding how these normalisation choices influence the *effective* clipping bounds (since the clipping range is fixed) would shed light on their indirect impact on convergence.

## Extending GSPO beyond language models (e.g., vision)

While GSPO is tailored to sequence‑to‑sequence language generation, its core idea—adopting a *sequence‑level* importance ratio and applying the proximal rule at that granularity—can be transferred to other domains where an agent’s output is inherently factored into discrete tokens or events.  Two promising directions include:

### 1. Vision‑based sequence generation (e.g., captioning, VQA)
In visual question answering or image captioning, the policy outputs a sequence of tokens conditioned on a visual input.  The reward usually reflects correctness, fluency, or alignment with a ground truth caption.  Adapting GSPO to this setting requires (i) computing the sequence likelihood under the visual–language policy, and (ii) re‑using the same group‑based advantage normalisation.  Challenges involve handling high‑dimensional visual embeddings and potentially *latent* visual modalities (e.g., region proposals) that influence text generation.

### 2. Video or audio event generation
Generating events in audio (e.g., speech synthesis, music creation) or video (e.g., scene describing generative models) can be framed as producing *temporal* sequences of tokens or frames.  A natural extension of GSPO would treat a full sequence (e.g., an entire audio clip) as the unit for importance weighting and clipping.  This might obviate the need for token‑level value functions that are notoriously difficult to estimate in continuous‑time domains.

In both cases, a key research question is: *to what extent does the discrete token nature of the output enable or restrict the use of GSPO?*  In continuous output regimes (e.g., RGB pixel values), defining a per‑token likelihood becomes more delicate, and one may need to resort to *probability density* functions rather than discrete probabilities.  Future work could investigate *density estimation* mechanisms (e.g., normalising flows) as a substitute for token‑level likelihoods while still leveraging sequence‑level advantage signals.

## Combining with KL regularisation

The vanilla GSPO objective deliberately omits explicit KL regularisation, focusing on the clipped importance ratio and advantage scaling.  Nevertheless, a KL penalty can mitigate catastrophic policy drift by discouraging large divergence from the old policy:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{combined}}(\theta) = \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) - \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[ \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)} \right].
\]

When incorporating a KL term, several practical issues arise:

1. **Competing objectives**—The KL loss tends to push the policy toward the older distribution, which might counteract the positive reinforcement signal from high‑reward sequences.  Choosing \(\lambda\) requires careful tuning; too high a value stalls learning, too low a value may lead to instability.

2. **Clipping misalignment**—Since GSPO already uses an importance ratio that is clipped at the sequence level, adding a separate KL penalty could double‑count the same divergence measure, potentially leading to redundant regularisation or conflicting gradients.

3. **Convergence guarantees**—Analytic results for PPO with a KL penalty are subtle; incorporating GSPO’s bespoke clipping could break the assumptions underpinning such theorems. There is no existing proof that the combination preserves convergence properties.

A promising research direction is to replace the traditional KL term with a *soft* upper‑bound on the importance ratio, effectively rendering the KL penalty “implicitly” through the clipping mechanism.  Experimental work could evaluate whether this implicit regularisation suffices to prevent policy collapse in extreme settings (e.g., when rewards are extremely sparse).  Conversely, a deliberate KL penalty may help when the reward signal is too weak, encouraging exploration of untapped policy regions.

## Theoretical guarantees of convergence

Unlike standard PPO, which has been analysed under the assumption of *on‑policy* sampling and certain boundedness conditions, GSPO pushed the use of *off‑policy* importance sampling to the sequence level.  The theoretical corpus for such an approach is sparse.  Key open questions include:

- **Variance bounds**  
  What are tight upper bounds on the variance of the GSPO gradient estimator?  The expectation of the sequence likelihood ratio across a group is not trivially bounded, especially for very long sequences where the product of token probabilities can be extremely small.

- **Convergence rates**  
  Can we establish a convergence rate (e.g., \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) in expectation) for GSPO under realistic assumptions about the reward distribution and policy smoothness?  This requires handling the interplay between discrete sampling, clipping, and sequence‑level importance ratios.

- **Robustness to function approximation**  
  Since language models are large non‑linear function approximators, classical perturbed mirror descent analyses may not directly apply.  Future theoretical work could adapt *online convex optimisation* tools to the discrete context, perhaps leveraging the fact that token probabilities lie on a simplex.

- **Effect of group size**  
  The variance and bias of the GSPO gradient will depend on \(G\).  A formal analysis could relate \(G\) to guarantees on stable policy improvement—perhaps yielding a *sample‑complexity* result that informs best‑practice hyperparameter tuning.

Until such guarantees are established, practitioners must rely on empirical sanity checks.  Yet, a rigorous theoretical scaffold would lend credence to GSPO’s stability claims and could guide principled extensions (e.g., dynamic clipping ranges, adaptive group sizing).

---

## Concluding Remarks

GSPO surmounts several practical hurdles in scaling reinforcement learning for large language models—most notably, the volatility of token‑level importance ratios and the fragmentation introduced by sparse activation in Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures.  However, as with any algorithm, it inhabits a narrow region of the design space.  The avenues outlined above point toward a richer, more flexible framework where sequence‑level stability coexists with token‑level nuance, robust advantage estimation, and principled convergence guarantees.  The community’s next challenge is to fill these gaps, thereby turning the empirical triumphs of GSPO into solid theoretical foundations scalable to the next generation of intelligent systems.
