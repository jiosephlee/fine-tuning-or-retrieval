\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Existing Algorithms: PPO and GRPO  

Large language models are optimized through reinforcement learning (RL) by treating the model as an autoregressive policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  Two influential algorithms that predate GSPO are **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** and its value‑free variant **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  Understanding the mechanics, strengths, and inherent pitfalls of these methods is crucial for appreciating the motivation behind GSPO.  In this chapter we dissect PPO’s clipping scheme, its reliance on a value network, and the practical difficulties that arise when the policy generates long sequences.  We then turn to GRPO, which eliminates the value model at the cost of token‑level importance weights, and we illustrate with concrete examples how this design choice introduces high‑variance gradients and destabilizes training.  

## PPO review: clipping, advantage estimation, value model dependence  

PPO is grounded in the idea of staying close to the **old policy** $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ while improving the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  Using rollouts generated from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, PPO defines a surrogate objective that attempts to maximally increase expected reward but penalizes large deviations from the old policy via a **clipping** operation (Equation (1) in the paper).  For each token $y_t$ in a generated response $y$, PPO introduces an **importance ratio**  

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})},
\]

and an **advantage estimate** $\widehat{A}_t$ that captures how much better or worse the token is relative to a baseline.  The baseline is typically a separate neural network, called the **value model**, trained to predict the expected return of a partial sequence.  

Formally, PPO’s objective is  

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \!\!
 \left[ \frac{1}{|y|}\!\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
 \min\!\bigl(
 w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,
 \mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_t
 \bigr)\right].
\]

The clipping mechanism guarantees that the update is “proximal”: if the ratio $w_t(\theta)$ drifts beyond the range $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$, the objective reverts to a clipped value that limits the influence of that token on the gradient.  In effect, PPO suppresses the contribution of drastically off‑policy tokens, protecting against large, destabilizing policy changes.  

Crucially, the **advantage** $\widehat{A}_t$ is computed as  

\[
\widehat{A}_t = R_t - V_{\theta_{\text{val}}}(x, y_{<t}),
\]

where $R_t$ is the observed return from time $t$ onward and $V_{\theta_{\text{val}}}$ is the value network.  Training a high‑quality value model is nontrivial: it must be small enough to fit within the same memory budget as the policy, yet accurate enough to provide reliable estimates over *long* sequences.  This requirement grows more challenging when the model generates hundreds of tokens or specialized tasks such as problem solving, where the return distribution can be highly skewed.

In sum, PPO’s strengths are its simplicity, theoretical guarantees, and the elegant balance between exploitation and policy stability.  Its major practical limitation lies in the dependency on a high‑dimension value network that is expensive to train and evaluate, particularly for large, sparse models or for tasks that demand long‑form generation.

## Practical issues of PPO (memory, long sequences)

For large language models, especially those with a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) architecture, several implementation constraints surface:

1. **Memory Bandwidth** – Every token in a rollout requires accessing the value network, which is comparable in size to the policy itself.  When the rollout batch must contain a *large number* of samples to saturate GPU cores, memory traffic quickly overwhelms inter‑device bandwidth, leading to stalls or the need for aggressive gradient accumulation.

2. **Bias for Short Sequences** – The clipping range $\varepsilon$ is typically tuned on *short* sequences.  When applied to longer responses, the relative importance ratios tend to become larger in magnitude, thereby shrinking the effective clipping window.  This can cause tokens far from the start of a response to be over‑re‑weighted, inflating variance and reducing convergence speed.

3. **Computational Latency** – The value network must be evaluated for every partial sequence, adding a non‑trivial forward‑pass cost that substantially slows down training.  In practice, this cost becomes a bottleneck when scaling to thousands of GPUs, forcing practitioners to settle for sub‑optimal settings (e.g., fewer tokens per rollout) that compromise the quality of the model.

Because of these challenges, researchers have looked for alternatives that bypass the value model, giving rise to GRPO.

## GRPO formulation: group sampling, token‑level importance ratios  

GRPO is a *value‑free* variant of PPO that replaces the per‑token advantage estimate with a relative advantage computed over a *group* of responses generated to the same query.  
Given a query $x$, GRPO samples a batch of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)$, and then defines the advantage for token $y_{i,t}$ as

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i
:= \frac{r(x, y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
\quad 
\mu=\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G},
\quad
\sigma=\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G},
\]

where $r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$ denotes the verifier‑scored reward.  
All tokens in a response share the same advantage, but each token retains its **token‑level importance ratio**  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The GRPO objective mirrors PPO’s structure but aggregates over the entire group and token:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\bigr)
\right].
\]

GRPO’s appeal lies in *eliminating the value network*, thereby saving memory and computational budget and simplifying implementation.  Furthermore, the group‑wise advantage normalisation mitigates reward scale issues, a common problem when using raw scores from verifiers.

## Advantages of GRPO (value-free)

* **Memory Efficiency** – Without a separate value model, GRPO can allocate more of the limited GPU memory to larger batch sizes or more powerful policy heads, especially valuable for MoE architectures that already push the limits of memory usage.

* **Simplified Training Pipeline** – Removing the value network reduces the number of hyperparameters and the need for separate pre‑training or fine‑tuning steps for a value head, streamlining the RL pipeline.

* **End‑to‑End Stability in Short‑Length Tasks** – For many early RL‑training setups, GRPO can achieve stable convergence on tasks with short generation lengths, as the token‑level importance ratios have relatively low variance in this regime.

These merits, however, mask deeper issues that become manifest when GRPO is applied to longer, more complex sequences or to sparse MoE models.

## Illustrative examples of token‑level weighting problems  

Consider a toy scenario where a response $y$ consists of five tokens generated to a query $x$.  Suppose the policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ produces the sequence
\[
y = (\texttt{a},\texttt{b},\texttt{c},\texttt{d},\texttt{e}),
\]
and the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ assigns a significantly higher probability to token $\texttt{c}$ while sharply reducing probabilities for the others.  Concretely, let

| token | $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)$ | $\pi_{\theta}(y_t)$ |
|-------|---------------------------------|---------------------|
| a     | 0.15 | 0.05 |
| b     | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| c     | 0.25 | 0.50 |
| d     | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| e     | 0.20 | 0.05 |

The token‑level importance ratios become  

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}=
(0.33,\;0.50,\;2.00,\;0.50,\;0.25).
\]

GRPO would weight the gradient of each token by its corresponding ratio: the third token’s gradient is amplified eight‐fold ($2.00$), while the first and last become negligible ($0.33$ and $0.25$).  If the reward is assigned to the entire sequence, such token‑level re‑weighting injects *high‑variance* noise into the gradient: one token dominates the learning signal, even though the reward is a single scalar for the whole response.  When sequences become long, this variance propagates multiplicatively across tokens, making the gradient estimation highly unstable.

Furthermore, when the importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$ exceeds $1+\varepsilon$, the clipping term in GRPO truncates the effective weight to $1+\varepsilon$, which for token–wise ratios can be as severe as clipping a factor of $2$ down to $1.27$ (for $\varepsilon=0.27$).  Tokens with ratios below $1-\varepsilon$ are likewise capped, potentially discarding informative gradients.  In large language models that produce *hundreds* of tokens, the probability that *any* token’s ratio falls outside the clipping band increases dramatically, leading to excessive gradient *sparsification* and wasted samples.

These practical pitfalls become particularly acute in MoE settings, where *expert‑activation volatility* disturbs the per‑token likelihoods more than the overall sequence likelihood.  As the paper demonstrates, GRPO’s token‑level re‑weighting can cause catastrophic model collapse that is difficult to recover from, especially when the training batch size is scaled up for efficiency.

In contrast, the GSPO algorithm eschews token‑level weighting entirely.  It defines a *sequence‑level* importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

which normalises the effect of sequence length and treats every token in a response as part of a single, shared reward signal.  By clipping on the sequence level, GSPO preserves inter‑token gradient correlations and eliminates the high‑variance artefacts inherent to GRPO while retaining the value‑free benefit.  This design, rooted in the principled theory of importance sampling, ultimately yields a more stable and efficient RL algorithm for large language models.
