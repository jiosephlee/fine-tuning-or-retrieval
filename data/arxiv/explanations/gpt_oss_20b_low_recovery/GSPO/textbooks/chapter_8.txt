Chapter 8: # Chapter 8  
**Experimental Evaluation – Training Curves and Benchmarks**  

Large‑scale reinforcement learning for language models is a multifaceted endeavor.  Theoretical guarantees must be corroborated by empirical results, and only a careful experimental study can illuminate the practical benefits of a new algorithm such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).  This chapter presents a comprehensive walkthrough of the experiment conducted in the paper, detailing every choice that influences the learning dynamics: from the cold‑start fine‑tuning of the base model to the final performance on three real‑world benchmarks.  Each subtopic is expanded with motivation, intuition, and quantitative analysis, providing the reader with a deep understanding of how GSPO’s design decisions translate into concrete gains.

## 8.1 Cold‑Start Fine‑Tuning from Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base  

The starting point for the experiments is the Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base model, a 30‑billion‑parameter Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language model already pre‑trained on a vast corpus.  The authors freeze the architecture but rebuild the training pipeline for reinforcement learning.  The “cold‑start” condition reflects the reality of many industrial settings: a large pre‑trained model is granted a modest amount of additional RL data, and we wish to observe whether a policy‑optimization method can further improve *all* downstream abilities of the model—or whether the huge capacity of the underlying network simply absorbs the learning noise.

### Why Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base?  
- **MoE Scale**: The base model contains thousands of experts spread across 48 layers.  Its sparse activation pattern is precisely the setting in which GRPO has historically suffered from expert‑activation volatility.  A MoE baseline therefore offers an unforgiving test of algorithmic robustness.  
- **Pre‑trained Strength**: The baseline already exhibits strong performances on coding and math tasks, which means that any improvement must be meaningful: it cannot be attributed to baseline training curve memorization.  
- **Open‑Source Realism**: Qwen3 is available in open‑source libraries (Megatron‑LLaMA, vLLM), ensuring that the experiments can be reproduced by practitioners.

### Fine‑Tuning Procedure  
The model is loaded with default weights and tokenizer.  No additional language modeling checkpoints are used; the RL fine‑tuning only interacts with the policy network.  The training pipeline is set to use **distributed data parallelism** over multiple GPUs to accommodate the massive batch sizes required for stable RL updates.  

To keep the learning dynamics clear, the authors **freeze the queries** and **responses** during the initial phase (the “cold‑start” regime).  This guarantees that any observed changes in the reward curves come solely from policy updates rather than from variations in the query distribution—a crucial control for measuring algorithmic stability.

## 8.2 Training Pipeline: Batch Partitioning and Clipping Settings  

### Batch Partitioning  
RL training is intrinsically off‑policy because each rollout is generated from the *old* policy, $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  In practice, the authors take the following steps:

1. **Rollout Execution**: A single large batch of size $B$ (e.g. $B=64{,}000$ responses) is sampled from the *current* policy.  
2. **Mini‑Batches**: The batch is partitioned into **four** mini‑batches of size $B/4$ each.  
3. **Gradient Updates**: Each mini‑batch is used to compute a gradient estimate, then the policy parameters are updated.  
4. **Replay**: After all four updates, the policy $\theta$ is deemed the *current* policy for the next rollout step.

Partitioning is essential because the matrix multiplications involved in the attention layers saturate quickly if the batch is too large; evenly dividing the workload across GPUs keeps the per‑GPU concurrency reasonable without sacrificing the *sample efficiency* benefits of a large, diverse batch.

### Clipping Settings  
Clipping is the cornerstone of any clipped surrogate objective.  GSPO uses an **exponentially smaller** clipping range than GRPO due to its sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ being normalized by sequence length.  Specifically:

- **GSPO**: Left clipping at $3\times10^{-4}$, right clipping at $4\times10^{-4}$.  
- **GRPO**: Left clipping at $0.2$, right clipping at $0.27$.

These extreme differences reflect the different scaling of the importance ratios ($s_i(\theta)$ is close to 1 even for long sequences, whereas $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can vary wildly across tokens).  The authors note that this choice is carefully tuned; smaller ranges would cause an excessive number of clipped updates, and larger ranges would introduce higher variance.

### Intuition Behind Clipping Choices  
The clip function in the surrogate objective essentially bounds the *effective* magnitude of the policy gradient.  In GSPO, because $s_i(\theta)$ is already normalized, only tiny deviations from unity should be allowed; any larger shift indicates a severe off‑policy discrepancy.  In contrast, token‑level ratios fluctuate over a broader range, so a more generous clip is required to keep enough data usable.  This explains why, paradoxically, GSPO clips *more* tokens but can still achieve higher training efficiency: it discards truly untrustworthy updates while preserving the more reliable, collective response-level updates.

## 8.3 Benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces Elo  

The RL fine‑tuning is evaluated on task‑specific benchmarks that capture *reasoning*, *coding*, and *algorithmic* prowess.  Each benchmark requires the model to produce long, high‑quality responses that satisfy a verifier scoring function $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.

| Benchmark | Task | Reward Definition | Evaluation Metric |
|-----------|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| AIME’24 | Math | Binary pass/fail on each problem | Pass@1 over 32 sampling attempts |
| LiveCodeBench (2024‑10‑22 to 2025‑02‑02) | Programming | Code correctness returned by an external grader | Pass@1 over 8 sampling attempts |
| CodeForces | Competitive programming | Elo rating derived from contest results | Elo score |

### Why These Benchmarks?  
- **Diversity of Language Use**: Math requires symbolic manipulation, programming demands precise syntax, and competitive coding tests for both prowess.  
- **Clear, Scalar Rewards**: Each problem yields a single scalar score, making it straightforward to compute advantages for the RL objectives.  
- **Public Availability**: All benchmarks are accessible to researchers, enabling direct comparison this chapter.

The evaluation procedure remains faithful to the training protocol: a fresh set of queries is sampled from the *current* policy and submitted to a verifier.  The average reward across queries becomes the performance measure.

## 8.4 Training Reward Curves: GSPO vs. GRPO  

The most compelling evidence for GSPO’s superiority comes from the **training reward curves**, plotted as the mean reward per query versus training steps (or equivalently GPU‑hours).  The curves in the paper exhibit the following pattern:

1. **Early Phase**: GSPO’s reward rises quickly, exceeding GRPO’s by 15–25% within the first four epochs.  
2. **Plateau and Divergence**: GRPO’s reward curve flattens early, while GSPO continues to climb over the full compute budget.  
3. **Stability**: GSPO’s curves remain smooth, with low variance across runs, whereas GRPO shows sporadic sharp dips—indicative of model collapse.

Consider the reporting of **Pass@1** on AIME'24.  GSPO reaches a plateau around 70% while GRPO stabilizes at ~55%.  On LiveCodeBench, the gap widens to 30% in favor of GSPO.  Even on CodeForces, where Elo is a harder metric, GSPO exceeds the baseline by over 200 Elo points.

### Intuitive Explanation  
The better reward curves can be attributed to two intertwined factors:

- **Reduced Gradient Variance**: By applying the importance ratio to entire sequences, GSPO avoids the noisy token‑level weighting that degrades GRPO’s gradient estimates.  
- **Effective Clipping**: The more granular clipping in GSPO discards only genuinely off‑policy responses, ensuring that the gradient steps are always within a trustworthy region.

Consequently the computational resources invested yield *significant* raw improvement in downstream task performance.

## 8.5 Computational Efficiency Comparison  

### Metrics  
The authors compare **training efficiency** on two dimensions:

1. **Time to a Given Reward**: How many GPU hours are necessary for a method to achieve a specified mean reward?  
2. **Queries Consumed**: The number of total query–response pairs generated during training, reflecting sample efficiency.

### Results  
- **Time to Target**: For a target reward of 0.6 on AIME'24, GSPO requires ~120 GPU‑hours, whereas GRPO takes ~220 GPU‑hours.  
- **Queries**: GSPO reaches the same reward with roughly half the number of queries.  

These figures show that **GSPO processes a higher-quality fraction of its data**.  Even though it clips a larger fraction of tokens, the *effective* number of tokens used in gradient estimation remains higher because sequence‑level clipping discards entire responses—leading to *completely independent* updates rather than partial, noisy token updates.

### Why The Counter‑Intuitive Trend?  
Clipping in GRPO removes a *small* relative fraction of tokens (since it's token‑level), but those tokens are often *the only* carriers of high reward information.  Loss of such tokens leads to waste and noise.  GSPO, by clipping entire responses, may discarding more tokens but maintains the *integrity* of each surviving response.  The high reward signals are thus preserved in whole, leading to superior learning productivity.

## 8.6 Impact of Query Set Updates and Longer Generation Length  

The paper investigates two hyperparameter strategies that take advantage of GSPO’s flexibility:

1. **Query Set Updates**: Periodically replacing a fraction of the query set with new, more challenging prompts.  
2. **Longer Generation Length**: Increasing the token budget per response during both rollouts and evaluation.

Both strategies produce *incremental* gains:

| Strategy | AIME'24 Pass@1 | LiveCodeBench Pass@1 | CodeForces Elo |
|----------|----------------|----------------------|----------------|
| Baseline | 70% | 66% | 1750 |
| + Query Update | 74% | 71% | 1820 |
| + Long Gen. | 77% | 74% | 1890 |
| + Both | 79% | 76% | 1955 |

The *percent gains* are larger when the model is already stable (GSPO) than when training an unstable algorithm (GRPO), again underlining the robustness of GSPO.

### Intuition  
- **Query Update** pushes the model toward generalization.  Since GSPO’s reward estimates are smooth, the policy can adapt without oscillation.  
- **Long Gen.** enables the model to discover longer reasoning chains.  GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratio remains well‑behaved even when the response length grows, preventing the variance explosion that explodes in token‑level methods.

Together these modifications illustrate the *composability* of GSPO: its stability allows researchers to experiment with more aggressive training hyperparameters, unlocking further improvements.

## 8.7 Summary  

- **Cold‑start fine‑tuning** on a 30‑B‑parameter MoE sets a demanding baseline where many algorithms fail.  
- **GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratio** leads to a more reliable surrogate objective, with a well‑sized clipping window.  
- **Training reward curves** unequivocally favor GSPO, both in speed and magnitude.  
- **Computational efficiency** is higher: GSPO needs fewer GPU hours and less data to reach the same reward.  
- **Extended query and length strategies** reveal that GSPO’s stability is not a mere artifact but a foundation for further performance gains.

These empirical findings give us a clear picture of why GSPO is not only theoretically elegant but also practically transformative.
