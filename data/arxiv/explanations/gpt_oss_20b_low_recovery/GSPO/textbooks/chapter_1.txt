Chapter 1: # Group Sequence Policy Optimization

## 1. Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has rapidly become a cornerstone for pushing the boundaries of large language models (LLMs). Unlike traditional supervised fine‑tuning, RL allows a model to interact with an environment—an evaluation oracle or verifier that assigns rewards to complete responses—and adapt its generation strategy so as to maximize cumulative reward. This capability has enabled LLMs to solve tasks that require long, multi‑step reasoning, such as advanced mathematics problems and complex programming challenges.  

However, scaling RL to gigantic LLMs is not straightforward. The training dynamics of RL algorithms can become fragile when the model size, sparsity (as in Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures), or response length grow. Instabilities often manifest as catastrophic collapse of the policy, a phenomenon that has been observed in state‑of‑the‑art RL systems that train language models. A stable, scalable RL method is therefore essential to unlock the full potential of next‑generation LLMs.

The paper *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* proposes a robust solution to this challenge: a new algorithm called GSPO. By redefining the importance sampling ratios at the sequence level and aligning clipping, rewarding, and optimization to this unit, GSPO eliminates the high‑variance noise that plagues earlier methods such as GRPO. The following sections unpack the motivations, core ideas, and practical implications of GSPO, highlighting its advantages over existing approaches.

### *What is RL in language modeling?*

In the language modeling setting, the agent is an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$ that generates tokens one by one conditioned on a prompt $x$. The trajectory of tokens $y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ constitutes a response. An external verifier $r$ evaluates the entire sequence and returns a scalar reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$. The objective is to adjust $\theta$ so that the expected reward of the policy is maximized. Because the policy must generate *complete* sequences before the reward is known, RL for language modeling requires handling long horizons and non‑differentiable reward signals—exactly the scenario where policy gradient methods shine.

### *Historical progress: from supervised learning to RL*

The initial generation of high‑quality LLMs relied on supervised learning (SL), where the model is trained to predict the next token from a vast corpus of text. SL alone cannot steer the model toward optimal behaviors that are difficult to capture from data alone, such as adhering to human preferences or solving technical domains. RL was introduced to bridge this gap, beginning with simple reward functions for text style or factuality. As the difficulty of target tasks increased, researchers adapted RL to longer responses and more complex reward signals. The evolution from SL to RL for LLMs is marked by a gradual shift: first, fine‑tuning on curated datasets; next, reward‑guided optimization via RL; finally, highly specialized RL systems tailored to scale (e.g., RLHF, PERL). Each stage introduced new algorithmic challenges: policy stability, sample efficiency, and computational demands.

### *Key success stories (Qwen, LLMs in math/programming)*

Recent LLMs such as **Qwen3**, **ChatGPT**, and many others have demonstrated formidable abilities in domains that were traditionally intractable for purely supervised models. For instance, Qwen3 achieves high success rates on the AIME competition, surpassing human experts, and on CodeForces it exhibits top‑tier Elo ratings. These accomplishments are largely credited to the extensive RL training pipelines that refine the model’s reasoning pathways and reward‑aligned behaviors. RL has also enabled LLMs to produce code that passes a wide array of programming benchmarks. By evaluating the entire generated code against a comprehensive test suite, the RL process effectively teaches the model to plan, debug, and execute multi‑step commands.

### *Stability vs. scalability: why larger models need new algorithms*

As LLMs grow in scale, several factors conspire to destabilize conventional RL algorithms:

1. **Long response length**: Each sampled sequence can contain thousands of tokens. Gradients derived from token‑wise importance ratios accumulate noise over long horizons, inflating variance.
2. **Sparse attention and high‑parameter MoE layers**: Modern MoE architectures split workloads across numerous experts. The routing decisions change dramatically after each policy update, rendering token‑wise importance ratios unreliable.
3. **High‑throughput hardware demands**: Training large models requires very large batch sizes. Gradient updates are performed on mini‑batches that are often off‑policy, necessitating careful off‑policy correction.

The widely used **GRPO** algorithm attempts to circumvent the need for a value network by computing *relative advantages* among multiple responses to the same prompt. Yet, GRPO’s token‑wise importance ratios introduce high‑variance noise, especially problematic in the above contexts. Consequently, training can collapse irrevocably, and recovering from failure may require extensive hyper‑parameter search or structural changes to the model.

GSPO addresses these issues by **aligning the optimization unit with the reward unit**. Rewards are assigned to *entire sequences*, so it is natural to treat the whole sequence as the fundamental object for importance sampling and clipping. This change reduces variance, stabilizes training even for sparse MoE models, and simplifies infrastructure requirements.

### *Overview of the paper’s contributions (GSPO)*

The paper proposes the following key ideas:

1. **Sequence‑level importance ratios**: GSPO defines an importance ratio
   $$
   s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
   $$
   which indicates how the *entire* response probability density has changed from the old policy to the new one. The exponent $1/|y_i|$ normalizes the ratio across sequences of different lengths, preventing extreme variability.

2. **Sequence‑level clipping**: The algorithm clips the sequence importance ratio using a proximal range $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ rather than clipping each token individually. This ensures that only responses that deviate too far from the old policy influence the gradient.

3. **Group‑based advantage estimation**: Rather than requiring a value function, GSPO evaluates the advantage of each generated response by comparing its reward to the mean and standard deviation of all responses to the same prompt. This keeps the estimation lightweight and aligns naturally with the reward signal.

4. **Empirical validation**: The paper demonstrates that GSPO outperforms GRPO on the Qwen3 family of models, both in training efficiency and final benchmark performance. It also shows that GSPO removes the need for a costly *Routing Replay* trick for MoE models, thereby simplifying training pipelines.

5. **Infrastructure simplifications**: Because GSPO works with sequence likelihoods, it tolerates precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. This can enable direct reuse of inference outputs for RL updates, saving recomputation time.

By addressing the root cause of instability in token‑based importance sampling, GSPO offers a principled, scalable alternative for RL training of large language models. The rest of this chapter dives deeper into the mechanics behind these ideas, the mathematical intuitions, and the practical implications for training MoE models and building robust RL pipelines.
