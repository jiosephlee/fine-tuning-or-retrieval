\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 10: # 10. GSPO for Mixture‑of‑Experts Models

Large language models that employ a **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architecture distribute computation across a sparse set of expert parameters. During each decoding step, only a subset of experts is activated for a given token. This sparsity gives MoE models a substantial computational advantage, but it also introduces a unique challenge when training these models with reinforcement learning (RL). In this chapter we dissect the difficulties that arise from the volatile expert‑activation patterns, examine how the earlier **Routing Replay** strategy was conceived to mitigate this issue, and explain why the **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm renders such a workaround unnecessary.

---

## MoE Architecture and Expert Activation Volatility

In a typical MoE language model, the transformer layers are augmented with a gating mechanism that selects a small number of experts from a larger pool at each token. At decoding time, each token \(y_t\) is produced by a **routing** function that determines which experts are responsible for computing the next‑token probability under the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). Because the routing decision depends on the token history \(x, y_{<t}\), the specific set of experts activated for a particular response \(y\) can change dramatically as the model parameters \(\theta\) are updated during RL training.

This phenomenon is referred to as **expert‑activation volatility**. Empirical observations in the paper reveal that, for a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base MoE model, the set of experts activated under the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) for a given rollout sample differs from that under the previous policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for roughly ten percent of the tokens. In deeper MoE models, this volatility tends to increase. Consequently, the probability \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) that appears in token‑level importance ratios can fluctuate significantly when the experts shift, thereby undermining the assumption that these ratios represent a reliable off‑policy correction factor.

---

## Problems with Token‑level Importance Ratios in MoE

Reinforcement learning algorithms such as **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** rely on token‑level importance ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
These ratios are intended to correct for the difference between the current policy and the policy that produced the samples. However, when expert activation changes between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\), the numerator and denominator are computed on entirely different sets of active experts, leading to **unreliable and highly variable importance ratios**. 

In the importance‑sampling framework, the ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) is meant to re‑weight samples drawn from the behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) so that the expectation under the target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) is correctly estimated. This requires that the ratio be **estimated accurately for each sample**. The token‑level ratios in MoE do not satisfy this requirement because the denominator and numerator are derived from different functional sub‑graphs of the model. Consequently, the variance introduced by these ratios can accumulate over long sequences, getting amplified by the clipping mechanism that GRPO uses to restrict overly large or small ratios. The net effect is destabilisation of the training dynamics, often leading to catastrophic model collapse that cannot be recovered even by reverting to earlier checkpoints.

---

## Routing Replay Strategy and Its Overhead

To mitigate the volatility problem, a strategy called **Routing Replay** was previously introduced. The key idea is to **cache the routing decisions** (i.e., the set of experts activated) that were used by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for each rollout sample. During the gradient update, the algorithm **replays** these stored routing modes while computing the current policy’s probabilities. As a result, the numerator and denominator in the token‑level importance ratio are evaluated on the *same* expert configuration:
\[
w_{i,t}^{\text{Replay}}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t};\,\text{cached routing})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t};\,\text{cached routing})}.
\]
By forcing the two probability estimates to share the same active experts, Routing Replay reduces the variance of the importance ratios and restores more reliable off‑policy corrections.

However, **Routing Replay carries significant practical costs**:
1. **Memory overhead**: The routing masks for each token in each response must be stored between training steps, leading to non‑trivial memory consumption, especially for long responses.
2. **Communication overhead**: In distributed training, routing masks need to be synchronized across workers, adding extra communication bandwidth.
3. **Capacity constraints**: By enforcing that the current policy use the same experts as the old policy, the method artificially restricts the exploration of new expert combinations, potentially limiting the full expressive capacity of the MoE model.

These downsides motivated the search for a RL algorithm that can **avoid the need for Routing Replay** while maintaining or improving stability.

---

## GSPO’s Sequence‑Likelihood Focus and Immunity to Routing Shifts

**GSPO** rethinks the unit at which importance ratios are applied. Instead of weighting each token separately, GSPO defines a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Here \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)\) denotes the **full sequence likelihood** under the current policy. Even though the sequence is produced by passing through a sparse subset of experts, the *overall* probability of generating the entire response changes **more smoothly** than the token‑level probabilities. The reason is that the activation pattern for a response can fluctuate at individual tokens, but the product of all token probabilities (i.e., the sequence likelihood) remains comparatively stable. 

Because GSPO applies clipping and weighting with respect to \(s_i(\theta)\) *instead of* token‑level ratios, the training dynamics are no longer sensitive to the per‑token expert activation. The sequence likelihood covers the entire decoding path, integrating over any transient expert shifts. Thus, even if the experts activated for a token change between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\), the impact on \(s_i(\theta)\) is attenuated, preventing the variance spike that destabilised GRPO.

---

## Experimental Evidence of Stability without Routing Replay

The paper presents empirical comparisons between a GRPO‑based MoE training that employs Routing Replay and a GSPO‑based MoE training that does **not** use Routing Replay. Figure 4 in the manuscript shows that, without Routing Replay, the GRPO model fails to converge: the training reward curves stagnate or exhibit erratic drops. In stark contrast, the GSPO‑trained MoE model demonstrates a **stable, monotonic increase** in both training reward and downstream benchmark performance over the same number of training steps.

Furthermore, the GSPO experiments substitute the **left and right clipping ranges** in Equation (4) with values in the order of \(10^{-4}\), whereas GRPO uses ranges around \(0.2\)–\(0.27\). Despite the much narrower clipping window in GSPO, the training proceeds without collapse, underscoring that the sequence‑level importance ratio eliminates the high‑variance token‑level importance ratios that cause instability.

These results confirm that GSPO can **train MoE models effectively without any auxiliary routing manipulation**.

---

## Benefits: Memory, Communication, and Full Model Capacity

By eliminating the need for Routing Replay, GSPO yields several concrete advantages:

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Reduced Memory Footprint** | No caching of per‑token routing masks is required, freeing memory that can be used for larger batch sizes or deeper models. |
| **Lower Communication Overhead** | Distributed training no longer needs to synchronize routing patterns across devices, simplifying the communication schedule. |
| **No Explored Capacity Restrictions** | The current policy may freely activate any combination of experts for a response, allowing the MoE to **explore its full capacity** rather than being constrained to the old routing patterns. |
| **Simplified Training Pipeline** | The algorithmic complexity drops: no special replay buffers, no edited forward passes, and fewer hyperparameters to tune for routing stability. |

These benefits align directly with the broader goal of scaling RL training of large language models: to leverage the computational advantages of MoE while maintaining robust, efficient training dynamics.

---

## Takeaway

The **GSPO** framework fundamentally shifts the RL training objective from token‑wise to sequence‑wise. This subtle but crucial change aligns the importance sampling correction with the unit of reward, thereby **bypassing the expert‑activation volatility** that plagues token‑level algorithms like GRPO. Consequently, GSPO enables **stable, efficient RL training of MoE language models without the costly Routing Replay strategy**, unlocking the full expressive power of Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures and paving the way for further scaling of RL‑based language model capabilities.
