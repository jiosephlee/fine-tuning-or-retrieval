## Autoregressive Policy Representation

In language modeling, we view a trained transformer as a *policy* in the reinforcement‑learning sense: given a prompt (or *query*) \(x\), the model emits tokens one after another until a stopping criterion is met.  
Because each token prediction depends on everything seen so far, we speak of an **autoregressive policy** \(\pi_{\theta}\) parameterized by weights \(\theta\).  

Formally, for a query \(x\), the policy specifies a conditional distribution over the vocabulary at time step \(t\):
\[
\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t\mid x,\;y_{<t}\bigr)
\;,
\quad t=1,2,\dots,|y|
\;,
\]
where \(y_{<t} = (y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})\) are the previously generated tokens.

The autonomy of this policy means that, at inference time, sampling is a straightforward sequential process: draw \(y_1\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\), then draw \(y_2\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_1)\), and so on.  
In reinforcement‑learning parlance, the *action* at each time step \(t\) is the chosen token \(y_t\), and the entire trajectory \((x,y)\) is a stochastic episode whose probability we can compute exactly.

## Queries, Responses, and Dataset Notation

We work with a collection of **queries** \(\mathcal{D}\).  
A query \(x\) is simply a prompt or instruction that initiates a language generation episode.  
To study policy learning we need a dataset of query–response pairs:
\[
\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N , \qquad x^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}.
\]
The dataset \( \mathcal{D}\) is typically a large collection of user intents, test problems, or any domain‑specific prompts relevant to the downstream task (e.g., programming, mathematics, or dialogue).  

During training we will sample queries uniformly from \(\mathcal{D}\) and then generate several responses for each query using the current policy.  The number of responses per query is denoted by \(G\), referred to as the **group size**.  
When \(G>1\), the responses can be interpreted as *rollouts* of the same environment, providing multiple realizations of the stochastic policy for subsequent importance‑sampling corrections.

## Sequence Likelihood Formulation

Given a fixed query \(x\) and a complete response \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\), the likelihood of that response under policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is the product of the per‑token probabilities:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
\;=\;
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}
\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,\;y_{<t}\bigr).
\tag{2.1}
\]
The term \(|y|\) counts the number of tokens in the response.  
Equation (2.1) reflects the very definition of an autoregressive process: the joint probability is the chain rule decomposition.

The *sequence likelihood* is a scalar number that indicates how strongly the policy regards the entire response as likely.  
It will be the backbone of the importance‑sampling ratios used later in the training objective.

## Reward Definition and Verification

Reinforcement learning requires a **reward** that evaluates the quality of a complete response rather than each token in isolation.  
We denote a generic **verifier** or **estimator** by \(r\).  The verifier receives a query–response pair \((x,y)\) and outputs a scalar reward
\[
r(x,y) \;\in\; [0,1].
\]
In many practical settings, \(r(x,y)\) is obtained by an external system: for example, a grader that compares the model output with a reference solution, a human annotator, or a rule‑based metric.  
The crucial property is that rewards are only defined for entire responses, not for partial prefixes or individual tokens.

Given a group \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) generated for the same query, we convert these raw rewards into **normalized advantages**:
\[
\widehat{A}_i
\;=\;
\frac{
  r(x,y_i)
  \;-\;
  \mathrm{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}{
  \mathrm{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}.
\tag{2.2}
\]
This standardization centers rewards around zero and scales them by their empirical standard deviation, providing a stable signal that mitigates drastic reward spikes and encourages balanced updates.

## Importance Sampling Fundamentals

Many reinforcement‑learning algorithms rely on **importance sampling** to reuse previously collected data.  
Suppose we wish to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(z)\) under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) but have samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
  \!\left[
    \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,
    f(z)
  \right].
\tag{2.3}
\]
The *importance weight* \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) corrects for the fact that the sample was not drawn from the target distribution.  
In practice, the target is the *new* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) we wish to update, while the behavior is the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) that produced the data.  
Crucially, importance sampling is unbiased only when the expectation is taken over *many* independent samples; a single sample has high variance and can lead to unstable updates.

## Brief Refresher: PPO Objective

The **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm is a cornerstone of modern policy‑gradient methods.  
Using data generated by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), PPO imposes a *clipping* constraint that keeps the updated policy close to the old one, thereby limiting the variance of the gradient estimates.

For token‑level updates, PPO’s objective (ignoring the KL‑regularization term for clarity) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\left[
  \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  \min\!\!\left(
    w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,
    \;\mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_t 
  \right)
\right].
\tag{2.4}
\]
Here, the **importance ratio** at token \(t\) is
\[
w_t(\theta)
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,\,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,\,y_{<t})},
\]
and \(\widehat{A}_t\) is an advantage estimate typically obtained from a separate value network.

The clipping operation ensures that if the ratio \(w_t(\theta)\) deviates too much from 1 (the “on‑policy” case), the contribution is bounded.  
Nevertheless, PPO’s reliance on token‑level importance ratios introduces substantial sensitivity: each token can swing the objective widely, especially for long sequences where the product of small deviations accumulates.
