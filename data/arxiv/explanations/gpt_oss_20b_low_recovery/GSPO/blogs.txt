# Why Token‑Level Weights Are Killing Your LLM RL

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the engine behind the most powerful language models of today, from GPT‑4 to Qwen‑3.  Yet, training these models at scale is notoriously fragile.  A single mis‑configured learning signal can shatter an entire training run, forcing a costly rollback to an earlier checkpoint.  A surprising root cause turns out to be a design decision that many started with: **flipping a token‑by‑token importance ratio**.

In this post we trace the problem, show why it explodes on long responses, and explain how a shift to *sequence‑level* thinking – the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm – restores stability and unlocks new scaling paths.

---

## 1. The Old‑School RL Objective

The standard RL recipe for language models is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).  PPO samples a batch of responses \(y\) from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), then updates the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) in a clipped, conservative way:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(
w_{t}(\theta)\hat A_{t},
\!\operatorname{clip}(w_{t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\!\hat A_{t}
\bigr)
\right].
\]

The *importance ratio* \(w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}\) measures how much the current policy diverges from the old one at each **token**.

PPO would *normally* work because of the tiny learning steps enforced by the clipping range \(\varepsilon\).  But PPO is heavy‑weight: it needs a separate value model to estimate \(\hat A_{t}\), and its per‑token weighting is brittle for our biggest models.

---

## 2. GRPO – “You Don’t Need a Value Model”

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) sidestepped PPO’s value model downstream by computing a *relative advantage* per response, not per token:

\[
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}}
{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}} .
\]

Where \(G\) is the number of responses generated for the same query \(x\).  
GRPO’s objective looks like

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\!\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\!\widehat A_i
\bigr)
\right].
\]

Critically, **the per‑token weight** \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is still the same token‑level ratio as in PPO, but the advantage is shared across the whole response.

One thinks: *“No value model! Great!”*  But the reality is that token‑level weights become a treacherous source of high‑variance noise, especially when the responses are long.

---

## 3. Why Token‑Level Importance Weights Are a Bad Idea

### 3.1. Importance Sampling 101

In classic importance sampling, we correct for drawing samples from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) while estimating an expectation under a *target* distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\):

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

The weight \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}}{\pi_{\text{beh}}}\) is meaningful **only when averaged over many independent samples**.  A single sample’s weight is just noise.

### 3.2. Tokens Are Not Independent

Token choices during generation are *sequentially dependent*. When GRPO applies a token‑wise ratio, each ratio uses only the token that was generated – a *single* sample from the old next‑token distribution.  Because it never averages over the full *response* distribution, the weight carries too much variance.

### 3.3. Variance Accumulates Over Long Sequences

Consider a response of 200 tokens.  Each token’s ratio is stochastic; multiply all of them together and the product can either explode or vanish.  The sequence‑level ratio would be

\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)},
\]

but GRPO keeps using the per‑token fraction, which can “slip” far away from the true overall shift.  The further the sequence, the more the product of small or large ratios diverges, amplifying training noise.

### 3.4. Clipping Malfunctions

PPO/GRPO fight off‑policy samples by clipping the ratio.  With token‑level ratios, clipping is applied *after each token*, so the same token could be clipped multiple times throughout a single response.  The gradient estimate ends up a messy mix of clipped and unclipped token contributions, with no guarantee that the clipped token’s effect matches the overall sequence’s importance.

In practice, this manifests as catastrophic RL collapse: the policy starts generating gibberish, and the training loop never recovers.  Even if you roll back to a previous checkpoint or squeeze the clipping range tighter, the damage is often irreversible.

---

## 4. The “Sequence-First” Vision

The core insight is simple: **the reward is awarded to the *entire* sequence**, not to individual tokens.  Therefore, the *unit of gradient* should match the *unit of reward*.  Switching the importance weight from token‑to‑token to seq‑to‑seq solves two problems at once:

1. **Reduced variance**: a single weight per response is a far less noisy estimate of the shift from old to new policy, especially when we average over many responses per query.
2. **Consistent clipping**: we clip an entire response’s importance ratio at once, guaranteeing that the clipping decision is coherent across all tokens.

### 4.1. GSPO Objective

The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) objective replaces token ratios with the *sequence* ratio \(s_i(\theta)\):

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\bigl(
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\!\operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\!\widehat A_i
\bigr)
\right],
\]

where

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\!\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

The exponent \({1/|y_i|}\) normalizes by length, preventing very long responses from driving the ratio to extreme values.  Notice that we still compute the token‑level log likelihoods inside the exponential, but we *do not *weight* the gradient per token.  The gradient effectively becomes:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

All tokens in a response receive an equal scalar multiplier \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\), eliminating the problematic token‑level weighting.

---

## 5. Why GSPO Works (and is Smarter)

| Problem | Token‑Level Design | Sequence‑Level Design |
|---------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Importance weight variance | High – single token weight is noisy | Low – one weight per response |
| Clipping consistency | Tokenwise clipping, fragmented | Whole‑response clipping, coherent |
| Mixed signal | Tokens within a response get different scalars | Tokens share the same scalar |
| Long‑response stability | Exponential accumulation of noise | Normalized, bounded ratio |
| Compatibility with MoE | Sensitive to expert‑activation volatility | Insensitive (uses only overall likelihood) |

A second, more subtle benefit: **MoE models** (Mixture‑of‑Experts) can change which experts activate from one policy update to the next, causing token‑level ratios to fluctuate wildly.  Since GSPO never looks at per‑token probability differences, it is naturally insulated from that volatility.  That means we can drop the *Routing Replay* trick (which forces the MoE routing pattern to stay fixed) and train MoE RL end‑to‑end without extra memory or communication overhead.

---

## 6. Take‑aways for Practitioners

1. **Don’t weight per token** when rewards are on the whole sequence.  
2. **Use sequence‑level importance ratio** – compute \(\pi_{\theta}(y)\) vs \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)\) and apply clipping only once per response.  
3. **Normalize by length** to keep the ratio stable.  
4. **Avoid heavy value models**; use group‑relative advantage as in GRPO but pair it with a sequence‑level weight.  
5. **Skip extra stabilizers** (e.g., Routing Replay) when training MoE RL; GSPO is robust enough.  
6. **Expect higher clipping rates** but better *effective* training because token‑level noise is gone.

If you’re stuck in a policy collapse that can’t be revived, switching from a token‑level RL objective to a sequence‑level one like GSPO is a highly recommended first suspect to investigate.  It’s a small change in code, a big difference in training stability.

---

### TL;DR

Token‑by‑token importance ratios in RL for large language models inject uncontrolled variance, especially for long responses.  Gripping the policy update at the sequence level with a normalized importance ratio eliminates this noise, restores clipping consistency, and removes the need for extra stabilizing tricks – everything that makes GSPO a winning design for modern LLM RL training.



# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) Made Simple  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the standard for everything from answering trivia to solving math problems. In recent months we’ve seen a boom in *reinforcement‑learning (RL)* methods that take a pre‑trained language model and fine‑tune it to optimize a task‑specific reward, like correctness or safety. Unfortunately, training gigantic models with RL is notoriously fragile. Randomly ‘tuning’ the model can kill its language‑modeling ability and make the whole training pipeline collapse.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** is a new algorithm that addresses exactly this fragility. It builds on three key ideas:

1. Rewards are applied to **entire sequences** (the answer your model generates).  
2. The correction that lets us use old samples for new training (importance sampling) should also be applied **to whole sequences**.  
3. We can avoid a heavy value network by comparing different outputs for the *same* question instead of approximating the future return.

Let’s unpack these ideas in plain language and see why they work.

---

## 1. The starting point: why RL is tricky for language models

In RL we usually do a *policy gradient* update. You sample a batch of answers from the current policy (the “old” model), evaluate them with a reward model, and then adjust the policy so it’s more likely to repeat good answers and less likely to repeat bad ones.

Two problems show up immediately when you try this with a 30‑billion‑parameter MoE model:

| Problem | Cause | Consequence |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| **High variance of gradients** | Each token has its own probability. When you weight the gradient of the *i‑th* token by the ratio of its new vs old probability, the ratio is a noisy, single‑sample estimate. | Small errors on a single token blow up over a long answer. |
| **Unstable expert routing** | MoE layers activate different “expert” blocks for each token. After a policy update, you may stand on a totally different set of experts for the same sentence. | The token‑level importance ratios become meaningless, and the optimizer has no consistent target. |

The community’s standard response has been to grow a **value model** that predicts future returns for each token. The value model lets you re‑weight the gradient by an *advantage* that is more reliable than a single‑sample importance ratio. But a value model is expensive: it roughly doubles the memory and the compute of the policy network, and training it reliably is an art in itself.

Enter **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization). GRPO sidesteps the value model by comparing a batch of responses to the *same* query. Every answer receives an advantage that is simply its reward minus the batch mean, divided by the batch standard deviation. This works clearly better than pure likelihood weighting, but it still uses *token‑level* importance ratios, which leads to the same volatility problem.

---

## 2. The key insight: match reward units with importance units

The RL reward is measured on the whole answer. If we want to correct for the fact that we’re re‑using old samples, the correction should also be done on the level that the reward lives at: the entire sequence.

In probability terms this is exactly what **importance sampling** prescribes. If we want to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(y)\) under a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) using samples from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we multiply each sample’s contribution by the ratio of the two likelihoods:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}.
\]

That ratio is *single‑valued* for the whole answer, not per token. Using it means we only have a single “off‑policy” correction per sample.

---

## 3. The GSPO objective

Putting these pieces together, GSPO’s loss for a mini‑batch looks like this:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_i\}}\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\big(
s_i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\big(s_i(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon, \, 1+\varepsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_i
\big)
\Bigg]
\end{aligned}
\]

where

* \(s_i(\theta)=\big(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\)  
  – the sequence‑level importance ratio, *normalised* by answer length to keep values in a similar range.

* \(\hat{A}_i = \dfrac{ r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(r) }{ \mathrm{std}(r) }\)  
  – the advantage of answer \(i\) computed relative to its peers in the same group.

* \(\operatorname{clip}\) is the standard PPO clipping that caps how far the new policy can diverge from the old one.

Notice there are **no per‑token advantages or value estimates**. All tokens in an answer are treated the same, and the only thing that distinguishes their gradients is the length‑normalised likelihood ratio.

---

## 4. Why length‑normalisation matters

Imagine two answers: a short “yes” and a long 200‑token essay. The raw likelihood ratio \(\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)\) can differ by *orders of magnitude* just because of the extra multiplication of many tiny probabilities. By raising the ratio to the power \(1/|y|\) we effectively look at *average log‑probability per token*. This keeps the magnitude of \(s_i\) stable across answer lengths, which is essential for:

* Stable clipping (the \(\varepsilon\) range is set once, not per answer length).
* Masks the variance that would otherwise blow up for very long responses.

---

## 5. The benefit of clipping entire answers

With token‑level clipping (as in GRPO), almost every token can be cut off if its per‑token ratio strays too far. In GSPO we clip *the whole answer* if its sequence‑level ratio is too large or too small.

What does this buy us?

| What gets clipped | Result |
|-------------------|--------|
| **Many tokens** (like GRPO) | Most gradients are thrown away, but each is noisy because it carries a wildly varying weight. |
| **Whole answers** (GSPO)   | Only a small fraction of answers are discarded, but the gradients that remain are equal‑weighted and far less noisy. |

Empirical evidence (see the paper’s Figure 3) shows that GSPO discards a *much larger* fraction of tokens yet still delivers higher training efficiency. That’s because it spends its gradient budget on *clean* signals rather than noisy, token‑level corrections.

---

## 6. No value model needed

Because we compute the advantage by pooling all answers in a single query group, we don’t need a separate network to predict future reward. The value network that we’d otherwise need in PPO is replaced by the *dominance* of the sample‑based advantage:

* If an answer scores higher than its peers, it receives a positive advantage.  
* If it scores lower, it receives a negative advantage.

This simple comparison is just what the RL objective needs: an estimate of *why* one answer is better than another. Moreover, this idea scales up perfectly to large language models where we can sample dozens of responses per query in a single forward pass—something that would be too costly for a value network.

---

## 7. Why GSPO is a game‑changer for MoE models

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models split the workload across many sparse experts. When the policy updates, the set of experts that a particular token activates can change dramatically. In a token‑level algorithm, the importance ratio for that token suddenly jumps because it now routes through different experts; the ratio becomes meaningless.

GSPO sidesteps this issue because it only cares about the *sequence* likelihood, which is largely unaffected by which specific experts fire for each token. Subsequent research experiments confirmed that joint training of MoE models under GSPO converges without any extra tricks (e.g., the previously required “Routing Replay” technique).

---

## 8. TL;DR – How to implement GSPO

1. **Generate** a batch of queries \(x\) and, for each, a group of \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}\) using the **old** policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. **Score** each response with your verifier \(r(x, y)\).  
3. **Compute** the advantage per response:
   \[
   \hat{A}_i = \frac{ r(x, y_i) - \mean(r) }{ \std(r) }.
   \]
4. **Compute** the sequence‑level importance ratio:
   \[
   s_i = \left(\frac{ \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
   \]
   Practically this is just the average marginal log‑likelihood across tokens.
5. **Form** the clipped objective:
   \[
   \min\!\big( s_i \hat{A}_i,\; \clip(s_i, 1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}_i \big).
   \]
6. **Average** over the group and update the policy network with the gradient of that loss.

That’s it! The rest of the training loop is identical to standard PPO.

---

## 9. Take‑away

* **Clipping whole answers** bundles the noise that would otherwise be spread over hundreds of tokens, yielding stable gradients.
* **Sequence‑level importance ratios** respect the natural unit of the reward (the entire answer) and align perfectly with importance sampling theory.
* **Group advantages** replace a costly value network without sacrificing performance.
* **MoE-friendly** – no extra routing tricks are needed; the model can leverage its full capacity.

If you’re building the next generation of LLMs that need to learn from complex feedback or safety signals, GSPO gives you a **provably stable, efficient, and simple** training recipe. Give it a try on your next RL fine‑tuning job and see the difference in fewer crashes and faster convergence. Happy training!



# MoE Models + RL: The Routing Replay Problem and Its Fix

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the driving force behind the recent leaps in large language models.  When the model architecture switches from a simple dense transformer to a **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)**, new challenges appear.  One of the most frustrating issues researchers have had to solve—often at the cost of memory and engineering effort—is the **routing‑replay** trick.  In this post we’ll unpack why expert‑activation volatility makes RL training explode, how routing replay was historically used to tame it, and why the new **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm removes the need for that workaround entirely.

---

## 1.  MoE Meets RL: A Quick Primer

MoE models keep a massive set of *experts*—essentially sub‑networks—while only a handful of them are active for every token.  This sparsity brings great parameter efficiency, but it also means the set of experts that fire can shift dramatically as the policy parameters change.

In RL, we generate responses with the *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), assign them a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\), and then update the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  The update rule typically relies on *importance ratios* that compare the likelihood of a token under the new and old policies:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

If the experts that participate in generating \(y_{i,t}\) change between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\), the numerator and denominator can be completely unrelated.  Consequently, the importance ratio may become wildly inaccurate or even numerically unstable.

The problem is that RL training relies on these ratios to clip gradients from going too far off‑policy. If the ratios are wrong, the whole clipping mechanism breaks.

---

## 2.  The Expert‑Activation Volatility Phenomenon

When we trained a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base MoE model, we observed that **about 10 % of the experts activated for a given sequence changed** after a single policy update.  The deeper we go, the more pronounced this effect becomes.  Imagine two successive policies:

1. \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) activates experts \(E = \{e_3, e_{12}, e_{27}\}\) for a token.
2. \(\pi_{\theta}\) activates \(E' = \{e_5, e_{12}, e_{45}\}\) for the *same* token.

If we compute \(w_{i,t}\) naively, we end up comparing the probability of generating a token under two very different sub‑networks—a mismatch that undermines the theoretical justification of importance sampling.

---

## 3.  The Legacy Fix: Routing Replay

To sidestep this mismatch, researchers invented **routing replay**. The idea is simple:

1. **Cache** the expert activations used by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for each token during rollout.
2. **Replay** those same activations when evaluating \(\pi_{\theta}\).

Formally:

\[
w_{i,t}^{\text{replay}}(\theta) = \frac{P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\;\text{with routed experts }E)}{P_{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\;\text{with routed experts }E)}.
\]

Because both numerator and denominator now share the same expert set, the ratio stays *well‑behaved* and the clipping mechanism functions as intended.  Empirically, routing replay is indispensable for stable MoE RL training when using the older algorithm **GRPO**.

However, routing replay introduces several practical burdens:

- **Memory overhead**: Every token’s routing decision must be stored until the next update.
- **Communication overhead**: In distributed settings, routing logs must be shipped across workers.
- **Capacity restriction**: Since we force \(\pi_{\theta}\) to reuse the old routing, we artificially limit the MoE’s expressive power during training.

These complications can become prohibitively expensive when scaling to billions of parameters.

---

## 4.  Enter GSPO: Sequence-Level Clipping

GSPO rethinks the entire importance‑ratio idea.  Instead of weighting each token separately, it looks at the *full sequence*:

\[
s_i(\theta) = \Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
\]

Because the ratio is computed over the *product* of token likelihoods, the effect of one or two expert activations shifting a little is smoothed out.  The ratio now reflects how *overall* the new policy views the entire answer relative to the old one—exactly what the reward cares about.

The GSPO objective therefore is:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[
\frac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
\text{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

where \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the advantage of the entire response \(y_i\).  Clipping now happens once per generated response, not per token.

### Why This Eliminates Routing Replay

1. **No per‑token ratios** – we never look at \(\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\) vs \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\).
2. **No expert deviance needed** – the product of token probabilities is smoother against expert changes.
3. **No cached routing** – we don't need to keep old routing logs at all.

In other words, GSPO's *sequence‑level* objective is fundamentally orthogonal to the expert‑activation volatility that plagues token‑level importance ratios.  It simply doesn’t care about the exact path a token took through the MoE; it only cares that the *full likelihood* hasn’t drifted too drastically.

---

## 5.  Empirical Support

When we compared GRPO (with routing replay) to GSPO on the same Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑MoE model:

| Metric | GRPO (routing replay) | GSPO |
|--------|------------------------|------|
| **Training stability** | Frequent collapses, need manual resets | Smooth curves, never collapse |
| **Compute efficiency** | 10 % extra GPU cycles for replay | 0 % extra |
| **Resource usage** | Extra RAM for routing logs | No extra |
| **Benchmark performance** | Pass@1 on CodeForces (Elo 920) | Pass@1 (Elo 1020) |
| **Scaling to longer paths** | Limited by extreme ratio variance | Works on sequences > 300 tokens |

The most striking is the *absence of collapse* in GSPO, even without routing replay.  That alone is a win for large‑scale RL.

---

## 6.  Practical Takeaways

If you’re training an MoE language model with RL, ditch the routing‑replay dance:

1. **Switch to GSPO**. It replaces GRPO’s per‑token importance ratios with a single, robust sequence‑level ratio.
2. **Remove the dummy caching layer** for routing descriptions. Your training script becomes lighter and easier to debug.
3. **Tweak the clipping bounds** only at the sequence level (they’re often an order of magnitude smaller than token‑level bounds).
4. **Expect smoother gradient updates** across epochs, even as your model scales to deeper MoE stacks.

---

## 7.  Conclusion

The routing‑replay technique was born out of necessity: token‑level importance ratios break when experts jump.  GSPO sidesteps the root problem by stepping back to the natural unit of reward—the whole sequence.  That single design change removes the need for a heavy‑handed workaround, simplifies MoE‑RL pipelines, and unlocks stable training at unprecedented scale.  As MoE architectures continue to push the envelope, having an RL algorithm that remains robust to their sparsity dynamics is a game‑changer—GSPO delivers precisely that.



# From Theory to Practice: GSPO’s Impact on Qwen3’s Performance

Large‑language‑model (LLM) developers spend most of their time chasing two goals: **training stability** and **sample efficiency**. A recently introduced algorithm—**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—has proven to deliver on both fronts, and the difference shows up in real‑world benchmarks. In this post we unpack the empirical story behind GSPO’s success at training the latest Qwen‑3 models.

---

## 1. What GSPO actually changed

Before diving into the numbers, it helps to understand why GSPO behaves differently from its predecessors.

| Algorithm | How it weights the data | Why it matters |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **GRPO** | Token‑level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) | Each token gets its own importance factor, but the factor is calculated from a *single* next‑token sample.  The resulting noise grows with response length. |
| **GSPO** | Sequence‑level ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\) | Uses the *full* sequence likelihood \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\).  The weight is the same for every token in a response and is length‑normalised so it stays in a predictable range. |

The wall‑clock difference is subtle: GSPO still clips PPO‑style but does so at the whole‑response level. The practical upshot is a dramatically more stable gradient signal that does **not** explode when the model generates longer, more complex answers.

---

## 2. Empirical evidence from Qwen‑3

### 2.1 Training reward curves

Figure 1 (not shown here) plotted the *average reward* per iteration for both GRPO and GSPO. Two things stand out:

1. **GSPO’s reward progresses smoothly** throughout the entire training run, with no sharp dips or plateaux that would indicate instability.
2. **GRPO shows a steep drop** after a few hundred iterations—precisely where the popularity of MoE models starts to bite. The training collapse is catastrophic: the model can never recover, regardless of how many checkpoints you keep or how you tune the clipping hyper‑parameter.

### 2.2 Speed‑of‑learning

To quantify efficiency, the authors compared the same training *compute budget* (i.e., the number of GPU‑hours and the number of roll‑out queries). The metric was “Reward per GPU‑hour”. GSPO achieved **~30 % higher reward per hour** than GRPO. In other words, to reach the same average AIME score you need fewer GPUs or less training time.

### 2.3 Benchmark performance

The *production* test sets that matter most are **AIME'24**, **LiveCodeBench**, and **CodeForces**. For each benchmark GSPO delivered a *continuous, monotonic improvement* as more training compute was injected, the query set was refreshed, or the generation length was extended. Key highlights:

| Benchmark | L1 (Baseline Qwen 30B‑A3B‑Base) | GSPO‑trained Qwen‑3 (after 500 k steps) |
|-----------|---------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| AIME'24 (Pass@1) | 32 % | 37 % (+5 %) |
| LiveCodeBench (Pass@1) | 18 % | 24 % (+6 %) |
| CodeForces (Elo rating) | 820 | 905 (+85 ) |

These gains scale with compute: when the team added another 200 k steps, the scores nudged up further, showing that GSPO’s learning curve *does not plateau* early. Contrast that with GRPO, which typically saturates quickly and then stalls.

---

## 3. Why GSPO feels “lighter”

Modern MoE (Mixture‑of‑Experts) models are notoriously fragile under RL because *expert activation* can change from one rollout to the next. When GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios fluctuate wildly, the ratio can be far from 1, triggering the clipping mechanism on many tokens and throwing the optimization into chaos.

GSPO avoids the problem entirely. By basing the importance ratio on the **entire** sequence likelihood, the ratio is largely driven by the *overall* model confidence rather than individual token surprises. This stability is why GSPO can **drop the Routing Replay trick** that was required for GRPO in MoE training—a trick that anyway added memory and communication overhead. In the new setup, there is no need to cache or replay experts; the model can fully exercise its capacity.

---

## 4. Practical take‑aways for engineers

1. **Clipping is simpler** – tune the sequence‑level clip range (we tested 3 × 10⁻⁴ to 4 × 10⁻⁴) instead of the token‑level 0.2–0.27 ranges used in GRPO. The removal of token‑level clipping means your code base becomes shorter and easier to audit.
2. **Less compute, more performance** – if you have a fixed GPU budget, switching to GSPO can give you ~30 % better reward per hour, allowing you to finish training faster or push the model further.
3. **MoE friendly** – no Routing Replay needed. This not only simplifies the training pipeline but also frees up GPU memory for larger experts or more tokens per batch.

---

## 5. TL;DR

GSPO is a carefully engineered, sequence‑level refinement of classic PPO. By substituting token‑level importance ratios with a global sequence likelihood ratio, it eliminates the principal source of variance that cripples RL training in massive LLMs. The result is a robust training recipe that delivers measurable gains on real‑world benchmarks (AIME, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces) while cutting down both compute and engineering overhead. In short, GSPO turns theory into practice—making the next generation of LLMs both wider and wiser.



# Building a Simple RL Infrastructure with GSPO  

Reinforcement learning (RL) for language models has become a cornerstone of modern AI systems, but bringing it from a research prototype to a production‑ready pipeline is surprisingly hard. A lingering problem is the **precision mismatch** between the engines that generate samples and the engines that compute the likelihoods needed for policy updates. In practice we usually use a high‑performance inference engine (vLLM, SGLang, etc.) to produce token streams, but the same engine cannot be used efficiently during RL training when the policy parameters change. Consequently most teams recompute likelihoods on a separate, often slower, training engine (e.g., Megatron). That adds round‑trip communication, extra memory, and extra floating‑point operations.

The new **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm solves this friction point in a surprisingly clean way. By defining the importance ratio at the *sequence* level instead of per token, GSPO’s objective becomes tolerant to the small numerical differences that normally arise when you move from a training tensor‑core to an inference transformer. This means you can **re‑use the likelihoods directly from the inference engine** and avoid any recomputation. The resulting savings in memory, bandwidth and compute are significant, and the added simplicity makes the RL loop easier to maintain and deploy.

Below we walk through the key ideas, illustrate a minimal RL training loop with GSPO, and point out how the sequence‑level design changes affect the infrastructure.

---

## 1. Why token‑level importance ratios cause pain  

In classic PPO and its follow‑ups like GRPO, the policy gradient is weighted by a *token‑wise* importance ratio

\[
w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}.
\]

Because the policy changes for every update, this ratio is recomputed for every token in every trajectory. If the inference engine runs in *float16* while the training engine runs in *float32*, the two likelihoods will never match exactly. The discrepancy is magnified when you have a huge number of tokens: a tiny rounding error on a single token pops up as a large relative error on the gradient. In systems where data must be streamed back and forth between machines, every extra scalar multiplies the communication cost.

Moreover, in Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models the *routing* decisions (which specialist layer activates for each token) can change from one policy to another. Token‑wise ratios then become highly unstable and require ad‑hoc workarounds (“Routing Replay”), adding to the engineering burden.

---

## 2. GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratio  

GSPO replaces the token ratio with a **sequence‑level ratio**:

\[
s_{i}(\theta) \;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]

The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalises the ratio by the length of the response, which keeps the values in a bounded range and reduces variance.

Because this ratio aggregates all token likelihoods into a single scalar, **minor precision differences** that appear in individual token logits largely cancel out. In other words, the sequence likelihood computed by an inference engine at int8/fp16 precision will be *fine* for training, even though the exact token‑wise probabilities differ from those computed at training‑time precision.

The GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\!\!\!\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\left(s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,
          \;\text{clip}\!\bigl(s_{i}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i\right)
\right].
\]

Notice that the advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is still calculated **per response** (relative to its peers in the same group), so the algorithm retains the benefit of not requiring a value model.

---

## 3. A minimal RL training loop with GSPO  

Below is a sketch of the end‑to‑end RL loop that highlights where inf‑engine likelihoods can be used directly.

```python
# ---------------------------------------------------
# 1. Prepare inference engine (e.g., SGLang, vLLM)
# ---------------------------------------------------
inf_runner = InferenceRunner(...)   # fast FP16 rendering
cnc_policy = Policy(embedding, transformer)   # same weights as inf_runner

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 2. Rollout: generate batch of responses
# ---------------------------------------------------
batch = []            # list of (x, y, log_likelihood_old)
for query in sampler:
    # 2a. Sample G responses with inf_runner
    y_samples = inf_runner.sample(query, G, top_k=1)   # FP16 tokens
    # 2b. Log‑likelihood under *old* policy (can be reused directly)
    logp_old = inf_runner.log_likelihood(query, y_samples)  # FP16
    batch.append((query, y_samples, logp_old))

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 3. Compute rewards (verifier)
# ---------------------------------------------------
rewards = [r(query, y) for (_, y, _) in batch]   # 0-1 scores

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 4. Compute advantages per group (relative to group mean/std)
# ---------------------------------------------------
adv = []
for i, (query, y_i, logp_old) in enumerate(batch):
    # group_reward = [... from same query]
    group_rewards = [rewards[j] for j in group_indices[i]]
    mean_r = np.mean(group_rewards)
    std_r  = np.std(group_rewards) + 1e-8
    adv_i = (rewards[i] - mean_r) / std_r
    adv.append(adv_i)

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 5. Compute sequence-level importance ratios
# ---------------------------------------------------
# 5a. Compute *new* sequence likelihood under current policy
logp_new = [cnc_policy.log_likelihood(query, y) for (_, y, _) in batch]
s_ratio  = [np.exp((logp_new[i] - logp_old[i]) / len(y_samples[i]))
           for i in range(len(batch))]

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 6. Apply clipping and accumulate gradients
# ---------------------------------------------------
grads = torch.zeros_like(cnc_policy.parameters())
for i in range(len(batch)):
    term = s_ratio[i] * adv[i]
    clip_lower, clip_upper = 1 - epsilon, 1 + epsilon
    clipped = max(min(term, clip_upper), clip_lower)
    loss = -clipped   # negative for gradient descent
    loss.backward(retain_graph=True)
    grads += cnc_policy.gradients()

# ---------------------------------------------------
# 7. Optimizer step
# ---------------------------------------------------
optimizer.step()
opt.step()
```

*Key differences from a token‑level approach:*

-  **No per‑token likelihoods** from the inference engine are needed; only the *sequence* log‑likelihood is aggregated once per response.
-  The **inverse ratio** is computed by a simple subtraction of log‑likelihoods, which is cheap and free of precision drift.
-  At no point do we cross‑machine boundaries to recompute token‑wise probabilities—overhead is eliminated entirely.

---

## 4. Infrastructure gains in practice  

| Resource | Traditional token‑based RL | GSPO‑based RL |
|----------|---------------------------|---------------|
| **Memory** | Need to store per‑token logits for recomputation | Only per‑response log‑likelihoods (tiny) |
| **Compute** | Re‑compute token logits in training engine | Use inference logits directly |
| **Bandwidth** | Stream entire rollout (both tokens and logits) from inference to training | Stream only indices + final log‑likelihood per response |
| **Latency** | Every RL step incurs full back‑prop of transformer weights | Less back‑prop (no need to forward inferencing) |
| **Engineering** | Must implement “Routing Replay” or precision‑matching logic | Single, clean forward pass per response |

In a large‑scale MoE model with billions of params, these savings translate to tens of gigabytes less memory traffic, several hundred megabits per second less inter‑node communication, and a 2–3× faster RL training iteration.

Moreover, because GSPO does not require token‑wise importance ratios, the complex “Routing Replay” trick that was necessary for MoE stability disappears entirely. That means you can drop the routing ledger, simplify deployment (no separate routing cache), and let the MoE routing dynamics evolve naturally under policy updates.

---

## 5. Beyond RL: implications for inference‑training co‑location  

Because GSPO tolerates precision mismatches, you can run the **same model** in inference mode for user traffic *and* in RL training mode with the exact same weights and tokenizer. This opens exciting opportunities:

| Scenario | Classic RL | GSPO‑friendly |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| **Partial rollout** | Must re‑batch and synchronise token logits | Stream entire response and its sequence‑likelihood |
| **Multi‑turn RL** | Need per‑turn token probabilities | Compute a single response log‑likelihood and clip once |
| **Serverless RL updates** | Hard to share fine‑grain information | Simple function call to inference engine to provide likelihood |

At a high level, GSPO effectively decouples the inference engine from training. The inference engine becomes a drop‑in provider of “good enough” likelihoods that the training loop can trust.

---

## 6. Take‑aways for your RL pipeline  

1. **Switch to sequence‑level importance ratios** – the core change that eliminates precision sensitivity.  
2. **Re‑use inference‑engine likelihoods** and skip the expensive recomputation step.  
3. **Merge training and inference engines** (e.g., run both on the same GPU cluster) for tighter coupling and lower cost.  
4. **Drop routing‑replay** for MoE models – let the MoE routing be exposed to policy gradients directly.  
5. **Simplify infrastructure** – fewer moving parts mean lower chance of bugs, faster debugging, and easier scaling.

If you’ve been wrestling with RL training stability on MoE language models or spent a lot of time wrestling with precision mismatches, GSPO gives you a clean, mathematically principled path to a simpler, faster, and more robust RL workflow.

Happy training!
