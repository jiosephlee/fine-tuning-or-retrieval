\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the token‑level importance ratio in GRPO lead to high‑variance gradients?
Question:
In GRPO the importance ratio is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\theta(y_{i,t}\big|x,y_{i,<t})}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\big|x,y_{i,<t})}$. The paper claims that this is a misuse of importance sampling because it applies a single‑sample weight to each token. Can someone explain, with a small example, how this single‑token weighting breaks the expectation‑correctness of importance sampling and why that causes the gradient variance to blow up as the output length increases?
Answer:
**Why the token‑level ratio in GRPO is a “misuse” of importance sampling**

---

### 1.  What importance sampling is supposed to do

Suppose we want to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(y)\) under a *target* policy  
\(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\) but we only have samples from the *behavior* policy  
\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)\).  
Importance sampling rewrites

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f(y)]
      = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
        \Bigl[\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\, f(y)\Bigr].
\]

The key is that the *ratio* \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\) is the likelihood ratio of **whole sequences** – it tells us how much more (or less) probable the entire sampled sequence is under the new policy than under the old one. Averaging many such re‑weighted samples restores the target expectation.

---

### 2.  What GRPO actually does

GRPO breaks the above recipe in two ways:

| GRPO | What we need for a valid importance weight |
|------|---------------------------------------------|
| \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\) | Ratio of the **single token** log‑probability, not of the entire sequence |

Every token sees the same advantage \(\widehat A_i\) (the sequence‑level reward normalized over the group), but the weight that multiplies it is *only* the single‑token likelihood ratio.

---

### 3.  Why that breaks the expectation

Consider a very simple situation with a binary vocabulary \(\{a,b\}\), a fixed query \(x\), and a group size of \(G=2\).  
Both tokens in the response are independent, so a sequence is just a pair \((t_1,t_2)\).

Let the old policy always output \(a\) with probability \(0.9\) and \(b\) with \(0.1\).  
The new policy has flipped the probabilities: \(a\) with \(0.1\), \(b\) with \(0.9\).

#### Sampled sequences (under the old policy)

| seq | probability under old | likelihood ratio of the *sequence* |
|-----|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| \(aa\) | \(0.81\) |  \((0.1/0.9)^2 \approx 0.0123\) |
| \(ab\) | \(0.09\) |  \((0.1/0.9)(0.9/0.1)=1\) |
| \(ba\) | \(0.09\) |  same as above |
| \(bb\) | \(0.01\) |  \((0.9/0.1)^2 \approx 81\) |

The true importance‑sampling weight for a *sequence* is therefore very small for \(aa\) and huge for \(bb\).

#### What GRPO actually uses

GRPO uses token‑level ratios:

- For \(t_1\) (first token) the ratio is either \(0.1/0.9\) (if token is \(a\)) or \(0.9/0.1\) (if token is \(b\)).
- The same holds for \(t_2\).

So the weight applied to the *second* token in \(aa\) is \(0.1/0.9\), the same as the first token, even though the *overall* probability of \(aa\) under the new policy is far smaller than that of \(ab\).  

The product of two token‑level ratios **does not equal** the sequence‑level ratio unless you average over *all* tokens in the sequence – but GRPO never does that. It merely multiplies each token’s advantage by **its own single‑step ratio**, treating it as if that ratio were the full correction factor.

---

### 4.  Consequence: high‑variance gradients

Because the token‑level ratio is a *very noisy* proxy for the sequence‑level correction:

1. **Variance inflates multiplicatively.**  
   Each token introduces its own random ratio; for a sequence of length \(L\), the gradient contains a sum of \(L\) terms each multiplied by a different ratio.  
   The expected value of the sum is still the target, but the variance scales roughly with \(L\), since the ratios are largely uncorrelated across tokens in a long sequence.

2. **Clipping magnifies the problem.**  
   GRPO clips each token’s ratio individually. Tokens whose ratios fall outside \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) receive a *reduced* multiplier.  
   In long sequences, many tokens will be clipped; the surviving ones may have wildly different ratios, so the effective coefficient on the advantage fluctuates dramatically from one training step to the next.

3. **Accumulation over time.**  
   Gradients with high variance cause large, erratic parameter updates.  
   With a large rollout batch and multiple mini‑batches (the standard RL training regime), these noisy updates accumulate, destabilising optimisation and ultimately leading to the “model collapse” that the paper reports.

---

### 5.  Intuition for why the sequence‑level weight fixes it

A single sequence likelihood ratio is deterministic given the sampled sequence.  
When you re‑weight the reward by this ratio, every token in that sequence gets the *same* quality correction that exactly represents how the sequence as a whole moved from being unlikely under the old policy to being more likely under the new policy.  
Hence, the variance induced by the stochasticity of the policy is properly accounted for **once per sequence**, not once per token. The expected gradient remains unbiased, and the variance grows only linearly with the number of sequences, not with the total number of tokens.

The paper’s GSPO adopts this idea: it computes

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

which is exactly the *geometric mean* of the token‑level ratios, but now used as a single weight per sequence. The gradient is then a sum over tokens of the *unweighted* log‑probability gradients multiplied by this single, lower‑variance scalar. This eliminates the “single‑sample weight per token” pathology that causes GRPO’s high‑variance updates.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

- **Token‑level importance sampling in GRPO** is not a correct application of the importance‑sampling principle; it treats a single token’s likelihood ratio as if it were the correction for the entire sequence.
- The resulting **high‑variance gradients** grow with sequence length and are worsened by token‑wise clipping, leading to unstable training and, in large‑scale settings, catastrophic model collapse.
- **GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio** restores the proper importance‑sampling interpretation, yielding low‑variance, stable updates even for very long responses.

### How exactly does length‑normalisation in $s_i(\theta)$ reduce variance?
Question:
GSPO defines the sequence‑level importance ratio as $s_{i}(\theta)=\big(\frac{\theta(y_i|x)}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\big)^{1/|y_i|}$. The authors say this normalises by the sequence length to keep $s_i(\theta)$ in a unified range. Could someone derive the variance of $s_i(\theta)$ with and without this exponent for a toy distribution? Why does this particular form avoid the extreme fluctuations that would otherwise happen when a few token log‑probabilities differ?
Answer:
**Why GSPO raises the sequence‑ratio to the power \(1/|y|\)**  

In GSPO the whole‑sequence importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
        =\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

Without the exponent the ratio would be the *un‑normalised* product of per‑token ratios.  
Because
\[
\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
\quad
\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y|x)=\prod_{t}\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
\]
the product equals \(\prod_t r_t\) where \(r_t=\pi_\theta(y_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_t)\).  
For a sequence of \(N\) tokens the variance of this product grows **exponentially** with \(N\) (cf. the properties of log‑normal random variables), because the logarithm is a sum of \(N\) random terms; the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances.  
Consequently, a single token whose probability changes a little (e.g. a rare token) can multiply the whole ratio by a factor of \(10\) or \(0.1\), and short sequences will have ratios in the thousands while long sequences will have ratios close to zero—so the range of \(s_i(\theta)\) is wildly different for different lengths.

The exponent \(1/N\) simply **takes the geometric mean** of the per‑token ratios. In log‑space it becomes an *average*:
\[
\log s_i(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N
   \log r_t.
\]
Averages have a variance that shrinks like \(1/N\) (by the central‑limit theorem) rather than exploding. Thus, the exponent turns the product into a quantity that stays roughly within a narrow band (e.g. \([0.5,2]\) for most training‑time ratios) irrespective of length. This makes the clipping band defined by \(\varepsilon\) meaningful for all responses.

---

### Toy derivation

Assume each token ratio \(r_t\) is a positive random variable. For simplicity let
\(r_t=1+\varepsilon_t\) where \(\varepsilon_t\) are i.i.d. with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and are small (so that higher‑order terms can be neglected).  
Two cases:

| Case | Formula for \(S\) | Expected value | Variance |
|------|------------------|----------------|----------|
| **Unnormalised product** | \(S=\prod_{t=1}^N r_t\) | \(\mathbb{E}[S]\approx 1\) | \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx \left(1+\sigma^2\right)^N-1\) |
| **Length‑normalised** | \(S=\bigl(\prod_{t=1}^N r_t\bigr)^{1/N}\) | \(\mathbb{E}[S]\approx 1\) | \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\) |

*Derivation*  
Using the log‑normal approximation:

\[
\log S =
\begin{cases}
\sum_{t=1}^N \log(1+\varepsilon_t) \;\approx\; \sum \varepsilon_t, & \text{product case}\\[4pt]
\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N \log(1+\varepsilon_t) \;\approx\; \frac1N\sum \varepsilon_t, & \text{geom‑mean case}
\end{cases}
\]

The sum of i.i.d. zero‑mean terms has variance \(N\sigma^2\).  
Exponentiating a normal variable \(Z\) changes variances: if \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,N\sigma^2)\) then \(S=e^Z\) has variance \(\exp(N\sigma^2)-1\).  
When we average first (\(Z=\frac1N\sum\varepsilon_t\)) the variance becomes \(\sigma^2/N\), and exponentiating gives \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx e^{\sigma^2/N}-1\approx\sigma^2/N\).

Thus, the **unnormalised product’s variance grows exponentially with sequence length**, while the **length‑normalised ratio’s variance shrinks inversely with length**. In practice \(\sigma^2\) (the spread of per‑token log‑ratios) is non‑negligible, so the product will wildly overshoot for short sequences and plummet for long ones, exactly what the paper observes.

---

### Intuition behind “extreme fluctuations”

Consider a sequence of length \(N=5\). Suppose four tokens stay almost unchanged (\(r_t\approx1\)) but a single token is mildly mis‑rated by the new policy (\(r_5=2\)).  
* Unnormalised product: \(S=1\times1\times1\times1\times2 = 2\).
* Length‑normalised: \(S=(2)^{1/5}\approx1.15\).

The first case changes the whole ratio two‑fold because a single outlier token multiplies the product. In an RL objective the clip band would treat this as “very off‑policy” and either discard the sample or give it a huge weight—both undesirable.  
The second case dampens that influence to only one‑fifteenth of a factor; the other four tokens’ near‑unity contributions keep the ratio close to 1, so the sample behaves like an on‑policy one.

---

### Connection to broader RL and language‑model practice

Length‑normalisation mirrors what *per‑token* rewards do in standard language‑model training: the loss is a sum of token‑level cross‑entropies, each term typically lies in a narrow range. By normalising the sequence ratio, GSPO keeps the “importance weighting” analogous to per‑token scales, enabling a single clipping hyper‑parameter to apply across all lengths.

Because the variance of \(s_i(\theta)\) is controlled, the gradient estimate of equation (8) in the paper becomes markedly more stable. The exponential growth of variance in the unnormalised product would otherwise push the gradient into the clipping region for almost every longer sequence, wasting useful samples and causing the variance of the policy gradient to diverge—precisely the training collapse observed in GRPO.

In short, the \(1/|y|\) exponent turns a product of possibly wildly varying per‑token ratios into a bounded, length‑independent *geometric mean*, which keeps the importance ratios in a unified, low‑variance range and prevents the extreme fluctuations that would otherwise destabilise RL training of large language and MoE models.

### What is the relationship between GRPO's clipping range and GSPO's clipping range?
Question:
GRPO uses $\text{clip}(w_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$ with $\text{min}(w_{i,t}\tilde A_{i,t},\text{clip}(...))$, and GSPO uses $\text{clip}(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$. The paper notes that the magnitude of these ratios, and hence the appropriate $\text{min/clip}$, differ by orders of magnitude. How can we formally relate the two sets of importance ratios and understand why a tiny GSPO clipping interval (e.g. $3\times10^{-4}$) is appropriate while a GRPO interval of $0.2$ is not?
Answer:
**Short answer**

GRPO’s clipping interval is applied to *token‑level* importance ratios  
\(w_{i,t}= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\),  
which can vary wildly from one token to the next and can easily be far
from 1 on long sequences. To keep the updated policy within a useful
proximal region the hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\) therefore has to be
relatively large (≈ 0.2–0.3) so that many tokens are clipped.

GSPO, on the other hand, clips the *sequence‑level* importance ratio  

\[
s_i = \Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|} 
      = \exp\!\Biggl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Biggr).
\]

Because the ratio is averaged over the whole sequence (the exponent
\(1/|y_i|\) is a *length‑normalisation* step), its numerical value stays
much closer to 1, regardless of how many tokens the response contains.
Consequently a very tight clipping window (on the order of \(10^{-4}\))
is sufficient to exclude “strongly off‑policy” responses while keeping
almost all tokens in the effective training batch.

Below we formalise this relation and explain why the two intervals differ
by orders of magnitude.

---

### 1.  From token ratios to sequence ratio

For a fixed response \(y_i=(y_{i,1},\dots ,y_{i,L_i})\) we have

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{L_i}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),\qquad
\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{L_i}\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Taking the ratio and the logarithm gives

\[
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
          {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}
 =\sum_{t=1}^{L_i}\log w_{i,t},
\]
so the *sequence ratio* is the product of every token ratio
\(w_{i,t}\).  
If one token has an extreme likelihood change, the product will be
neither large nor small; instead, the *average* of the log ratios,
which is what GSPO uses, is what matters:

\[
\frac{1}{L_i}\sum_{t=1}^{L_i}\log w_{i,t}
   = \log s_i.
\]

Thus, \(s_i\) is the *geometric mean* of the token ratios.  
A geometric mean is bounded tightly around 1 even if a few tokens
deviate significantly, whereas each individual \(w_{i,t}\) can be far
away from 1.

### 2.  Magnitude of the intervals

Because of this averaging, the typical variance of \(s_i\) across a
batch of responses is an order of magnitude smaller than the variance
of individual \(w_{i,t}\).  
Empirically in language‑model training:

* Token ratios \(w_{i,t}\) range roughly from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{3}\)
  (depending on the temperature, training stage, etc.).  
  With ε = 0.2 we clip the interval \([0.8,1.2]\), still leaving a
  large portion of token ratios outside the window, which hurts
  exploration.

* Sequence ratios \(s_i\) lie extremely close to 1, often within
  \([0.9995,1.0005]\) after length normalisation.  
  Therefore a tiny hardness \(\varepsilon=3\times10^{-4}\) (clipping in
  \([0.9997,1.0003]\)) suffices to reject genuinely off‑policy
  responses while preserving most of the batch.

The different scales are a direct consequence of the *geometric mean* in
GSPO vs. *raw product* in GRPO.  Formally,
\[
\mathbb{V}[s_i] \approx \frac{1}{L_i}\mathbb{V}[\log w_{i,t}]
\quad\text{vs.}\quad
\mathbb{V}[w_{i,t}]\ \text{is unnormalised},
\]
so the standard deviation of \(s_i\) shrinks as the length increases,
exactly what the experiments in the paper show.

### 3.  Intuitive picture

Imagine each token as a small “step” that moves the model from the old
policy to the new one.  
In GRPO you take the product of these steps: a single large step can
destabilise the whole product.  
In GSPO you average the log‑steps; the small, large, and tiny steps
all contribute proportionally, so no single token can dominate the
overall ratio.

Because of this averaging, when you clip the *sequence* ratio, you are
essentially clipping the *average* change over the whole response.
That average is naturally constrained because a language model that
generates coherent text cannot wildly change its overall likelihood in
one update.  Hence a very small clipping interval is enough.

### 4.  Practical consequence

The tight clipping interval in GSPO offers two benefits:

1. **Stability** – only responses that really differ from the old
   policy (average log‑ratio beyond \(3\times10^{-4}\)) are excluded,
   so most gradients are kept, which mitigates the high‑variance noise
   that plagues GRPO.

2. **No need for auxiliary tricks** – because the token‑level weights
   are never used, the expert–activation volatility problem in
   Mixture‑of‑Experts models disappears, and the extra “Routing
   Replay” cost vanishes.

---

**Bottom line**

The GRPO and GSPO clipping ranges differ because the importance ratios
they clip are measured on *different scales*:

* **Token‑level \(w_{i,t}\)** – highly variable, product‑like,
  requiring a *wide* interval such as \(0.2\).
* **Sequence‑level \(s_i\)** – geometric mean of the token ratios,
  tightly concentrated around 1, allowing a *narrow* interval such as
  \(3\times10^{-4}\).

Formally, \(s_i=\exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum \log w_{i,t}\bigr)\)
directly links the two sets of ratios; the length normalisation in GSPO
shrinks their variance, which explains why a tiny clipping window is
both adequate and preferable.

### Why does GSPO avoid the need for a value model?
Question:
PPO typically requires a value model to estimate the advantage $\tilde A_t$. GSPO replaces this with a group‑based advantage that uses the standardised reward over a batch of responses. In what scenarios does GRPO fail without a value model, and how does the group standardisation in GSPO effectively replace the value estimate? Does GSPO suffer from any bias because it only uses relative rewards within a group?
Answer:
**Why GSPO can dispense with a separate value model**

The core of RL‑policy updates is an *advantage* that is multiplied by the importance ratio.  
In classic PPO the advantage  
\[
\widehat A_t = V_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x,y_{<t})-V_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x,y_{<t+1})
\]
is produced by a value function that estimates the expected return of a *partial* sequence.  
This value network must be large, be updated in a large‑batch, and its quality drags the whole training process.

GRPO simply replaces the value network by a *group advantage*:
\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{ r(x,y_i) - \overline r}{\operatorname{std}(r)} ,
\]
where the mean and standard deviation are computed over \(G\) responses issued for the same query.  
In other words, the advantage is **relative to the cohort of responses generated by the *same* policy**.  
Because the reward is a scalar attached to the full response, GRPO’s advantage captures exactly the *difference* in reward between a particular response and the typical response of the current policy for that query—this is precisely what you would want a value function to predict, but done implicitly.

However, GRPO still uses **token‑level** importance ratios
\(w_{i,t}\) and therefore re‑weights each token’s log‑likelihood by a *single* next‑token likelihood ratio.  
During long generation this weighting is highly variable and introduces large variance in the gradient (the paper’s Section 3 explains why the importance weight is “invalid” at the token level).  When this is combined with the usual clipping, the signal becomes noisy and can cause catastrophic collapse, especially for large models or MoE architectures (Section 4.3).

**How GSPO replaces the value estimate**

GSPO keeps the same *relative* advantage idea but moves the importance ratio to the **sequence level**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}\!,
\]
which is simply the geometric mean of the token‑wise ratios for a particular response.  
Because the reward is a property of the whole response, the geometric mean is the correct importance weight according to the principle of importance sampling (see Eq. 7 of the paper).  
The gradient derivation in Subsection 3.2 shows that GSPO’s gradient is the expectation of
\[
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
i.e. **every token in a chosen response receives the *same* weight**.  No value model is required because the advantage already captures the relative reward signal, and the importance ratio is now a single scalar per response rather than a noisy vector of token ratios.

**When a value model is still needed**

1. **Very short or deterministic responses** – if a query’s answer is usually a single token (e.g. yes/no), the group standardisation has little variance to work with and the relative advantage may be too noisy to guide learning.  
2. **High‑stakes metrics that depend on internal states** – if the reward depends not just on the final output but on intermediate dialogue turns or on model states that cannot be inferred from the final sequence, a value network that estimates the expected return from a partial sequence may still be useful.  
3. **Multi‑turn or structured dialogue** – Section 3.3 introduces GSPO‑token, a token‑level variant that can plug in a value estimate if needed; if the task requires fine‑grained credit assignment, a value network can be recombined with GSPO‑token’s tokenwise advantage \(\widehat A_{i,t}\).

In practice, the experiments in the paper show that for large LLMs with long responses, the group advantage *alone* yields stable, efficient training and even improves MoE training without the expensive “routing replay”. Thus, GSPO’s design intentionally removes the value network as a bottleneck for large‑scale RL.

**Bias introduced by relative rewards within a group**

Using a relative reward does introduce a *bias* relative to the true absolute value function:

- The estimator is centred by the group mean and normalised by the group standard deviation.  
- If the group is not representative of the true distribution of rewards (e.g., the query is easy and all responses score high, or the query is hard and all scores are low), the relative advantage can be close to zero even though the absolute reward is large or small.  
- The scaling by \(\text{std}\) can inflate noisy groups: a group with very little reward variation will produce large \(\widehat A_i\) for a single outlier.

Nevertheless, this bias is mitigated by:

- **Large group sizes**: with \(G \gg 1\) the sample mean and variance converge to population values, and the relative advantage becomes a good proxy for the true advantage.  
- **Conservative clipping**: the clipping ranges in GSPO (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\)) keep the update step controlled, so even if the advantage is biased the policy does not move too far in a single step.  
- **Stability benefits**: the experimental curves (Section 4.1) demonstrate that bias does *not* hurt learning speed or final performance; in fact, GSPO outperforms GRPO and PPO across several benchmarks.

In summary, GSPO removes the need for a separate value network by using the group‑standardised reward as the advantage and by estimating the importance weight at the sequence level.  This eliminates the high variance token‑wise importance ratios that caused instability in GRPO, while still providing a reliable, relative advantage signal.  The only trade‑off is a mild bias from normalising within a small cohort, but empirically this bias is outweighed by the gains in stability, efficiency, and simplicity.

### What exactly is the ‘expert‑activation volatility’ problem in MoE models that GRPO encounters?
Question:
The paper explains that after a gradient update, about 10% of the experts activated on a given token change between $\theta$ and $\theta_{\text{old}}$. Why does this volatility make token‑level importance ratios unreliable? Can we derive an expression showing how a change in expert selection amplifies the variance of $w_{i,t}(\theta)$? Also, how does GSPO’s reliance on sequence‑likelihood make it immune to this issue?
Answer:
**What is “expert‑activation volatility” and why does it hurt GRPO?**  
In a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) transformer the hidden state at each layer is routed to a small subset of expert sub‑networks.  
For a token \(y_{t}\) generated by an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) the routing decision \(\tau_{t}^{\text{old}}\) (a 0‑1 vector indicating which experts are used) is fixed.  
After a gradient step the policy parameters change → the elementary probabilities
\(\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})\) change, and the router revisits the same input
state.  
Because the router is usually a top‑\(k\) (or probability‑threshold) selector,
a small change in the logits can flip which experts get activated.  
In practice the paper reports that **≈ 10 % of the experts that were active for a
token under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) are different from those active under the
updated policy \(\pi_{\theta}\)**.  
We call this **expert‑activation volatility**.

The token‑level importance ratio used by GRPO is  

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})}\, .
\]

This ratio is a *product* of the per‑token likelihood ratio, which happens to
be **computed on a network that changes its routing**.  
The two denominators \(\pi_{\theta}\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) are thus evaluated on
**different sub‑networks**.

---

### Why does volatility make the ratios unreliable?

The ratio can be written as

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=
\exp\!\Bigl(
   \log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t}\bigr)}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t}\bigr)}
     \Bigr)
\]

and the log‑ratio is the difference of two log‑likelihoods that were computed
with **different expert activations**.  
Let the routing change for a subset of experts \(\Delta\).  
The logits for those experts are replaced with new parameters; we can model
the log‑likelihood of token \(t\) as

\[
\log\!\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})
= \log\!\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})
 + \sum_{e\in\Delta}\Delta \ell_{t,e},
\]

where \(\Delta \ell_{t,e}\) is the change in log‑likelihood contributed by the
forgot‑or‑added expert \(e\).  Because \(\Delta\) is random, the expectation
of \(\Delta \ell_{t,e}\) is 0, but its variance is

\[
\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\Delta \ell_{t,e}\bigr]
= \sigma^{2}_{e}\, p(e\in\Delta)(1-p(e\in\Delta)),
\]

with \(\sigma^{2}_{e}\) reflecting the magnitude of the logits in expert \(e\).
Since about 10 % of the experts flip, \(\operatorname{Var}\) is non‑negligible.
Hence

\[
\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[ w_{t}(\theta) \bigr]
\simeq
w_{t}(\theta)^{2}\,
\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[ \sum_{e\in\Delta}\Delta \ell_{t,e}\bigr]
\approx
w_{t}(\theta)^{2}\; \kappa ,
\]

with \(\kappa\) proportional to the number of volatile experts.
Thus the *variance of the token‑level importance ratio scales quadratically
with the variability of routing decisions*.  In practice this leads to very
unstable clipping: some tokens are overly enlarged (because the new expert
produces a higher likelihood) while others are overly reduced (because a
different expert now produces a lower likelihood).  The clipped signal
therefore contains a large amount of noise that cannot be washed out by
averaging over the group of responses.

---

### What does this mean for the gradient?

From equation (12) in the paper,

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}
=
\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[
\frac{1}{G|y|}\sum_{t}
w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})\Bigr].
\]

The weight \(w_{t}(\theta)\) appears multiplicatively and is highly
heterogeneous across tokens because of routing volatility.  
If we decompose the gradient into a sum of independent terms
\(Z_{t}=w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(\cdot)\),

\[
\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}\bigr]
=
\frac{1}{G^{2}|y|^{2}}
\sum_{t}\operatorname{Var}[Z_{t}].
\]

Since \(\operatorname{Var}[Z_{t}]\propto\operatorname{Var}[w_{t}(\theta)]\) is amplified
by the routing volatility, the overall gradient variance grows with both
sequence length \(|y|\) and the number of volatile experts.  This high‑variance
signal is precisely what the paper attributes to the catastrophic collapse
of GRPO when training long MoE models.

---

### How does GSPO sidestep the problem?

GSPO replaces every token‑level ratio with a **sequence‑level ratio**

\[
s_{i}(\theta)=
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\,|\,x)}\right)^{1/|y_{i}|}
=
\exp\!\Bigl(
 \frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
                                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
\Bigr).
\]

The key facts are

1. **Same routing for the whole sequence.**  
   The forward pass of the whole response \(y_{i}\) uses a *single* routing
   configuration determined by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) when the data are
   collected.  
   When re‑evaluating \(\pi_{\theta}\) to compute the likelihood
   \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\,|\,x)\), the router uses the *current* parameters but
   the sequence is still evaluated under the same set of expert activations
   as for \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) (because the factorisation into tokens
   is only a mathematical trick; the actual network processing uses
   the same routing for all tokens of \(y_{i}\)).  
   Therefore, any change in the set of active experts affects *both*
   the numerator and the denominator in the same multiplicative way,
   almost cancelling out the volatility.

2. **Length‑normalisation.**  
   By taking the \(|y_{i}|\)-th root, the ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\) is bounded
   close to 1 even for long sequences, so clipping operates on a stable
   scale.  The variance of \(s_{i}(\theta)\) is thus bounded by
   \(\operatorname{Var}[s_{i}(\theta)]\approx \frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\operatorname{Var}
     \!\bigl[\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\bigr]\),
   which grows only as \(\mathcal{O}(1/|y_{i}|)\).

3. **Uniform weighting of tokens** in the gradient (see Eq. (15)).  
   All tokens in a response are weighted by the *same* \(s_{i}(\theta)\), so
   the high‑variance per‑token importance ratios never propagate
   into the update.

Because the sequence ratio aggregates the effect of routing changes
uniformly, the variance introduced by expert volatility is suppressed
dramatically.  Consequently, the importance ratios employed by GSPO are
stable, the clipping mechanism remains meaningful, and the gradient
variance stays within a manageable range even for very long, sparse‑
activated MoE models.

---

### Bottom line

* **Expert‑activation volatility** causes large, random changes in the
  per‑token likelihoods when the policy is updated.  
  This injects high variance into token‑level importance ratios, which
  mis‑applies importance sampling and amplifies noise in GRPO’s gradient.

* The variance of a token’s importance ratio is proportional to the
  number of volatile experts; a simple derivation shows it scales
  quadratically with the routing change magnitude.

* **GSPO’s sequence‑level ratios** are immune because they compare whole‑
  sequence likelihoods that share the same routing pattern, normalised
  by length.  The resulting importance ratios have bounded variance,
  leading to stable clipping and a much cleaner gradient signal.

Thus, by redefining the unit of importance sampling from tokens to
sequences, GSPO removes the instability that plagues MoE RL training
under GRPO.

### Why are the advantages $A_{i,t}$ in GSPO‑token identical to the sequence‑level advantage $A_i$?
Question:
Equation (9) in the paper shows that GSPO‑token and GSPO are numerically identical when $\tilde A_{i,t}=\tilde A_{i}$ for all tokens. Could someone walk through the algebra that shows $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ reduces to $s_i(\theta)$ using the `sg` operation, and explain why this makes the two objectives the same? What practical difference does this have if we actually set $\tilde A_{i,t}$ to different values per token?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When every token in a response is given the same advantage
\(\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_{i}\),
the *numeric* value of the per‑token importance ratio in GSPO‑token,
\(s_{i,t}(\theta)\), collapses to the response‑level ratio
\(s_i(\theta)\).
Because the clipping operator sees the same number for every token, the
inner \(\min(\cdot)\) expression is identical to the
sequence‑level expression in GSPO.
Hence the two objectives – and therefore their gradients – are
exactly the same.  
If we let the token advantages differ, the objectives diverge:
GSPO‑token now rewards or penalises each token separately while still
using the *same* sequence‑level weight, which gives more flexibility
(in multi‑turn or hierarchical rewards) but introduces per‑token variance.

---

### Detailed walk‑through

1. **GSPO objective**

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
   =\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
   \min\!\Bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
        \;\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\Bigr)
   \Bigg].
   \]

   Here

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                                          {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
   \]

2. **GSPO‑token objective**

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
   =\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G\frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\Bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_{i,t},
        \;\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_{i,t}\Bigr)
   \Bigg].
   \]

   The per‑token importance ratio is defined as

   \[
   s_{i,t}(\theta)
   =\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
     \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]} .
   \tag{1}
   \]

   Here \(\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]\) (the “stop‑gradient” or “detach”
   operation) returns the *value* of its argument but blocks gradients.
   Consequently, the fraction in (1) is numerically equal to 1, because
   we divide the true probability by its own detached copy.

   **Algebraic reduction**

   \[
   s_{i,t}(\theta)
   = \underbrace{\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]}_{\text{numeric }s_i(\theta)}
     \times 1
   = s_i(\theta).
   \]

   Thus, for *any* response length, every token’s weight is exactly the
   same number \(s_i(\theta)\).

3. **Why the objectives match**

   * When \(\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i\) for all \(t\),

     \[
     \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \min\!\bigl(s_{i,t}\widehat A_{i,t},\;\operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t},\dots)\widehat A_{i,t}\bigr)
     = \min\!\bigl(s_i\widehat A_i,\;\operatorname{clip}(s_i,\dots)\widehat A_i\bigr).
     \]

     The factor \(1/|y_i|\) cancels because it multiplies the same
     expression \(|y_i|\) times.

   * Consequently,

     \[
     \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
     = \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta),
     \]

     and the derived gradients (Equations (10) and (11) in the paper)
     are identical.

4. **Practical implication of unequal token advantages**

   * **Flexibility**  
     GSPO‑token allows \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) to vary across tokens. This is
     useful in settings like multi‑turn dialogue or hierarchical
     objectives where only a subset of tokens contributes to a reward.

   * **Variance**  
     Even though the weighting factor \(s_i(\theta)\) is the same for all
     tokens, different advantages inject per‑token noise into the
     gradient. When rewards are sparse or heavily discounted, this
     noise can make learning less efficient compared to a single
     sequence‑level advantage.

   * **No extra cost**  
     The detached computation does not add extra GPU memory or FLOPs
     beyond the standard forward pass, because the only operation
     required is the attachement‑level `detach` which is a zero‑cost
     operator in practice.

---

### Bottom line

- If you set all token advantages equal, GSPO‑token reduces *exactly*
  to GSPO: the objective, the clipping condition, and the gradient are
  the same.
- The advantage is that GSPO‑token restores the ability to adjust rewards
  at a finer granularity, which can be crucial for complex reward
  structures, while keeping the robust, sequence‑level importance ratio
  that stabilises training.  
- In practice, using per‑token advantages can help when rewards are
  localised (e.g., operator‑level feedback) but may also increase
  sample variance; the choice depends on the particular RL setting.

### Is the gradient of GSPO equivalent to that of a simple likelihood ratio policy gradient?
Question:
Equation (8) gives the GSPO gradient as $
abla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}} = \frac{1}{G}\big(\frac{\theta(y_i|x)}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\big)^{1/|y_i|}\tilde A_i \frac{1}{|y_i|}
abla_\theta\big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\big)\frac{\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}$. Ignoring clipping, how does this compare to the classic REINFORCE update $
abla_\theta J = \frac{\theta(y_i|x)}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\tilde A_i 
abla_\theta\theta(y_i|x)$? What role does the $1/|y_i|$ factor play?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The GSPO gradient is a *sequence‑level* REINFORCE update, not a token‑wise one.  
If you drop the clipping and the length‑normalisation, the GSPO gradient reduces to  

\[
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}
   = \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\,\widehat A_i\;
        \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\,
        \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)\Big],
\]

which is exactly the vanilla REINFORCE update for a sequence reward.  
The extra factor \(1/|y_i|\) that appears in the paper is **only the length normalisation** applied to the importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\). It makes the per‑token gradient terms have the same magnitude across responses of different length, avoiding the situation where a long response would dominate the gradient simply because it contains many tokens. Technically it is a way of *average‑over‑tokens* the log‑likelihood gradient, not a change to the REINFORCE algorithm itself.

---

**Detailed explanation**

1. **What GSPO does**

   GSPO replaces the token‑wise importance weights of GRPO by a *single* sequence‑wise importance weight

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|},
   \]

   and applies clipping to the whole sequence.  
   Consequently its objective is

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
     =\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
        \min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\;
           \text{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon ,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i\bigr).
   \]

2. **Gradient derivation**

   Ignoring the clipping, differentiate inside the expectation:

   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
   &=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
        \frac1G\sum_{i} s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,
        \nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)
      \Bigg]               \\
   &=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
        \frac1G\sum_{i}
        \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
        \widehat A_i\,
        \frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\!\mid\!x,y_{i,<t})
      \Bigg].
   \end{aligned}
   \]

   Using \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i|x)=\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) gives

   \[
   \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
   =\mathbb{E}\Bigg[
      \widehat A_i\;
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\;
      \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i|x)
    \Bigg].
   \]

   This is *exactly* the standard REINFORCE (likelihood‑ratio) estimator for a sequence reward.

3. **Why the \(1/|y_i|\) factor appears**

   Two reasons:

   * **Length normalisation** – The raw likelihood ratio \(\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)\) scales exponentially with length. Raising it to the power \(1/|y_i|\) keeps the magnitude roughly constant across responses of different lengths, reducing variance and making the clipping interval meaningful for all lengths.
   * **Token‑level gradient balance** – Inside the expectation we divide the sum of token‑level gradients by \(|y_i|\). This turns the sum into an *average* derivative, again preventing long sentences from unduly dominating the update.

   Mathematically, the factor does **not** change the expectation of the gradient (it cancels with the corresponding exponent in the importance ratio). It is purely a *variance‑reduction* trick tied to the length of the response.

4. **Comparison to GRPO**

   In GRPO each token is weighted by its own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Those weights vary across tokens and can introduce high‑variance, biased updates, especially when token likelihoods change dramatically because of expert activation in MoE models. GSPO’s single weight per sequence eliminates this variability and aligns the importance sampling correction with the unit of the reward (the whole sequence).

5. **Practical implication**

   Because the GSPO gradient is a clean REINFORCE estimator, it inherits all the stability properties of on‑policy policy gradients. The length‑normalisation further ensures that the clipping mechanism remains effective across diverse response lengths, which is crucial when training large language models that generate variable‑length outputs.

---

**Bottom line**

The GSPO gradient is *equivalent* to the classic REINFORCE update for sequence rewards; the \(1/|y_i|\) factor is simply a length‑normalisation applied to the importance ratio to control variance and make clipping meaningful across responses of different lengths.

### How does GSPO handle very long generation lengths without exploding variance?
Question:
The authors claim that GSPO remains stable even when the expected response length increases, whereas GRPO collapses. Can someone provide a theoretical explanation (or a simulation sketch) showing how the variance of the sequence‑level importance ratio scales with $|y_i|$ compared to the token‑level ratio? Does normalising by $|y_i|$ fully eliminate the length‑dependent variance?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
In GRPO each token contributes to the gradient with its own importance ratio  
\(w_{i,t}=(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot))\).  
Those ratios are independent across tokens, so the variance of the *sequence‑level* gradient grows **linearly** with the length \(|y_i|\) (and in practice even faster because the tails of \(w_{i,t}\) are heavy).  
GSPO replaces the token‑wise ratios by the *length‑normalised* sequence ratio  

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Bigr),
\]

i.e. the geometric mean of \(w_{i,t}\).  
Because the logarithm turns a product into a sum, the variance of  
\(\log s_i(\theta)=\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log w_{i,t}\) scales as \(1/|y_i|\): each extra token contributes an *additive* amount that is averaged over the whole sequence. Consequently the variance of \(s_i(\theta)\) stays bounded and, after exponentiation, varies only **sub‑linearly** in \(|y_i|\).  Length normalisation is therefore not a cosmetic trick— it is the mathematical mechanism that prevents the importance‑ratio variance from exploding.

---

### 1.  Variance of the token‑level ratio

Assume that for a fixed query \(x\) the token‑wise ratios \(w_{i,t}\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu_w\) and variance \(\sigma_w^2\).  
The (unclipped) GRPO gradient for response \(y_i\) contains the term  

\[
g_{i}= \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}\,\hat A_i ,
\]
  
where \(\hat A_i\) is the (shared) advantage.  
Because the sum of independent variables has variance that *adds*,  

\[
\operatorname{Var}(g_i)
= \frac{1}{|y_i|^2} |y_i|\,\sigma_w^2\,\hat A_i^2
= \frac{\sigma_w^2}{|y_i|}\,\hat A_i^2 .
\]

So the variance of the gradient **does not grow** with \(|y_i|\); but this is misleading.  In practice the \(w_{i,t}\) are *high‑variance* positive random variables (they are ratios of two multinomial probabilities).  Their distribution is highly skewed and heavy‑tailed; the tail probability that a single \(w_{i,t}\) is huge dominates the sum.  The expected maximum of \(k\) i.i.d. heavy‑tailed variables behaves like \(k^{1/\alpha}\) (for a Pareto tail with exponent \(\alpha\)).  Thus the variance of the *maximum* token ratio in a sequence of length \(|y_i|\) grows polynomially with \(|y_i|\).  Because GRPO’s clipping only bounds individual \(w_{i,t}\) within \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\), the *effective* variance after clipping is still proportional to the number of tokens that fall outside the clipping band, which grows roughly linearly with \(|y_i|\).  In summary, with token‑level importance ratios the gradient noise is a linear (and often super‑linear) function of the length.

---

### 2.  Variance of the geometric‑mean ratio

For GSPO we use the geometric mean

\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}\Bigr).
\]

Let \(Z_t=\log w_{i,t}\).  
Assume \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t]=\mu_Z\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_t)=\sigma_Z^2\).  
Then

\[
\mathbb{E}[\,\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t Z_t\,]=\mu_Z,\qquad 
\operatorname{Var}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t Z_t\Bigr)=\frac{\sigma_Z^2}{|y_i|}. 
\]

Thus the **log‑ratio** has variance that decays as \(1/|y_i|\).  
Exponentiation is a smooth, monotone function; if the variance of the exponent is small (as it is for long sequences) the distribution of \(s_i(\theta)\) is tightly concentrated around \(\exp(\mu_Z)\).  Consequently the *relative* variance of \(s_i(\theta)\) behaves like

\[
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(s_i)}{\mathbb{E}[s_i]^2}\;\approx\;
e^{\sigma_Z^2/|y_i|}-1
\;\approx\;
\frac{\sigma_Z^2}{|y_i|},
\]

again decaying with length.  
Even for moderate \(\sigma_Z^2\) the ratio variance is essentially bounded by a constant that does **not** grow with \(|y_i|\).  This property is precisely what stabilises long generations: the weight used to scale the whole sequence is almost deterministic because the fluctuations of many tokens average out.

---

### 3.  Does length normalisation *fully* eliminate length‑dependent variance?

The normalisation factor \(1/|y_i|\) in the exponent is essential but not *strictly* sufficient when the underlying distribution of \(w_{i,t}\) is extremely heavy‑tailed.  In practice:

| Length | Expected maximum \(w_{i,t}\) (Pareto \(\alpha=1.1\)) | Expected geometric mean (log‑normal approximation) |
|--------|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| 10     | \(\propto 10^{1/\alpha}\approx 6\) | \(\approx 1.3\) |
| 200    | \(\propto 200^{1/\alpha}\approx 35\) | \(\approx 1.2\) |

Thus token‑level clipping cannot prevent occasional large \(w_{i,t}\) from occurring in very long sequences.  GSPO’s clipping operates at the *sequence* level: if the geometric mean falls outside \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\), the whole response is discarded.  Because the probability that **all** tokens of a long sequence simultaneously produce a large compensating deviation is astronomically small, the chance of clipping the gradient because of outliers is *independent* of sequence length.  Therefore, in theory, GSPO eliminates the length‑dependence of variance *asymptotically*.  In finite samples, a tiny residual dependence may remain, but it is negligible compared to the linear growth observed in GRPO.

---

### 4.  Intuitive picture

1. **GRPO** – Think of each token as a noisy hand‑shake with the old policy.  The more tokens you have, the more hand‑shakes you perform, and each noisy shake can dominate the whole.  Even after clipping individual shakes, the *count* of uncliped shakes grows with length, so the total noise grows.

2. **GSPO** – Think of the whole response as a single handshake whose firmness is the *average* of all token‑handshakes.  Averaging many noisy signals reduces noise.  The extra normalisation by length makes the handshake strength independent of how many tokens there are.  Hence the organi​zing trick of computing a geometric mean is mathematically akin to averaging log‑probabilities, which is the natural way to combine independent probabilities.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- GRPO variance grows roughly linearly (or worse) with response length because token‑wise ratios do not average out; heavy tails of a single token dominate.  
- GSPO replaces the product of ratios by its geometric mean.  The logs of the ratios sum, so the average behaves like a central‑limit average: variance shrinks as \(1/|y_i|\).  
- The length normalisation in the exponent is the mathematical device that turns the product into a sum, enforcing this decay.  
- Consequently, GSPO remains stable even when the expected response length is very large, while GRPO collapses because its gradient noise keeps piling up.

This explains the empirical observation that GSPO can train MoE models for far longer generations without collapsing, whereas GRPO cannot.

### Can GSPO be trivially extended to multi‑turn dialogue with token‑level objectives?
Question:
GSPO-token offers a token‑level objective for multi‑turn RL. How would one set up the group‑based advantage for a dialogue consisting of multiple turns? Is there a risk that the per‑turn standardisation reduces the signal at earlier turns? What practical tricks might mitigate this?
Answer:
**Answer – Extending GSPO‑token to multi‑turn dialogue**

GSPO‑token, as presented in the paper, is a token‑level variant of GSPO that still uses the *group‑based* advantage:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}\;=\;\frac{ r(x,y_i)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},\qquad
\mu_x=\operatorname{mean}_{i}\{r(x,y_i)\},\;
\sigma_x=\operatorname{std}_{i}\{r(x,y_i)\},
\]
where the same advantage is assigned to every token of a *single* response \((x,y_i)\).
For a dialogue that consists of \(T\) turns (each turn a separate token‑sequence),
we have \(\{(x^{(1)},y^{(1)}),\dots,(x^{(T)},y^{(T)})\}\).
To create a *group‑based advantage for the whole dialogue* we can treat the
entire conversation as one long response,
or, more intuitively, pool the rewards across turns:

\[
\widehat{A}^{\text{dlg}}_{i,t}
      =\frac{ r\!\bigl(\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(T)}\},
                      \{y^{(1)}_i,\dots,y^{(T)}_i\}\bigr)
              -\mu_{\text{dlg}}}
             {\sigma_{\text{dlg}}},
\]
where \(\mu_{\text{dlg}}\) and \(\sigma_{\text{dlg}}\) are the mean and standard deviation of
the *dialogue‑level* rewards over the \(G\) responses sampled for the same
query‑set of turns.  
The advantage can be split token‑wise whenever a different turn has a
different reward component (e.g., user satisfaction per turn); then
\(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) would simply inherit the advantage of its turn.

---

### 1.  Group‑based advantage over multiple turns

*Case A – One joint reward*:  
If the verifier produces a single scalar for the whole conversation,
we only need a single advantage per response and can keep the token‑level
clipping identical to the single‑turn case:
\[
s_{i,t}^{\text{dlg}}(\theta)
   =\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\bigsqcup_{k\le t}y_{i,<k})}
          {\operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid\bigsqcup_{k\le t}y_{i,<k})]} .
\]
Because \(s_i(\theta)\) depends only on the *whole* dialogue likelihood,
the per‑token ratios stay identical to those of a single‑turn GSPO‑token.

*Case B – Turn‑wise rewards*:  
If each turn receives an independent reward \(r^{(t)}\), we define
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}= \frac{ r^{(t)}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)}_i)-\mu^{(t)} }{\sigma^{(t)}},
\]
and keep the same sequence‑likelihood ratio \(s_i(\theta)\).
Consequently, the gradient in Eq. (18) becomes a sum of token–wise
advantage terms, each weighted by the same \(s_i(\theta)\) factor.
Thus *every turn’s signal is preserved*, while the sequence‑length
normalisation in \(s_i(\theta)\) keeps the stability guarantees of GSPO.

---

### 2.  Does per‑turn standardisation hurt early‐turn signals?

When normalising by \(\mu^{(t)}\) and \(\sigma^{(t)}\) *within* each turn, the early‑turn advantages are not scaled by later‑turn rewards, so the signal strength is the same regardless of position.  
If, however, one performs a *global* normalisation over the whole dialogue, then a high reward on a late turn can inflate the denominator \(\sigma_{\text{dlg}}\), dampening the early‑turn advantages.  
Empirically, the authors recommend normalising **within the group of responses** for each *query set*, but *not* across turns unless the reward structure demands it.  
Hence, the trick is to keep the normalisation scope tight:

1. **Turn‑wise normalisation** for turn‑level rewards.  
2. **Dialogue‑wise normalisation** only when the verifier aggregates the full conversation into a single scalar.  
3. If both are needed, use a two‑stage normalisation: first normalise turn rewards, then normalise the list \(\{r_{\text{turn}}^{(t)}\}\) before passing to the policy.

This preserves the signal at earlier turns while still controlling variance.

---

### 3.  Practical tricks to mitigate any residual risk

| Trick | What it does | Why it helps |
|-------|---------------|--------------|
| **Length‑normalised importance ratio** \(s_i(\theta) = (\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)})^{1/|y_i|}\) | Keeps sequence ratios on the same numeric scale regardless of length. | Avoids late‑turn tokens dominating the ratio, especially for long dialogues. |
| **Early‑turn clipping threshold** | Use a slightly wider clipping window for tokens from earlier turns. | Allows more exploration when early turns are still under‑trained. |
| **Gradient accumulation across turns** | Accumulate per‑turn gradients before a back‑prop step. | Reduces variance introduced by per‑turn reward noise. |
| **Reward sharding** | Split the dialogue into non‑overlapping shards (e.g., first half vs second half) and compute separate advantages. | Makes each shard independent, preventing a bad reward on one half from drowning the other. |
| **Calibration with a value head** | Add a lightweight value network that estimates turn‑level value as a sanity check. | Detects abnormal advantage scalings without full‑size value models. |
| **Monitoring clipped fraction per turn** | Log the fraction of clipped tokens per turn. | If early turns clip too heavily, adjust the clipping range or normalisation. |

---

### 4.  Connecting back to GSPO’s core principle

GSPO’s key innovation is that the *importance ratio* is a **sequence‑level** quantity, ensuring that 
\[
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}[s_i(\theta)]=1,
\]
and that the *advantage* is computed **per response**, not per token.  
By simply treating a multi‑turn dialogue as a *single* response or splitting it into turn‑specific responses but keeping the same \(s_i(\theta)\), we preserve this property.  
The per‑turn normalisation trick is merely a *local* re‑weighting carried out **after** the policy‑update step, which is orthogonal to GSPO’s theoretical guarantees.

---

**Bottom line**

- Build the group‐based advantage either over the entire dialogue (single reward) or over each turn (turn rewards).  
- Keep the sequence‑likelihood ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) unchanged; it naturally handles variable lengths.  
- Avoid global normalisation across turns unless required; otherwise normalise per turn to keep early‑turn signals intact.  
- Use the practical tricks above if you observe early‑turn advantage variance or excessive clipping.  

With these guidelines, GSPO‑token can be extended to multi‑turn dialogue while retaining stability and ensuring that early turns receive a strong training signal.

### What is the impact of using inference‑engine likelihoods for GSPO optimization?
Question:
Section 4.4 mentions that GSPO is tolerant to precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, allowing the reuse of likelihoods from the inference engine. In practice, how significant can rounding or quantisation differences be, and how does GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio mitigate the resulting bias? Would you recommend any specific precision settings or post‑processing steps?
Answer:
**Short answer**

GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
\]
is much less sensitive to the kind of rounding or quantisation errors that plague token‑level ratios.   A single‑token probability might be zero or shift by \(10^{-3}\) when evaluated in a 16‑bit engine, but the product of all token probabilities (the sequence likelihood) is dominated by the cumulative Evidence – a product of many numbers – so small per‑token glitches almost cancel out.  In practice the largest observed discrepancy between a training‑engine computed \(s_i(\theta)\) and one produced by an inference‑engine was on the order of a few‑hundredths of a percent, and GSPO’s clipping window (≈\(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\)) comfortably contains that spread.  

**Why the ratio is tolerant**

1. **Length normalisation**  
   The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) effectively averages log‑likelihoods.  An \(\mathcal{O}(10^{-4})\) shift per token turns into an \(\mathcal{O}(10^{-4}/|y_i|)\) shift in the log space, which for a 100‑token response is only \(10^{-6}\).

2. **High‑level unit matching**  
   Rewards are assigned once per sequence.  The optimisation objective only ever multiplies the whole‑sequence ratio by the sequence‑level advantage.  Any token‑level jitter therefore has a *second‑order* effect on the gradient.

3. **Clipping over the sequence**  
   GSPO clips the entire \(s_i(\theta)\) instead of each token‑ratio.  Thus, a token that, by chance, receives a wildly different likelihood due to a 16‑bit rounding does not create a large outlier term in the gradient; the whole sequence is suppressed uniformly if its overall likelihood is too far from the old policy.

**Practical recommendations**

| Setting | Why it helps |
|---------|--------------|
| **Use inference‑engine likelihoods** | Saves extra batch‑recomputations and keeps the inference & training pipelines decoupled. |
| **Keep token‑level precision at FP16 or BF16** | The exponent normalisation mitigates most of the quantisation error; only the final likelihood product needs to be accurate enough for a 1‑digit difference in log‑scale. |
| **Apply a small “trust‑range” for \(s_i(\theta)\)** | The paper uses \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\); too wide a window re‑introduces the high‑variance problem, too narrow a window will clip almost all responses. |
| **Post‑processing: soft‑max temperature** | Slightly increasing the temperature in the inference engine (e.g. 1.2) flattens the distribution, reducing the variance of individual token probabilities and further stabilising the ratio. |
| **Avoid recomputing per‑token likelihoods** | Since GSPO only needs the total sequence probability, a simple product of one‑token probs (already available from the inference pipeline) suffices. |

In summary, GSPO’s design aligns the optimisation signal with the reward signal and aggregates over the whole sequence, thereby making it robust to the small but inevitable differences between training and inference engines.  The length‑normalised sequence ratio essentially washes out per‑token precision errors, and the modest clipping range leaves enough room for genuine policy updates while guarding against catastrophic divergence.

### Is GSPO theoretically guaranteed to converge under the same assumptions as PPO?
Question:
PPO’s convergence analysis relies on certain assumptions about the clipping region and the boundedness of advantage estimates. Does GSPO share these assumptions, or does the change from token‑ to sequence‑level weighting alter the proofs? If so, how can we interpret the stability results observed in the paper from a theoretical standpoint?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No, the paper does **not** provide a new convergence proof for GSPO, nor does it claim that GSPO inherits the convergence guarantees of vanilla PPO.  The change from a token‑level to a sequence‑level ratio just modifies the objective; the underlying assumptions that are needed for the classical PPO results (bounded advantage, a well behaved KL divergence, and a clipping‑safe policy update) still have to hold.  In practice, the authors observe empirical stability for the very long‑range, high‑variance regimes that broke GRPO, but that stability is not formally established by the same theoretical arguments that exist for PPO.

---

### 1.  What the PPO theory actually says

The convergence analysis of PPO (see e.g. Schulman et al., 2017) proceeds in two steps:

1. **Trust‑region argument** – The clipped surrogate objective  
   \[
   \min\!\Bigl( r_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_t \Bigr)
   \]
   is bounded above by the actual policy‑gradient term and below by a *modified* gradient step that guarantees a non‑negative surrogate improvement provided the KL divergence between the old and new policy stays below a small constant.  
2. **Bounded advantage** – The advantage estimator \(\hat A_t\) must be bounded (or at least \(\mathbb E\!\left[ \hat A_t^2\right]\) finite) so that the variance of the surrogate is controlled.

Because these two properties are satisfied, PPO can be shown to monotonically improve the true reward objective (up to second‑order terms) and to converge to a local optimum of the policy.

---

### 2.  Where GSPO plugs in

GSPO replaces the token‑wise importance ratio \(w_{i,t}\) with a **sequence‑wise** ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}.
\]

The surrogate objective keeps the same clipped‑min form:

\[
\min\!\bigl( s_i(\theta)\,\hat A_i,\;\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_i \bigr).
\]

Apart from the replacement of \(w_{i,t}\) by \(s_i\), the *structure* of the objective is identical:

* The *clipping* is performed on a scalar ratio (either token‑wise or sequence‑wise).  
* The *advantage* \(\hat A_i\) is still a (normalized) reward signal that is assumed to be bounded or at least square‑integrable.

Consequently, if the assumptions that underpin the PPO analysis are satisfied for the **sequence‑wise** ratio—as they are for the token‑wise ratio—then the same proof skeleton applies.  In particular:

|  | PPO (token‑wise) | GSPO (sequence‑wise) |
|---|------------------|----------------------|
| Ratio | \(w_{i,t} \in (0,\infty)\) | \(s_i \in (0,\infty)\) |
| Clipping range | \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) | \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) |
| Advantage | \(\hat A_t\) | \(\hat A_i\) |
| Boundedness | \(|\hat A_t|\le B\) | \(|\hat A_i|\le B\) |
| KL bound | \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{old}}\|\pi_{\theta})\) small | same |

The only new technical point is that the *ratio variance* is different: \(s_i\) aggregates over the whole sequence, while \(w_{i,t}\) aggregates over a single step.  The paper demonstrates empirically that the variance of \(s_i\) is considerably smaller, which is what gives GSPO its stability.  However, from a theoretical standpoint, *no new variance bound* is proven in the manuscript, so the proof that the surrogate is a reliable lower bound on the true objective remains the same as for PPO.

---

### 3.  Why the authors do not claim a new proof

1. **The surrogate is unchanged** – The clipped surrogate obeys the same mathematical properties as before; the ratio is still a positive scalar and the objective is concave in the ratio (up to clipping).  
2. **Variance control is empirical** – Although GSPO empirically exhibits lower variance, the paper does not provide a theorem that upper‑bounds \(\operatorname{Var}[s_i]\) or \(\operatorname{Var}[s_i \hat A_i]\) under realistic MoE or long‑sequence settings.  
3. **Focus on practical issues** – The motivation section emphasises that GRPO’s *mis‑application* of importance sampling (token‑wise) was the source of instability.  GSPO resolves that by aligning the unit of the ratio with the reward unit.  The resulting improvement is a *practical* construction, not a fundamentally new theoretical regime.

Hence, the authors present GSPO as a *practical alternative* that preserves the theoretical machinery of PPO rather than as an algorithm with its own convergence theorem.

---

### 4.  How to view the stability results theoretically

The most common theoretical tool to talk about stability in policy optimisation is the **relative entropy (KL) penalty** argument.  Under bounded advantages, the surrogate maximisation improves the true expected return provided the KL divergence stays below a constant threshold.  Since GSPO uses the same clipping mechanism, it inherits the same guarantee *provided* the sequence‑level KL divergence

\[
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|x)\;\Vert\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\right)
\]

remains small for all queries \(x\).  In practice, the authors tune \(\varepsilon\) so that the average clipping fraction is tiny (see Fig. 5).  This empirically ensures that the KL remains in the regime where the surrogate is trustworthy.

Formally, one could state:

> **If**  
> 1. \(s_i(\theta)\) is bounded in \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) for all sampled sequences, and  
> 2. The advantage \(\hat A_i\) is bounded (or its variance finite), then  
> the GSPO objective satisfies the same local improvement bound as PPO and therefore converges to a local optimum under standard stochastic‑gradient assumptions.

The paper does not spell out this theorem, but the reasoning follows directly from the existing PPO proof once the ratio is replaced with \(s_i\).

---

### 5.  Bottom‑line

* GSPO does **not** introduce a new convergence argument; it **re‑uses** the PPO proof structure.  
* The same assumptions—bounded advantages, controlled KL divergence, and clipping—are needed.  
* The empirical stability reported in the paper stems from lower variance of the sequence‑wise importance ratio, which mitigates the accumulation of noise that plagued GRPO, but this is not formalised in a theorem.

So, under the same assumptions that make PPO converge, GSPO would converge as well—provided those assumptions hold in the setting of interest.  That is the theoretical standpoint implicit in the paper.
