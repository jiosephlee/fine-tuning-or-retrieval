\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the token‑level importance ratio in GRPO lead to high‑variance gradients?
Question:
In GRPO the importance ratio is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\theta(y_{i,t}\big|x,y_{i,<t})}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\big|x,y_{i,<t})}$. The paper claims that this is a misuse of importance sampling because it applies a single‑sample weight to each token. Can someone explain, with a small example, how this single‑token weighting breaks the expectation‑correctness of importance sampling and why that causes the gradient variance to blow up as the output length increases?
Answer:
**Why the token‑level ratio in GRPO is a “misuse” of importance sampling**

---

### 1.  What importance sampling is supposed to do

Suppose we want to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(y)\) under a *target* policy  
\(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\) but we only have samples from the *behavior* policy  
\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)\).  
Importance sampling rewrites

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f(y)]
      = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
        \Bigl[\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\, f(y)\Bigr].
\]

The key is that the *ratio* \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\) is the likelihood ratio of **whole sequences** – it tells us how much more (or less) probable the entire sampled sequence is under the new policy than under the old one. Averaging many such re‑weighted samples restores the target expectation.

---

### 2.  What GRPO actually does

GRPO breaks the above recipe in two ways:

| GRPO | What we need for a valid importance weight |
|------|---------------------------------------------|
| \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\) | Ratio of the **single token** log‑probability, not of the entire sequence |

Every token sees the same advantage \(\widehat A_i\) (the sequence‑level reward normalized over the group), but the weight that multiplies it is *only* the single‑token likelihood ratio.

---

### 3.  Why that breaks the expectation

Consider a very simple situation with a binary vocabulary \(\{a,b\}\), a fixed query \(x\), and a group size of \(G=2\).  
Both tokens in the response are independent, so a sequence is just a pair \((t_1,t_2)\).

Let the old policy always output \(a\) with probability \(0.9\) and \(b\) with \(0.1\).  
The new policy has flipped the probabilities: \(a\) with \(0.1\), \(b\) with \(0.9\).

#### Sampled sequences (under the old policy)

| seq | probability under old | likelihood ratio of the *sequence* |
|-----|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| \(aa\) | \(0.81\) |  \((0.1/0.9)^2 \approx 0.0123\) |
| \(ab\) | \(0.09\) |  \((0.1/0.9)(0.9/0.1)=1\) |
| \(ba\) | \(0.09\) |  same as above |
| \(bb\) | \(0.01\) |  \((0.9/0.1)^2 \approx 81\) |

The true importance‑sampling weight for a *sequence* is therefore very small for \(aa\) and huge for \(bb\).

#### What GRPO actually uses

GRPO uses token‑level ratios:

- For \(t_1\) (first token) the ratio is either \(0.1/0.9\) (if token is \(a\)) or \(0.9/0.1\) (if token is \(b\)).
- The same holds for \(t_2\).

So the weight applied to the *second* token in \(aa\) is \(0.1/0.9\), the same as the first token, even though the *overall* probability of \(aa\) under the new policy is far smaller than that of \(ab\).  

The product of two token‑level ratios **does not equal** the sequence‑level ratio unless you average over *all* tokens in the sequence – but GRPO never does that. It merely multiplies each token’s advantage by **its own single‑step ratio**, treating it as if that ratio were the full correction factor.

---

### 4.  Consequence: high‑variance gradients

Because the token‑level ratio is a *very noisy* proxy for the sequence‑level correction:

1. **Variance inflates multiplicatively.**  
   Each token introduces its own random ratio; for a sequence of length \(L\), the gradient contains a sum of \(L\) terms each multiplied by a different ratio.  
   The expected value of the sum is still the target, but the variance scales roughly with \(L\), since the ratios are largely uncorrelated across tokens in a long sequence.

2. **Clipping magnifies the problem.**  
   GRPO clips each token’s ratio individually. Tokens whose ratios fall outside \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) receive a *reduced* multiplier.  
   In long sequences, many tokens will be clipped; the surviving ones may have wildly different ratios, so the effective coefficient on the advantage fluctuates dramatically from one training step to the next.

3. **Accumulation over time.**  
   Gradients with high variance cause large, erratic parameter updates.  
   With a large rollout batch and multiple mini‑batches (the standard RL training regime), these noisy updates accumulate, destabilising optimisation and ultimately leading to the “model collapse” that the paper reports.

---

### 5.  Intuition for why the sequence‑level weight fixes it

A single sequence likelihood ratio is deterministic given the sampled sequence.  
When you re‑weight the reward by this ratio, every token in that sequence gets the *same* quality correction that exactly represents how the sequence as a whole moved from being unlikely under the old policy to being more likely under the new policy.  
Hence, the variance induced by the stochasticity of the policy is properly accounted for **once per sequence**, not once per token. The expected gradient remains unbiased, and the variance grows only linearly with the number of sequences, not with the total number of tokens.

The paper’s GSPO adopts this idea: it computes

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

which is exactly the *geometric mean* of the token‑level ratios, but now used as a single weight per sequence. The gradient is then a sum over tokens of the *unweighted* log‑probability gradients multiplied by this single, lower‑variance scalar. This eliminates the “single‑sample weight per token” pathology that causes GRPO’s high‑variance updates.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

- **Token‑level importance sampling in GRPO** is not a correct application of the importance‑sampling principle; it treats a single token’s likelihood ratio as if it were the correction for the entire sequence.
- The resulting **high‑variance gradients** grow with sequence length and are worsened by token‑wise clipping, leading to unstable training and, in large‑scale settings, catastrophic model collapse.
- **GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio** restores the proper importance‑sampling interpretation, yielding low‑variance, stable updates even for very long responses.
