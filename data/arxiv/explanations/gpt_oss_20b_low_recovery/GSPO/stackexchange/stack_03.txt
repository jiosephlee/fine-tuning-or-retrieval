\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What is the relationship between GRPO's clipping range and GSPO's clipping range?
Question:
GRPO uses $\text{clip}(w_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$ with $\text{min}(w_{i,t}\tilde A_{i,t},\text{clip}(...))$, and GSPO uses $\text{clip}(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$. The paper notes that the magnitude of these ratios, and hence the appropriate $\text{min/clip}$, differ by orders of magnitude. How can we formally relate the two sets of importance ratios and understand why a tiny GSPO clipping interval (e.g. $3\times10^{-4}$) is appropriate while a GRPO interval of $0.2$ is not?
Answer:
**Short answer**

GRPO’s clipping interval is applied to *token‑level* importance ratios  
\(w_{i,t}= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\),  
which can vary wildly from one token to the next and can easily be far
from 1 on long sequences. To keep the updated policy within a useful
proximal region the hyper‑parameter \(\varepsilon\) therefore has to be
relatively large (≈ 0.2–0.3) so that many tokens are clipped.

GSPO, on the other hand, clips the *sequence‑level* importance ratio  

\[
s_i = \Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|} 
      = \exp\!\Biggl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Biggr).
\]

Because the ratio is averaged over the whole sequence (the exponent
\(1/|y_i|\) is a *length‑normalisation* step), its numerical value stays
much closer to 1, regardless of how many tokens the response contains.
Consequently a very tight clipping window (on the order of \(10^{-4}\))
is sufficient to exclude “strongly off‑policy” responses while keeping
almost all tokens in the effective training batch.

Below we formalise this relation and explain why the two intervals differ
by orders of magnitude.

---

### 1.  From token ratios to sequence ratio

For a fixed response \(y_i=(y_{i,1},\dots ,y_{i,L_i})\) we have

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{L_i}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),\qquad
\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)
  = \prod_{t=1}^{L_i}\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Taking the ratio and the logarithm gives

\[
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
          {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}
 =\sum_{t=1}^{L_i}\log w_{i,t},
\]
so the *sequence ratio* is the product of every token ratio
\(w_{i,t}\).  
If one token has an extreme likelihood change, the product will be
neither large nor small; instead, the *average* of the log ratios,
which is what GSPO uses, is what matters:

\[
\frac{1}{L_i}\sum_{t=1}^{L_i}\log w_{i,t}
   = \log s_i.
\]

Thus, \(s_i\) is the *geometric mean* of the token ratios.  
A geometric mean is bounded tightly around 1 even if a few tokens
deviate significantly, whereas each individual \(w_{i,t}\) can be far
away from 1.

### 2.  Magnitude of the intervals

Because of this averaging, the typical variance of \(s_i\) across a
batch of responses is an order of magnitude smaller than the variance
of individual \(w_{i,t}\).  
Empirically in language‑model training:

* Token ratios \(w_{i,t}\) range roughly from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{3}\)
  (depending on the temperature, training stage, etc.).  
  With ε = 0.2 we clip the interval \([0.8,1.2]\), still leaving a
  large portion of token ratios outside the window, which hurts
  exploration.

* Sequence ratios \(s_i\) lie extremely close to 1, often within
  \([0.9995,1.0005]\) after length normalisation.  
  Therefore a tiny hardness \(\varepsilon=3\times10^{-4}\) (clipping in
  \([0.9997,1.0003]\)) suffices to reject genuinely off‑policy
  responses while preserving most of the batch.

The different scales are a direct consequence of the *geometric mean* in
GSPO vs. *raw product* in GRPO.  Formally,
\[
\mathbb{V}[s_i] \approx \frac{1}{L_i}\mathbb{V}[\log w_{i,t}]
\quad\text{vs.}\quad
\mathbb{V}[w_{i,t}]\ \text{is unnormalised},
\]
so the standard deviation of \(s_i\) shrinks as the length increases,
exactly what the experiments in the paper show.

### 3.  Intuitive picture

Imagine each token as a small “step” that moves the model from the old
policy to the new one.  
In GRPO you take the product of these steps: a single large step can
destabilise the whole product.  
In GSPO you average the log‑steps; the small, large, and tiny steps
all contribute proportionally, so no single token can dominate the
overall ratio.

Because of this averaging, when you clip the *sequence* ratio, you are
essentially clipping the *average* change over the whole response.
That average is naturally constrained because a language model that
generates coherent text cannot wildly change its overall likelihood in
one update.  Hence a very small clipping interval is enough.

### 4.  Practical consequence

The tight clipping interval in GSPO offers two benefits:

1. **Stability** – only responses that really differ from the old
   policy (average log‑ratio beyond \(3\times10^{-4}\)) are excluded,
   so most gradients are kept, which mitigates the high‑variance noise
   that plagues GRPO.

2. **No need for auxiliary tricks** – because the token‑level weights
   are never used, the expert–activation volatility problem in
   Mixture‑of‑Experts models disappears, and the extra “Routing
   Replay” cost vanishes.

---

**Bottom line**

The GRPO and GSPO clipping ranges differ because the importance ratios
they clip are measured on *different scales*:

* **Token‑level \(w_{i,t}\)** – highly variable, product‑like,
  requiring a *wide* interval such as \(0.2\).
* **Sequence‑level \(s_i\)** – geometric mean of the token ratios,
  tightly concentrated around 1, allowing a *narrow* interval such as
  \(3\times10^{-4}\).

Formally, \(s_i=\exp\!\bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum \log w_{i,t}\bigr)\)
directly links the two sets of ratios; the length normalisation in GSPO
shrinks their variance, which explains why a tiny clipping window is
both adequate and preferable.
