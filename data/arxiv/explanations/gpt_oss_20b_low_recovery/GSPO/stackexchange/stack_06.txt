\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why are the advantages $A_{i,t}$ in GSPO‑token identical to the sequence‑level advantage $A_i$?
Question:
Equation (9) in the paper shows that GSPO‑token and GSPO are numerically identical when $\tilde A_{i,t}=\tilde A_{i}$ for all tokens. Could someone walk through the algebra that shows $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ reduces to $s_i(\theta)$ using the `sg` operation, and explain why this makes the two objectives the same? What practical difference does this have if we actually set $\tilde A_{i,t}$ to different values per token?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When every token in a response is given the same advantage
\(\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_{i}\),
the *numeric* value of the per‑token importance ratio in GSPO‑token,
\(s_{i,t}(\theta)\), collapses to the response‑level ratio
\(s_i(\theta)\).
Because the clipping operator sees the same number for every token, the
inner \(\min(\cdot)\) expression is identical to the
sequence‑level expression in GSPO.
Hence the two objectives – and therefore their gradients – are
exactly the same.  
If we let the token advantages differ, the objectives diverge:
GSPO‑token now rewards or penalises each token separately while still
using the *same* sequence‑level weight, which gives more flexibility
(in multi‑turn or hierarchical rewards) but introduces per‑token variance.

---

### Detailed walk‑through

1. **GSPO objective**

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
   =\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G
   \min\!\Bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
        \;\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_i\Bigr)
   \Bigg].
   \]

   Here

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                                          {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
   \]

2. **GSPO‑token objective**

   \[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
   =\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
   \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G\frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\Bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_{i,t},
        \;\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_{i,t}\Bigr)
   \Bigg].
   \]

   The per‑token importance ratio is defined as

   \[
   s_{i,t}(\theta)
   =\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\;
     \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\bigr]} .
   \tag{1}
   \]

   Here \(\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]\) (the “stop‑gradient” or “detach”
   operation) returns the *value* of its argument but blocks gradients.
   Consequently, the fraction in (1) is numerically equal to 1, because
   we divide the true probability by its own detached copy.

   **Algebraic reduction**

   \[
   s_{i,t}(\theta)
   = \underbrace{\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]}_{\text{numeric }s_i(\theta)}
     \times 1
   = s_i(\theta).
   \]

   Thus, for *any* response length, every token’s weight is exactly the
   same number \(s_i(\theta)\).

3. **Why the objectives match**

   * When \(\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i\) for all \(t\),

     \[
     \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \min\!\bigl(s_{i,t}\widehat A_{i,t},\;\operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t},\dots)\widehat A_{i,t}\bigr)
     = \min\!\bigl(s_i\widehat A_i,\;\operatorname{clip}(s_i,\dots)\widehat A_i\bigr).
     \]

     The factor \(1/|y_i|\) cancels because it multiplies the same
     expression \(|y_i|\) times.

   * Consequently,

     \[
     \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
     = \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta),
     \]

     and the derived gradients (Equations (10) and (11) in the paper)
     are identical.

4. **Practical implication of unequal token advantages**

   * **Flexibility**  
     GSPO‑token allows \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) to vary across tokens. This is
     useful in settings like multi‑turn dialogue or hierarchical
     objectives where only a subset of tokens contributes to a reward.

   * **Variance**  
     Even though the weighting factor \(s_i(\theta)\) is the same for all
     tokens, different advantages inject per‑token noise into the
     gradient. When rewards are sparse or heavily discounted, this
     noise can make learning less efficient compared to a single
     sequence‑level advantage.

   * **No extra cost**  
     The detached computation does not add extra GPU memory or FLOPs
     beyond the standard forward pass, because the only operation
     required is the attachement‑level `detach` which is a zero‑cost
     operator in practice.

---

### Bottom line

- If you set all token advantages equal, GSPO‑token reduces *exactly*
  to GSPO: the objective, the clipping condition, and the gradient are
  the same.
- The advantage is that GSPO‑token restores the ability to adjust rewards
  at a finer granularity, which can be crucial for complex reward
  structures, while keeping the robust, sequence‑level importance ratio
  that stabilises training.  
- In practice, using per‑token advantages can help when rewards are
  localised (e.g., operator‑level feedback) but may also increase
  sample variance; the choice depends on the particular RL setting.
