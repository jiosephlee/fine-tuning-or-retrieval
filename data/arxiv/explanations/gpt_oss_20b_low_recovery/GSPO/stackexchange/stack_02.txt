\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How exactly does length‑normalisation in $s_i(\theta)$ reduce variance?
Question:
GSPO defines the sequence‑level importance ratio as $s_{i}(\theta)=\big(\frac{\theta(y_i|x)}{\theta_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\big)^{1/|y_i|}$. The authors say this normalises by the sequence length to keep $s_i(\theta)$ in a unified range. Could someone derive the variance of $s_i(\theta)$ with and without this exponent for a toy distribution? Why does this particular form avoid the extreme fluctuations that would otherwise happen when a few token log‑probabilities differ?
Answer:
**Why GSPO raises the sequence‑ratio to the power \(1/|y|\)**  

In GSPO the whole‑sequence importance ratio is

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
        =\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

Without the exponent the ratio would be the *un‑normalised* product of per‑token ratios.  
Because
\[
\pi_\theta(y|x)=\prod_{t}\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
\quad
\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y|x)=\prod_{t}\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),
\]
the product equals \(\prod_t r_t\) where \(r_t=\pi_\theta(y_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_t)\).  
For a sequence of \(N\) tokens the variance of this product grows **exponentially** with \(N\) (cf. the properties of log‑normal random variables), because the logarithm is a sum of \(N\) random terms; the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances.  
Consequently, a single token whose probability changes a little (e.g. a rare token) can multiply the whole ratio by a factor of \(10\) or \(0.1\), and short sequences will have ratios in the thousands while long sequences will have ratios close to zero—so the range of \(s_i(\theta)\) is wildly different for different lengths.

The exponent \(1/N\) simply **takes the geometric mean** of the per‑token ratios. In log‑space it becomes an *average*:
\[
\log s_i(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N
   \log r_t.
\]
Averages have a variance that shrinks like \(1/N\) (by the central‑limit theorem) rather than exploding. Thus, the exponent turns the product into a quantity that stays roughly within a narrow band (e.g. \([0.5,2]\) for most training‑time ratios) irrespective of length. This makes the clipping band defined by \(\varepsilon\) meaningful for all responses.

### Toy derivation

Assume each token ratio \(r_t\) is a positive random variable. For simplicity let
\(r_t=1+\varepsilon_t\) where \(\varepsilon_t\) are i.i.d. with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and are small (so that higher‑order terms can be neglected).  
Two cases:

| Case | Formula for \(S\) | Expected value | Variance |
|------|------------------|----------------|----------|
| **Unnormalised product** | \(S=\prod_{t=1}^N r_t\) | \(\mathbb{E}[S]\approx 1\) | \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx \left(1+\sigma^2\right)^N-1\) |
| **Length‑normalised** | \(S=\bigl(\prod_{t=1}^N r_t\bigr)^{1/N}\) | \(\mathbb{E}[S]\approx 1\) | \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\) |

*Derivation*  
Using the log‑normal approximation:

\[
\log S =
\begin{cases}
\sum_{t=1}^N \log(1+\varepsilon_t) \;\approx\; \sum \varepsilon_t, & \text{product case}\\[4pt]
\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N \log(1+\varepsilon_t) \;\approx\; \frac1N\sum \varepsilon_t, & \text{geom‑mean case}
\end{cases}
\]

The sum of i.i.d. zero‑mean terms has variance \(N\sigma^2\).  
Exponentiating a normal variable \(Z\) changes variances: if \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,N\sigma^2)\) then \(S=e^Z\) has variance \(\exp(N\sigma^2)-1\).  
When we average first (\(Z=\frac1N\sum\varepsilon_t\)) the variance becomes \(\sigma^2/N\), and exponentiating gives \(\operatorname{Var}[S]\approx e^{\sigma^2/N}-1\approx\sigma^2/N\).

Thus, the **unnormalised product’s variance grows exponentially with sequence length**, while the **length‑normalised ratio’s variance shrinks inversely with length**. In practice \(\sigma^2\) (the spread of per‑token log‑ratios) is non‑negligible, so the product will wildly overshoot for short sequences and plummet for long ones, exactly what the paper observes.

### Intuition behind “extreme fluctuations”

Consider a sequence of length \(N=5\). Suppose four tokens stay almost unchanged (\(r_t\approx1\)) but a single token is mildly mis‑rated by the new policy (\(r_5=2\)).  
* Unnormalised product: \(S=1\times1\times1\times1\times2 = 2\).
* Length‑normalised: \(S=(2)^{1/5}\approx1.15\).

The first case changes the whole ratio two‑fold because a single outlier token multiplies the product. In an RL objective the clip band would treat this as “very off‑policy” and either discard the sample or give it a huge weight—both undesirable.  
The second case dampens that influence to only one‑fifteenth of a factor; the other four tokens’ near‑unity contributions keep the ratio close to 1, so the sample behaves like an on‑policy one.

---

### Connection to broader RL and language‑model practice

Length‑normalisation mirrors what *per‑token* rewards do in standard language‑model training: the loss is a sum of token‑level cross‑entropies, each term typically lies in a narrow range. By normalising the sequence ratio, GSPO keeps the “importance weighting” analogous to per‑token scales, enabling a single clipping hyper‑parameter to apply across all lengths.

Because the variance of \(s_i(\theta)\) is controlled, the gradient estimate of equation (8) in the paper becomes markedly more stable. The exponential growth of variance in the unnormalised product would otherwise push the gradient into the clipping region for almost every longer sequence, wasting useful samples and causing the variance of the policy gradient to diverge—precisely the training collapse observed in GRPO.

In short, the \(1/|y|\) exponent turns a product of possibly wildly varying per‑token ratios into a bounded, length‑independent *geometric mean*, which keeps the importance ratios in a unified, low‑variance range and prevents the extreme fluctuations that would otherwise destabilise RL training of large language and MoE models.
