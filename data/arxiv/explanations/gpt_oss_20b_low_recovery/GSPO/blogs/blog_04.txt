# From Theory to Practice: GSPO’s Impact on Qwen3’s Performance

Large‑language‑model (LLM) developers spend most of their time chasing two goals: **training stability** and **sample efficiency**. A recently introduced algorithm—**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—has proven to deliver on both fronts, and the difference shows up in real‑world benchmarks. In this post we unpack the empirical story behind GSPO’s success at training the latest Qwen‑3 models.

---

## 1. What GSPO actually changed

Before diving into the numbers, it helps to understand why GSPO behaves differently from its predecessors.

| Algorithm | How it weights the data | Why it matters |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **GRPO** | Token‑level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) | Each token gets its own importance factor, but the factor is calculated from a *single* next‑token sample.  The resulting noise grows with response length. |
| **GSPO** | Sequence‑level ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\) | Uses the *full* sequence likelihood \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\).  The weight is the same for every token in a response and is length‑normalised so it stays in a predictable range. |

The wall‑clock difference is subtle: GSPO still clips PPO‑style but does so at the whole‑response level. The practical upshot is a dramatically more stable gradient signal that does **not** explode when the model generates longer, more complex answers.

---

## 2. Empirical evidence from Qwen‑3

### 2.1 Training reward curves

Figure 1 (not shown here) plotted the *average reward* per iteration for both GRPO and GSPO. Two things stand out:

1. **GSPO’s reward progresses smoothly** throughout the entire training run, with no sharp dips or plateaux that would indicate instability.
2. **GRPO shows a steep drop** after a few hundred iterations—precisely where the popularity of MoE models starts to bite. The training collapse is catastrophic: the model can never recover, regardless of how many checkpoints you keep or how you tune the clipping hyper‑parameter.

### 2.2 Speed‑of‑learning

To quantify efficiency, the authors compared the same training *compute budget* (i.e., the number of GPU‑hours and the number of roll‑out queries). The metric was “Reward per GPU‑hour”. GSPO achieved **~30 % higher reward per hour** than GRPO. In other words, to reach the same average AIME score you need fewer GPUs or less training time.

### 2.3 Benchmark performance

The *production* test sets that matter most are **AIME'24**, **LiveCodeBench**, and **CodeForces**. For each benchmark GSPO delivered a *continuous, monotonic improvement* as more training compute was injected, the query set was refreshed, or the generation length was extended. Key highlights:

| Benchmark | L1 (Baseline Qwen 30B‑A3B‑Base) | GSPO‑trained Qwen‑3 (after 500 k steps) |
|-----------|---------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| AIME'24 (Pass@1) | 32 % | 37 % (+5 %) |
| LiveCodeBench (Pass@1) | 18 % | 24 % (+6 %) |
| CodeForces (Elo rating) | 820 | 905 (+85 ) |

These gains scale with compute: when the team added another 200 k steps, the scores nudged up further, showing that GSPO’s learning curve *does not plateau* early. Contrast that with GRPO, which typically saturates quickly and then stalls.

---

## 3. Why GSPO feels “lighter”

Modern MoE (Mixture‑of‑Experts) models are notoriously fragile under RL because *expert activation* can change from one rollout to the next. When GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios fluctuate wildly, the ratio can be far from 1, triggering the clipping mechanism on many tokens and throwing the optimization into chaos.

GSPO avoids the problem entirely. By basing the importance ratio on the **entire** sequence likelihood, the ratio is largely driven by the *overall* model confidence rather than individual token surprises. This stability is why GSPO can **drop the Routing Replay trick** that was required for GRPO in MoE training—a trick that anyway added memory and communication overhead. In the new setup, there is no need to cache or replay experts; the model can fully exercise its capacity.

---

## 4. Practical take‑aways for engineers

1. **Clipping is simpler** – tune the sequence‑level clip range (we tested 3 × 10⁻⁴ to 4 × 10⁻⁴) instead of the token‑level 0.2–0.27 ranges used in GRPO. The removal of token‑level clipping means your code base becomes shorter and easier to audit.
2. **Less compute, more performance** – if you have a fixed GPU budget, switching to GSPO can give you ~30 % better reward per hour, allowing you to finish training faster or push the model further.
3. **MoE friendly** – no Routing Replay needed. This not only simplifies the training pipeline but also frees up GPU memory for larger experts or more tokens per batch.

---

## 5. TL;DR

GSPO is a carefully engineered, sequence‑level refinement of classic PPO. By substituting token‑level importance ratios with a global sequence likelihood ratio, it eliminates the principal source of variance that cripples RL training in massive LLMs. The result is a robust training recipe that delivers measurable gains on real‑world benchmarks (AIME, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces) while cutting down both compute and engineering overhead. In short, GSPO turns theory into practice—making the next generation of LLMs both wider and wiser.
