# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) Made Simple  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the standard for everything from answering trivia to solving math problems. In recent months we’ve seen a boom in *reinforcement‑learning (RL)* methods that take a pre‑trained language model and fine‑tune it to optimize a task‑specific reward, like correctness or safety. Unfortunately, training gigantic models with RL is notoriously fragile. Randomly ‘tuning’ the model can kill its language‑modeling ability and make the whole training pipeline collapse.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** is a new algorithm that addresses exactly this fragility. It builds on three key ideas:

1. Rewards are applied to **entire sequences** (the answer your model generates).  
2. The correction that lets us use old samples for new training (importance sampling) should also be applied **to whole sequences**.  
3. We can avoid a heavy value network by comparing different outputs for the *same* question instead of approximating the future return.

Let’s unpack these ideas in plain language and see why they work.

---

## 1. The starting point: why RL is tricky for language models

In RL we usually do a *policy gradient* update. You sample a batch of answers from the current policy (the “old” model), evaluate them with a reward model, and then adjust the policy so it’s more likely to repeat good answers and less likely to repeat bad ones.

Two problems show up immediately when you try this with a 30‑billion‑parameter MoE model:

| Problem | Cause | Consequence |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| **High variance of gradients** | Each token has its own probability. When you weight the gradient of the *i‑th* token by the ratio of its new vs old probability, the ratio is a noisy, single‑sample estimate. | Small errors on a single token blow up over a long answer. |
| **Unstable expert routing** | MoE layers activate different “expert” blocks for each token. After a policy update, you may stand on a totally different set of experts for the same sentence. | The token‑level importance ratios become meaningless, and the optimizer has no consistent target. |

The community’s standard response has been to grow a **value model** that predicts future returns for each token. The value model lets you re‑weight the gradient by an *advantage* that is more reliable than a single‑sample importance ratio. But a value model is expensive: it roughly doubles the memory and the compute of the policy network, and training it reliably is an art in itself.

Enter **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization). GRPO sidesteps the value model by comparing a batch of responses to the *same* query. Every answer receives an advantage that is simply its reward minus the batch mean, divided by the batch standard deviation. This works clearly better than pure likelihood weighting, but it still uses *token‑level* importance ratios, which leads to the same volatility problem.

---

## 2. The key insight: match reward units with importance units

The RL reward is measured on the whole answer. If we want to correct for the fact that we’re re‑using old samples, the correction should also be done on the level that the reward lives at: the entire sequence.

In probability terms this is exactly what **importance sampling** prescribes. If we want to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(y)\) under a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) using samples from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we multiply each sample’s contribution by the ratio of the two likelihoods:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}.
\]

That ratio is *single‑valued* for the whole answer, not per token. Using it means we only have a single “off‑policy” correction per sample.

---

## 3. The GSPO objective

Putting these pieces together, GSPO’s loss for a mini‑batch looks like this:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_i\}}\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\big(
s_i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\big(s_i(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon, \, 1+\varepsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_i
\big)
\Bigg]
\end{aligned}
\]

where

* \(s_i(\theta)=\big(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\)  
  – the sequence‑level importance ratio, *normalised* by answer length to keep values in a similar range.

* \(\hat{A}_i = \dfrac{ r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(r) }{ \mathrm{std}(r) }\)  
  – the advantage of answer \(i\) computed relative to its peers in the same group.

* \(\operatorname{clip}\) is the standard PPO clipping that caps how far the new policy can diverge from the old one.

Notice there are **no per‑token advantages or value estimates**. All tokens in an answer are treated the same, and the only thing that distinguishes their gradients is the length‑normalised likelihood ratio.

---

## 4. Why length‑normalisation matters

Imagine two answers: a short “yes” and a long 200‑token essay. The raw likelihood ratio \(\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)\) can differ by *orders of magnitude* just because of the extra multiplication of many tiny probabilities. By raising the ratio to the power \(1/|y|\) we effectively look at *average log‑probability per token*. This keeps the magnitude of \(s_i\) stable across answer lengths, which is essential for:

* Stable clipping (the \(\varepsilon\) range is set once, not per answer length).
* Masks the variance that would otherwise blow up for very long responses.

---

## 5. The benefit of clipping entire answers

With token‑level clipping (as in GRPO), almost every token can be cut off if its per‑token ratio strays too far. In GSPO we clip *the whole answer* if its sequence‑level ratio is too large or too small.

What does this buy us?

| What gets clipped | Result |
|-------------------|--------|
| **Many tokens** (like GRPO) | Most gradients are thrown away, but each is noisy because it carries a wildly varying weight. |
| **Whole answers** (GSPO)   | Only a small fraction of answers are discarded, but the gradients that remain are equal‑weighted and far less noisy. |

Empirical evidence (see the paper’s Figure 3) shows that GSPO discards a *much larger* fraction of tokens yet still delivers higher training efficiency. That’s because it spends its gradient budget on *clean* signals rather than noisy, token‑level corrections.

---

## 6. No value model needed

Because we compute the advantage by pooling all answers in a single query group, we don’t need a separate network to predict future reward. The value network that we’d otherwise need in PPO is replaced by the *dominance* of the sample‑based advantage:

* If an answer scores higher than its peers, it receives a positive advantage.  
* If it scores lower, it receives a negative advantage.

This simple comparison is just what the RL objective needs: an estimate of *why* one answer is better than another. Moreover, this idea scales up perfectly to large language models where we can sample dozens of responses per query in a single forward pass—something that would be too costly for a value network.

---

## 7. Why GSPO is a game‑changer for MoE models

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models split the workload across many sparse experts. When the policy updates, the set of experts that a particular token activates can change dramatically. In a token‑level algorithm, the importance ratio for that token suddenly jumps because it now routes through different experts; the ratio becomes meaningless.

GSPO sidesteps this issue because it only cares about the *sequence* likelihood, which is largely unaffected by which specific experts fire for each token. Subsequent research experiments confirmed that joint training of MoE models under GSPO converges without any extra tricks (e.g., the previously required “Routing Replay” technique).

---

## 8. TL;DR – How to implement GSPO

1. **Generate** a batch of queries \(x\) and, for each, a group of \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}\) using the **old** policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. **Score** each response with your verifier \(r(x, y)\).  
3. **Compute** the advantage per response:
   \[
   \hat{A}_i = \frac{ r(x, y_i) - \mean(r) }{ \std(r) }.
   \]
4. **Compute** the sequence‑level importance ratio:
   \[
   s_i = \left(\frac{ \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
   \]
   Practically this is just the average marginal log‑likelihood across tokens.
5. **Form** the clipped objective:
   \[
   \min\!\big( s_i \hat{A}_i,\; \clip(s_i, 1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}_i \big).
   \]
6. **Average** over the group and update the policy network with the gradient of that loss.

That’s it! The rest of the training loop is identical to standard PPO.

---

## 9. Take‑away

* **Clipping whole answers** bundles the noise that would otherwise be spread over hundreds of tokens, yielding stable gradients.
* **Sequence‑level importance ratios** respect the natural unit of the reward (the entire answer) and align perfectly with importance sampling theory.
* **Group advantages** replace a costly value network without sacrificing performance.
* **MoE-friendly** – no extra routing tricks are needed; the model can leverage its full capacity.

If you’re building the next generation of LLMs that need to learn from complex feedback or safety signals, GSPO gives you a **provably stable, efficient, and simple** training recipe. Give it a try on your next RL fine‑tuning job and see the difference in fewer crashes and faster convergence. Happy training!
