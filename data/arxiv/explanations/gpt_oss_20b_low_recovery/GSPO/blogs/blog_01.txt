# Why Token‑Level Weights Are Killing Your LLM RL

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the engine behind the most powerful language models of today, from GPT‑4 to Qwen‑3.  Yet, training these models at scale is notoriously fragile.  A single mis‑configured learning signal can shatter an entire training run, forcing a costly rollback to an earlier checkpoint.  A surprising root cause turns out to be a design decision that many started with: **flipping a token‑by‑token importance ratio**.

In this post we trace the problem, show why it explodes on long responses, and explain how a shift to *sequence‑level* thinking – the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm – restores stability and unlocks new scaling paths.

---

## 1. The Old‑School RL Objective

The standard RL recipe for language models is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).  PPO samples a batch of responses \(y\) from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), then updates the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) in a clipped, conservative way:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(
w_{t}(\theta)\hat A_{t},
\!\operatorname{clip}(w_{t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\!\hat A_{t}
\bigr)
\right].
\]

The *importance ratio* \(w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}\) measures how much the current policy diverges from the old one at each **token**.

PPO would *normally* work because of the tiny learning steps enforced by the clipping range \(\varepsilon\).  But PPO is heavy‑weight: it needs a separate value model to estimate \(\hat A_{t}\), and its per‑token weighting is brittle for our biggest models.

---

## 2. GRPO – “You Don’t Need a Value Model”

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) sidestepped PPO’s value model downstream by computing a *relative advantage* per response, not per token:

\[
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}}
{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}} .
\]

Where \(G\) is the number of responses generated for the same query \(x\).  
GRPO’s objective looks like

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\!\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\!\widehat A_i
\bigr)
\right].
\]

Critically, **the per‑token weight** \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is still the same token‑level ratio as in PPO, but the advantage is shared across the whole response.

One thinks: *“No value model! Great!”*  But the reality is that token‑level weights become a treacherous source of high‑variance noise, especially when the responses are long.

---

## 3. Why Token‑Level Importance Weights Are a Bad Idea

### 3.1. Importance Sampling 101

In classic importance sampling, we correct for drawing samples from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) while estimating an expectation under a *target* distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\):

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

The weight \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}}{\pi_{\text{beh}}}\) is meaningful **only when averaged over many independent samples**.  A single sample’s weight is just noise.

### 3.2. Tokens Are Not Independent

Token choices during generation are *sequentially dependent*. When GRPO applies a token‑wise ratio, each ratio uses only the token that was generated – a *single* sample from the old next‑token distribution.  Because it never averages over the full *response* distribution, the weight carries too much variance.

### 3.3. Variance Accumulates Over Long Sequences

Consider a response of 200 tokens.  Each token’s ratio is stochastic; multiply all of them together and the product can either explode or vanish.  The sequence‑level ratio would be

\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i)},
\]

but GRPO keeps using the per‑token fraction, which can “slip” far away from the true overall shift.  The further the sequence, the more the product of small or large ratios diverges, amplifying training noise.

### 3.4. Clipping Malfunctions

PPO/GRPO fight off‑policy samples by clipping the ratio.  With token‑level ratios, clipping is applied *after each token*, so the same token could be clipped multiple times throughout a single response.  The gradient estimate ends up a messy mix of clipped and unclipped token contributions, with no guarantee that the clipped token’s effect matches the overall sequence’s importance.

In practice, this manifests as catastrophic RL collapse: the policy starts generating gibberish, and the training loop never recovers.  Even if you roll back to a previous checkpoint or squeeze the clipping range tighter, the damage is often irreversible.

---

## 4. The “Sequence-First” Vision

The core insight is simple: **the reward is awarded to the *entire* sequence**, not to individual tokens.  Therefore, the *unit of gradient* should match the *unit of reward*.  Switching the importance weight from token‑to‑token to seq‑to‑seq solves two problems at once:

1. **Reduced variance**: a single weight per response is a far less noisy estimate of the shift from old to new policy, especially when we average over many responses per query.
2. **Consistent clipping**: we clip an entire response’s importance ratio at once, guaranteeing that the clipping decision is coherent across all tokens.

### 4.1. GSPO Objective

The Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) objective replaces token ratios with the *sequence* ratio \(s_i(\theta)\):

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\bigl(
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\!\operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\!\widehat A_i
\bigr)
\right],
\]

where

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\!\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

The exponent \({1/|y_i|}\) normalizes by length, preventing very long responses from driving the ratio to extreme values.  Notice that we still compute the token‑level log likelihoods inside the exponential, but we *do not *weight* the gradient per token.  The gradient effectively becomes:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\,|\,x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\]

All tokens in a response receive an equal scalar multiplier \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\), eliminating the problematic token‑level weighting.

---

## 5. Why GSPO Works (and is Smarter)

| Problem | Token‑Level Design | Sequence‑Level Design |
|---------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Importance weight variance | High – single token weight is noisy | Low – one weight per response |
| Clipping consistency | Tokenwise clipping, fragmented | Whole‑response clipping, coherent |
| Mixed signal | Tokens within a response get different scalars | Tokens share the same scalar |
| Long‑response stability | Exponential accumulation of noise | Normalized, bounded ratio |
| Compatibility with MoE | Sensitive to expert‑activation volatility | Insensitive (uses only overall likelihood) |

A second, more subtle benefit: **MoE models** (Mixture‑of‑Experts) can change which experts activate from one policy update to the next, causing token‑level ratios to fluctuate wildly.  Since GSPO never looks at per‑token probability differences, it is naturally insulated from that volatility.  That means we can drop the *Routing Replay* trick (which forces the MoE routing pattern to stay fixed) and train MoE RL end‑to‑end without extra memory or communication overhead.

---

## 6. Take‑aways for Practitioners

1. **Don’t weight per token** when rewards are on the whole sequence.  
2. **Use sequence‑level importance ratio** – compute \(\pi_{\theta}(y)\) vs \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)\) and apply clipping only once per response.  
3. **Normalize by length** to keep the ratio stable.  
4. **Avoid heavy value models**; use group‑relative advantage as in GRPO but pair it with a sequence‑level weight.  
5. **Skip extra stabilizers** (e.g., Routing Replay) when training MoE RL; GSPO is robust enough.  
6. **Expect higher clipping rates** but better *effective* training because token‑level noise is gone.

If you’re stuck in a policy collapse that can’t be revived, switching from a token‑level RL objective to a sequence‑level one like GSPO is a highly recommended first suspect to investigate.  It’s a small change in code, a big difference in training stability.

---

### TL;DR

Token‑by‑token importance ratios in RL for large language models inject uncontrolled variance, especially for long responses.  Gripping the policy update at the sequence level with a normalized importance ratio eliminates this noise, restores clipping consistency, and removes the need for extra stabilizing tricks – everything that makes GSPO a winning design for modern LLM RL training.
