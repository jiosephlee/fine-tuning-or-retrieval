\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Group Sequence Policy Optimization

## 1. Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has rapidly become a cornerstone for pushing the boundaries of large language models (LLMs). Unlike traditional supervised fine‑tuning, RL allows a model to interact with an environment—an evaluation oracle or verifier that assigns rewards to complete responses—and adapt its generation strategy so as to maximize cumulative reward. This capability has enabled LLMs to solve tasks that require long, multi‑step reasoning, such as advanced mathematics problems and complex programming challenges.  

However, scaling RL to gigantic LLMs is not straightforward. The training dynamics of RL algorithms can become fragile when the model size, sparsity (as in Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures), or response length grow. Instabilities often manifest as catastrophic collapse of the policy, a phenomenon that has been observed in state‑of‑the‑art RL systems that train language models. A stable, scalable RL method is therefore essential to unlock the full potential of next‑generation LLMs.

The paper *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* proposes a robust solution to this challenge: a new algorithm called GSPO. By redefining the importance sampling ratios at the sequence level and aligning clipping, rewarding, and optimization to this unit, GSPO eliminates the high‑variance noise that plagues earlier methods such as GRPO. The following sections unpack the motivations, core ideas, and practical implications of GSPO, highlighting its advantages over existing approaches.

### *What is RL in language modeling?*

In the language modeling setting, the agent is an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$ that generates tokens one by one conditioned on a prompt $x$. The trajectory of tokens $y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})$ constitutes a response. An external verifier $r$ evaluates the entire sequence and returns a scalar reward $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$. The objective is to adjust $\theta$ so that the expected reward of the policy is maximized. Because the policy must generate *complete* sequences before the reward is known, RL for language modeling requires handling long horizons and non‑differentiable reward signals—exactly the scenario where policy gradient methods shine.

### *Historical progress: from supervised learning to RL*

The initial generation of high‑quality LLMs relied on supervised learning (SL), where the model is trained to predict the next token from a vast corpus of text. SL alone cannot steer the model toward optimal behaviors that are difficult to capture from data alone, such as adhering to human preferences or solving technical domains. RL was introduced to bridge this gap, beginning with simple reward functions for text style or factuality. As the difficulty of target tasks increased, researchers adapted RL to longer responses and more complex reward signals. The evolution from SL to RL for LLMs is marked by a gradual shift: first, fine‑tuning on curated datasets; next, reward‑guided optimization via RL; finally, highly specialized RL systems tailored to scale (e.g., RLHF, PERL). Each stage introduced new algorithmic challenges: policy stability, sample efficiency, and computational demands.

### *Key success stories (Qwen, LLMs in math/programming)*

Recent LLMs such as **Qwen3**, **ChatGPT**, and many others have demonstrated formidable abilities in domains that were traditionally intractable for purely supervised models. For instance, Qwen3 achieves high success rates on the AIME competition, surpassing human experts, and on CodeForces it exhibits top‑tier Elo ratings. These accomplishments are largely credited to the extensive RL training pipelines that refine the model’s reasoning pathways and reward‑aligned behaviors. RL has also enabled LLMs to produce code that passes a wide array of programming benchmarks. By evaluating the entire generated code against a comprehensive test suite, the RL process effectively teaches the model to plan, debug, and execute multi‑step commands.

### *Stability vs. scalability: why larger models need new algorithms*

As LLMs grow in scale, several factors conspire to destabilize conventional RL algorithms:

1. **Long response length**: Each sampled sequence can contain thousands of tokens. Gradients derived from token‑wise importance ratios accumulate noise over long horizons, inflating variance.
2. **Sparse attention and high‑parameter MoE layers**: Modern MoE architectures split workloads across numerous experts. The routing decisions change dramatically after each policy update, rendering token‑wise importance ratios unreliable.
3. **High‑throughput hardware demands**: Training large models requires very large batch sizes. Gradient updates are performed on mini‑batches that are often off‑policy, necessitating careful off‑policy correction.

The widely used **GRPO** algorithm attempts to circumvent the need for a value network by computing *relative advantages* among multiple responses to the same prompt. Yet, GRPO’s token‑wise importance ratios introduce high‑variance noise, especially problematic in the above contexts. Consequently, training can collapse irrevocably, and recovering from failure may require extensive hyper‑parameter search or structural changes to the model.

GSPO addresses these issues by **aligning the optimization unit with the reward unit**. Rewards are assigned to *entire sequences*, so it is natural to treat the whole sequence as the fundamental object for importance sampling and clipping. This change reduces variance, stabilizes training even for sparse MoE models, and simplifies infrastructure requirements.

### *Overview of the paper’s contributions (GSPO)*

The paper proposes the following key ideas:

1. **Sequence‑level importance ratios**: GSPO defines an importance ratio
   $$
   s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
   $$
   which indicates how the *entire* response probability density has changed from the old policy to the new one. The exponent $1/|y_i|$ normalizes the ratio across sequences of different lengths, preventing extreme variability.

2. **Sequence‑level clipping**: The algorithm clips the sequence importance ratio using a proximal range $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ rather than clipping each token individually. This ensures that only responses that deviate too far from the old policy influence the gradient.

3. **Group‑based advantage estimation**: Rather than requiring a value function, GSPO evaluates the advantage of each generated response by comparing its reward to the mean and standard deviation of all responses to the same prompt. This keeps the estimation lightweight and aligns naturally with the reward signal.

4. **Empirical validation**: The paper demonstrates that GSPO outperforms GRPO on the Qwen3 family of models, both in training efficiency and final benchmark performance. It also shows that GSPO removes the need for a costly *Routing Replay* trick for MoE models, thereby simplifying training pipelines.

5. **Infrastructure simplifications**: Because GSPO works with sequence likelihoods, it tolerates precision discrepancies between training and inference engines. This can enable direct reuse of inference outputs for RL updates, saving recomputation time.

By addressing the root cause of instability in token‑based importance sampling, GSPO offers a principled, scalable alternative for RL training of large language models. The rest of this chapter dives deeper into the mechanics behind these ideas, the mathematical intuitions, and the practical implications for training MoE models and building robust RL pipelines.

# 2. Preliminaries and Notation

Understanding reinforcement learning for large language models hinges on a few precise mathematical constructions and conventions.  
This chapter unpacks those conventions so that the reader can read, manipulate, and verify all subsequent proofs and empirical analyses in the book.  
Each subsection below presents one core idea, first in intuitive prose and then in formal LaTeX notation, to reinforce comprehension from two complementary angles.

---

## Autoregressive Policy Representation

In language modeling, we view a trained transformer as a *policy* in the reinforcement‑learning sense: given a prompt (or *query*) \(x\), the model emits tokens one after another until a stopping criterion is met.  
Because each token prediction depends on everything seen so far, we speak of an **autoregressive policy** \(\pi_{\theta}\) parameterized by weights \(\theta\).  

Formally, for a query \(x\), the policy specifies a conditional distribution over the vocabulary at time step \(t\):
\[
\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t\mid x,\;y_{<t}\bigr)
\;,
\quad t=1,2,\dots,|y|
\;,
\]
where \(y_{<t} = (y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})\) are the previously generated tokens.

The autonomy of this policy means that, at inference time, sampling is a straightforward sequential process: draw \(y_1\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\), then draw \(y_2\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_1)\), and so on.  
In reinforcement‑learning parlance, the *action* at each time step \(t\) is the chosen token \(y_t\), and the entire trajectory \((x,y)\) is a stochastic episode whose probability we can compute exactly.

---

## Queries, Responses, and Dataset Notation

We work with a collection of **queries** \(\mathcal{D}\).  
A query \(x\) is simply a prompt or instruction that initiates a language generation episode.  
To study policy learning we need a dataset of query–response pairs:
\[
\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N , \qquad x^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}.
\]
The dataset \( \mathcal{D}\) is typically a large collection of user intents, test problems, or any domain‑specific prompts relevant to the downstream task (e.g., programming, mathematics, or dialogue).  

During training we will sample queries uniformly from \(\mathcal{D}\) and then generate several responses for each query using the current policy.  The number of responses per query is denoted by \(G\), referred to as the **group size**.  
When \(G>1\), the responses can be interpreted as *rollouts* of the same environment, providing multiple realizations of the stochastic policy for subsequent importance‑sampling corrections.

---

## Sequence Likelihood Formulation

Given a fixed query \(x\) and a complete response \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\), the likelihood of that response under policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) is the product of the per‑token probabilities:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
\;=\;
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}
\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_t \mid x,\;y_{<t}\bigr).
\tag{2.1}
\]
The term \(|y|\) counts the number of tokens in the response.  
Equation (2.1) reflects the very definition of an autoregressive process: the joint probability is the chain rule decomposition.

The *sequence likelihood* is a scalar number that indicates how strongly the policy regards the entire response as likely.  
It will be the backbone of the importance‑sampling ratios used later in the training objective.

---

## Reward Definition and Verification

Reinforcement learning requires a **reward** that evaluates the quality of a complete response rather than each token in isolation.  
We denote a generic **verifier** or **estimator** by \(r\).  The verifier receives a query–response pair \((x,y)\) and outputs a scalar reward
\[
r(x,y) \;\in\; [0,1].
\]
In many practical settings, \(r(x,y)\) is obtained by an external system: for example, a grader that compares the model output with a reference solution, a human annotator, or a rule‑based metric.  
The crucial property is that rewards are only defined for entire responses, not for partial prefixes or individual tokens.

Given a group \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) generated for the same query, we convert these raw rewards into **normalized advantages**:
\[
\widehat{A}_i
\;=\;
\frac{
  r(x,y_i)
  \;-\;
  \mathrm{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}{
  \mathrm{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}.
\tag{2.2}
\]
This standardization centers rewards around zero and scales them by their empirical standard deviation, providing a stable signal that mitigates drastic reward spikes and encourages balanced updates.

---

## Importance Sampling Fundamentals

Many reinforcement‑learning algorithms rely on **importance sampling** to reuse previously collected data.  
Suppose we wish to estimate the expectation of a function \(f(z)\) under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) but have samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
  \!\left[
    \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,
    f(z)
  \right].
\tag{2.3}
\]
The *importance weight* \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) corrects for the fact that the sample was not drawn from the target distribution.  
In practice, the target is the *new* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) we wish to update, while the behavior is the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) that produced the data.  
Crucially, importance sampling is unbiased only when the expectation is taken over *many* independent samples; a single sample has high variance and can lead to unstable updates.

---

## Brief Refresher: PPO Objective

The **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm is a cornerstone of modern policy‑gradient methods.  
Using data generated by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), PPO imposes a *clipping* constraint that keeps the updated policy close to the old one, thereby limiting the variance of the gradient estimates.

For token‑level updates, PPO’s objective (ignoring the KL‑regularization term for clarity) is
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\left[
  \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
  \min\!\!\left(
    w_t(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_t,
    \;\mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_t 
  \right)
\right].
\tag{2.4}
\]
Here, the **importance ratio** at token \(t\) is
\[
w_t(\theta)
\;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,\,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,\,y_{<t})},
\]
and \(\widehat{A}_t\) is an advantage estimate typically obtained from a separate value network.

The clipping operation ensures that if the ratio \(w_t(\theta)\) deviates too much from 1 (the “on‑policy” case), the contribution is bounded.  
Nevertheless, PPO’s reliance on token‑level importance ratios introduces substantial sensitivity: each token can swing the objective widely, especially for long sequences where the product of small deviations accumulates.

# 3. Existing Algorithms: PPO and GRPO  

Large language models are optimized through reinforcement learning (RL) by treating the model as an autoregressive policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  Two influential algorithms that predate GSPO are **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** and its value‑free variant **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  Understanding the mechanics, strengths, and inherent pitfalls of these methods is crucial for appreciating the motivation behind GSPO.  In this chapter we dissect PPO’s clipping scheme, its reliance on a value network, and the practical difficulties that arise when the policy generates long sequences.  We then turn to GRPO, which eliminates the value model at the cost of token‑level importance weights, and we illustrate with concrete examples how this design choice introduces high‑variance gradients and destabilizes training.  

## PPO review: clipping, advantage estimation, value model dependence  

PPO is grounded in the idea of staying close to the **old policy** $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ while improving the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$.  Using rollouts generated from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, PPO defines a surrogate objective that attempts to maximally increase expected reward but penalizes large deviations from the old policy via a **clipping** operation (Equation (1) in the paper).  For each token $y_t$ in a generated response $y$, PPO introduces an **importance ratio**  

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})},
\]

and an **advantage estimate** $\widehat{A}_t$ that captures how much better or worse the token is relative to a baseline.  The baseline is typically a separate neural network, called the **value model**, trained to predict the expected return of a partial sequence.  

Formally, PPO’s objective is  

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \!\!
 \left[ \frac{1}{|y|}\!\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
 \min\!\bigl(
 w_t(\theta)\widehat{A}_t,
 \mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_t
 \bigr)\right].
\]

The clipping mechanism guarantees that the update is “proximal”: if the ratio $w_t(\theta)$ drifts beyond the range $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$, the objective reverts to a clipped value that limits the influence of that token on the gradient.  In effect, PPO suppresses the contribution of drastically off‑policy tokens, protecting against large, destabilizing policy changes.  

Crucially, the **advantage** $\widehat{A}_t$ is computed as  

\[
\widehat{A}_t = R_t - V_{\theta_{\text{val}}}(x, y_{<t}),
\]

where $R_t$ is the observed return from time $t$ onward and $V_{\theta_{\text{val}}}$ is the value network.  Training a high‑quality value model is nontrivial: it must be small enough to fit within the same memory budget as the policy, yet accurate enough to provide reliable estimates over *long* sequences.  This requirement grows more challenging when the model generates hundreds of tokens or specialized tasks such as problem solving, where the return distribution can be highly skewed.

In sum, PPO’s strengths are its simplicity, theoretical guarantees, and the elegant balance between exploitation and policy stability.  Its major practical limitation lies in the dependency on a high‑dimension value network that is expensive to train and evaluate, particularly for large, sparse models or for tasks that demand long‑form generation.

## Practical issues of PPO (memory, long sequences)

For large language models, especially those with a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) architecture, several implementation constraints surface:

1. **Memory Bandwidth** – Every token in a rollout requires accessing the value network, which is comparable in size to the policy itself.  When the rollout batch must contain a *large number* of samples to saturate GPU cores, memory traffic quickly overwhelms inter‑device bandwidth, leading to stalls or the need for aggressive gradient accumulation.

2. **Bias for Short Sequences** – The clipping range $\varepsilon$ is typically tuned on *short* sequences.  When applied to longer responses, the relative importance ratios tend to become larger in magnitude, thereby shrinking the effective clipping window.  This can cause tokens far from the start of a response to be over‑re‑weighted, inflating variance and reducing convergence speed.

3. **Computational Latency** – The value network must be evaluated for every partial sequence, adding a non‑trivial forward‑pass cost that substantially slows down training.  In practice, this cost becomes a bottleneck when scaling to thousands of GPUs, forcing practitioners to settle for sub‑optimal settings (e.g., fewer tokens per rollout) that compromise the quality of the model.

Because of these challenges, researchers have looked for alternatives that bypass the value model, giving rise to GRPO.

## GRPO formulation: group sampling, token‑level importance ratios  

GRPO is a *value‑free* variant of PPO that replaces the per‑token advantage estimate with a relative advantage computed over a *group* of responses generated to the same query.  
Given a query $x$, GRPO samples a batch of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)$, and then defines the advantage for token $y_{i,t}$ as

\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i
:= \frac{r(x, y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
\quad 
\mu=\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G},
\quad
\sigma=\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G},
\]

where $r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$ denotes the verifier‑scored reward.  
All tokens in a response share the same advantage, but each token retains its **token‑level importance ratio**  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The GRPO objective mirrors PPO’s structure but aggregates over the entire group and token:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\bigr)
\right].
\]

GRPO’s appeal lies in *eliminating the value network*, thereby saving memory and computational budget and simplifying implementation.  Furthermore, the group‑wise advantage normalisation mitigates reward scale issues, a common problem when using raw scores from verifiers.

## Advantages of GRPO (value-free)

* **Memory Efficiency** – Without a separate value model, GRPO can allocate more of the limited GPU memory to larger batch sizes or more powerful policy heads, especially valuable for MoE architectures that already push the limits of memory usage.

* **Simplified Training Pipeline** – Removing the value network reduces the number of hyperparameters and the need for separate pre‑training or fine‑tuning steps for a value head, streamlining the RL pipeline.

* **End‑to‑End Stability in Short‑Length Tasks** – For many early RL‑training setups, GRPO can achieve stable convergence on tasks with short generation lengths, as the token‑level importance ratios have relatively low variance in this regime.

These merits, however, mask deeper issues that become manifest when GRPO is applied to longer, more complex sequences or to sparse MoE models.

## Illustrative examples of token‑level weighting problems  

Consider a toy scenario where a response $y$ consists of five tokens generated to a query $x$.  Suppose the policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ produces the sequence
\[
y = (\texttt{a},\texttt{b},\texttt{c},\texttt{d},\texttt{e}),
\]
and the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ assigns a significantly higher probability to token $\texttt{c}$ while sharply reducing probabilities for the others.  Concretely, let

| token | $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)$ | $\pi_{\theta}(y_t)$ |
|-------|---------------------------------|---------------------|
| a     | 0.15 | 0.05 |
| b     | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| c     | 0.25 | 0.50 |
| d     | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| e     | 0.20 | 0.05 |

The token‑level importance ratios become  

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t)}=
(0.33,\;0.50,\;2.00,\;0.50,\;0.25).
\]

GRPO would weight the gradient of each token by its corresponding ratio: the third token’s gradient is amplified eight‐fold ($2.00$), while the first and last become negligible ($0.33$ and $0.25$).  If the reward is assigned to the entire sequence, such token‑level re‑weighting injects *high‑variance* noise into the gradient: one token dominates the learning signal, even though the reward is a single scalar for the whole response.  When sequences become long, this variance propagates multiplicatively across tokens, making the gradient estimation highly unstable.

Furthermore, when the importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$ exceeds $1+\varepsilon$, the clipping term in GRPO truncates the effective weight to $1+\varepsilon$, which for token–wise ratios can be as severe as clipping a factor of $2$ down to $1.27$ (for $\varepsilon=0.27$).  Tokens with ratios below $1-\varepsilon$ are likewise capped, potentially discarding informative gradients.  In large language models that produce *hundreds* of tokens, the probability that *any* token’s ratio falls outside the clipping band increases dramatically, leading to excessive gradient *sparsification* and wasted samples.

These practical pitfalls become particularly acute in MoE settings, where *expert‑activation volatility* disturbs the per‑token likelihoods more than the overall sequence likelihood.  As the paper demonstrates, GRPO’s token‑level re‑weighting can cause catastrophic model collapse that is difficult to recover from, especially when the training batch size is scaled up for efficiency.

In contrast, the GSPO algorithm eschews token‑level weighting entirely.  It defines a *sequence‑level* importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

which normalises the effect of sequence length and treats every token in a response as part of a single, shared reward signal.  By clipping on the sequence level, GSPO preserves inter‑token gradient correlations and eliminates the high‑variance artefacts inherent to GRPO while retaining the value‑free benefit.  This design, rooted in the principled theory of importance sampling, ultimately yields a more stable and efficient RL algorithm for large language models.

# 4. Motivation for a Sequence‑Level Approach

Training reinforcement learning (RL) agents that can generate long, high‑fidelity language responses demands a careful orchestration of data collection, sample reuse, and objective design. When a policy model is too large to generate every sample on demand, practitioners typically adopt a large rollout batch for computational efficiency. However, this engineering choice introduces an off‑policy learning setting that undermines the assumptions underpinning many RL algorithms. The section that follows unpacks how these practical necessities create a fertile ground for instability, why token‑level importance weights fail to satisfy the fundamental requirements of importance sampling, and how the accumulation of noise over long sequences can precipitate catastrophic model collapse. Finally, we synthesize these observations into a guiding principle: the unit of reward should align with the unit of optimization.

---

## Off‑policy learning in large batch RL

A core constraint when scaling language‑model RL is hardware utilization. Modern GPUs or tensor‑core accelerators perform most efficiently when batch sizes are on the order of several hundred thousand tokens per step. To reach this throughput, a common pipeline groups thousands of queries, each paired with several long responses, into one large *rollout batch*. Within this batch, the model—denoted \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\)—generates a set of responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) for each query \(x\). Because the policy to be updated, \(\pi_{\theta}\), has not yet seen these responses, we call this a *mismatch* between the behavior distribution and the target distribution.

Instead of recomputing new responses at every gradient step (which would be prohibitively expensive), practitioners divide the large batch into multiple *mini‑batches* and perform several gradient updates on the same data. This staggering of updates transforms a single on‑policy dataset into an *off‑policy* training regime: the mini‑batch samples are drawn from \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) while the updates push \(\pi_{\theta}\) toward a target that is gradually changing during training. The consequence is that each gradient step operates on data that no longer matches the distribution implicitly defined by the current parameters. Classical policy‑gradient methods, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its transformer‑specific variation GRPO, mitigate this mismatch by clipping importance ratios or using counter‑acting critics. Yet, as we shall illustrate, when the mismatch is compounded by very long sequences and many embedded queries, these mitigation strategies can become inadequate.

---

## Importance sampling and its requirements

Importance sampling (IS) is a statistical method for estimating expectations under a *target* distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). Its canonical form is

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\left[ f(z) \right]
 = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

Several assumptions underlie this identity:

1. **Sufficient overlap**: \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) and \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) must assign non‑zero probability to the same support. If \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) assigns weight to regions the behavior model never visited, the estimator becomes undefined.

2. **Multiple samples**: The replacement of a single sample by a weighted version only works when we aggregate a *large* number of i.i.d. samples from the behavior distribution. The law of large numbers guarantees that the weighted sum converges to the target expectation as the batch size grows.

3. **Weight stability**: The variance of the importance weight \(\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)/\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)\) must be controlled. Excessive variance inflates estimator noise and can dominate learning dynamics.

4. **Proper alignment**: The function \(f(z)\) to be integrated must be consistent with the level at which the weight is computed. If the weight is applied to token‑level events, then \(f(z)\) should also be token‑wise; if the weight is at sequence level, \(f(z)\) must be a sequence‑wise reward or loss.

In the RL context for language models, the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) defines the distribution of entire responses \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_{|y|})\) conditioned on a query \(x\). The reward function \(r(x,y)\) typically assigns a scalar score to the whole response (e.g., judged by a verifier or a skill metric). Consequently, the natural pairing between weights and rewards is *sequence‑level* rather than *token‑level*. When one inadvertently substitutes token‑level ratios for sequence‑level corrections, the IS assumptions are violated.

---

## Why token‑level weights violate importance sampling

The GRPO objective introduces a token‑wise importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta) \;=\;
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})},
\quad
i = 1,\dots,G,\; t = 1,\dots,|y_i|.
\]

Each token \(y_{i,t}\) is sampled once from the *next‑token* distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x, y_{i,<t})\). The ratio above therefore treats a single draw as if it could correct for the entire next‑token distribution—precisely the inverse of the usual IS setting, where many draws are available to estimate an expectation. As a result:

- The ratio provides no guarantee of proximity to the target probability because it is computed from a *single* observation. In other words, the expectation in the IS equation cannot be approximated by one sample; many samples are needed for a reliable estimate.

- The distribution of \(w_{i,t}\) becomes highly skewed; tokens that happen to be rare under the old policy can produce exceedingly large ratios, while common tokens yield ratios near one. This skew translates directly into high‑variance gradient estimates.

- Since each token in a long response contributes a distinct ratio, the final policy gradient is a weighted sum of many noisy terms, amplifying instability.

In contrast, a sequence‑wise weight

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\!,
\]

is naturally a ratio of two probabilities over the entire sequence. Although it may also exhibit variance, the variance is typically smoother because it aggregates over all tokens. More importantly, this ratio is theoretically justified by importance sampling applied at the correct granularity: one sequence sample replaces one target distribution evaluation, honoring the “multiple samples needed for variance reduction” principle.

---

## Accumulation of training noise in long sequences

Long‑distance dependencies inflate the risk of noise accumulation. In the token‑wise framework, consider a response of length \(L\). The gradient term for a single token is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\, \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}).
\]

Even if the advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) were stabilized, the amplification factor \(w_{i,t}\) is applied individually to each token. Suppose the expected magnitude of \(w_{i,t}\) deviates by a factor of \(1+\delta\) from unity (due to sampling variability). The cumulative effect across the sequence is a multiplicative drift that aggregates roughly like

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{L} (1+\delta_t) \;\approx\; \exp\!\left(\;\sum_{t=1}^{L} \delta_t\;\right),
\]

where \(\delta_t\) are zero‑mean but noisy fluctuations. Even if \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_t] = 0\), the variance of the sum grows as \(L\sigma^2\), leading to a log‑normal distribution with a long right‑tail. A few extreme tokens can dominate the overall weight, pushing it far out of the clipping band and yielding unreliable gradient updates.

Moreover, the *clipping* mechanism, designed to keep the ratio within \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\), counters this trend but does not eliminate the underlying high‑variance signal that comprises the bulk of the gradient. Consequently, over successive gradient steps, the model may be steered in erratic directions, especially when the sequence length grows to hundreds of tokens, as is typical in advanced reasoning tasks.

---

## Experiments showing model collapse

Empirical evidence of the fragility of token‑level importance weights emerged when training massive language models on tasks requiring elaborate reasoning. When the GRPO algorithm was applied with a moderate rollout batch, the training curve displayed erratic increases in reward followed by sudden drops to near‑zero, after which the model failed to recover. This phenomenon—termed *model collapse*—was irreversible even when the training process was halted, the model checkpoint was rolled back, and hyperparameters such as the clipping range were tuned.

A systematic ablation pinpoints the culprit: by replacing the token‑wise weight \(w_{i,t}\) with the sequence‑wise weight \(s_i(\theta)\) and recomputing the gradient accordingly (i.e., Group Sequence Policy Optimization), the model’s performance continued to climb steadily without any collapse. This experiment demonstrates that the instability is not inherent to the policy or the reward but stems from the misapplication of importance sampling at an inappropriate granularity.

---

## Principle: unit of reward ≈ unit of optimization

The discussion above leads to a clear guiding principle for designing RL objectives in large language models:

> **The granular unit of the objective function should match the granular unit of the reward signal.**

Rewards in language‑model RL are naturally defined over entire responses—complete and coherent texts. Therefore, the importance weighting, clipping, and gradient computation should operate at the sequence level. Token‑wise weighting introduces noise that violates the assumptions of importance sampling and aggregates in a manner that can destabilize the learning process. Sequence‑wise weighting aligns with the reward, respects the statistical foundations of IS, and mitigates variance accumulation.

Adhering to this principle transforms the training dynamics in several ways:

- **Stability**: The gradient estimates become less noisy, reducing the risk of catastrophic collapse.
- **Efficiency**: By avoiding unnecessary token‑wise computations that drown in variance, the loss surface is smoother and the model converges faster.
- **Simplicity**: Eliminating token‑level importance ratios eliminates the need for auxiliary mechanisms such as *Routing Replay* that were invented to rescue token‑level approaches in sparse MoE settings.

In sum, the root cause of RL instability in large‑scale language‑model training lies in treating length‑invariant rewards as if they were token‑wise. A sequence‑level perspective not only restores the theoretical integrity of importance sampling but also yields practical benefits that empower the next generation of RL‑enhanced language models.

# 5. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Core Idea

Reinforcement learning (RL) for language models is notoriously fragile. The policy is an autoregressive neural network that generates tokens one by one, and the reward is typically **assigned to the entire response**. When the number of tokens grows or the model becomes very large (e.g., Mixture‑of‑Experts, MoE), the unstable training dynamics of previous algorithms such as GRPO can lead to catastrophic model collapse. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) addresses the root cause of this instability by aligning the **unit of importance weighting** and **unit of reward**: both are defined at the sequence level. In the following sections we unpack the key components of GSPO, showing why they matter and how they are put together into a coherent, provably stable RL objective.

## Definition of sequence‑level importance ratio \( s_{k}(\theta) \)

A language model parameterized by \(\theta\) defines a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). For a query \(x\) and a response \(y\), the likelihood under the policy is

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t}),
\]

the product of token‑wise next‑token probabilities. In GRPO the importance weight is computed token by token:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})},
\]

and then multiplied into the gradient. This token‑level weighting breaks the principle of importance sampling because each token is weighted by a likelihood ratio that was computed **using only the single token sampled** from the old policy, not the full sequence. GSPO replaces this with a **sequence‑level** ratio that describes how much the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) diverges from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) over the whole response:

\[
s_{k}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_k|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_k|}.
\]

The exponent \(1/|y_k|\) normalizes the ratio to a per‑token scale and prevents extremely long responses from dominating the magnitude of \(s_{k}(\theta)\). The ratio can also be written as an average of log token‑level ratios:

\[
s_{k}(\theta)=
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_k|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_k|}
              \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k,t}|x,y_{k,<t})}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{k,t}|x,y_{k,<t})}\right).
\]

Thus, \(s_{k}(\theta)\) reflects the **expected change in likelihood per token** when moving from the old policy to the current one. Because it is constructed from an average, it has low variance and is well‑suited for stable gradient estimation.

## Length normalization and variance control

The raw likelihood ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)\) grows or shrinks exponentially with the length of \(y\). Consequently, sequences of different lengths would produce importance ratios that differ by orders of magnitude, forcing the clipping range to depend on response length. By **length normalization**—raising the ratio to the reciprocal of the sequence length—we obtain a quantity that is roughly on the same scale regardless of how many tokens were generated. This mitigates two crucial problems:

1. **Variance of the importance weight**: Long sequences can yield ratios that are extremely large or tiny, increasing gradient variance. Normalization keeps the weight within a narrow window.
2. **Clipping calibration**: Since the clipped range must know the typical value of the importance ratio, a length‑normalized ratio allows a single, fixed clipping interval to work for all sequence lengths.

Empirically, GSPO uses left and right clipping thresholds on the order of \(10^{-4}\), a magnitude compatible with the normalized ratios. In contrast, GRPO employs thresholds around \(10^{-1}\) due to the un‑normalized token‑level ratios.

## Group‑based advantage estimation

In RL, the advantage function quantifies how much better a response is compared to a baseline. GSPO adopts the same **group‑based** advantage used in GRPO because it avoids the expensive value network. For a query \(x\) and its \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\), the advantage of response \(y_i\) is

\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\quad
\mu_x=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r(x,y_j),\;
\sigma_x^2=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}\bigl(r(x,y_j)-\mu_x\bigr)^2.
\]

Thus each response is centered and normalized by the mean and standard deviation of rewards within its group. This has several benefits:

- **Variance reduction**: By normalizing, the scale of advantages is kept to roughly one, easing learning.
- **Implicit baselining**: Responses that are worse than the group mean receive negative advantages and are discouraged; better responses are rewarded.
- **Differentiable with respect to the model**: Since the rewards \(r(x,y)\) are not differentiable, the normalization is performed on the *non‑differentiable* reward values, not on the policy parameters.

Because all tokens in a response receive the same advantage, the objective will later reward or punish the entire sequence coherently.

## GSPO objective re‑derivation

Putting the pieces together, the GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Biggl[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl( s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
          \text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_i
          \bigr)
\Biggr].
\tag{5.1}
\]

The structure of this objective is directly inherited from PPO:

1. **Importance weight** (\(s_i(\theta)\)) multiplies the **advantage**.
2. **Clipping** limits the weight to a proximity band around 1. If the weight is too large or too small, the clipped value is used instead to guarantee the update does not stray far from the old policy.
3. **Expectation** is taken over the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) and the rollouts sampled from the old policy.

Deriving the gradient (ignoring the clipping for brevity) follows the standard REINFORCE trick:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
\right] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i
\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.2}
\]

Notice that the gradient **does not** involve any token‑wise importance weights: every token in a response contributes equally through the \(1/|y_i|\) factor. This is in stark contrast to GRPO where tokens are multiplied by their own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), which may be substantially larger or smaller than 1, thereby biasing the gradient artificially. By uniformly weighting tokens, GSPO eliminates the primary source of high‑variance and instability in GRPO.

## Clipping at the sequence level vs. token level

In PPO and GRPO, **clipping is applied at the token level**. Roughly speaking, each token \(y_t\) in the response receives its own importance ratio \(w_t(\theta)\). Tokens with extreme ratios (i.e., far from the old policy) are clipped to avoid over‑aggressive updates. However, because the reward is attached to the whole sequence, this token‑wise clipping produces a mismatch: tokens that should be rewarded together are sometimes clipped independently, leading to confusing gradient signals.

GSPO’s **sequence‑level clipping** solves this mismatch elegantly:

- The entire response is either accepted within the clipping window or rejected as a whole.
- Tokens that collectively deviate too much from the old policy are removed from the gradient estimator, regardless of how individual tokens behave.
- The resulting clip threshold is tuned on the per‑sequence importance ratios \(s_i(\theta)\), ensuring a consistent sensitivity across all lengths.

Empirically, the fraction of tokens clipped under GSPO is *an order of magnitude higher* than under GRPO. Yet GSPO still demonstrates higher training efficiency. This counter‑intuitive observation underscores that the tokens eliminated due to clipping carry noisy gradient information that would otherwise destabilize learning. By discarding them at the sequence level, GSPO preserves a cleaner, more reliable learning signal.

## Choice of clipping ranges and their impact

The clipping hyperparameters \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}\) define a symmetric window \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) around the value 1. In practice, GSPO uses smaller \(\varepsilon\) (e.g., \(\varepsilon \approx 3\times10^{-4}\)) than GRPO because the importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) is a length‑normalized quantity that stays close to 1 even when the underlying policy changes significantly. A narrow clip ensures that any large deviation is penalized swiftly, preserving the proximal nature of the update.

If \(\varepsilon\) is set too **large**, the clipping mechanism becomes ineffective; the algorithm essentially becomes a standard policy gradient with importance weighting, re‑introducing the high‑variance problem that led to collapse. Conversely, if \(\varepsilon\) is too **small**, too many sequences will be clipped, starving the gradient estimator of useful samples and slowing convergence.

Fine‑tuning \(\varepsilon\) is therefore crucial but not overly delicate: the ratio’s length‑normalization reduces the sensitivity to the precise value, and standard grid‑search strategies on a held‑out validation set quickly reveal the stable region.

---

In summary, GSPO redefines the importance ratio at the correct abstraction level, normalizes it to control variance, and aligns the reward, advantage, and objective within a single coherent mathematical framework. By doing so, it eliminates the pathological noise that plagued GRPO, stabilizes policy updates even in massive MoE architectures, and ultimately unlocks the full potential of reinforcement learning for large‑scale language models.

# 6. Gradient Analysis and Stability Insights

In this chapter we unpack the mathematics behind the key stability guarantees of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). We first revisit the exact gradient derivations for GSPO and its predecessor GRPO, then analyze the differences in their weighting schemes. Finally, we show how the accumulation of unstable terms in GRPO’s per‑token weighting can cause catastrophic failure in long‑response settings, while GSPO’s sequence‑level weighting ameliorates this effect and yields a more robust training signal.

---

## 6.1 Gradient Derivation for GSPO

Recall that the GSPO objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Bigl(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i
\Bigr)
\right].
\tag{6.1}
\]

For the moment we suppress the clipping operation (it does not affect the analytical form of the gradient) and focus on the core expression

\[
\ell_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\,\{y_i\}}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\right],
\]
where the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) is defined as

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
\;=\;
\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\tag{6.2}
\]

The expectation is taken over the distribution of groups of responses generated by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Since \(\widehat{A}_i\) depends solely on the reward of the full response \(y_i\), the gradient can be pushed inside the expectation:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&=\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\right] \\
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

Using \(\nabla_{\theta}s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)\) and noting that

\[
\log s_i(\theta)
=\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})-
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right] \\
&=\boxed{
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G}
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}}
\widehat{A}_i
\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.3}
\]

Equation (6.3) is precisely the gradient used in practice: each token in a response is weighted *identically* by the sequence‑level factor \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\). In other words, the gradient contribution of a given response is proportional to its likelihood ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i)\), spread uniformly across all tokens.

---

## 6.2 Gradient Derivation for GRPO

For GRPO the objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat{A}_i
\Bigr)
\right],
\tag{6.4}
\]
where the token‑wise importance ratio is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\tag{6.5}
\]
and every token in a response shares the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\).

Again dropping clipping for clarity and pulling the gradient inside:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
&=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta} w_{i,t}(\theta)
\right] .
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is a ratio of two likelihoods, we obtain

\[
\nabla_{\theta} w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]
because the denominator \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) is treated as constant with respect to \(\theta\). Substituting gives

\[
\boxed{
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
}
\tag{6.6}
\]

Equation (6.6) reveals that GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its *token‑level* importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Unlike GSPO, the weighting factor is not the same across tokens in the same response: if a particular token becomes highly unlikely under the new policy relative to the old one, its contribution to the gradient will be severely down‑weighted or even clipped to zero by the outer clipping operator.

---

## 6.3 Comparison of Per‑Token Weighting

Both algorithms ultimately aim to correct for the off‑policy distributional shift between the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and the new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). However, the *unit* at which this correction is applied differs:

| Algorithm | Weighting Scheme | Unit of Weight | Effect |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|--------|
| GSPO | Sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) | Entire response | Per‑token contribution is uniformly scaled by \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\). |
| GRPO | Token‑level importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) | Token | Each token is weighted by its own ratio. |

Because GSPO’s weight is built from the *product* of all token ratios in a response, it is naturally more stable and less sensitive to the variance of any single token. In contrast, GRPO couples each token’s gradient to the potentially volatile ratio of that single token, leading to *heterogeneous* scaling across the same response.

The difference is most clearly illustrated if we rewrite the two weighting factors in terms of the underlying token log likelihoods:

- GSPO: \[
s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\bigr]\right).
\]

- GRPO: \[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
= \exp\!\bigl[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid \cdot)\bigr].
\]

GSPO’s average over the sequence smooths out any outlier terms, while GRPO’s per‑token expression preserves them, magnifying the impact of rare tokens.

---

## 6.4 Accumulation of Unstable Terms in GRPO

When generating longer responses, the product of token probabilities becomes exponentially small, and small perturbations in a token’s likelihood can cause large swings in the importance ratio. Since GRPO’s gradient for a response \(\{y_i\}\) is an *average* of token‑level contributions, a single token that spikes or drops can dominate the update for that response.

Formally, suppose token \(t^\ast\) in response \(i\) receives an anomalously high importance ratio \(w_{i,t^\ast}(\theta)\gg 1\). In the gradient (6.6) the corresponding term receives a disproportionate boost:

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}\,w_{i,t^\ast}(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t^\ast}\mid \cdot).
\]

If \(t^\ast\) is also on the boundary of the clipping window, the gradient may be *sharply* modified, injecting high variance into the parameter update. Over many such tokens and many iterations this stochasticity accumulates, especially severe when the model is already near convergence and the policy does not change dramatically between updates. In the extreme, the token‑level ratios can grow unbounded, leading to exploding or vanishing gradients, precise the “model collapse” phenomenon described in the introduction.

Moreover, because the token‑level ratio is evaluated *after* the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) has been frozen, its denominator is fixed. If the policy changes the probability distribution enough to make the old token likelihood significantly larger (or smaller), \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can exceed the clipping range dramatically. The outer clipping then caps the contribution, but only after the potentially harmful token has already averted its influence on the gradient—yet the associated high variance remains in the averaging process and can provoke instability in subsequent steps.

---

## 6.5 Implications for Long‑Response Tasks

Long‑response tasks (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, code generation) exacerbate the issues highlighted above because:

1. **Sequence Length Amplifies Variance**: The likelihood of a long sequence is the product of many small probabilities, making the sequence likelihood highly sensitive to a few token changes. Thus, token‑level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) are naturally more volatile.

2. **Accumulation Over Time**: Each token’s contribution is added to the gradient sum. Even if the mean of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is close to one (as the policies are often similar), the *variance* can be large, leading to noisy updates on average.

3. **Clipping for Off‑Policy Corrections**: The clipping interval is typically chosen to be narrow for token‑level weights, otherwise the training would diverge. This means that many tokens are clipped to the boundary, effectively discarding their gradient contribution. When most tokens are clipped, the remaining few dominate the update, potentially in a non‑representative way.

In contrast, GSPO mitigates these issues in several ways:

- **Uniform Weighting per Response**: Since every token in a response receives the same factor \(s_i(\theta)^{1/|y_i|}\), a large deviation in any one token’s likelihood is averaged over the whole sequence, dramatically reducing variance.

- **Reduced Dependence on Token-By-Token Variations**: The gradient for a response is driven by the *overall* likelihood shift, which is a smoother function of the policy parameters because it aggregates across the entire sequence.

- **Effective Clipping at the Response Level**: Clipping the entire sequence likelihood ratio is much less likely to truncate many tokens simultaneously, preserving more gradient signal.

- **Length Normalization**: By taking the \(|y_i|\)-th root of the likelihood ratio, the magnitude of \(s_i(\theta)\) remains in a manageable range, preventing extreme scaling that could destabilize training.

Concrete numerical experiments (see Section 4.2 of the paper) show that GSPO reduces the variance of the policy gradient estimates by almost an order of magnitude compared to GRPO when evaluated on long‑response datasets, confirming the theoretical intuition.

---

## 6.6 Numerical Illustration of Variance Reduction

Consider a synthetic example with a response of length \(L=10\). Suppose the old policy assigns a uniform probability \(p_{\text{old}}=0.1\) to every token, while the new policy changes the probability of each token in denomination \(\pm 5\%\) around the old value. The per‑token weight for token \(t\) is

\[
w_{t}= \frac{p_{\text{new}}^t}{p_{\text{old}}} = 1 \pm 0.05.
\]

The variance of \(w_t\) is \(\sigma^2_w = 0.05^2 = 0.0025\). For GRPO, the gradient for the response is the average of \(L\) such terms, so the variance of the *average* is

\[
\sigma^2_{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{\sigma^2_w}{L} = \frac{0.0025}{10} = 0.00025.
\]

Now consider GSPO’s sequence‑level weight:

\[
s = \prod_{t=1}^{L} w_t^{1/L} = \left(\prod_{t=1}^L w_t\right)^{1/L}.
\]

Assuming independence, the variance of the product is

\[
\sigma^2_s \approx \exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^L \log^2 w_t\right) - 1 \approx L \sigma^2_{\log w},
\]
where \(\sigma^2_{\log w}\) is the variance of \(\log w_t\), which is smaller than that of \(w_t\) itself because \(\log(1+x)\approx x\) for small \(x\). After taking the \(|y_i|\)-th root, the variance is further reduced:

\[
\sigma^2_{s^{1/L}} \approx \frac{\sigma^2_s}{L^2} \approx \frac{\sigma^2_{\log w}}{L} < \sigma^2_{\text{GRPO}}.
\]

Thus, even in this toy example, GSPO inherently produces a smaller variance estimate for the policy gradient than GRPO does. This variance advantage scales more strongly as the sequence length grows, explaining why GSPO is particularly effective for long‑response tasks.

---

## 6.7 Concluding Remarks

The derivations above demonstrate a clear mathematical reason for GSPO’s superior stability: its gradient is **sequence‑level** and **uniformly weighted**, whereas GRPO’s gradient is **token‑level** and **heterogeneously weighted**. By moving the importance ratio from the token domain to the sequence domain, GSPO reduces high‑variance contributions that can corrupt the learning signal, particularly when dealing with long responses or sparse MoE activations. This insight not only explains earlier empirical observations regarding model collapse in GRPO but also motivates the design of GSPO as a robust, scalable RL algorithm for large language models.

# 7. GSPO‑token: Token‑Level Variant for Fine‑Grained Control

In the original Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) design, the policy update is driven by sequence‑level advantages. Every response \(y_i\) to a query \(x\) receives a single scalar advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\), which is then shared uniformly with all tokens in that response. This yields highly stable training dynamics because the importance weight \(s_i(\theta)\) is applied uniformly across all tokens. However, many practical reinforcement‑learning scenarios—such as multi‑turn dialogue or curriculum‑aware training—require a finer granularity: we might wish to reward or penalize certain tokens (e.g., fillers, corrections, or crucial information tokens) more strongly than others within the same response.

To accommodate this need without sacrificing the stability advantages of GSPO, the authors propose the token‑level variant **GSPO‑token**. In what follows we unpack the rationale, formal definition, and implications of this algorithmic variant.

---

## Motivation for Token‑Level Granularity

The essential motivation lies in the mismatch between the *unit of reward* and the *unit of policy update*. While the reward \(r(x, y)\) is naturally defined at the sequence level, there are circumstances where a *per-token* shaping signal can accelerate learning or enforce fine‑grained linguistic constraints. Examples include:

- **Multi‑turn reinforcement learning**: Different turns in a dialogue may deserve distinct advantages; a token generating a question is worth more than one filling an answer slot.
- **Attribute‑aware training**: We might want to explicitly penalize certain style attributes (e.g., excessive pronoun usage) at the token level.
- **Exploratory signal injection**: Introducing token‑based intrinsic rewards (like curiosity) can guide the policy toward novel token sequences.

If we retain GSPO’s sequence‑level advantage, we inevitably ignore these token‑specific incentives, potentially wasting valuable training signals. GSPO‑token remedies this shortcoming by extending the objective so that each token \(y_{i,t}\) can receive its own advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\).

---

## GSPO‑token Objective (Equation 13)

Mathematically, GSPO‑token replaces the sequence‑averaged advantage in the core objective with a token‑wise one. The resulting update rule reads:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\Bigl(
 \min \bigl(
  s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t},
  \;\mathrm{clip}\bigl(s_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}
 \bigr)
\Bigr)
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
\]

The structure mirrors GRPO’s token‑wise objective: each token experiences its own clipped importance weight \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) and advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\). However, the critical difference is that the importance weight \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) is *derived from the sequence‑level ratio* (see below), thereby preserving the desirable stability properties of GSPO.

---

## Definition of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) and Use of the Stop‑Gradient Operator

The token‑level importance weight \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) is defined by re‑parameterizing the sequence‑level weight so that each token implicitly carries the same scalar multiplicative factor but still upholds the correct theoretical form. Concretely:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)
= \underbrace{\mathrm{sg}\!\left[s_i(\theta)\right]}_{\text{detached scalar}}
\;\times\;
\frac{ \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,\,y_{i,<t}\bigr) }
     { \mathrm{sg}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,\,y_{i,<t}\bigr)\bigr] }.
\tag{14}
\]

Here, \(s_i(\theta)\) is the *sequence‑level* importance weight defined in GSPO:

\[
s_i(\theta)
= \Bigl(\frac{ \pi_{\theta}(y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i | x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
\]

The notation \(\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]\) denotes the *stop‑gradient* operation (a.k.a. “detach”). It passes the numerical value of its argument to the forward pass but blocks any gradient flow through it. Thus, in the computation of \(s_{i,t}(\theta)\), the term \(\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\) behaves as a fixed constant during back‑propagation, while the fraction
\(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)/\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)]\) simplifies to 1 in value and disappears from the gradient.

In effect, **\(s_{i,t}(\theta)\) equals \(s_i(\theta)\) numerically** but is treated as a separate scalar for each token during the forward pass. The stop‑gradient trick ensures that the only trainable term in the gradient is \(s_i(\theta)\) (the sequence‑level weight), not individual token probabilities. This design makes the algorithm consistent with sequence‑level stability while allowing token‑wise advantage flexibility.

---

## Gradient Derivation (Equation 15)

The expectation in Eq. (13) is differentiated with respect to \(\theta\). After excluding the clipping term for clarity, the gradient can be derived as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
&= \nabla_{\theta}\,
\mathbb{E}_{x,\,\{y_i\}}\,
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x,\,\{y_i\}}\,
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
s_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_{i,t}(\theta).
\end{aligned}
\]

Because of the stop‑gradient, \(\log s_{i,t}(\theta) = \log s_i(\theta)\) for all tokens, hence

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_{i,t}(\theta)
= \nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
= \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{u=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,u}\mid x, y_{i,<u}).
\]

Plugging this back yields

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\,\{y_i\}}\,
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\Bigl(\frac{ \pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}\!
\widehat{A}_{i,t}\,
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}).
\tag{15}
\]

The final expression shows that, **in expectation, GSPO‑token generates the same gradient update as GSPO** when the per‑token advantages \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) are identical to the sequence‑level advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\). Thus, the two algorithms are numerically indistinguishable under those conditions.

---

## Relation to Standard GSPO

The relationship outlined above is formalized in the identity:

- *If* \(\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i\) for all tokens in all responses, then
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta) = \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta),
\quad
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}(\theta) = \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta).
\]

In other words, GSPO‑token is a *drop‑in, token‑aware* wrapper over GSPO. When no finer‑grained token advantages are available or desired, one can simply set \(\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_i\) and recover the conventional GSPO update path. This design choice grants the practitioner flexibility without compromising correctness.

---

## When to Use GSPO vs. GSPO‑token

| Scenario | Recommended Variant |
|----------|---------------------|
| **Standard RL with only sequence rewards** | GSPO (simpler, fewer hyperparameters) |
| **Multi‑turn dialogue or per‑token constraints** | GSPO‑token (introduces token‑level advantages) |
| **Ablation studies: effect of token‑level shaping** | GSPO‑token |
| **Memory‑bounded training where token advantages are expensive** | GSPO (avoids per‑token advantage computation) |

In practice, adopting GSPO‑token does not incur the usual computational overhead of evaluating token‑level rewards because the *advantage computation* and the *importance weight* are still performed at the sequence level. Only the *clipping* and *multiplication* step recognize per‑token advantages, which is embarrassingly parallel. Therefore, if token‑level signals provide meaningful task‑specific information, GSPO‑token offers a lightweight yet principled way to exploit them without sacrificing the stability that has proven crucial in large‑scale MoE reinforcement learning.

---

## Summary

GSPO‑token elegantly bridges the gap between **sequence‑level stability** and **token‑level expressiveness**. By embedding the sequence‑level importance weight into a token‑wise objective and employing the stop‑gradient trick, it inherits GSPO’s robust training dynamics while granting access to fine‑grained reward shaping. Its gradient expression confirms theoretical equivalence to GSPO when token advantages are uniform, and its design paralysis–friendly when token‑specific rewards are unavailable. Consequently, GSPO‑token stands as a versatile extension ready to be deployed in a wide array of reinforcement‑learning workloads for large language models.

# Chapter 8  
**Experimental Evaluation – Training Curves and Benchmarks**  

Large‑scale reinforcement learning for language models is a multifaceted endeavor.  Theoretical guarantees must be corroborated by empirical results, and only a careful experimental study can illuminate the practical benefits of a new algorithm such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).  This chapter presents a comprehensive walkthrough of the experiment conducted in the paper, detailing every choice that influences the learning dynamics: from the cold‑start fine‑tuning of the base model to the final performance on three real‑world benchmarks.  Each subtopic is expanded with motivation, intuition, and quantitative analysis, providing the reader with a deep understanding of how GSPO’s design decisions translate into concrete gains.

## 8.1 Cold‑Start Fine‑Tuning from Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base  

The starting point for the experiments is the Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base model, a 30‑billion‑parameter Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language model already pre‑trained on a vast corpus.  The authors freeze the architecture but rebuild the training pipeline for reinforcement learning.  The “cold‑start” condition reflects the reality of many industrial settings: a large pre‑trained model is granted a modest amount of additional RL data, and we wish to observe whether a policy‑optimization method can further improve *all* downstream abilities of the model—or whether the huge capacity of the underlying network simply absorbs the learning noise.

### Why Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base?  
- **MoE Scale**: The base model contains thousands of experts spread across 48 layers.  Its sparse activation pattern is precisely the setting in which GRPO has historically suffered from expert‑activation volatility.  A MoE baseline therefore offers an unforgiving test of algorithmic robustness.  
- **Pre‑trained Strength**: The baseline already exhibits strong performances on coding and math tasks, which means that any improvement must be meaningful: it cannot be attributed to baseline training curve memorization.  
- **Open‑Source Realism**: Qwen3 is available in open‑source libraries (Megatron‑LLaMA, vLLM), ensuring that the experiments can be reproduced by practitioners.

### Fine‑Tuning Procedure  
The model is loaded with default weights and tokenizer.  No additional language modeling checkpoints are used; the RL fine‑tuning only interacts with the policy network.  The training pipeline is set to use **distributed data parallelism** over multiple GPUs to accommodate the massive batch sizes required for stable RL updates.  

To keep the learning dynamics clear, the authors **freeze the queries** and **responses** during the initial phase (the “cold‑start” regime).  This guarantees that any observed changes in the reward curves come solely from policy updates rather than from variations in the query distribution—a crucial control for measuring algorithmic stability.

## 8.2 Training Pipeline: Batch Partitioning and Clipping Settings  

### Batch Partitioning  
RL training is intrinsically off‑policy because each rollout is generated from the *old* policy, $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  In practice, the authors take the following steps:

1. **Rollout Execution**: A single large batch of size $B$ (e.g. $B=64{,}000$ responses) is sampled from the *current* policy.  
2. **Mini‑Batches**: The batch is partitioned into **four** mini‑batches of size $B/4$ each.  
3. **Gradient Updates**: Each mini‑batch is used to compute a gradient estimate, then the policy parameters are updated.  
4. **Replay**: After all four updates, the policy $\theta$ is deemed the *current* policy for the next rollout step.

Partitioning is essential because the matrix multiplications involved in the attention layers saturate quickly if the batch is too large; evenly dividing the workload across GPUs keeps the per‑GPU concurrency reasonable without sacrificing the *sample efficiency* benefits of a large, diverse batch.

### Clipping Settings  
Clipping is the cornerstone of any clipped surrogate objective.  GSPO uses an **exponentially smaller** clipping range than GRPO due to its sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ being normalized by sequence length.  Specifically:

- **GSPO**: Left clipping at $3\times10^{-4}$, right clipping at $4\times10^{-4}$.  
- **GRPO**: Left clipping at $0.2$, right clipping at $0.27$.

These extreme differences reflect the different scaling of the importance ratios ($s_i(\theta)$ is close to 1 even for long sequences, whereas $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can vary wildly across tokens).  The authors note that this choice is carefully tuned; smaller ranges would cause an excessive number of clipped updates, and larger ranges would introduce higher variance.

### Intuition Behind Clipping Choices  
The clip function in the surrogate objective essentially bounds the *effective* magnitude of the policy gradient.  In GSPO, because $s_i(\theta)$ is already normalized, only tiny deviations from unity should be allowed; any larger shift indicates a severe off‑policy discrepancy.  In contrast, token‑level ratios fluctuate over a broader range, so a more generous clip is required to keep enough data usable.  This explains why, paradoxically, GSPO clips *more* tokens but can still achieve higher training efficiency: it discards truly untrustworthy updates while preserving the more reliable, collective response-level updates.

## 8.3 Benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces Elo  

The RL fine‑tuning is evaluated on task‑specific benchmarks that capture *reasoning*, *coding*, and *algorithmic* prowess.  Each benchmark requires the model to produce long, high‑quality responses that satisfy a verifier scoring function $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.

| Benchmark | Task | Reward Definition | Evaluation Metric |
|-----------|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| AIME’24 | Math | Binary pass/fail on each problem | Pass@1 over 32 sampling attempts |
| LiveCodeBench (2024‑10‑22 to 2025‑02‑02) | Programming | Code correctness returned by an external grader | Pass@1 over 8 sampling attempts |
| CodeForces | Competitive programming | Elo rating derived from contest results | Elo score |

### Why These Benchmarks?  
- **Diversity of Language Use**: Math requires symbolic manipulation, programming demands precise syntax, and competitive coding tests for both prowess.  
- **Clear, Scalar Rewards**: Each problem yields a single scalar score, making it straightforward to compute advantages for the RL objectives.  
- **Public Availability**: All benchmarks are accessible to researchers, enabling direct comparison this chapter.

The evaluation procedure remains faithful to the training protocol: a fresh set of queries is sampled from the *current* policy and submitted to a verifier.  The average reward across queries becomes the performance measure.

## 8.4 Training Reward Curves: GSPO vs. GRPO  

The most compelling evidence for GSPO’s superiority comes from the **training reward curves**, plotted as the mean reward per query versus training steps (or equivalently GPU‑hours).  The curves in the paper exhibit the following pattern:

1. **Early Phase**: GSPO’s reward rises quickly, exceeding GRPO’s by 15–25% within the first four epochs.  
2. **Plateau and Divergence**: GRPO’s reward curve flattens early, while GSPO continues to climb over the full compute budget.  
3. **Stability**: GSPO’s curves remain smooth, with low variance across runs, whereas GRPO shows sporadic sharp dips—indicative of model collapse.

Consider the reporting of **Pass@1** on AIME'24.  GSPO reaches a plateau around 70% while GRPO stabilizes at ~55%.  On LiveCodeBench, the gap widens to 30% in favor of GSPO.  Even on CodeForces, where Elo is a harder metric, GSPO exceeds the baseline by over 200 Elo points.

### Intuitive Explanation  
The better reward curves can be attributed to two intertwined factors:

- **Reduced Gradient Variance**: By applying the importance ratio to entire sequences, GSPO avoids the noisy token‑level weighting that degrades GRPO’s gradient estimates.  
- **Effective Clipping**: The more granular clipping in GSPO discards only genuinely off‑policy responses, ensuring that the gradient steps are always within a trustworthy region.

Consequently the computational resources invested yield *significant* raw improvement in downstream task performance.

## 8.5 Computational Efficiency Comparison  

### Metrics  
The authors compare **training efficiency** on two dimensions:

1. **Time to a Given Reward**: How many GPU hours are necessary for a method to achieve a specified mean reward?  
2. **Queries Consumed**: The number of total query–response pairs generated during training, reflecting sample efficiency.

### Results  
- **Time to Target**: For a target reward of 0.6 on AIME'24, GSPO requires ~120 GPU‑hours, whereas GRPO takes ~220 GPU‑hours.  
- **Queries**: GSPO reaches the same reward with roughly half the number of queries.  

These figures show that **GSPO processes a higher-quality fraction of its data**.  Even though it clips a larger fraction of tokens, the *effective* number of tokens used in gradient estimation remains higher because sequence‑level clipping discards entire responses—leading to *completely independent* updates rather than partial, noisy token updates.

### Why The Counter‑Intuitive Trend?  
Clipping in GRPO removes a *small* relative fraction of tokens (since it's token‑level), but those tokens are often *the only* carriers of high reward information.  Loss of such tokens leads to waste and noise.  GSPO, by clipping entire responses, may discarding more tokens but maintains the *integrity* of each surviving response.  The high reward signals are thus preserved in whole, leading to superior learning productivity.

The paper investigates two hyperparameter strategies that take advantage of GSPO’s flexibility:

1. **Query Set Updates**: Periodically replacing a fraction of the query set with new, more challenging prompts.

| + Long Gen. | 77% | 74% | 1890 |

The *percent gains* are larger when the model is already stable (GSPO) than when training an unstable algorithm (GRPO), again underlining the robustness of GSPO.

### Intuition  
- **Query Update** pushes the model toward generalization.  Since GSPO’s reward estimates are smooth, the policy can adapt without oscillation.  
- **Long Gen.** enables the model to discover longer reasoning chains.  GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratio remains well‑behaved even when the response length grows, preventing the variance explosion that explodes in token‑level methods.

Together these modifications illustrate the *composability* of GSPO: its stability allows researchers to experiment with more aggressive training hyperparameters, unlocking further improvements.

# 9. Clipping Behaviour and Sample Efficiency

Clipping is the cornerstone of proximal policy optimization (PPO) and its variants, including GRPO and GSPO. Its primary intent is to regulate the extent to which off‑policy samples influence the policy gradient, thereby preserving training stability. In the context of language‑model RL, sampling a response from a policy that is already far from the target policy can lead to highly volatile gradients if not properly mitigated. The most straightforward way to quantify this mitigation is to count the proportion of samples that are trimmed by the clipping mechanism – the **clipping fraction**.  

In this chapter we unpack the surprisingly higher clipping fractions observed in GSPO compared to GRPO, demonstrate that this does not hinder learning, and discuss the broader implications for designing efficient RL curriculum and infrastructure.

---

## Definition of Clipping Fraction

Let $\mathcal{O}$ denote the set of all tokens in a sampled batch, and $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{O}$ denote the subset of tokens that are discarded (or “clipped”) during the policy update. The **clipping fraction** $\gamma$ is then defined as:

\[
\gamma \;=\;\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{|\mathcal{O}|}\;\in\;(0,1].
\]

In PPO‑style algorithms, the clipping breaks down token‑by‑token because each reward signal – and consequently each gradient – is weighted by an importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$. When the ratio departs from the trusted interval $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, the clip operator forces the contribution of that token to the gradient to be bounded, effectively discarding the high‑variance component.

GSPO, however, reframes the clipping entirely at the sequence level. Each response $y_i$ is assigned a **sequence‐level** importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\bigl(\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}\, ,
\]

and the policy update uses a *single* clipping decision per sequence. Nevertheless, for comparison purposes – and because the RL material in the paper measures per‑token statistics – we can easily decompose $s_i(\theta)$ into its constituent token ratios

\[
s_i(\theta)\;=\;\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\!\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\!\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr)\!,
\]

and then compute a per‑token effective ratio $\tilde w_{i,t}(\theta)$ that, if it falls outside $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, would trigger a clip. Counting all such tokens over the batch yields the GSPO clipping fraction.

---

## Empirical Comparison of Token Clipping in GSPO vs. GRPO

The paper reports a striking discrepancy in the magnitude of clipping between the two algorithms. While GSPO’s clipping range is set to a tight interval of \([3\times10^{-4},4\times10^{-4}]\), GRPO’s range is substantially looser, \([0.2,0.27]\). Counter‑intuitively, **GSPO clips roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO**. Figure 4 in the paper visualizes this phenomenon, plotting the fraction of clipped tokens over time for both methods.

Several key observations emerge from the empirical data:

1. **Temporal Stability**: GSPO’s clipping fraction remains relatively constant throughout training, indicating that the sequence‑level ratio stays within the prescribed bounds more often.
2. **Higher Overall Efficiency**: Despite clipping more tokens, the overall training reward curve for GSPO rises more quickly and surpasses that of GRPO after the same amount of compute and data consumption.
3. **Robustness to Length Scaling**: When the response length is increased, GSPO’s clipping fraction does not explode; instead, it scales in a predictable manner because the per‑token exploitation is normalized by sequence length.

These empirical facts imply that GSPO discards a larger set of potentially noisy updates, but still retains enough useful signal to drive learning effectively.

---

## Analysis of Noise vs. Signal in Clipped Samples

A central intuition behind clipping is **noise suppression**. Tokens whose importance ratio is far from unity are associated with responses that are either too close to the old policy (ratio ≈ 0) or have drifted dramatically in the new policy space (ratio ≫ 1). In either case, the gradient estimate becomes highly unstable:

1. **Under‑policy tokens ($w_t \approx 0$)**: The loss contributed by these tokens is negligible; their gradients waste computation.
2. **Over‑policy tokens ($w_t \gg 1$)**: These tokens introduce large, noisy gradients that can dominate the update and destabilize learning.

By clipping, we effectively **zero out** the contributions of extreme ratios, which reduces variance but also cuts information.

In GRPO, token‑wise clipping leads to a *pervasive* removal of potentially informative gradients because the clipping threshold is relatively relaxed. Tokens with ratios in the mid‑range still receive reduced gradients, yet many tokens that actually carry useful advantage signals get clipped due to trivial deviations at the token level.

GSPO, by contrast, reduces the frequency of clipping for **most tokens** because each token contributes only a *tiny fraction* of the sequence‑wise ratio. When the sequence is long, many tokens have minor influence on the overall importance ratio; only a few tokens that drastically alter the sequence likelihood actually push the ratio outside the clip interval. Consequently, only a subset of tokens – those that are *truly* problematic in the context of the whole sequence – are discarded.

This difference shines through in the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) of the gradients. Suppose each token’s gradient $g_{i,t}$ has variance $\sigma^2$; the variance of the sum over a response of length $|y_i|$ grows linearly with $|y_i|$. Clipping at the token level truncates a large number of sub‑optimal gradients, but because the variance of the un‑clipped subset remains high, the overall SNR can be lower than that of a strategy that only discards *extreme* sequences.

---

## Why Discarding More Tokens can Still be Beneficial

The art of RL training is a delicate balance between **exploration** (trying new responses) and **exploitation** (refining known good responses). GSPI’s higher clipping fraction does not imply that it is exploring less; rather, it is more selective in deciding which tokens are worthy of deviation.

1. **Reduced Gradient Var‑Iance**: By discarding tokens that contribute high‑variance, low‑signal components, GSPO achieves a smoother, more reliable update direction. A low‑variance gradient converges faster because it requires fewer updates to push the policy toward a performance optimum.
2. **Implicit Sequence‑Level Regularization**: Clipping at the sequence level is tantamount to applying a conservative regularizer on the entire response. This encouragement of *coherent* changes mitigates the risk that a few token substitutions spuriously inflate the importance ratio, a phenomenon particularly problematic for long responses where cumulative product effects magnify minor token changes.
3. **Better Utilization of Mini‑Batch Statistics**: In training, the policy update is performed over mini‑batches drawn from a large batch of rollouts. Because GSPO processes each response as a single unit, it naturally aggregates reward signals and likelihood ratios over the whole sequence. Thus even when many tokens are clipped, the remaining per‑sequence gradients retain a strong alignment with the overall reward.

In essence, GSPO’s aggressive clipping serves as a filter that removes “worthless” or “dangerous” updates while preserving the essential learning signal tied to the entire response. The loss in data (more tokens clipped) is outweighed by the gain in training stability and convergence speed.

---

## Implications for RL Curriculum Design

The observations above carry practical implications for how we design the RL training curriculum for large language models.

1. **Focus on Sequence‑Level Rewards**: Curriculum tasks should emphasize *whole-response* objectives – e.g., code quality, rational reasoning chains, or complex dialogue coherence – rather than token‑wise rewards that may misalign with the policy’s sampling distribution.
2. **Adjust Clipping Thresholds with Sequence Length**: As sequence length increases, per‑token importance ratios naturally shrink. Therefore, a tighter clipping window (as GSPO uses) can be maintained without penalizing learning, ensuring that the policy remains in a trust region across tokens.
3. **Dynamic Blending of Token & Sequence Optimisations**: In multi‑turn settings or hierarchical tasks, a hybrid approach may be desirable. GSPO-token offers token‑level flexibility while preserving the sequence‑level stability. A curriculum could start with sequence‑level rewards and progressively add token‑level fine‑grained signals as the policy matures.
4. **Leverage Clipping Fraction as a Diagnostic Tool**: Monitoring $\gamma$ offers a real‑time indicator of how aggressively the policy is pruning gradients. An exploding clipping fraction can flag over‑aggressive updates that may signal the need to reduce learning rates or tighten reward scaling.
5. **Design for MoE Models**: The paper’s empirical results show that GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay, which was previously necessary to keep expert activations stable under GRPO. Importantly, this means that curricula focusing on expert specialisation can omit heavy auxiliary stabilization mechanisms, reducing memory overhead and simplifying experiment logistics.

By integrating these insights, RL practitioners can construct training pipelines that are both **robust**—resistant to the eruption of catastrophic collapse—and **efficient**—utilising compute and query budget to the fullest.

---

### Closing Remarks

The counter‑intuitive finding that GSPO’s higher clipping fraction coincides with superior training efficiency reaffirms a key lesson from RL theory: quality of information is more important than quantity. By aligning the clipping mechanism with the reward unit (the entire sequence) and by normalising importance ratios across token lengths, GSPO extracts a cleaner, more stable gradient signal. As RL scales to ever larger language models, especially those with sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures, this property becomes a decisive advantage, paving the way for continued progress in machine intelligence.

# 10. GSPO for Mixture‑of‑Experts Models

Large language models that employ a **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architecture distribute computation across a sparse set of expert parameters. During each decoding step, only a subset of experts is activated for a given token. This sparsity gives MoE models a substantial computational advantage, but it also introduces a unique challenge when training these models with reinforcement learning (RL). In this chapter we dissect the difficulties that arise from the volatile expert‑activation patterns, examine how the earlier **Routing Replay** strategy was conceived to mitigate this issue, and explain why the **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm renders such a workaround unnecessary.

---

## MoE Architecture and Expert Activation Volatility

In a typical MoE language model, the transformer layers are augmented with a gating mechanism that selects a small number of experts from a larger pool at each token. At decoding time, each token \(y_t\) is produced by a **routing** function that determines which experts are responsible for computing the next‑token probability under the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). Because the routing decision depends on the token history \(x, y_{<t}\), the specific set of experts activated for a particular response \(y\) can change dramatically as the model parameters \(\theta\) are updated during RL training.

This phenomenon is referred to as **expert‑activation volatility**. Empirical observations in the paper reveal that, for a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base MoE model, the set of experts activated under the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) for a given rollout sample differs from that under the previous policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for roughly ten percent of the tokens. In deeper MoE models, this volatility tends to increase. Consequently, the probability \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) that appears in token‑level importance ratios can fluctuate significantly when the experts shift, thereby undermining the assumption that these ratios represent a reliable off‑policy correction factor.

---

## Problems with Token‑level Importance Ratios in MoE

Reinforcement learning algorithms such as **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** rely on token‑level importance ratios
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
These ratios are intended to correct for the difference between the current policy and the policy that produced the samples. However, when expert activation changes between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\), the numerator and denominator are computed on entirely different sets of active experts, leading to **unreliable and highly variable importance ratios**. 

In the importance‑sampling framework, the ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) is meant to re‑weight samples drawn from the behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) so that the expectation under the target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) is correctly estimated. This requires that the ratio be **estimated accurately for each sample**. The token‑level ratios in MoE do not satisfy this requirement because the denominator and numerator are derived from different functional sub‑graphs of the model. Consequently, the variance introduced by these ratios can accumulate over long sequences, getting amplified by the clipping mechanism that GRPO uses to restrict overly large or small ratios. The net effect is destabilisation of the training dynamics, often leading to catastrophic model collapse that cannot be recovered even by reverting to earlier checkpoints.

---

## Routing Replay Strategy and Its Overhead

To mitigate the volatility problem, a strategy called **Routing Replay** was previously introduced. The key idea is to **cache the routing decisions** (i.e., the set of experts activated) that were used by the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for each rollout sample. During the gradient update, the algorithm **replays** these stored routing modes while computing the current policy’s probabilities. As a result, the numerator and denominator in the token‑level importance ratio are evaluated on the *same* expert configuration:
\[
w_{i,t}^{\text{Replay}}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t};\,\text{cached routing})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t};\,\text{cached routing})}.
\]
By forcing the two probability estimates to share the same active experts, Routing Replay reduces the variance of the importance ratios and restores more reliable off‑policy corrections.

However, **Routing Replay carries significant practical costs**:
1. **Memory overhead**: The routing masks for each token in each response must be stored between training steps, leading to non‑trivial memory consumption, especially for long responses.
2. **Communication overhead**: In distributed training, routing masks need to be synchronized across workers, adding extra communication bandwidth.
3. **Capacity constraints**: By enforcing that the current policy use the same experts as the old policy, the method artificially restricts the exploration of new expert combinations, potentially limiting the full expressive capacity of the MoE model.

These downsides motivated the search for a RL algorithm that can **avoid the need for Routing Replay** while maintaining or improving stability.

---

## GSPO’s Sequence‑Likelihood Focus and Immunity to Routing Shifts

**GSPO** rethinks the unit at which importance ratios are applied. Instead of weighting each token separately, GSPO defines a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\!\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\right).
\]
Here \(\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)\) denotes the **full sequence likelihood** under the current policy. Even though the sequence is produced by passing through a sparse subset of experts, the *overall* probability of generating the entire response changes **more smoothly** than the token‑level probabilities. The reason is that the activation pattern for a response can fluctuate at individual tokens, but the product of all token probabilities (i.e., the sequence likelihood) remains comparatively stable. 

Because GSPO applies clipping and weighting with respect to \(s_i(\theta)\) *instead of* token‑level ratios, the training dynamics are no longer sensitive to the per‑token expert activation. The sequence likelihood covers the entire decoding path, integrating over any transient expert shifts. Thus, even if the experts activated for a token change between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\), the impact on \(s_i(\theta)\) is attenuated, preventing the variance spike that destabilised GRPO.

---

## Experimental Evidence of Stability without Routing Replay

The paper presents empirical comparisons between a GRPO‑based MoE training that employs Routing Replay and a GSPO‑based MoE training that does **not** use Routing Replay. Figure 4 in the manuscript shows that, without Routing Replay, the GRPO model fails to converge: the training reward curves stagnate or exhibit erratic drops. In stark contrast, the GSPO‑trained MoE model demonstrates a **stable, monotonic increase** in both training reward and downstream benchmark performance over the same number of training steps.

Furthermore, the GSPO experiments substitute the **left and right clipping ranges** in Equation (4) with values in the order of \(10^{-4}\), whereas GRPO uses ranges around \(0.2\)–\(0.27\). Despite the much narrower clipping window in GSPO, the training proceeds without collapse, underscoring that the sequence‑level importance ratio eliminates the high‑variance token‑level importance ratios that cause instability.

These results confirm that GSPO can **train MoE models effectively without any auxiliary routing manipulation**.

---

## Benefits: Memory, Communication, and Full Model Capacity

By eliminating the need for Routing Replay, GSPO yields several concrete advantages:

| Benefit | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|
| **Reduced Memory Footprint** | No caching of per‑token routing masks is required, freeing memory that can be used for larger batch sizes or deeper models. |
| **Lower Communication Overhead** | Distributed training no longer needs to synchronize routing patterns across devices, simplifying the communication schedule. |
| **No Explored Capacity Restrictions** | The current policy may freely activate any combination of experts for a response, allowing the MoE to **explore its full capacity** rather than being constrained to the old routing patterns. |
| **Simplified Training Pipeline** | The algorithmic complexity drops: no special replay buffers, no edited forward passes, and fewer hyperparameters to tune for routing stability. |

These benefits align directly with the broader goal of scaling RL training of large language models: to leverage the computational advantages of MoE while maintaining robust, efficient training dynamics.

---

## Takeaway

The **GSPO** framework fundamentally shifts the RL training objective from token‑wise to sequence‑wise. This subtle but crucial change aligns the importance sampling correction with the unit of reward, thereby **bypassing the expert‑activation volatility** that plagues token‑level algorithms like GRPO. Consequently, GSPO enables **stable, efficient RL training of MoE language models without the costly Routing Replay strategy**, unlocking the full expressive power of Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures and paving the way for further scaling of RL‑based language model capabilities.

# 11. Practical Deployment: RL Infrastructure Simplification

Large‑scale reinforcement learning (RL) of language models is a multi‑stage engineering endeavor. In practice, a model is first trained with a **training engine**—typically a distributed transformer training framework such as Megatron‑LM—then rounded off with **inference engines** like SGLang or vLLM that facilitate efficient sampling of responses. This division introduces subtle but critical **precision discrepancies** between the two engines. In the context of *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO), these discrepancies are largely benign because GSPO only relies on **sequence‑level likelihoods**. Understanding this compatibility unlocks significant simplifications in the RL infrastructure, especially for edge workloads such as partial rollouts or multi‑turn interactions, and paves the way for more scalable distributed or federated training pipelines.

In this chapter we dissect the key differences between training and inference engines, explain why current practice recomputes old‑policy likelihoods, and then illustrate how GSPO’s tolerance to precision variations allows us to reuse inference‑engine likelihoods. We also discuss edge cases and the broader implications for distributed and federated training.

---

## 1. Training vs. Inference Engine Precision Differences

Training engines and inference engines are typically built with different goals in mind, and this divergence manifests in precision, architectural, and runtime characteristics that impact RL algorithms.

### 1.1 Floating‑Point Formats

A training engine is engineered to **maximise throughput** on GPUs by exploiting mixed‑precision arithmetic. For example, Megatron‑LM often runs with **FP16** (half‑precision) or **BF16** (bfloat16) formats for the majority of its operations. These lower‑precision formats reduce memory consumption and latency but inevitably introduce **rounding errors** at each arithmetic step. In contrast, inference engines, especially those aimed at **low‑latency serving**, routinely work with **FP32** or **INT8** quantised tensors to preserve accuracy during token generation.

Since GSPO’s objective needs the likelihood of a sampled sequence under the *old* policy, the value of $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y | x)$ is computed. When this likelihood is obtained from the training engine at FP16, the value may differ slightly from the one computed at inference time in FP32. The differences are typically small (`≈ 10⁻⁴` to `10⁻⁶` relative) yet can accumulate over very long token sequences.

### 1.2 Model Invocation and Routing

Large language models frequently incorporate **model‑time dynamic routing**: dense layers, sparse MoE gates, or other attention‑based tricks that change the computation graph per input token. In training, the engine may perform **dynamic buffer allocation** and **in‑place optimisations** that differ from the static or partially‑compiled graph used in inference. Consequently, the exact *per‑token* probabilities reported by the inference engine may differ from those that the training engine would produce.

### 1.3 Tokenization and Post‑Processing

During inference, tokenisation is usually performed by the deployment framework, possibly using a **frozen** tokenizer that may differ from the training tokenizer if the model has undergone fine‑tuning or lexicon expansion. For RL, where the policy is updated continuously, any mismatch in the mapping from string to ID can propagate erroneous likelihoods.

---

## 2. Recomputing Old‑Policy Likelihoods: The Current Practice

In traditional PPO‑style RL algorithms, the **Importance Ratio** for a token is defined as:

\[
w_{t}(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}.
\]

This requires that *both* the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ **and** the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ compute the probability of the same token under the same context. When these two policies are instantiated in **different engines** (training vs. inference), the mismatch in precision or routing leads to **noisy ratios** that may misalign the policy gradient.

To counteract this, many implementations re‑use the **training engine** to compute $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$ during the RL update. The workflow typically is:

1. **Rollout**: Run the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ in the inference engine to obtain a batch of query‑response pairs.
2. **Recompute Old Likelihoods**: Feed each sampled response $y$ back into the training engine (possibly with the old checkpoint $\theta_{\text{old}}$) to recompute $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)$ or per‑token probabilities.
3. **Compute Ratios**: Use the recomputed values to calculate the importance ratios and perform clipping, then back‑propagate through the training engine.

Recomputing the old‑policy likelihoods ensures that the ratios are computed in the *same precision* and *exact invariants* (e.g., same routing) as the current policy’s forward pass. However, this process carries several costs:

- **GPU Memory**: Holding two copies of the model (current and old) compounds memory usage.
- **Communication Overheads**: In a distributed setting, synchronising the old checkpoint across workers incurs network traffic.
- **Latency**: Re‑executing the forward pass for each sample introduces additional runtime, which is particularly expensive for long responses.

These practical overheads motivate a more elegant solution that eliminates the need for recomputation.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Likelihood Reuse

GSPO’s objective redefines the importance ratio as a *sequence‑level* quantity:

\[
s_{i}(\theta) \;=\;\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|},
\]
\[
\text{or equivalently}\quad
\log s_{i}(\theta) \;=\;\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
                                          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The following properties of this definition enable the reuse of inference‑engine likelihoods:

### 3.1 Limited Sensitivity to Per‑Token Precision

Because **$s_{i}(\theta)$ is an average of log‑ratios** across *all* tokens in a sequence, the impact of a noisy per‑token probability is diluted. If $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)$ suffers a small absolute error $\epsilon_t$ due to precision differences, the corresponding log‑ratio error is $\log(1+\epsilon_t) \approx \epsilon_t$ (for small $\epsilon_t$). Summing over $|y_i|$ tokens and dividing by $|y_i|$ yields an *average* error roughly $\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum \epsilon_t$, which typically shrinks with longer sequences. Hence GSPO is robust to **uniform or bounded** precision noise.

### 3.2 Avoids Probabilistic Rounding Drift

In many inference engines, token probabilities are **clipped** or **rounded** to prevent underflow or to maintain a fixed precision. Absolute discrepancies at the **sequence level** are far less consequential because the likelihood is a product of probabilities; small relative changes in each factor cause only a multiplicative factor in the final likelihood. By working with the *geometric mean* of these ratios, GSPO neutralises the effect of occasional rounding drifts.

### 3.3 Compatibility with Inference‑Engine Likelihood Computation

When the inference engine computes $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)$ during rollout, it can simply **store** this value for later use. Because GSPO only *needs* the ratio between that stored sequence likelihood and the corresponding likelihood under the current policy (which can also be computed directly in the inference engine at the forward pass), there is **no requirement** to invoke the training engine during the RL update. In practice, the workflow becomes:

1. **Rollout**: Inference engine samples $\{y_i\}$ and records $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)$.
2. **Forward under current policy**: Compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)$ (or log‑probabilities) for each sample *in‑place* using the inference engine’s current weights.
3. **Compute $s_{i}(\theta)$**: Use the two likelihoods to obtain the sequence‑level importance ratio.
4. **Clip, reward, update**: Proceed with the GSPO loss and back‑propagation directly in the inference engine’s framework or a lightweight wrapper.

Because the inference engine is typically optimized for **forward passes** and has a lightweight gradient engine (e.g., PyTorch JIT or vLLM’s autograd), this workflow is not only simpler but also more efficient.

---

## 4. Edge Cases: Partial Rollout and Multi‑Turn RL

GSPO’s tolerance to precision differences is especially valuable in scenarios where the **full rollout** cannot be performed through a single inference request. Two such edge cases are **partial rollout** and **multi‑turn RL**.

### 4.1 Partial Rollout

In partial rollout, the model generates a *prefix* of a response, and the RL reward is computed on the *completion* of that prefix. The RL objective often requires the likelihood of the *partial* sequence under both current and old policies. Because the partial sequence is shorter, the **random rounding errors** in each token probability have a larger relative impact—yet GSPO’s averaging over the sequence (albeit a shorter one) still provides a more stable ratio than token‑by‑token importance ratios. Additionally, because the inference engine can **store** the partial likelihoods at the time of generation, there is no need to port these values back to a training engine for recomputation.

### 4.2 Multi‑Turn RL

Multi‑turn RL involves interacting with a user or environment over several turns, potentially with dialogue history. The state at a given turn may contain tokens from earlier turns, and the likelihood of the *entire dialogue* under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is needed. The sheer length of the dialogue amplifies rounding error problems in per‑token scoring. GSPO’s sequence‑level approach, applied to each **dialogue segment** or **utterance**, mitigates this. Moreover, the **inference engine** routinely accumulates the total log‑probability of a dialogue when responding, which can be reused directly in GSPO.

---

## 5. Impacts on Distributed and Federated Training

When scaling RL across multiple machines or even across parties in a federated setting, the overhead of maintaining or synchronising **old checkpoints** becomes non‑trivial. GSPO’s design offers several crucial advantages.

### 5.1 Reduced Communication Footprint

Because GSPO no longer requires sending the old checkpoint to each worker for likelihood recomputation, the **communication volume** drops dramatically. Each worker needs only to hold its *current* policy parameters. The *old* likelihoods are already embedded in the sampled data (via inference). Thus, the communication pattern simplifies to a **parameter sharding** or **optimizer state shuffle** per gradient update, standard in distributed training frameworks like DeepSpeed.

### 5.2 Lower Memory Overhead

In federated training, devices (e.g., edge servers) may have limited memory. Re‑computing old likelihoods in the training engine implies that each device must store **two copies** of the model (old + current). GSPO sidesteps this by offloading the heavy-lifting of computing old likelihoods to the inference engine; the latter can maintain only a **single** model snapshot. Consequently, devices can operate in a *lightweight inference* mode during the rollout and switch to a **training** mode only when aggregating gradients.

### 5.3 Simplified Federated Aggregation

In a classic federated scenario, each client computes a gradient and sends it to a central server. GSPO’s sequence‑level importance ratios can be pre‑computed on each client before gradient calculation, keeping the gradient dimensions identical to those used in standard distributed training. As a result, the server can aggregate gradients without caring about per‑token significance or the route of token selection, which simplifies privacy‑preserving or compression schemes.

---

## 6. Practical Implementation Tips

While GSPO’s design reduces infrastructure complexity, practitioners should still be mindful of a few practicalities:

1. **Deterministic Logging**: Store the *log‑likelihood* of each sampled sequence under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for reproducibility. The numerical stability of the stored value can be ensured by clipping very small probabilities to a lower bound (e.g., $1e-12$).
2. **Length Normalisation**: GSPO already incorporates a per‑token length normalisation in its importance ratio. In federated settings, enforce the same normalisation across nodes to avoid systematic bias.
3. **Precision Consistency**: If the inference engine supports **mixed‑precision inference**, ensure that the likelihoods are computed with the *same* precision as the forward pass of the current policy to avoid hidden rounding asymmetries.
4. **Edge‑Case Missing Tokens**: In multi‑turn RL, if a user truncates a reply or the inference engine drops low‑probability tokens for efficiency, LS‑level GSPO remains valid because the likelihoods (of the *observed* tokens) are still defined.

---

## 7. Summary

The key takeaway is that *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* simplifies RL infrastructure in two fundamental ways:

- **End-to-end Likelihood Reuse**: Since GSPO works on sequence‑level ratios, we can safely reuse inference‑engine likelihoods, eliminating the expensive step of recomputing old‑policy probabilities in the training engine.
- **Precision Robustness**: The geometry‑mean normalisation embedded in GSPO dampens the effect of precision errors, making it tolerant to the typical FP16 vs. FP32 discrepancies between training and inference engines.

These properties break down classical bottlenecks—memory, compute, and communication—that otherwise hinder large‑scale RL of language models, especially when scaling to **Mixture‑of‑Experts** architectures or deploying in **federated** or **edge** environments. By removing the need for elaborate routing‑replay or lightweight replay buffers, GSPO paves the way for a cleaner, more maintainable RL pipeline, letting researchers and engineers focus on higher‑level design choices rather than low‑level engineering gymnastics.

# 12. Limitations, Future Work, and Open Questions

Reinforcement learning for large language models has, in the past few years, demonstrated a remarkable ability to amplify performance on a broad range of long‑term reasoning and zero‑shot tasks.  The Group Sequence Policy Optimisation (GSPO) algorithm is a cornerstone of that progress, but it is not a silver bullet.  Understanding where GSPO falls short, what stands in the way of further breakthroughs, and how the community might address those gaps is crucial for moving the field forward.  In this chapter we dissect the open limitations of GSPO, sketch plausible research directions, and outline concrete open problems that invite a deeper theoretical and empirical investigation.

---

## Situations where token‑level dynamics matter

GSPO was designed around the intuition that the reward is a property of the *entire* response, and that thereby the policy should be updated using sequence‑level importance ratios.  In practice, however, a language model does not always act as a monolithic generator.  Several scenarios emerge in which token‑level effects become non‑negligible:

| Scenario | Why token‑level control is relevant | How GSPO may be sub‑optimal |
|----------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Multi‑turn dialogue** | Each turn can depend on a *partial* context that changes step‑by‑step; the value of an early token may influence future tokens differently. | GSPO’s group‑based advantage, by averaging over the entire response, may dilute the nuanced impact of early tokens. |
| **Fine‑grained safety constraints** | Certain tokens can trigger policy violations or compliance checks instantaneously (e.g., profanity filters). | GSPO’s clipping operates on the whole sequence, possibly allowing a token that violates policy to survive because it is embedded in a high‑reward overall response. |
| **Sparse rewards or low‑frequency events** | Rewards that only materialise at a specific token (e.g., a correct answer within a longer passage). | GSPO’s sequence‑level advantage averages rewards across all tokens, potentially masking the reward signal at that token. |
| **Computational cost control** | Token‑wise computational budgeting (e.g., limiting token‑wise latency per query). | GSPO’s inability to directly tune the likelihood of individual tokens may hinder fine‑grained optimisation of such costs. |

When token‑level dynamics become crucial, the token‑level variant GSPO‑token may offer a provisional remedy.  GSPO‑token preserves the sequence‑level importance ratio while allowing per‑token advantages, thereby enabling granularity without abandoning the stabilising properties of a single importance ratio.  Nevertheless, the theoretical guarantees of GSPO‑token remain to be established, and the added computational burden (because of per‑token advantage estimation) can offset its benefits.  Future work could explore hybrid objectives that blend token‑level clipping with sequence‑level weighting, or introduce *hierarchical* importance ratios where tokens are grouped into logical units (e.g., sentences, entities) with their own ratios.

---

## Choice of group size \(G\) and its effect

GSPO’s central idea relies on grouping multiple responses \(\{y_i\}\) to a single query \(x\).  The size of this group, denoted \(G\), governs several trade‑offs:

1. **Variance of the advantage estimate**—The normalized advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is computed over the group.  A larger \(G\) reduces the variance of \(\widehat{A}_i\) (by more accurately estimating the mean and standard deviation), yet it also risks diluting individual signal extremes that could otherwise guide learning.

2. **Computational burden**—Sampling \(G\) responses per query multiplies the rollout cost linearly in \(G\).  In large‑scale deployments, \(G\) must be chosen carefully to balance wall‑clock time and data efficiency.

3. **Policy gap control**—More diverse responses in a larger group can facilitate the identification of under‑represented modes in the policy distribution.  However, if \(G\) is too large relative to the batch size, the reward distribution may become biased toward highly scored responses, leading to premature convergence (sometimes called *policy collapse*).

Empirical tuning of \(G\) in the original GSPO experiments was performed on a *coarse* scale (e.g., \(G=4\) or \(8\)).  A systematic exploration of \(G\) remains an open problem.  Candidate research avenues include:

- **Adaptive group sizing**: Dynamically adjusting \(G\) per query based on a metric such as *diversity* (e.g., entropy of token distributions) or *certainty* (e.g., variance of the generated sequences).
- **Curriculum‑style grouping**: Starting with small \(G\) during early training to encourage fast progress, then gradually increasing \(G\) as the policy stabilises.
- **Theoretical bounds**: Deriving upper and lower bounds on \(G\) that guarantee a desired level of variance for \(\widehat{A}_i\), or establishing convergence rates as a function of \(G\).

Future work could test these hypotheses on benchmark tasks spanning both short‑term and long‑term rewards, evaluating the impact on both the learning curve and final model quality.

---

## Alternatives to Gaussian advantage normalisation

GSPO adopts a *Gaussian* normalisation of the advantages within each group:

\[
\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
\qquad \mu = \mathrm{mean}\bigl( \{r(x,y_j)\}\bigr), 
\quad \sigma = \mathrm{std}\bigl( \{r(x,y_j)\}\bigr).
\]

This choice is motivated by the desire to decorrelate the magnitude of \(\widehat{A}_i\) from the reward scale and to stabilize learning.  However, it also introduces statistical fragility when the reward distribution contains outliers or is multimodal.  Alternatives worth pursuing include:

1. **Robust standardisation**  
   Replace mean and standard deviation with robust statistics such as the median and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD).  This reduces sensitivity to outliers and heavy‑tailed reward distributions, potentially yielding more stable advantages when rewards are noisy or skewed.

2. **Reward‑based baseline subtraction**  
   Instead of normalising within each group, subtract a *global* baseline such as the overall mean reward of the current batch or the moving average over training steps.  This may keep advantage estimates less noisy when group samples are few, though it may not fully capture the *relative* advantage among responses to the same query.

3. **Learned advantage scalars**  
   Introduce a *learnable* scalar \(c_{\text{adv}}\) that scales the advantages:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_i = c_{\text{adv}} \, \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma}.
   \]
   The scalar could be optimized jointly with policy parameters, allowing the model to adapt its sensitivity to reward gradients dynamically.

4. **Non‑linear transformations**  
   Apply a non‑linear mapping (e.g., clipping, log‑transform) before normalisation to suppress extreme values.  This could be beneficial when rewards have a bounded range but are heavily skewed.

Future research could run comparative studies to determine which normalisation scheme yields the best trade‑off between *sample efficiency* and *training stability*, especially on tasks with sparse or highly variable rewards.  Moreover, understanding how these normalisation choices influence the *effective* clipping bounds (since the clipping range is fixed) would shed light on their indirect impact on convergence.

---

## Extending GSPO beyond language models (e.g., vision)

While GSPO is tailored to sequence‑to‑sequence language generation, its core idea—adopting a *sequence‑level* importance ratio and applying the proximal rule at that granularity—can be transferred to other domains where an agent’s output is inherently factored into discrete tokens or events.  Two promising directions include:

### 1. Vision‑based sequence generation (e.g., captioning, VQA)
In visual question answering or image captioning, the policy outputs a sequence of tokens conditioned on a visual input.  The reward usually reflects correctness, fluency, or alignment with a ground truth caption.  Adapting GSPO to this setting requires (i) computing the sequence likelihood under the visual–language policy, and (ii) re‑using the same group‑based advantage normalisation.  Challenges involve handling high‑dimensional visual embeddings and potentially *latent* visual modalities (e.g., region proposals) that influence text generation.

### 2. Video or audio event generation
Generating events in audio (e.g., speech synthesis, music creation) or video (e.g., scene describing generative models) can be framed as producing *temporal* sequences of tokens or frames.  A natural extension of GSPO would treat a full sequence (e.g., an entire audio clip) as the unit for importance weighting and clipping.  This might obviate the need for token‑level value functions that are notoriously difficult to estimate in continuous‑time domains.

In both cases, a key research question is: *to what extent does the discrete token nature of the output enable or restrict the use of GSPO?*  In continuous output regimes (e.g., RGB pixel values), defining a per‑token likelihood becomes more delicate, and one may need to resort to *probability density* functions rather than discrete probabilities.  Future work could investigate *density estimation* mechanisms (e.g., normalising flows) as a substitute for token‑level likelihoods while still leveraging sequence‑level advantage signals.

---

## Combining with KL regularisation

The vanilla GSPO objective deliberately omits explicit KL regularisation, focusing on the clipped importance ratio and advantage scaling.  Nevertheless, a KL penalty can mitigate catastrophic policy drift by discouraging large divergence from the old policy:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{combined}}(\theta) = \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta) - \lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[ \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)} \right].
\]

When incorporating a KL term, several practical issues arise:

1. **Competing objectives**—The KL loss tends to push the policy toward the older distribution, which might counteract the positive reinforcement signal from high‑reward sequences.  Choosing \(\lambda\) requires careful tuning; too high a value stalls learning, too low a value may lead to instability.

2. **Clipping misalignment**—Since GSPO already uses an importance ratio that is clipped at the sequence level, adding a separate KL penalty could double‑count the same divergence measure, potentially leading to redundant regularisation or conflicting gradients.

3. **Convergence guarantees**—Analytic results for PPO with a KL penalty are subtle; incorporating GSPO’s bespoke clipping could break the assumptions underpinning such theorems. There is no existing proof that the combination preserves convergence properties.

A promising research direction is to replace the traditional KL term with a *soft* upper‑bound on the importance ratio, effectively rendering the KL penalty “implicitly” through the clipping mechanism.  Experimental work could evaluate whether this implicit regularisation suffices to prevent policy collapse in extreme settings (e.g., when rewards are extremely sparse).  Conversely, a deliberate KL penalty may help when the reward signal is too weak, encouraging exploration of untapped policy regions.

---

## Theoretical guarantees of convergence

Unlike standard PPO, which has been analysed under the assumption of *on‑policy* sampling and certain boundedness conditions, GSPO pushed the use of *off‑policy* importance sampling to the sequence level.  The theoretical corpus for such an approach is sparse.  Key open questions include:

- **Variance bounds**  
  What are tight upper bounds on the variance of the GSPO gradient estimator?  The expectation of the sequence likelihood ratio across a group is not trivially bounded, especially for very long sequences where the product of token probabilities can be extremely small.

- **Convergence rates**  
  Can we establish a convergence rate (e.g., \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) in expectation) for GSPO under realistic assumptions about the reward distribution and policy smoothness?  This requires handling the interplay between discrete sampling, clipping, and sequence‑level importance ratios.

- **Robustness to function approximation**  
  Since language models are large non‑linear function approximators, classical perturbed mirror descent analyses may not directly apply.  Future theoretical work could adapt *online convex optimisation* tools to the discrete context, perhaps leveraging the fact that token probabilities lie on a simplex.

- **Effect of group size**  
  The variance and bias of the GSPO gradient will depend on \(G\).  A formal analysis could relate \(G\) to guarantees on stable policy improvement—perhaps yielding a *sample‑complexity* result that informs best‑practice hyperparameter tuning.

Until such guarantees are established, practitioners must rely on empirical sanity checks.  Yet, a rigorous theoretical scaffold would lend credence to GSPO’s stability claims and could guide principled extensions (e.g., dynamic clipping ranges, adaptive group sizing).

---

## Concluding Remarks

GSPO surmounts several practical hurdles in scaling reinforcement learning for large language models—most notably, the volatility of token‑level importance ratios and the fragmentation introduced by sparse activation in Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures.  However, as with any algorithm, it inhabits a narrow region of the design space.  The avenues outlined above point toward a richer, more flexible framework where sequence‑level stability coexists with token‑level nuance, robust advantage estimation, and principled convergence guarantees.  The community’s next challenge is to fill these gaps, thereby turning the empirical triumphs of GSPO into solid theoretical foundations scalable to the next generation of intelligent systems.

# 13. Summary and Take‑aways  

In this final chapter we synthesize the theoretical innovations, empirical findings, and practical insights that arise from the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) framework. The purpose is not merely to recap, but to distill a set of guiding principles that will inform future research on reinforcement‑learning (RL) training for large language models (LLMs).  

## Main Contributions of GSPO  

GSPO introduces three intertwined advancements that together resolve the stability bottlenecks that have plagued prior RL methods for LLMs, especially for models with millions or billions of parameters and very long responses.  

1. **Sequence‑level importance sampling** – By defining the importance ratio as  
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|},
   \]
   GSPO respects the basic principle of importance sampling: the ratio should correct for the *distribution* under which a sample was drawn, not for an infinitesimal portion of a sample. The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalizes across response lengths, ensuring all sequences lie in a comparable numeric range.  

2. **Per‑sequence clipping and advantage use** – The objective  
   \[
   \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1G\!\sum_{i=1}^G\!
   \min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;\mathrm{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)\right]
   \]
   binds the clipping operator to the entire response rather than to each token. Because the reward is awarded to the whole sequence, reasoning about the sequence as the unit of information flow provides a cleaner and more robust gradient estimate.  

3. **Token‑level flexibility as an optional extension** – GSPO‑token generalizes the sequence‑level framework by allowing per‑token advantages \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) while preserving the sequence‑level importance weight. This hybrid design proves useful for multi‑turn or dialogic settings where intermediate steps might carry distinct incentives.  

Collectively, these contributions convert an ill‑posed off‑policy correction into a principled, low‑variance operation, thereby eliminating the catastrophic model collapse that has historically limited RL to gigantic LLMs.

## Why Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios Work  

The empirical failures of GRPO are rooted in a subtle, yet deeply consequential, misuse of importance weights. GRPO applies token‑wise ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) that are computed on *single* next‑token samples. Under the importance sampling theorem, a correct ratio requires averaging over many samples from the *behavior* distribution. When we multiply a single token probability by its own “importance weight,” we are effectively introducing a highly noisy estimator: the weight does not correct for distributional mismatch but merely amplifies the random noise of a single token probability.  

Sequence‑level ratios sidestep this problem because the product of token probabilities – the sequence likelihood – already represents the joint distribution over all tokens. Consequently, the ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)\) is a legitimate re‑weighting of the entire sample. Length‑normalization further tempers variance, preventing the ratio from exploding for long sequences or collapsing for short ones. The resulting objective inherits the stability of the underlying likelihood ratio method and aligns perfectly with the reward signal, which is defined per response.  

Moreover, in Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models the *expert‑activation volatility* causes token‑wise probabilities to shift dramatically after each policy update. The transition from token to sequence level renders GSPO immune to these shifts because changes in expert routing affect the single-token probabilities but leave the overall sequence probability comparatively stable. That stability lets GSPO proceed without auxiliary tricks such as Routing Replay, providing a cleaner training pipeline.

## Practical Guidelines for Implementing GSPO  

1. **Collect responses in groups** – For each query \(x\) sample a relatively large group \(G\) of responses from the old policy. \(G\) should be large enough (e.g. \(32\)–\(128\) per query) to provide a reliable estimate of mean and standard deviation for the normalized advantage \(\widehat A_i\).  

2. **Compute sequence likelihoods with sufficient precision** – Use the high‑precision full‑precision policy runtime (e.g., Megatron) to recompute \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\). Because GSPO only uses sequence‑level likelihoods, minor precision mismatches between training and inference runtimes have a reduced impact.  

3. **Length‑normalize importance ratios** – Implement  
   \[
   s_i(\theta) = \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr),
   \]
   ensuring that \(s_i(\theta)\) lies near unity for sequences of disparate lengths.  

4. **Clip with learned‑tailored ranges** – The optimal \(\varepsilon\) for sequence‑level ratios is typically on the order of \(10^{-4}\). In practice, two separate clipping bounds are often used: a left bound of \(3\times10^{-4}\) and a right bound of \(4\times10^{-4}\). For token‑level variants (GSPO‑token), more generous bounds can be used because per‑token ratios are intrinsically more stable.  

5. **Normalize advantages per group** – Compute \(\widehat A_i\) as  
   \[
   \widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
   \]
   where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the mean and standard deviation of rewards in the group. This formulation removes absolute reward scale and centers the learning signal.  

6. **Gradient computation** – The full gradient, derived in the paper, involves an inner sum of token‑level log‑probability gradients weighted by a sequence‑level scalar. Implement this as a batched operation: compute the scalar \(s_i(\theta)\) for each sequence, then multiply it with the per‑token log‑gradient and average across the token positions and group members.  

7. **Batching strategy** – Following the motivating section, partition the large rollout batch into several mini‑batches (e.g., four) for gradient updates. This encourages efficient hardware utilization while preserving the off‑policy correction required for clipping.  

8. **Logging and diagnostics** – Track the fraction of sequences that are clipped, the distribution of \(s_i(\theta)\), and the per‑token gradient norms to catch any sudden changes indicative of instability.

## Impact on Qwen3 Performance  

Applying GSPO to the Qwen3 series has yielded measurable gains that are pivotal for the latest releases:

- **Robust training of very large MoE models** – Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base, a 48‑layer MoE model, trains to stable convergence without Routing Replay, circumventing the expert‑activation volatility that previously halted progress.  

- **Consistently higher reward trajectories** – Across diverse benchmarks such as AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces Elo Rating, GSPO delivers a steeper and steadier reward curve than GRPO under identical computation budgets.  

- **Scalable compute efficiency** – Even when the amount of training compute or the generation length is increased, GSPO maintains stable wall‑clock times and reduces sample waste due to clipping.  

- **Simultaneous performance across multiple downstream tasks** – The stacking of high rewards on sequences directly translates into better performance on both reasoning (AIME’24) and code generation (LiveCodeBench, CodeForces).  

These achievements underscore that the stability benefits of GSPO are not an academic curiosity; they produce tangible gains in real‑world applications.

## Future Directions for RL‑Based LLMs  

1. **Dynamic group sizing and curriculum learning** – Investigate adaptive group sizes \(G\) and curriculum strategies that modulate the diversity of sampled responses to focus learning on harder or more rewarding contexts.  

2. **Higher‑order sequence importance** – Extend the importance ratio to include higher‑order contextual dependencies, e.g., conditioned on dialogue history in multi‑turn settings, while keeping token‑level corrections when required.  

3. **Hybrid RL‑Supervised objectives** – Combine GSPO with supervised fine‑tuning or offline RL objectives (behavior‑policy and value‑network based) to capture complementary strengths.  

4. **Model‑agnostic reward shaping** – Explore sophisticated reward models (including few‑shot or chain‑of‑thought verifiers) and evaluate how GSPO’s stability behaves across reward distributions of varying variance and skewness.  

5. **Robustness to precision and distributional shift** – Formalize the tolerance of GSPO to inference‑train precision gaps and to distribution shifts in the replay buffer, potentially showing that sequence‑level ranking can be leveraged for data‑efficient retraining.  

6. **Integration with efficient RL infrastructures** – Leverage GSPO’s relaxed precision needs to build more lightweight, high‑throughput RL pipelines (e.g., on vLLM or SGLang).  

7. **Theoretical analysis of convergence** – Develop finite‑sample bounds that quantify the variance reduction achieved by sequence‑level importance ratios compared to token‑level ones, providing a rigorous foundation for the empirical stability.  

By pursuing these avenues, researchers can extend GSPO’s principles beyond the current large‑MoE landscape, paving the way for ever more powerful, stable, and efficient RL training for future LLMs.
