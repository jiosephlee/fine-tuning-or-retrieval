# Beyond Euclidean Distance: Why Angles Matter More Than Magnitude in AI Image Generation

When we fine-tune large text-to-image diffusion models, we are walking a tightrope. On one side lies the need to teach the model something new, like a specific person's face or a particular artistic style. On the other side is the risk of "catastrophic forgetting," where the model loses its vast repository of general knowledge and ability to generate diverse, high-quality images. Traditionally, researchers have tried to balance this act by ensuring the updated model weights stay close to the original pretrained weights in terms of Euclidean distance. The logic seems sound: if the numbers don't change much, the behavior shouldn't change much. However, recent research suggests this intuition is flawed. A new method called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) argues that preserving the *angles* between neurons is far more critical for maintaining semantic meaning than preserving their absolute values or magnitudes.

## The Flaw in Measuring Distance

Most standard fine-tuning approaches, including simple gradient descent with a small learning rate or methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), operate under an implicit assumption. They assume that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{W}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ is sufficient to preserve the model's original capabilities. Mathematically, they aim to minimize $\| \bm{W} - \bm{W}^0 \|$. While this keeps the numerical values similar, it fails to account for the structural relationships within the neural network.

The problem is that Euclidean distance does not fully capture semantic preservation. You can shift weights slightly in a way that drastically alters the directional relationships between neurons, effectively scrambling the semantic information the model learned during pretraining. Conversely, you can make significant changes to the weights that preserve these directional relationships, allowing the model to adapt to new tasks without losing its core intelligence. This realization led researchers to look beyond simple distance metrics and investigate the geometry of the weight space itself.

## Phase Over Magnitude: An Insight from Signal Processing

To understand why angles matter more than magnitude, it helps to borrow an analogy from signal processing and the Fourier Transform. Any image can be decomposed into two components: a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The magnitude spectrum tells us how much of each frequency is present, while the phase spectrum describes how these frequencies align with each other.

Remarkably, experiments have shown that if you take an image, keep its phase spectrum but replace its magnitude spectrum with random noise, you can still reconstruct an image that looks remarkably like the original. The semantic content—the shapes, edges, and objects—remains intact. However, if you keep the magnitude and scramble the phase, the result is unrecognizable noise. This suggests that the angular relationships (phase) carry the bulk of the semantic information, while the magnitude is secondary.

Translating this to neural networks, neurons can be viewed as vectors in a high-dimensional space. The "angle" between two neuron vectors represents their relational structure. If we treat these neurons as points on a unit hypersphere, their relative positions define how the network processes information. OFT posits that to preserve the generative ability of a diffusion model, we must preserve these pairwise angles, or more formally, the "hyperspherical energy" of the network layers.

## Introducing Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) puts this theory into practice by restricting how weights are updated during training. Instead of adding a small delta to the weights (which changes both direction and magnitude arbitrarily), OFT applies an orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ to the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. The new weights are calculated as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ satisfies the condition $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$.

An orthogonal matrix represents a rotation or reflection in space. Crucially, rotating a set of vectors does not change the angles between them. By learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix for each layer in the diffusion model, OFT ensures that while the neurons might rotate to accommodate new data (like a specific subject or control signal), their relative geometric structure remains identical to the pretrained model. This mathematically guarantees that the hyperspherical energy—a measure of neuron uniformity and relational structure—is preserved exactly.

This approach offers a principled alternative to heuristic constraints. Rather than hoping that a small learning rate prevents damage to the model's knowledge, OFT structurally enforces semantic preservation through geometry. It allows the model to explore new directions in its capability space without breakingthe intricate web of relationships established during pretraining.

## Stability and Efficiency in Practice

The benefits of focusing on angles extend beyond theoretical elegance; they translate directly into improved performance and stability. In tasks like subject-driven generation (creating images of a specific person from just a few photos) and controllable generation (guiding image creation with edge maps or segmentation), OFT has demonstrated superior results compared to existing methods.

Because OFT operates on a smooth hypersphere manifold rather than navigating rugged Euclidean terrain, it tends to converge faster and more stably. Traditional methods often require careful tuning of iteration counts; run them too long, and they collapse into repetitive outputs or lose subject identity entirely due to overfitting driven by magnitude drifts with incorrect directions . OFT , however , remains robust even after many training steps because it cannot deviate from its fundamental structural integrity . Furthermore , variants like Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning ( COFT ) add an extra layer of safety by limiting how far these rotations can stray from identity , offering precise control over flexibility versus stability .

Another practical advantage is efficiency . During inference , there is no computational overhead . Since multiplying by an orthogonal matrix results in another valid weight matrix , developers can simply merge $\bm { R }$ into $\bm { W } ^ 0$ before deployment . The resulting model runs at exactly same speed baseline Stable Diffusion unlike adapter based approaches which add latency every time generate image .
