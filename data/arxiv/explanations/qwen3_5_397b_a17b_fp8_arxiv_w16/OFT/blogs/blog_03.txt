# Total Control Without the Bloat: Introducing Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)

Large text-to-image diffusion models have revolutionized how we create art, capable of generating photorealistic images from simple text prompts. However, relying solely on text often leaves us wanting more precise control. What if you want to generate an image that strictly follows a specific sketch, a segmentation map, or a particular facial pose? This challenge, known as controllable generation, typically requires finetuning massive models to accept these new signals. The problem is that traditional finetuning methods often cause the model to "forget" its original generative capabilities or require cumbersome architectural changes that slow down inference.

Enter Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), a principled approach designed to adapt these powerful models to downstream tasks without sacrificing their core intelligence. Unlike existing methods that add heavy components or risk destabilizing the model, COFT imposes strict mathematical boundaries to ensure the model learns new controls while rigorously preserving its pre-trained knowledge. The result is a technique that offers superior precision and image fidelity compared to heavyweights like ControlNet, all without adding a single bit of computational overhead during image generation.

## The Problem with Traditional Finetuning

When we finetune a large diffusion model for a specific task, such as converting Canny edges into photos, we face a delicate balancing act. We need the model to be flexible enough to learn the new control signal but stable enough to retain its ability to generate high-quality, diverse images. Standard finetuning approaches usually update neuron weights using a small learning rate or add low-rank adapters like LoRA. While these methods reduce the number of trainable parameters, they lack a theoretical guarantee that the model's semantic understanding remains intact.

Existing strategies often rely on the assumption that keeping the Euclidean distance between the new weights and the original weights small is sufficient to preserve performance. However, research suggests that Euclidean distance alone fails to capture the structural relationships between neurons that encode semantic meaning. If the relative angles between neurons shift too drastically during training, the model can lose its grasp on concepts it previously mastered, leading to collapsed outputs or artifacts. Furthermore, methods like ControlNet achieve controllability by appending entire auxiliary networks to the base model. While effective, this significantly increases the parameter count and adds latency during the inference stage, making deployment less efficient.

## The Power of Angles: Hyperspherical Energy

To solve this stability issue, we must look at how neural networks store information. Empirical evidence suggests that the directional relationships between neurons—specifically the angles on a unit hypersphere—are far more critical for encoding semantics than their magnitudes. This concept is quantified by "hyperspherical energy," which measures the uniformity and pairwise similarity of neurons within a layer. If we can preserve this hyperspherical energy during finetuning, we can theoretically guarantee that the model retains its pre-trained generative abilities.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) leverages this insight by updating weights through orthogonal transformations. An orthogonal transformation rotates or reflects the neuron vectors without changing the angles between them. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pre-trained weight matrix, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that the new weights are $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal (satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$), the pairwise angles between all neurons remain exactly unchanged. This ensures that the hyperspherical energy of the layer is perfectly preserved, allowing the model to adapt to new tasks like subject-driven generation or contour mapping without forgetting how to create realistic textures and lighting.

## Adding Constraints for Stability: Enter COFT

While standard OFT successfully preserves semantic structure by maintaining neuron angles, it can sometimes be too flexible for highly sensitive tasks. To address this, researchers introduced Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). COFT adds an explicit boundary to how much the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can deviate from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. In essence, while OFT allows the neurons to rotate freely as long as their relative angles stay fixed, COFT restricts this rotation to a small neighborhood around the original configuration.

This is achieved by imposing an additional constraint where the norm of the difference between the learned transformation and the identity matrix is bounded by a hyperparameter $\epsilon$:
$$ \|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon $$
By enforcing this constraint, COFT ensures that the finetuned model does not drift too far from its pre-trained state, even in terms of magnitude and absolute orientation. This dual constraint—preserving pairwise angles via orthogonality and limiting total deviation via $\epsilon$—provides exceptional training stability. It allows practitioners to use larger learning rates or train for more epochs without fearing model collapse, effectively solving the hyperparameter sensitivity issues common in other finetuning techniques.

## Outperforming Heavy Architectures

The true test of any controllable generation method lies in its performance against established baselines like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. In rigorous evaluations across tasks such as Canny edge-to-image conversion, segmentation map-to-image synthesis, and landmark-to-face generation, COFT demonstrates remarkable advantages. Quantitative metrics reveal that COFT achieves higher control consistency scores than both adapter-based methods and LoRA. For instance, when generating faces from landmarks, COFT converges significantly faster, reaching optimal performance in roughly 8 epochs compared to 20 epochs required by LoRA.

Qualitatively, images generated by COFT exhibit sharper details and more coherent geometric structures. While LoRA often struggles to adhere strictly to segmentation maps or edge contours, and adapter-based methods can introduce blurriness due to their indirect conditioning mechanisms, COFT maintains high fidelity to both the control signal and the text prompt. Perhaps most impressively, COFT achieves these results with far fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet. Where ControlNet might duplicate large portions of network architecture to handle control signals, COFT modifies only a fraction of parameters within existing layers using efficient block-diagonal orthogonal matrices.
