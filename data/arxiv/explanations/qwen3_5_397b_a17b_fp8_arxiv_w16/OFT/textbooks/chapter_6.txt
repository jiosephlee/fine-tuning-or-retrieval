Chapter 6: # Conclusion and Future Directions

The advent of large-scale text-to-image diffusion models has revolutionized the field of generative artificial intelligence, enabling the synthesis of photorealistic imagery from textual descriptions with unprecedented fidelity. However, the sheer power of these pretrained models presents a paradox: while they possess vast semantic knowledge, directing them to perform specific downstream tasks—such as generating a particular subject in novel contexts or adhering to strict structural constraints like edge maps—remains a significant challenge. Traditional finetuning approaches often struggle to balance the acquisition of new capabilities with the preservation of the model's original generative diversity and quality. In response to this critical gap, this work has introduced Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), as a principled framework for adapting diffusion models. By grounding the finetuning process in the geometry of hyperspherical energy, OFT offers a robust mechanism to guide powerful models without destroying the intricate semantic structures learned during pretraining. This concluding chapter synthesizes the theoretical foundations, methodological innovations, and empirical successes of OFT, while delineating the frontier of open problems that define the next steps in this evolving research area.

## Summary of Contributions: Theory, Method, and Empirical Results

The primary contribution of this work lies in establishing a rigorous theoretical connection between the geometric arrangement of neurons and the preservation of semantic information in deep neural networks. Central to this theory is the concept of hyperspherical energy, which quantifies the pairwise relationships between neurons on a unit hypersphere. Previous finetuning strategies implicitly assumed that maintaining a small Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights was sufficient to preserve generative capability. However, we demonstrated that Euclidean proximity is an inadequate proxy for semantic preservation. Instead, we posited that the angular relationships between neurons—their relative positions on the hypersphere—are the true carriers of semantic meaning. Drawing inspiration from observations in signal processing where phase spectra (angular information) dominate magnitude spectra in defining image content, we hypothesized that a successful finetuning method must preserve the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. OFT achieves this by restricting weight updates to orthogonal transformations. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weight matrix and $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the finetuned weights are given by $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal transformations correspond to rotations or reflections, they strictly preserve the angles between any pair of column vectors (neurons) in $\bm{W}^0$, thereby guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains invariant: $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

Building upon this theoretical foundation, we developed a practical and efficient methodology for implementing orthogonal transformations within large-scale diffusion architectures. A naive application of orthogonal constraints would be computationally prohibitive; thus, we employed Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices differentiably. Specifically, we constructed the rotation matrix as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. To further enhance parameter efficiency, we introduced a block-diagonal structure to $\bm{R}$, reducing the complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ with shared blocks, where $r$ is the number of blocks. This design allows OFT to compete favorably with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) in terms of trainable parameters while offering superior structural guarantees. Furthermore, recognizing that pure orthogonality might occasionally allow for deviations that are too large for stable convergence in highly sensitive tasks, we proposed Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). COFT augments the orthogonality constraint with an explicit bound on the deviation from the identity matrix, enforcing $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This ensures that the finetuned model remains within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained weights, providing an additional layer of stability without sacrificing the core benefit of angular preservation.

Empirically, the OFT framework has demonstrated substantial improvements over state-of-the-art baselines across two distinct and challenging domains: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. In subject-driven tasks, akin to DreamBooth scenarios where a model must learn to generate a specific object from few-shot examples, OFT and COFT exhibited remarkable stability. While standard finetuning and LoRA often suffered from mode collapse or loss of subject identity after extended training iterations, OFT maintained consistent control over both subject fidelity and text prompt adherence even after thousands of iterations. Quantitative metrics utilizing DINO and CLIP embeddings confirmed that OFT achieves higher subject fidelity than competing methods while retaining comparable diversity and prompt alignment. In controllable generation tasks involving Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks, OFT converged significantly faster than ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. Notably, OFT achieved competitive performance with only a fraction of the training data required by adapter-based methods. We also introduced a novel control consistency metric to rigorously evaluate these tasks, revealing that OFT produces images that adhere more accurately to input control signals while maintaining higher visual fidelity. Crucially, unlike adapter methods that add computational branches during inference, OFT absorbs the learned orthogonal matrix into the original weights ($\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$), resulting in zero additional inference overhead.

## Open Problem 1: Scalability Challenges in Matrix Inversion

Despite its theoretical elegance and empirical success, the current implementation of OFT relies on Cayley parameterization, which introduces a computational bottleneck related to matrix inversion. The construction of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ requires computing $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ at every step of the optimization process. While this operation is feasible for moderate dimensions or when utilizing block-diagonal structures with small block sizes, it poses scalability challenges as model dimensions increase or if one wishes to remove the block-diagonal bias entirely to allow for full-rank orthogonal updates. The complexity of matrix inversion generally scales cubically with dimension size in naive implementations, although specialized algorithms can mitigate this; nevertheless, it remains more expensive than simple matrix additions or multiplications used in other adaptation techniques like LoRA or standard gradient descent updates. This reliance on inversion slightly limits the scalability of OFT when applied to extremely wide layers or when attempting to scale up to next-generation foundation models with billions of parameters per layer.

Future research must address how to accelerate this matrix inverse operation in a fully differentiable manner without compromising numerical stability or orthogonality guarantees. One potential avenue involves exploring alternative parameterizations of orthogonal groups that do not require explicit inversion during backpropagation or investigating approximation techniques that maintain high precision while reducing computational cost. Additionally，发展 iterative methods or hardware-accelerated solvers specifically tailored for skew-symmetric systems could offer pathways to more efficient computation solving $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\bm{x} = \boldsymbol b$. Resolving this scalability issue is essential for making OFT universally applicable across all layers of massive transformer-based diffusion models without necessitating aggressive blocking strategies that might restrict expressivity prematurely until necessary approximations are proven safe..
