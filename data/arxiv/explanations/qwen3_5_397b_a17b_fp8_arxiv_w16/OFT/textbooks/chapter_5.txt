Chapter 5: # Advanced Variants: Constrained and Re-scaled OFT

While the foundational Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method provides a robust mechanism for adapting text-to-image diffusion models by preserving hyperspherical energy, practical applications often demand finer control over the trade-off between model plasticity and stability. The base OFT approach ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons remain invariant through orthogonal transformations, thereby maintaining the semantic structure of the pretrained model. However, in scenarios requiring extreme stability or specific adjustments to neuron magnitudes, additional architectural modifications become necessary. This chapter delves into two critical extensions of the OFT framework: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which explicitly limits the deviation of finetuned weights from their pretrained values, and Re-scaled OFT, which introduces learnable magnitude coefficients to enhance flexibility without compromising the preservation of hyperspherical energy. These advanced variants address specific limitations of the base method, offering improved convergence properties and adaptability for complex downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable image synthesis.

# Motivation for Constraints: Balancing Flexibility and Stability

The design of any effective finetuning strategy hinges on finding an optimal balance between flexibility—the ability to learn new tasks—and stability—the capacity to retain the generative capabilities acquired during pretraining. Standard finetuning methods typically rely on small learning rates to implicitly minimize the Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$. While this heuristic often works, it lacks a theoretical guarantee that the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained model are preserved. The core insight behind OFT is that semantic information is primarily stored in the angular relationships between neurons rather than their absolute positions in weight space. Consequently, preserving hyperspherical energy, which quantifies these pairwise angular similarities, is more critical than minimizing Euclidean distance alone.

However, even with the orthogonality constraint inherent in OFT, there exists a risk that the learned orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ might deviate too significantly from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, especially when training data is scarce or noisy. Such large deviations, while theoretically preserving angles, could shift the model into a region of the parameter space where the pretrained knowledge is effectively inaccessible or distorted in practice. This phenomenon highlights a fundamental tension: while orthogonal transformations protect the internal structure of neuron relationships, they do not inherently restrict how far the entire subspace can rotate away from its original orientation. To mitigate this, it becomes essential to impose an explicit constraint on the magnitude of this rotation. By limiting the flexibility of the orthogonal transformation, we can ensure that the finetuned model remains within a trusted neighborhood of the pretrained model, thereby enhancing stability without sacrificing the structural benefits of orthogonality. This motivation leads directly to the formulation of Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which augments the base OFT framework with a rigorous bound on parameter deviation.

# Constrained OFT (COFT): Imposing Radius Constraints on the Hypersphere

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) extends the principles of OFT by introducing an additional radius constraint that confines the finetuned model within a fixed hypersphere centered on the pretrained neurons. In mathematical terms, while standard OFT seeks an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, COFT imposes a secondary condition that limits the distance between $\bm{R}$ and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. The forward pass for a layer in COFT is defined as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, subject to two constraints: first, $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$), and second, the spectral norm of the difference between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{I}$ must not exceed a predefined threshold $\epsilon$, expressed as $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$.

This dual-constraint formulation serves a distinct purpose compared to existing finetuning techniques. Traditional methods implicitly assume that smaller Euclidean distances correlate with better preservation of pretrained abilities, often enforcing this through tiny learning rates. COFT makes this assumption explicit and mathematically rigorous by defining a hard boundary on how much the coordinate system of the neurons can rotate. Geometrically, this can be visualized as restricting the movement of neurons on the unit hypersphere to a localized cap around their original positions. By doing so, COFT ensures that while the relative angles between neurons (hyperspherical energy) are perfectly preserved due to orthogonality, the absolute orientation of these neurons does not drift arbitrarily far from their initialized state. This approach effectively combines two powerful regularizers: minimal hyperspherical energy difference and constrained deviation from the pretrained model. Empirical evidence suggests that this explicit constraint yields superior finetuning stability compared to unconstrained OFT, particularly in tasks where overfitting or catastrophic forgetting is a concern. The result is a model that adapts to new control signals or subject identities while rigorously maintaining its high-fidelity generative performance.
