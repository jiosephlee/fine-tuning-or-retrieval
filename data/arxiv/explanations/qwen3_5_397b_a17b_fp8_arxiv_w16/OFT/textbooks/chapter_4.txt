Chapter 4: # Algorithmic Implementation and Parameter Efficiency

Transitioning from the theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) to practical application requires addressing the significant computational challenges associated with optimizing orthogonal matrices. While the core premise of OFT—preserving the hyperspherical energy of a pretrained model by applying layer-shared orthogonal transformations—is mathematically elegant, implementing this constraint within a gradient-based optimization framework presents non-trivial hurdles. Standard methods for enforcing orthogonality, such as the Gram-Schmidt process or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), are often computationally prohibitive when applied iteratively during the training of large-scale diffusion models. Furthermore, a naive implementation of a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix introduces a quadratic parameter complexity, $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, which can negate the efficiency benefits sought in parameter-efficient finetuning. This chapter details the algorithmic strategies employed to overcome these obstacles, specifically focusing on the adoption of Cayley parameterization for differentiable orthogonality and the introduction of block-diagonal structures to drastically reduce parameter counts. We will also explore advanced sharing strategies and provide a rigorous comparative analysis against Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to elucidate why OFT offers a superior trade-off between flexibility, stability, and computational cost.

# Challenges in Differentiable Orthogonalization

The primary obstacle in deploying Orthogonal Finetuning lies in maintaining the strict mathematical property of orthogonality throughout the stochastic gradient descent process. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ must satisfy the condition $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. In standard neural network training, weights are updated by subtracting a scaled gradient from their current values. If one were to apply this standard update rule directly to the elements of an orthogonal matrix, the resulting matrix would almost immediately lose its orthogonality, thereby violating the fundamental constraint that ensures the preservation of hyperspherical energy. Without this preservation, the semantic relationships between neurons established during pretraining would be distorted, leading to the degradation of generative quality that OFT aims to prevent.

Traditional numerical linear algebra offers several algorithms to project a arbitrary matrix back onto the manifold of orthogonal matrices, such as the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process or computing the polar decomposition via SVD. However, these methods are ill-suited for deep learning contexts for two main reasons. First, they are computationally expensive; performing an SVD or a full Gram-Schmidt procedure at every optimization step for high-dimensional layers creates a severe bottleneck that slows down training prohibitively. Second, and perhaps more critically, many of these classical algorithms involve operations that are not easily differentiable or require complex custom backward passes to propagate gradients correctly through the orthogonalization step. While recent research has developed differentiable versions of these algorithms, they often remain too heavy for practical use in finetuning massive text-to-image diffusion models where efficiency is paramount. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a parameterization strategy that inherently guarantees orthogonality by construction, allowing standard optimizers to operate freely on a set of unconstrained parameters while ensuring the resulting transformation matrix remains strictly orthogonal.

# Cayley Parameterization: Generating Orthogonal Matrices from Skew-Symmetric Inputs

To resolve the challenge of differentiable orthogonalization efficiently, OFT employs the Cayley transform. This mathematical tool provides a bijective mapping between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $1$, known as the special orthogonal group. Instead of optimizing the entries of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ directly, the method optimizes a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which satisfies the property $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The orthogonal matrix is then constructed dynamically during the forward pass using the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, this transformation mathematically guarantees that the resulting $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal without requiring any post-hoc projection steps. This approach allows the optimizer to update the independent elements of $\bm{Q}$ using standard gradient descent, confident that every intermediate state corresponds to a valid orthogonal transformation.

The elegance of Cayley parameterization extends beyond mere constraint satisfaction; it also facilitates stable initialization crucial for finetuning tasks. In OFT, it is essential that the model begins training exactly at the pretrained weights to avoid abrupt changes in generation quality before learning commences. By initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix ($\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$), the Cayley transform yields $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$. Since multiplying by an identity matrix leaves weights unchanged ($\ bm W = \ bm I  bm W^0$), this ensures that OFT starts precisely from pretrained model configuration $W^0$.
