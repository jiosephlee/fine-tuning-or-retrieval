Chapter 1: # Introduction: The Challenge of Controlling Diffusion Models

The landscape of computer vision has been fundamentally reshaped by the advent of large text-to-image diffusion models. Systems such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and Imagen have demonstrated a remarkable capacity to synthesize photorealistic, high-fidelity images from natural language descriptions. These models operate by learning to reverse a gradual noise-addition process, effectively denoising random Gaussian noise into coherent visual structures that align with a given text prompt. The underlying architecture typically combines a powerful text encoder, which maps linguistic inputs into a semantic embedding space, with a diffusion backbone that iteratively refines latent representations or pixel values. Through training on massive datasets comprising billions of image-text pairs, these models acquire a profound understanding of visual concepts, styles, and compositions, allowing users to generate complex scenes simply by typing a sentence. Despite these impressive capabilities, the interface between human intent and machine generation remains imperfect, particularly when the user requires precise, fine-grained control over the output that goes beyond broad semantic descriptions.

While text guidance is sufficient for generating generic imagery, it often proves ambiguous and insufficient for tasks demanding specific structural or identity constraints. Natural language is inherently imprecise; describing the exact contour of an object, the specific pose of a subject, or the intricate texture of a particular item using only words is difficult and prone to misinterpretation by the model. Consequently, relying solely on text prompts can lead to generated images that capture the general spirit of the request but fail to adhere to strict requirements regarding geometry, layout, or subject identity. This limitation necessitates the development of robust mechanisms to guide or control these powerful pre-trained models toward specific downstream tasks without compromising the rich generative priors they have acquired during their initial training phases. The central challenge lies in adapting these massive models efficiently while ensuring they retain their ability to generate diverse, high-quality images outside the narrow scope of the adaptation task.

To address the shortcomings of pure text guidance, researchers have identified two primary categories of downstream tasks that require enhanced controllability: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Subject-driven generation focuses on personalization, where the goal is to teach the model a new concept based on a small set of reference images. Given just a few images of a specific subject—such as a particular dog, a piece of jewelry, or a person—the task is to generate novel images of that same subject embedded in entirely new contexts dictated by text prompts. The critical requirement here is identity preservation; the model must learn to render the specific visual features of the subject while maintaining its general ability to compose scenes according to textual instructions. Conversely, controllable generation involves conditioning the model on additional spatial or structural signals alongside the text prompt. In this setting, the user provides explicit control signals such as Canny edge maps, segmentation masks, depth maps, or skeletal poses. The objective is for the generated image to strictly adhere to these structural constraints while still reflecting the semantic content described in the text. Both tasks本质上 boil down to the same fundamental problem: how to effectively finetune a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model to accept new conditions or concepts without erasing its pre-existing knowledge base.

The pursuit of an effective finetuning strategy reveals a significant dilemma balancing training efficiency and generalizability preservation. An ideal finetuning method must satisfy two competing desiderata. First, it must be training-efficient, requiring fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs to converge, thereby reducing computational costs and making adaptation accessible on limited hardware. Second, and perhaps more critically, it must preserve generalizability. This means the adapted model should maintain the high-fidelity and diverse generative performance of the original pre-trained model when operating outside the specific constraints of the finetuning task. If a model overfits too aggressively to the few subject images or control signals provided during training, it risks "catastrophic forgetting," where it loses its ability to generate varied content or adheres too rigidly to the training distribution, resulting in collapsed or unrealistic outputs. Achieving this balance is non-trivial because large diffusion models contain millions or billions of parameters, and naive adjustments can easily destabilize the delicate equilibrium learned during pre-training.

Current approaches to finetuning generally fall into two camps, neither of which offers a guaranteed solution to this dilemma. The first approach involves updating the neuron weights directly using gradient descent with a very small learning rate. Methods like DreamBooth exemplify this strategy, relying on the assumption that tiny incremental steps will adjust the model just enough to learn the new task without drifting too far from its original configuration. The second approach involves freezing the original weights and adding small auxiliary components with re-parameterized neuron weights, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). These methods inject trainable low-rank matrices into the network layers to facilitate adaptation while keeping the bulk of parameters static. While both strategies are simple and widely used, they lack a principled theoretical foundation for ensuring that pre-training performance is preserved. They implicitly assume that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pre-trained weights $\bm{M}^0$—expressed as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$—is sufficient to maintain semantic integrity. Consequently, practitioners are forced to rely on heuristic hyperparameter tuning, such as selecting extremely small learning rates or early stopping criteria, without a clear metric to quantify how much "semantic damage" has been inflicted on the model during adaptation.

The reliance on Euclidean distance as a proxy for semantic preservation is fundamentally flawed because it fails to capture the structural relationships within the neural network that encode meaning. A small change in weight values in Euclidean space does not necessarily equate to a small change in function if that change disrupts critical geometric properties ofthe neuron activations. There is currently no principled measure for quantifying whether finetuned weights remain capable if they drift structurally from their optimal configuration merely because they are close numerically in value space .
