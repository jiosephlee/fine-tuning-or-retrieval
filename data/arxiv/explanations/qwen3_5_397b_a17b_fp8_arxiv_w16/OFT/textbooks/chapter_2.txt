Chapter 2: # Theoretical Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Semantics

To understand the mechanics of controlling large text-to-image diffusion models, one must first revisit the fundamental nature of how neural networks encode semantic information. Traditional approaches to finetuning often rely on the intuition that keeping the updated model weights close to the pretrained weights in a Euclidean sense—minimizing the straight-line distance between them—is sufficient to preserve the model's original capabilities. However, this chapter argues that such a metric is fundamentally inadequate for capturing the structural integrity of a neural network's knowledge. Instead, we posit that the semantic power of a model resides primarily in the angular relationships between its neurons, a property quantified by a measure known as hyperspherical energy. By shifting our perspective from Euclidean proximity to angular preservation, we establish the theoretical bedrock necessary for developing robust finetuning strategies that adapt powerful generators to new tasks without erasing their pre-existing intelligence.

# Angular Features vs. Magnitude: Evidence from Autoencoders

The assertion that directional information is paramount to semantic representation is not merely theoretical; it is supported by compelling empirical evidence derived from simple architectural experiments. Consider the operation of a standard convolutional layer within an autoencoder trained on image data. In the forward pass, the output feature map is typically generated via an inner product between the input patch $\bm{x}$ and the convolution kernel weights $\bm{w}$. Mathematically, this operation combines both the magnitude (length) of the vectors and their relative orientation (angle). To disentangle which component carries the semantic load, researchers have conducted ablation studies where the reconstruction capability of the network is tested using only specific components of this interaction.

In a illustrative toy experiment involving a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images, three distinct methods were employed to generate feature maps during the testing phase, despite the model being trained solely on standard inner products. The first method utilized the full inner product as intended. The second method isolated only the magnitude information of the neurons, discarding their directional alignment with the input. The third method isolated only the angular information, effectively normalizing both inputs and weights to unit length before computing their similarity. The results of this experiment were stark: reconstructions relying solely on magnitude information contained virtually no recognizable semantic content, appearing as noise or unstructured artifacts. Conversely, reconstructions utilizing only angular information were able to recover the input images with near-perfect fidelity, preserving edges, textures, and object shapes.

This phenomenon mirrors observations in signal processing, specifically regarding the two-dimensional Fourier transform. An image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. It is a well-established fact that the phase spectrum, which encodes angular relationships between frequency components, retains the vast majority of an image's structural and semantic information. One can reconstruct a recognizable image using only the phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum, whereas an image reconstructed from only the magnitude spectrum appears unintelligible. Translating this insight back to neural networks, it suggests that modifying the "phase" or direction of neurons is the most effective mechanism for altering semantic output, while changes in magnitude are secondary. Consequently, any finetuning strategy aimed at steering a generative model toward new behaviors should prioritize manipulating neuron directions while rigorously protecting their relative angular configurations to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

To formalize the concept of angular relationships within a neural network layer, we introduce the metric known as hyperspherical energy (HE). This measure provides a scalar value that characterizes the collective geometry of neurons projected onto a unit hypersphere. Let us consider a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each column $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents an individual neuron. To analyze purely angular properties, we first normalize each neuron vector to lie on the surface of a unit hypersphere. We denote the normalized $i$-th neuron as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of pairwise interactions between all distinct neurons in the layer, measured by their Euclidean distance on this unit hypersphere. Specifically, for a layer with $n$ neurons, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is calculated as:

$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$

Here, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ represents the chordal distance between two normalized neurons on the hypersphere. Because these vectors are normalized, this distance is directly related to their cosine similarity; vectors pointing in similar directions have small distances (resulting in large inverse values), while vectors pointing in opposite directions have large distances (resulting in small inverse values). Essentially, HE aggregates these repulsive forces across all pairs of neurons. A high hyperspherical energy indicates that many neurons are clustered closely together in direction space, implying redundancy or lack of diversity in feature representation. Conversely, a low hyperspherical energy suggests that neurons are spread out uniformly across the hypersphere.

It is crucial to recognize that HE is invariant to scaling operations on individual neurons or global rotations/reflections applied uniformly to all neurons within a layer because it relies exclusively on normalized vectors and relative positions. This scale-invariance distinguishes it sharply from traditional norms like Frobenius norm or spectral norm used in regularization techniques like weight decay or spectral normalization (SN).
