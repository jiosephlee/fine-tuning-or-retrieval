\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why does preserving hyperspherical energy matter more than minimizing Euclidean distance in finetuning?
Question:
The paper argues that existing methods assume a small Euclidean distance $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$ implies better preservation of pretrained abilities. However, the authors claim that hyperspherical energy (HE) is a superior measure. 

I understand that HE is defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$, where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ are normalized neurons. But intuitively, why does keeping the sum of inverse distances between normalized neurons constant preserve 'semantics' better than just keeping the weights close to their original values? Does this imply that the relative angles between neurons are the primary carrier of semantic knowledge, while their absolute magnitudes or positions in weight space are less important?
Answer:
The preference for preserving hyperspherical energy (HE) over minimizing Euclidean distance stems from the fundamental insight that the **relative angular relationships** between neurons encode the semantic knowledge of a pretrained model, whereas their absolute positions or magnitudes in weight space are less critical for maintaining generative capability.

Existing finetuning methods operate on the implicit assumption that keeping the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ close to the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$ in terms of Euclidean distance ($\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0 \|$) ensures the preservation of pretrained abilities. The paper argues this is insufficient because Euclidean proximity does not guarantee the preservation of the internal structural geometry of the network. In contrast, hyperspherical energy, defined as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ are normalized neurons, measures the uniformity and pairwise similarity of neurons on a unit hypersphere. By ensuring that $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) guarantees that the angles between any pair of neurons remain unchanged.

The intuition behind why this matters is drawn from signal processing and empirical observations cited in the paper. Just as the phase spectrum of an image carries the majority of its semantic content while the magnitude spectrum contributes less to recognition, the **directions** (angles) of neural weights carry the primary semantic information, while their magnitudes are secondary. The authors demonstrate this with a toy autoencoder experiment where reconstructing images using only angular information yields near-perfect results, whereas using only magnitude information fails completely. Consequently, modifying the model to adapt to new tasks requires changing these directions slightly, but doing so arbitrarily (as standard gradient descent might) disrupts the delicate relational structure learned during pretraining.

Minimizing Euclidean distance restricts how *far* weights move but allows their relative angles to distort unpredictably, potentially destroying the "semantic map" of the model. Conversely, applying an orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ (where $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$) rotates or reflects the entire set of neurons in a layer rigidly. This operation provably preserves all pairwise angles and thus keeps the hyperspherical energy constant ($\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|=0$). This approach treats finetuning as adjusting the canonical coordinate system of the neurons rather than altering their intrinsic relationships. It strikes a specific balance: it offers enough flexibility to learn new tasks by rotating the feature space, but imposes enough regularity (via orthogonality) to prevent the catastrophic forgetting associated with unstructured weight updates. Essentially, preserving HE ensures that while the model learns to respond to new inputs, it retains the original geometric configuration that defines its ability to generate coherent, high-fidelity images.

### How does the Cayley parameterization ensure orthogonality without expensive operations like SVD?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the paper proposes using the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ to generate the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. 

My confusion lies in the practical implementation: 
1. Why does this specific formula guarantee that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$?
2. The formula requires computing a matrix inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. For large layers where $d$ is large, isn't this inversion computationally prohibitive during backpropagation? How does this compare to other orthogonalization techniques like Gram-Schmidt or SVD in terms of actual GPU memory and speed?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization guarantees orthogonality through a direct algebraic property of skew-symmetric matrices, and its computational cost is mitigated in practice by restricting the operation to small block-diagonal subspaces rather than inverting large dense matrices.

To address your first point regarding the mathematical guarantee, the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ ensures that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$ solely because $\bm{Q}$ is constrained to be skew-symmetric, meaning $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$. When you compute the product $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}$, you substitute the definition to get $[(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}]^\top (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top (\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. Using the properties that $(\bm{A}\bm{B})^\top = \bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top$ and $(\bm{A}^{-1})^\top = (\bm{A}^\top)^{-1}$, along with the skew-symmetry of $\bm{Q}$, the term $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^\top$ becomes $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ and $[(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}]^\top$ becomes $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})^{-1}$. Crucially, the matrices $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ commute because they are both polynomials of the same matrix $\bm{Q}$. This commutativity allows the middle terms to cancel out perfectly: $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{i}-\ bm {Q}) = \ bm {I} - \ bm {Q}^2 = (\ bm {I}-\ bm {Q})(\ bm {I}+\ bm {Q})$. Consequently, the entire expression simplifies to the identity matrix $\ bm {I}$, proving orthogonality without needing iterative projection steps like Gram-Schmidt or expensive decompositions like SVD during every forward pass.

Regarding your concern about computational prohibitivity for large dimensions $d$, the paper explicitly addresses this by noting that applying the Cayley transform to a full $d \times d$ matrix would indeed be inefficient. Instead of inverting a massive matrix, the authors propose representing $\ bm {R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix composed of $r$ smaller blocks, where each block $\ bm {R}_i$ has dimensions roughly $d/r \times d/r$. The inversion operation $(\ bm {I}-\ bm {Q})^{-1}$ is then performed independently on these much smaller blocks. Since the complexity of matrix inversion scales cubically with dimension, breaking a large problem into many small disjoint problems drastically reduces both memory usage and computation time. For instance, if you split a $1024 \times 1024$ matrix into sixteen $64 \times 64$ blocks, you avoid the prohibitive cost of a single huge inversion while maintaining the orthogonality constraint locally within each subspace. Furthermore, because $\ bm {Q}$ is sparse (block-diagonal), automatic differentiation frameworks can efficiently compute gradients through these localized inverses during backpropagation.

When comparing this to other techniques, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization requires sequential normalization steps that are difficult to parallelize effectively on GPUs and can suffer from numerical instability in deep networks. SVD, while robust, is significantly slower than solving linear systems for inverses and introduces discontinuous gradients when singular values cross, which can hinder optimization stability. The Cayley parameterization offers a smooth, differentiable manifold traversal that maps unconstrained skew-symmetric parameters directly to the orthogonal group (specifically the special orthogonal group with determinant 1). While this restricts the solution space to rotations excluding reflections (determinant -1), the paper notes empirically that this limitation does not degrade performance for finetuning diffusion models. Thus, the combination of algebraic exactness and block-diagonal localization makes Cayley parameterization superior in speed and stability for this specific application compared to explicit projection methods.

### What is the precise relationship between OFT's block-diagonal structure and LoRA's low-rank updates?
Question:
The paper states in Section 4 that OFT and LoRA represent two distinct ways to be parameter-efficient: LoRA uses low-rank structure, while OFT uses sparsity (block-diagonal orthogonality). 

If we set the number of blocks $r$ in OFT such that the parameter count matches LoRA's rank $r'$, are these methods approximating similar subspaces? Specifically, if $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal with many small blocks, doesn't this restrict the transformation to only mix features within those specific blocks? Doesn't this severely limit the model's ability to learn global correlations across channels compared to LoRA, which theoretically allows mixing across all dimensions via low-rank matrices $\bm{B}\bm{A}$?
Answer:
While it is tempting to equate the block count $r$ in Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with the rank $r'$ in Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) simply because they can be tuned to match parameter counts, the two methods operate on fundamentally different geometric principles and do not approximate the same subspaces. Your concern that the block-diagonal structure of OFT restricts feature mixing to local neighborhoods is technically correct regarding the immediate operation of the matrix $\bm{R}$, but this interpretation misses how OFT leverages the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ to achieve global expressivity.

The core distinction lies in how each method modifies the weight matrix. LoRA adds a low-rank update, modeling the new weights as $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, where the update term $\bm{B}\bm{A}$ explicitly lives in a low-dimensional subspace spanned by the columns of $\bm{B}$. In contrast, OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation, $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. As noted in Section 4 ("Connection to LoRA"), we can rewrite the OFT update as $(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$. While LoRA constrains the *update itself* to be low-rank, OFT constrains the *transformation matrix* $\bm{R}$ to be orthogonal (specifically, block-diagonal orthogonal). Even if $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$ has a sparse or blocked structure, when it multiplies the full-rank pretrained matrix $\bm{W}^0$, the resulting effective update can span a much richer subspace than a direct low-rank addition. The pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ act as a basis that "mixes" the localized rotations of $\bm{R}$ across all output dimensions, allowing OFT to influence global correlations despite the block-diagonal constraint on $\bm{R}$.

Furthermore, the goals of these constraints differ significantly. LoRA's low-rank assumption posits that task-specific adaptations lie in a small intrinsic dimensionality. OFT's block-diagonal orthogonality is designed to preserve "hyperspherical energy," which characterizes the pairwise angular relationships between neurons. The paper argues in Sections 3 and 4 that preserving these angles is crucial for maintaining the semantic generation ability of diffusion models. The block-diagonal structure is primarily a mechanism for parameter efficiency (reducing complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$) rather than a strict limitation on representational capacity. By rotating groups of neurons together, OFT adjusts the "canonical coordinate system" of the features without distorting their relative geometric structure. Empirically, Section 5 demonstrates that OFT often outperforms LoRA in controllability and stability, suggesting that the subspace accessed via orthogonal rotation of pretrained features is more effective for these tasks than the subspace accessed via additive low-rank updates. Therefore, matching parameter counts does not imply equivalent expressive power; OFT explores a manifold of orthogonal transformations that preserves semantic structure, whereas LoRA explores a linear subspace of additive perturbations.

### How is the epsilon constraint implemented in COFT given the non-linear Cayley transform?
Question:
In Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), the goal is to enforce $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. The paper mentions using a Neumann series approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ to move this constraint to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$. 

My question is: Is this approximation valid throughout the entire training process? If the optimization pushes $\bm{Q}$ to have a large norm, wouldn't the higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ become significant, causing the actual deviation $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|$ to violate the intended $\epsilon$ bound? How sensitive is the method to the choice of $\epsilon'$ versus the true geometric deviation?
Answer:
The validity of the Neumann series approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) relies entirely on the enforcement of the constraint $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ during the optimization process. The paper explicitly addresses your concern about higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ becoming significant by noting that the constraint on $\bm{Q}$ is enforced via **projected gradient descent**. This mechanism ensures that at every step of the training, the norm of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is clipped if it exceeds the threshold $\epsilon'$. Consequently, the optimization never allows $\bm{Q}$ to grow large enough for the quadratic and higher-order terms in the expansion of the Cayley transform $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ to dominate or cause a violation of the intended geometric bound.

The relationship between the proxy constraint $\epsilon'$ and the true geometric deviation $\epsilon$ is governed by the convergence of the Neumann series in the operator norm. The paper states that moving the constraint from $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon'$ is valid "under the assumption that the Neumann series converges." By keeping $\epsilon'$ sufficiently small, you ensure that $\|\bm{Q}\| < 1$, which is the necessary condition for the series $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{Q}^k$ to converge. In this regime, the linear term $2\bm{Q}$ is the dominant component of the deviation $\bm{R}-\bm{I}$, making $\epsilon'$ a reliable proxy for controlling the actual distance from the identity matrix. The paper empirically supports this theoretical framing in Figure 5 (referenced as Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft} in the text), demonstrating that varying $\epsilon$ smoothly controls the flexibility of the finetuning: smaller values keep the model close to the pretrained weights (behaving like a rigid constraint), while larger values allow behavior approaching standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). Therefore, the method is not sensitive to approximation errors blowing up, provided $\epsilon'$ is chosen to maintain the small-norm regime required for the series approximation to hold.

### Why does minimizing hyperspherical energy destroy pretrained semantics while preserving it helps?
Question:
The paper distinguishes OFT from previous work on orthogonal over-parameterized training (Liu et al., 2021), noting that while Liu et al. aim to *minimize* hyperspherical energy (making neurons uniformly spaced), OFT aims to *preserve* the existing energy. 

The authors state that minimizing energy could 'destroy the original semantic structures.' Could someone explain mechanistically why a uniform distribution of neurons on a hypersphere is bad for a *finetuned* diffusion model but good for a classifier trained from scratch? Does the pretrained diffusion model rely on specific clusters or non-uniform arrangements of neuron angles to encode complex image priors that would be lost if forced toward uniformity?
Answer:
The distinction lies in the fundamental difference between *learning* a representation from scratch versus *adapting* a highly specialized, pretrained one. When training a classifier from scratch, as in the work by Liu et al. (2021) referenced in the paper, the goal is to maximize generalization by ensuring neurons do not become redundant. Minimizing hyperspherical energy forces neurons to spread out uniformly across the unit hypersphere, effectively decorrelating them and maximizing the information capacity of the layer for random initializations. In this context, a uniform distribution is optimal because there is no prior structure to preserve; the network must discover efficient features de novo, and uniform spacing prevents "neuron collapse" where multiple units learn identical features.

However, this logic breaks down completely when finetuning a massive text-to-image diffusion model like Stable Diffusion. These models are not random collections of filters; they encode intricate, high-dimensional semantic manifolds learned from billions of image-text pairs. The paper argues that the specific, non-uniform arrangement of neuron angles—their pairwise relationships on the hypersphere—constitutes the core of the model's generative knowledge and semantic integrity. The authors draw a parallel to the Fourier transform, noting that just as the phase spectrum (angular information) preserves the structural semantics of an image while magnitude carries less critical detail, the relative angles between neurons encode the sophisticated priors required for photorealistic generation.

If you were to minimize hyperspherical energy during finetuning, you would actively force these carefully tuned, clustered neuron configurations toward a uniform distribution. Mechanistically, this acts as a destructive regularizer that overwrites the complex correlations established during pretraining. The "original semantic structures" mentioned in the paper refer to these specific angular dependencies that allow the model to understand concepts like texture, lighting, and object coherence. By forcing uniformity, you effectively scramble these relationships, causing the model to lose its ability to generate coherent images or adhere to text prompts, even if the individual neuron magnitudes remain intact.

Therefore, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) adopts a different strategy: it restricts updates to orthogonal transformations (rotations and reflections). Mathematically, applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ preserves the cosine similarity between any pair of neurons. Since hyperspherical energy is defined solely by these pairwise distances on the unit sphere, an orthogonal transformation leaves the energy invariant ($\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$). This allows the model to rotate its feature space to adapt to new tasks (like subject-driven generation or control signals) without distorting the internal geometric structure that holds its pretrained intelligence. In essence, minimizing energy is good for building a house from raw materials (scratch training), but preserving the existing architecture via rigid rotation is essential when renovating a masterpiece (finetuning).

### Clarification on the 'Re-scaled OFT' forward pass errata and its impact on HE preservation
Question:
In Section 3.5, there is an errata note regarding Re-scaled OFT. The corrected forward pass is $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\ bm{x}$, where $\ bm {D}$ is a learnable diagonal scaling matrix. 

The paper claims that re-scaling neurons does not change hyperspherical energy because magnitude is normalized out in the HE calculation ($\\hat {\\ bm {w}}_i = \\ bm {w}_i / \\|\\ bm {w}_i\\|$). However, if we scale individual neurons differently via $\\ bm {D}$, doesn't this alter the effective contribution of each neuron to the layer output before normalization? If HE depends only on angles, why do we need to learn scales at all? Is this purely for recovering magnitude information lost by strict orthogonality, and if so, does introducing $\\ bm {D}$ break any theoretical guarantees of stability provided by pure OFT?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction you identify stems from distinguishing between the *structural relationships* among neurons (which define semantic knowledge) and the *absolute magnitude* of neuron outputs (which affects signal strength). The paper argues that hyperspherical energy (HE) depends exclusively on the angles between normalized neuron vectors $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. Because the diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ only modifies the magnitude of each neuron column in $\bm{W}^0$ before the orthogonal rotation $\bm{R}$ is applied, the direction of the resulting finetuned neurons remains identical to what it would be without scaling, provided we normalize them for the HE calculation. Specifically, if a neuron vector is scaled by a scalar $s_i > 0$, its normalized version $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ remains unchanged, meaning the pairwise distances $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ and consequently the HE are invariant to $\bm{D}$.

However, learning these scales is crucial because strict orthogonality in the original OFT formulation ($\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$) preserves not only angles but also the relative magnitudes inherent in the pretrained weights. While angles encode semantic direction, the absolute magnitude of neuron activations influences the confidence and strength of features passed to subsequent layers. By introducing $\bm{D}$, Re-scaled OFT decouples the preservation of semantic structure (angles) from the flexibility to adjust feature magnitudes. This allows the model to recover or adapt magnitude information that might be suboptimal for a specific downstream task without distorting the critical angular relationships that prevent "catastrophic forgetting" of the pretrained generative capability.

Regarding your concern about theoretical guarantees, introducing $\bm{D}$ does not break the stability guarantees related to semantic preservation because those guarantees are explicitly tied to the invariance of hyperspherical energy, which, as noted, is untouched by rescaling. The stability provided by pure OFT comes from restricting updates to orthogonal transformations that maintain the "shape" of the weight distribution on the hypersphere. Re-scaled OFT maintains this shape while allowing radial expansion or contraction along individual neuron axes. The paper notes in Section 3.5 that this extension offers "neglectable" additional parameters but generally yields better performance empirically (as shown in Appendix A), suggesting that while pure orthogonality is sufficient for stability, adding magnitude flexibility improves the trade-off between retaining pretrained knowledge and adapting to new tasks. The errata correction ensuring $\bm{D}$ multiplies $\bm{W}^0$ before transposition ($\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D}$) simply ensures that each column (neuron) of the weight matrix is scaled individually, which is consistent with the goal of per-neuron magnitude adjustment.
