# Introduction: The Challenge of Controlling Diffusion Models

The landscape of computer vision has been fundamentally reshaped by the advent of large text-to-image diffusion models. Systems such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 2, and Imagen have demonstrated a remarkable capacity to synthesize photorealistic, high-fidelity images from natural language descriptions. These models operate by learning to reverse a gradual noise-addition process, effectively denoising random Gaussian noise into coherent visual structures that align with a given text prompt. The underlying architecture typically combines a powerful text encoder, which maps linguistic inputs into a semantic embedding space, with a diffusion backbone that iteratively refines latent representations or pixel values. Through training on massive datasets comprising billions of image-text pairs, these models acquire a profound understanding of visual concepts, styles, and compositions, allowing users to generate complex scenes simply by typing a sentence. Despite these impressive capabilities, the interface between human intent and machine generation remains imperfect, particularly when the user requires precise, fine-grained control over the output that goes beyond broad semantic descriptions.

While text guidance is sufficient for generating generic imagery, it often proves ambiguous and insufficient for tasks demanding specific structural or identity constraints. Natural language is inherently imprecise; describing the exact contour of an object, the specific pose of a subject, or the intricate texture of a particular item using only words is difficult and prone to misinterpretation by the model. Consequently, relying solely on text prompts can lead to generated images that capture the general spirit of the request but fail to adhere to strict requirements regarding geometry, layout, or subject identity. This limitation necessitates the development of robust mechanisms to guide or control these powerful pre-trained models toward specific downstream tasks without compromising the rich generative priors they have acquired during their initial training phases. The central challenge lies in adapting these massive models efficiently while ensuring they retain their ability to generate diverse, high-quality images outside the narrow scope of the adaptation task.

To address the shortcomings of pure text guidance, researchers have identified two primary categories of downstream tasks that require enhanced controllability: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Subject-driven generation focuses on personalization, where the goal is to teach the model a new concept based on a small set of reference images. Given just a few images of a specific subject—such as a particular dog, a piece of jewelry, or a person—the task is to generate novel images of that same subject embedded in entirely new contexts dictated by text prompts. The critical requirement here is identity preservation; the model must learn to render the specific visual features of the subject while maintaining its general ability to compose scenes according to textual instructions. Conversely, controllable generation involves conditioning the model on additional spatial or structural signals alongside the text prompt. In this setting, the user provides explicit control signals such as Canny edge maps, segmentation masks, depth maps, or skeletal poses. The objective is for the generated image to strictly adhere to these structural constraints while still reflecting the semantic content described in the text. Both tasks本质上 boil down to the same fundamental problem: how to effectively finetune a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model to accept new conditions or concepts without erasing its pre-existing knowledge base.

The pursuit of an effective finetuning strategy reveals a significant dilemma balancing training efficiency and generalizability preservation. An ideal finetuning method must satisfy two competing desiderata. First, it must be training-efficient, requiring fewer trainable parameters and fewer training epochs to converge, thereby reducing computational costs and making adaptation accessible on limited hardware. Second, and perhaps more critically, it must preserve generalizability. This means the adapted model should maintain the high-fidelity and diverse generative performance of the original pre-trained model when operating outside the specific constraints of the finetuning task. If a model overfits too aggressively to the few subject images or control signals provided during training, it risks "catastrophic forgetting," where it loses its ability to generate varied content or adheres too rigidly to the training distribution, resulting in collapsed or unrealistic outputs. Achieving this balance is non-trivial because large diffusion models contain millions or billions of parameters, and naive adjustments can easily destabilize the delicate equilibrium learned during pre-training.

Current approaches to finetuning generally fall into two camps, neither of which offers a guaranteed solution to this dilemma. The first approach involves updating the neuron weights directly using gradient descent with a very small learning rate. Methods like DreamBooth exemplify this strategy, relying on the assumption that tiny incremental steps will adjust the model just enough to learn the new task without drifting too far from its original configuration. The second approach involves freezing the original weights and adding small auxiliary components with re-parameterized neuron weights, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). These methods inject trainable low-rank matrices into the network layers to facilitate adaptation while keeping the bulk of parameters static. While both strategies are simple and widely used, they lack a principled theoretical foundation for ensuring that pre-training performance is preserved. They implicitly assume that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned weights $\bm{M}$ and the pre-trained weights $\bm{M}^0$—expressed as $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$—is sufficient to maintain semantic integrity. Consequently, practitioners are forced to rely on heuristic hyperparameter tuning, such as selecting extremely small learning rates or early stopping criteria, without a clear metric to quantify how much "semantic damage" has been inflicted on the model during adaptation.

The reliance on Euclidean distance as a proxy for semantic preservation is fundamentally flawed because it fails to capture the structural relationships within the neural network that encode meaning. A small change in weight values in Euclidean space does not necessarily equate to a small change in function if that change disrupts critical geometric properties ofthe neuron activations. There is currently no principled measure for quantifying whether finetuned weights remain capable if they drift structurally from their optimal configuration merely because they are close numerically in value space . # Theoretical Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Semantics

To understand the mechanics of controlling large text-to-image diffusion models, one must first revisit the fundamental nature of how neural networks encode semantic information. Traditional approaches to finetuning often rely on the intuition that keeping the updated model weights close to the pretrained weights in a Euclidean sense—minimizing the straight-line distance between them—is sufficient to preserve the model's original capabilities. However, this chapter argues that such a metric is fundamentally inadequate for capturing the structural integrity of a neural network's knowledge. Instead, we posit that the semantic power of a model resides primarily in the angular relationships between its neurons, a property quantified by a measure known as hyperspherical energy. By shifting our perspective from Euclidean proximity to angular preservation, we establish the theoretical bedrock necessary for developing robust finetuning strategies that adapt powerful generators to new tasks without erasing their pre-existing intelligence.

# Angular Features vs. Magnitude: Evidence from Autoencoders

The assertion that directional information is paramount to semantic representation is not merely theoretical; it is supported by compelling empirical evidence derived from simple architectural experiments. Consider the operation of a standard convolutional layer within an autoencoder trained on image data. In the forward pass, the output feature map is typically generated via an inner product between the input patch $\bm{x}$ and the convolution kernel weights $\bm{w}$. Mathematically, this operation combines both the magnitude (length) of the vectors and their relative orientation (angle). To disentangle which component carries the semantic load, researchers have conducted ablation studies where the reconstruction capability of the network is tested using only specific components of this interaction.

In a illustrative toy experiment involving a convolutional autoencoder trained on flower images, three distinct methods were employed to generate feature maps during the testing phase, despite the model being trained solely on standard inner products. The first method utilized the full inner product as intended. The second method isolated only the magnitude information of the neurons, discarding their directional alignment with the input. The third method isolated only the angular information, effectively normalizing both inputs and weights to unit length before computing their similarity. The results of this experiment were stark: reconstructions relying solely on magnitude information contained virtually no recognizable semantic content, appearing as noise or unstructured artifacts. Conversely, reconstructions utilizing only angular information were able to recover the input images with near-perfect fidelity, preserving edges, textures, and object shapes.

This phenomenon mirrors observations in signal processing, specifically regarding the two-dimensional Fourier transform. An image can be decomposed into a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. It is a well-established fact that the phase spectrum, which encodes angular relationships between frequency components, retains the vast majority of an image's structural and semantic information. One can reconstruct a recognizable image using only the phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum, whereas an image reconstructed from only the magnitude spectrum appears unintelligible. Translating this insight back to neural networks, it suggests that modifying the "phase" or direction of neurons is the most effective mechanism for altering semantic output, while changes in magnitude are secondary. Consequently, any finetuning strategy aimed at steering a generative model toward new behaviors should prioritize manipulating neuron directions while rigorously protecting their relative angular configurations to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

To formalize the concept of angular relationships within a neural network layer, we introduce the metric known as hyperspherical energy (HE). This measure provides a scalar value that characterizes the collective geometry of neurons projected onto a unit hypersphere. Let us consider a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each column $\bm{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents an individual neuron. To analyze purely angular properties, we first normalize each neuron vector to lie on the surface of a unit hypersphere. We denote the normalized $i$-th neuron as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

Hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of pairwise interactions between all distinct neurons in the layer, measured by their Euclidean distance on this unit hypersphere. Specifically, for a layer with $n$ neurons, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is calculated as:

$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$

Here, $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ represents the chordal distance between two normalized neurons on the hypersphere. Because these vectors are normalized, this distance is directly related to their cosine similarity; vectors pointing in similar directions have small distances (resulting in large inverse values), while vectors pointing in opposite directions have large distances (resulting in small inverse values). Essentially, HE aggregates these repulsive forces across all pairs of neurons. A high hyperspherical energy indicates that many neurons are clustered closely together in direction space, implying redundancy or lack of diversity in feature representation. Conversely, a low hyperspherical energy suggests that neurons are spread out uniformly across the hypersphere.

It is crucial to recognize that HE is invariant to scaling operations on individual neurons or global rotations/reflections applied uniformly to all neurons within a layer because it relies exclusively on normalized vectors and relative positions. This scale-invariance distinguishes it sharply from traditional norms like Frobenius norm or spectral norm used in regularization techniques like weight decay or spectral normalization (SN). # Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Core Methodology

The advent of large text-to-image diffusion models has revolutionized the field of generative artificial intelligence, enabling the creation of photorealistic images from textual descriptions. However, translating this raw capability into precise control for specific downstream tasks—such as generating a specific subject in new contexts or adhering to strict structural constraints like edge maps—remains a significant challenge. Traditional finetuning methods often struggle to balance the acquisition of new skills with the preservation of the model's original, broad generative abilities. This chapter introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled approach designed to adapt these massive models efficiently while rigorously maintaining their intrinsic semantic structures. At its heart, OFT relies on the mathematical properties of orthogonal transformations to ensure that the relationships between neurons remain invariant during adaptation, thereby preventing the catastrophic forgetting or degradation often seen in standard finetuning pipelines.

# The Principle of Orthogonal Transformation in Weight Spaces

To understand why orthogonal transformation is the cornerstone of OFT, one must first appreciate how neural networks encode semantic information. Empirical observations in deep learning suggest that the angular relationships between neurons, rather than their magnitudes, are the primary carriers of semantic meaning. When a neural network processes data, the direction of a neuron's weight vector on a unit hypersphere determines how it responds to input features. Research indicates that training networks with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields comparable capacity and superior generalizability, implying that the directional alignment of neurons captures the most critical information from the data. This concept is analogous to signal processing, where the phase spectrum of an image (representing angular information) preserves the majority of the image's semantics, whereas the magnitude spectrum contributes less to perceptual recognition. Consequently, modifying the directions of neurons is the most effective way to alter the semantic output of a model, such as teaching it to recognize a new subject or follow a new control signal.

However, simply rotating neuron directions arbitrarily poses a severe risk. If every neuron is allowed to rotate independently with high degrees of freedom, the delicate pairwise relationships established during pretraining are likely to be destroyed. These pairwise relationships define how different features interact and combine to form coherent concepts. Destroying them results in a loss of the model's pretraining generative performance, leading to artifacts or a complete collapse in image quality. The solution lies in finding a "sweet spot" between flexibility and regularity. Orthogonal transformation offers this balance by allowing us to rotate or reflect the entire set of neurons within a layer simultaneously using a single transformation matrix. Unlike independent rotations which would scramble the relative geometry of the weight space, an orthogonal transformation applies a rigid body motion to the neuron configuration. This ensures that while the absolute orientation of the neurons changes to accommodate new task requirements, their internal structural integrity remains intact. By restricting updates to orthogonal matrices, OFT leverages the sufficient expressive power needed for learning while imposing a strong regularizing constraint that protects the pretrained knowledge base.

# Mathematical Proof: Invariance of Hyperspherical Energy under Rotation/Reflection

The theoretical guarantee provided by OFT centers on a quantity known as hyperspherical energy. Hyperspherical energy serves as a metric to characterize the pairwise relational structure among neurons within a layer. It is defined as the sum of inverse distances between all pairs of normalized neurons on the unit hypersphere. Mathematically, for a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ containing $n$ neurons $\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, we first normalize each neuron to obtain $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is then calculated as:

$$
\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$

This value essentially measures the uniformity and distribution of neurons on the hypersphere; a lower energy typically corresponds to neurons being more evenly spread out, which is associated with better generalization in classification tasks. Crucially, for finetuning diffusion models, the goal is not necessarily to minimize this energy from scratch, but to preserve the specific energy profile learned during pretraining. The hypothesis driving OFT is that a successful finetuned model must maintain a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to its pretrained counterpart ($\text{HE}(\bm{W}) \approx \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$). Any significant deviation implies that the semantic topology of the network has been altered, potentially degrading its ability to generate high-fidelity images.

The power of orthogonal transformations lies in their provable invariance property regarding this energy metric. Consider an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, which satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$ (where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix). Such a matrix represents either a rotation (if $\det(\bm{R}) = 1$) or a reflection (if $\det(\bm{R}) = -1$). If we apply this transformation to all neurons in a layer such that the new weights are $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, then every individual neuron vector becomes $\bm{w}_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$. # Algorithmic Implementation and Parameter Efficiency

Transitioning from the theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) to practical application requires addressing the significant computational challenges associated with optimizing orthogonal matrices. While the core premise of OFT—preserving the hyperspherical energy of a pretrained model by applying layer-shared orthogonal transformations—is mathematically elegant, implementing this constraint within a gradient-based optimization framework presents non-trivial hurdles. Standard methods for enforcing orthogonality, such as the Gram-Schmidt process or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), are often computationally prohibitive when applied iteratively during the training of large-scale diffusion models. Furthermore, a naive implementation of a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix introduces a quadratic parameter complexity, $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, which can negate the efficiency benefits sought in parameter-efficient finetuning. This chapter details the algorithmic strategies employed to overcome these obstacles, specifically focusing on the adoption of Cayley parameterization for differentiable orthogonality and the introduction of block-diagonal structures to drastically reduce parameter counts. We will also explore advanced sharing strategies and provide a rigorous comparative analysis against Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to elucidate why OFT offers a superior trade-off between flexibility, stability, and computational cost.

# Challenges in Differentiable Orthogonalization

The primary obstacle in deploying Orthogonal Finetuning lies in maintaining the strict mathematical property of orthogonality throughout the stochastic gradient descent process. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ must satisfy the condition $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. In standard neural network training, weights are updated by subtracting a scaled gradient from their current values. If one were to apply this standard update rule directly to the elements of an orthogonal matrix, the resulting matrix would almost immediately lose its orthogonality, thereby violating the fundamental constraint that ensures the preservation of hyperspherical energy. Without this preservation, the semantic relationships between neurons established during pretraining would be distorted, leading to the degradation of generative quality that OFT aims to prevent.

Traditional numerical linear algebra offers several algorithms to project a arbitrary matrix back onto the manifold of orthogonal matrices, such as the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process or computing the polar decomposition via SVD. However, these methods are ill-suited for deep learning contexts for two main reasons. First, they are computationally expensive; performing an SVD or a full Gram-Schmidt procedure at every optimization step for high-dimensional layers creates a severe bottleneck that slows down training prohibitively. Second, and perhaps more critically, many of these classical algorithms involve operations that are not easily differentiable or require complex custom backward passes to propagate gradients correctly through the orthogonalization step. While recent research has developed differentiable versions of these algorithms, they often remain too heavy for practical use in finetuning massive text-to-image diffusion models where efficiency is paramount. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a parameterization strategy that inherently guarantees orthogonality by construction, allowing standard optimizers to operate freely on a set of unconstrained parameters while ensuring the resulting transformation matrix remains strictly orthogonal.

# Cayley Parameterization: Generating Orthogonal Matrices from Skew-Symmetric Inputs

To resolve the challenge of differentiable orthogonalization efficiently, OFT employs the Cayley transform. This mathematical tool provides a bijective mapping between skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $1$, known as the special orthogonal group. Instead of optimizing the entries of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ directly, the method optimizes a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, which satisfies the property $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. The orthogonal matrix is then constructed dynamically during the forward pass using the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, this transformation mathematically guarantees that the resulting $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal without requiring any post-hoc projection steps. This approach allows the optimizer to update the independent elements of $\bm{Q}$ using standard gradient descent, confident that every intermediate state corresponds to a valid orthogonal transformation.

The elegance of Cayley parameterization extends beyond mere constraint satisfaction; it also facilitates stable initialization crucial for finetuning tasks. In OFT, it is essential that the model begins training exactly at the pretrained weights to avoid abrupt changes in generation quality before learning commences. By initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix ($\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$), the Cayley transform yields $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$. # Advanced Variants: Constrained and Re-scaled OFT

While the foundational Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method provides a robust mechanism for adapting text-to-image diffusion models by preserving hyperspherical energy, practical applications often demand finer control over the trade-off between model plasticity and stability. The base OFT approach ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons remain invariant through orthogonal transformations, thereby maintaining the semantic structure of the pretrained model. However, in scenarios requiring extreme stability or specific adjustments to neuron magnitudes, additional architectural modifications become necessary. This chapter delves into two critical extensions of the OFT framework: Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which explicitly limits the deviation of finetuned weights from their pretrained values, and Re-scaled OFT, which introduces learnable magnitude coefficients to enhance flexibility without compromising the preservation of hyperspherical energy. These advanced variants address specific limitations of the base method, offering improved convergence properties and adaptability for complex downstream tasks such as subject-driven generation and controllable image synthesis.

# Motivation for Constraints: Balancing Flexibility and Stability

The design of any effective finetuning strategy hinges on finding an optimal balance between flexibility—the ability to learn new tasks—and stability—the capacity to retain the generative capabilities acquired during pretraining. Standard finetuning methods typically rely on small learning rates to implicitly minimize the Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights $\bm{M}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^0$. While this heuristic often works, it lacks a theoretical guarantee that the semantic relationships encoded in the pretrained model are preserved. The core insight behind OFT is that semantic information is primarily stored in the angular relationships between neurons rather than their absolute positions in weight space. Consequently, preserving hyperspherical energy, which quantifies these pairwise angular similarities, is more critical than minimizing Euclidean distance alone.

However, even with the orthogonality constraint inherent in OFT, there exists a risk that the learned orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ might deviate too significantly from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, especially when training data is scarce or noisy. Such large deviations, while theoretically preserving angles, could shift the model into a region of the parameter space where the pretrained knowledge is effectively inaccessible or distorted in practice. This phenomenon highlights a fundamental tension: while orthogonal transformations protect the internal structure of neuron relationships, they do not inherently restrict how far the entire subspace can rotate away from its original orientation. To mitigate this, it becomes essential to impose an explicit constraint on the magnitude of this rotation. By limiting the flexibility of the orthogonal transformation, we can ensure that the finetuned model remains within a trusted neighborhood of the pretrained model, thereby enhancing stability without sacrificing the structural benefits of orthogonality. This motivation leads directly to the formulation of Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which augments the base OFT framework with a rigorous bound on parameter deviation.

# Constrained OFT (COFT): Imposing Radius Constraints on the Hypersphere

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) extends the principles of OFT by introducing an additional radius constraint that confines the finetuned model within a fixed hypersphere centered on the pretrained neurons. In mathematical terms, while standard OFT seeks an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, COFT imposes a secondary condition that limits the distance between $\bm{R}$ and the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. The forward pass for a layer in COFT is defined as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, subject to two constraints: first, $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$), and second, the spectral norm of the difference between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{I}$ must not exceed a predefined threshold $\epsilon$, expressed as $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$.

This dual-constraint formulation serves a distinct purpose compared to existing finetuning techniques. Traditional methods implicitly assume that smaller Euclidean distances correlate with better preservation of pretrained abilities, often enforcing this through tiny learning rates. COFT makes this assumption explicit and mathematically rigorous by defining a hard boundary on how much the coordinate system of the neurons can rotate. Geometrically, this can be visualized as restricting the movement of neurons on the unit hypersphere to a localized cap around their original positions. By doing so, COFT ensures that while the relative angles between neurons (hyperspherical energy) are perfectly preserved due to orthogonality, the absolute orientation of these neurons does not drift arbitrarily far from their initialized state. This approach effectively combines two powerful regularizers: minimal hyperspherical energy difference and constrained deviation from the pretrained model. Empirical evidence suggests that this explicit constraint yields superior finetuning stability compared to unconstrained OFT, particularly in tasks where overfitting or catastrophic forgetting is a concern. The result is a model that adapts to new control signals or subject identities while rigorously maintaining its high-fidelity generative performance.

# Conclusion and Future Directions

The advent of large-scale text-to-image diffusion models has revolutionized the field of generative artificial intelligence, enabling the synthesis of photorealistic imagery from textual descriptions with unprecedented fidelity. However, the sheer power of these pretrained models presents a paradox: while they possess vast semantic knowledge, directing them to perform specific downstream tasks—such as generating a particular subject in novel contexts or adhering to strict structural constraints like edge maps—remains a significant challenge. Traditional finetuning approaches often struggle to balance the acquisition of new capabilities with the preservation of the model's original generative diversity and quality. In response to this critical gap, this work has introduced Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), as a principled framework for adapting diffusion models. By grounding the finetuning process in the geometry of hyperspherical energy, OFT offers a robust mechanism to guide powerful models without destroying the intricate semantic structures learned during pretraining. This concluding chapter synthesizes the theoretical foundations, methodological innovations, and empirical successes of OFT, while delineating the frontier of open problems that define the next steps in this evolving research area.

## Summary of Contributions: Theory, Method, and Empirical Results

The primary contribution of this work lies in establishing a rigorous theoretical connection between the geometric arrangement of neurons and the preservation of semantic information in deep neural networks. Central to this theory is the concept of hyperspherical energy, which quantifies the pairwise relationships between neurons on a unit hypersphere. Previous finetuning strategies implicitly assumed that maintaining a small Euclidean distance between pretrained and finetuned weights was sufficient to preserve generative capability. However, we demonstrated that Euclidean proximity is an inadequate proxy for semantic preservation. Instead, we posited that the angular relationships between neurons—their relative positions on the hypersphere—are the true carriers of semantic meaning. Drawing inspiration from observations in signal processing where phase spectra (angular information) dominate magnitude spectra in defining image content, we hypothesized that a successful finetuning method must preserve the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. OFT achieves this by restricting weight updates to orthogonal transformations. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weight matrix and $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, the finetuned weights are given by $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal transformations correspond to rotations or reflections, they strictly preserve the angles between any pair of column vectors (neurons) in $\bm{W}^0$, thereby guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains invariant: $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$.

Building upon this theoretical foundation, we developed a practical and efficient methodology for implementing orthogonal transformations within large-scale diffusion architectures. A naive application of orthogonal constraints would be computationally prohibitive; thus, we employed Cayley parameterization to generate orthogonal matrices differentiably. Specifically, we constructed the rotation matrix as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. To further enhance parameter efficiency, we introduced a block-diagonal structure to $\bm{R}$, reducing the complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or even $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$ with shared blocks, where $r$ is the number of blocks. This design allows OFT to compete favorably with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) in terms of trainable parameters while offering superior structural guarantees. Furthermore, recognizing that pure orthogonality might occasionally allow for deviations that are too large for stable convergence in highly sensitive tasks, we proposed Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). COFT augments the orthogonality constraint with an explicit bound on the deviation from the identity matrix, enforcing $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This ensures that the finetuned model remains within a controlled neighborhood of the pretrained weights, providing an additional layer of stability without sacrificing the core benefit of angular preservation.

Empirically, the OFT framework has demonstrated substantial improvements over state-of-the-art baselines across two distinct and challenging domains: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. In subject-driven tasks, akin to DreamBooth scenarios where a model must learn to generate a specific object from few-shot examples, OFT and COFT exhibited remarkable stability. While standard finetuning and LoRA often suffered from mode collapse or loss of subject identity after extended training iterations, OFT maintained consistent control over both subject fidelity and text prompt adherence even after thousands of iterations. Quantitative metrics utilizing DINO and CLIP embeddings confirmed that OFT achieves higher subject fidelity than competing methods while retaining comparable diversity and prompt alignment. In controllable generation tasks involving Canny edges, segmentation maps, and facial landmarks, OFT converged significantly faster than ControlNet and T2I-Adapter. Notably, OFT achieved competitive performance with only a fraction of the training data required by adapter-based methods. We also introduced a novel control consistency metric to rigorously evaluate these tasks, revealing that OFT produces images that adhere more accurately to input control signals while maintaining higher visual fidelity. Crucially, unlike adapter methods that add computational branches during inference, OFT absorbs the learned orthogonal matrix into the original weights ($\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$), resulting in zero additional inference overhead.

## Open Problem 1: Scalability Challenges in Matrix Inversion

Despite its theoretical elegance and empirical success, the current implementation of OFT relies on Cayley parameterization, which introduces a computational bottleneck related to matrix inversion. The construction of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ requires computing $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ at every step of the optimization process. While this operation is feasible for moderate dimensions or when utilizing block-diagonal structures with small block sizes, it poses scalability challenges as model dimensions increase or if one wishes to remove the block-diagonal bias entirely to allow for full-rank orthogonal updates. The complexity of matrix inversion generally scales cubically with dimension size in naive implementations, although specialized algorithms can mitigate this; nevertheless, it remains more expensive than simple matrix additions or multiplications used in other adaptation techniques like LoRA or standard gradient descent updates. This reliance on inversion slightly limits the scalability of OFT when applied to extremely wide layers or when attempting to scale up to next-generation foundation models with billions of parameters per layer.

Future research must address how to accelerate this matrix inverse operation in a fully differentiable manner without compromising numerical stability or orthogonality guarantees. One potential avenue involves exploring alternative parameterizations of orthogonal groups that do not require explicit inversion during backpropagation or investigating approximation techniques that maintain high precision while reducing computational cost. Additionally，发展 iterative methods or hardware-accelerated solvers specifically tailored for skew-symmetric systems could offer pathways to more efficient computation solving $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})\bm{x} = \boldsymbol b$. Resolving this scalability issue is essential for making OFT universally applicable across all layers of massive transformer-based diffusion models without necessitating aggressive blocking strategies that might restrict expressivity prematurely until necessary approximations are proven safe..
