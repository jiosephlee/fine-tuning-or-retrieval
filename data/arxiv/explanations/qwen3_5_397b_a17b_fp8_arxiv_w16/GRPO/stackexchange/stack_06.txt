\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How was benchmark contamination avoided given reliance on Common Crawl?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath Corpus is built entirely from Common Crawl using a fastText classifier iteratively trained on seeds like OpenWebMath. Since benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH are widely available online, how exactly did their decontamination pipeline work?

The paper mentions filtering any text segment containing a 10-gram match with benchmark substrings. Given that many websites might host solutions or discussions of these famous problems without copying them verbatim (e.g., paraphrased solutions), was there any risk of subtle leakage? Did they perform any semantic similarity checks beyond exact n-gram matching?
Answer:
The decontamination pipeline described in the paper relies exclusively on strict lexical matching rather than semantic similarity checks, meaning there is indeed a theoretical risk of subtle leakage from paraphrased solutions that do not share exact substrings with the benchmarks.

According to the "Data Collection and Decontamination" section, the authors explicitly state that they filter out web pages containing questions or answers from English benchmarks (like GSM8K and MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (like CMATH and AGIEval) by removing any text segment that contains a **10-gram string** matching exactly with any substring from the evaluation sets. For benchmark texts that are shorter than 10 grams but contain at least 3 grams, they employ **exact matching** to filter contaminated pages. The paper cites following the methodology of *DeepSeek-Coder* for this process.

Crucially, the text makes no mention of employing embedding-based semantic similarity models, fuzzy matching algorithms, or paraphrase detection tools to catch variations where the mathematical logic or solution structure is identical but the wording differs. The strategy assumes that if a webpage hosts a benchmark problem or its solution, it will likely retain a contiguous sequence of at least 10 tokens (or 3 for very short segments) identical to the official benchmark text. While this is highly effective against verbatim copies, discussions, or slightly modified reposts found on forums or educational sites, it would technically fail to detect a human-written explanation that completely rephrases the problem statement and solution without preserving those specific n-gram sequences. Consequently, while the pipeline rigorously removes direct overlaps, the possibility of "soft" contamination via semantically equivalent but lexically distinct content remains an inherent limitation of using only exact n-gram filtering.
