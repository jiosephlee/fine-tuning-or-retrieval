\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on arXiv papers seem ineffective for mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors state that training on arXiv papers (using corpora like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama) resulted in 'no notable improvements or even deterioration' on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. This seems counter-intuitive since arXiv contains high-level rigorous mathematics. 

Did the authors hypothesize why this happened? Was it due to the formatting of LaTeX files, the mismatch between proof-style writing and solution-style reasoning required by the benchmarks, or simply that the web-scraped data from Common Crawl was surprisingly higher quality for this specific task?
Answer:
The authors explicitly address this counter-intuitive finding in the "Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training" section, stating that training on arXiv papers resulted in "no notable improvements or even deterioration" across various mathematical benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F. While the paper does not pinpoint a single definitive cause, it strongly implies that the issue stems from a domain mismatch between the nature of arXiv content and the specific reasoning skills required by these benchmarks.

The core hypothesis supported by the text is that arXiv papers consist primarily of high-level, rigorous proof-style writing intended for experts, whereas benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH evaluate solution-style reasoning akin to competition mathematics or grade-school problems. The data in arXiv focuses on formal theorem proving and advanced theoretical exposition, which appears ineffective—or potentially detrimental—for teaching a model the step-by-step deductive logic needed to solve elementary or competition-level quantitative problems. This is evidenced by the fact that models trained exclusively on arXiv corpora (such as MathPile, which is over $85\%$ arXiv, or ArXiv-RedPajama) failed to improve performance compared to models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus.

In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus, derived from Common Crawl via a meticulous filtering pipeline, yielded superior results. The authors suggest that publicly available web data contains a higher density of the specific type of mathematical discourse found in educational contexts, forums, and solution manuals that align better with benchmark requirements. Furthermore, the paper highlights that the effectiveness of pre-training data is not just about "rigor" but about relevance to the target task distribution. The authors caution that this conclusion has limitations, noting they did not test whether arXiv data might benefit specific tasks like informal-to-formal theorem conversion or if its utility might emerge at larger model scales, but for the specific goal of improving competition math scores in $1.3\text{B}$ and $7\text{B}$ models, web-scraped data proved significantly more potent than academic literature.
