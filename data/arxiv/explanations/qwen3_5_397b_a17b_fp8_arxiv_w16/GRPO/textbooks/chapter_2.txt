\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus: Data Engineering at Scale

The development of large language models capable of sophisticated mathematical reasoning relies fundamentally on the quality and scale of the data used during pre-training. While model architecture and optimization algorithms are critical, the DeepSeekMath study posits that the primary driver for achieving state-of-the-art performance in open-source models is a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline. This chapter explores the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive dataset comprising 120 billion tokens of mathematical content sourced primarily from the public web. Unlike traditional approaches that rely heavily on curated academic repositories, this work demonstrates that publicly available web data, when filtered through rigorous iterative processes, contains a vast reservoir of high-quality mathematical knowledge. The creation of this corpus involves a sophisticated interplay of machine learning classifiers, domain-based expansion strategies, human annotation, and strict decontamination protocols to ensure the integrity of evaluation benchmarks. Furthermore, the corpus is designed to be inherently multilingual, addressing a gap in previous datasets that often prioritize English to the detriment of other languages such as Chinese. By understanding the engineering behind this corpus, one gains insight into how data curation can compensate for smaller model sizes, allowing a 7-billion parameter model to compete with significantly larger counterparts.

# The Potential of Web Data vs. Academic Papers (ArXiv)

A prevailing assumption in the field of mathematical language modeling has been that academic papers, particularly those hosted on arXiv, represent the gold standard for training data. It is intuitive to believe that formal scientific literature, replete with rigorous proofs and structured arguments, would provide the most effective signal for teaching a model mathematical reasoning. Consequently, many prior efforts have constructed training corpora where arXiv papers constitute the majority of the tokens. However, the empirical findings presented in the DeepSeekMath research challenge this convention, suggesting that web-scraped data may hold untapped potential that exceeds that of academic repositories when properly harvested. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is built on the hypothesis that the Common Crawl—a massive archive of publicly accessible web pages—contains valuable mathematical information that has been largely underutilized due to noise and lack of filtering.

The study provides compelling evidence through controlled experiments comparing models trained on web-derived data versus those trained exclusively on arXiv papers. When models were trained on corpora consisting entirely of arXiv data, such as MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama, the results were counter-intuitive: there were no notable improvements in mathematical reasoning capabilities across various benchmarks. In some instances, performance even deteriorated compared to baselines. This stagnation occurred despite arXiv papers being dense with mathematical content. The researchers observed this phenomenon across different model scales, from 1.3 billion to 7 billion parameters, indicating that simply ingesting formal academic text does not automatically translate to improved problem-solving skills on standardized tests like GSM8K or MATH. This suggests that while arXiv papers are rich in content, they may lack the diversity of problem styles, explanatory depths, or pedagogical structures found in broader educational web contexts, or perhaps their specific formatting and linguistic style do not generalize well to the types of queries posed in benchmarks.

In stark contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus, derived almost entirely from Common Crawl via a specialized filtering pipeline, yielded dramatic improvements. The final corpus comprises 120 billion tokens, which is approximately seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath. Models pre-trained on this web-sourced corpus demonstrated superior performance on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks compared to models trained on arXiv-heavy datasets. The success of this approach indicates that the "long tail" of the internet—including educational forums, course notes, solution manuals, and tutorial sites—offers a richer and more varied distribution of mathematical reasoning patterns than formal publications alone. The sheer scale achievable through web mining allows for a breadth of exposure that static academic databases cannot match. Consequently, the lesson drawn from DeepSeekMath is that the key to unlocking mathematical reasoning lies not necessarily in the prestige of the source material but in the volume and diversity of high-quality examples extracted from the open web through intelligent filtering mechanisms.

# Iterative Data Selection Pipeline using fastText Classifiers

Extracting high-quality mathematical content from the chaotic expanse of Common Crawl requires more than simple keyword matching; it demands a robust classification system capable of distinguishing genuine mathematical discourse from irrelevant text containing occasional numbers or symbols. To achieve this, DeepSeekMath employs an iterative data selection pipeline centered around a fastText-based classifier. FastText was chosen for its efficiency in handling large-scale text classification tasks, allowing for rapid scoring of billions of web pages. The core philosophy behind this pipeline is recursion: the classifier is not static but evolves over multiple iterations, becoming increasingly proficient at identifying relevant content as it is exposed to more diverse and verified examples. This iterative refinement is crucial because an initial classifier trained on a limited seed set will inevitably miss valid mathematical pages that differ stylistically or topically from the seed examples.

The process begins with an initial training phase where the fastText model learns to differentiate between positive and negative examples. Positive examples are drawn from a seed corpus known to contain high-quality mathematics, while negative examples are sampled randomly from the broader Common Crawl dump to represent typical non-mathematical web content.
