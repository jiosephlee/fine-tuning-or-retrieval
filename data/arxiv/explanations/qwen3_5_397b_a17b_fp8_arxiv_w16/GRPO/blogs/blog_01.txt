# Quality Over Quantity: How DeepSeekMath Beat Giant Models with Just 7 Billion Parameters

In the race to build smarter artificial intelligence, the prevailing wisdom has often been simple: bigger is better. For years, researchers have chased performance by stacking billions of parameters onto massive neural networks, assuming that scale alone would solve complex reasoning problems. However, a new study titled "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models" challenges this assumption head-on. The authors introduce DeepSeekMath 7B, a remarkably compact model that not only outperforms open-source giants but also rivals closed-source behemoths like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on rigorous mathematical benchmarks. What makes this achievement truly startling is that DeepSeekMath 7B accomplishes this feat with only 7 billion parameters, dwarfing the performance of Minerva 540B, a model with 77 times more parameters.

The secret to this efficiency lies not in architectural magic, but in a relentless focus on data quality. The research team demonstrates that a smaller model, when trained on a meticulously curated, high-quality dataset, can surpass much larger counterparts trained on noisier or less relevant data. This post explores how the creation of the DeepSeekMath Corpus and a novel reinforcement learning technique called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) allowed a tiny model to punch far above its weight class.

## The Myth of Parameter Scaling

For a long time, the trajectory of Large Language Model (LLM) development suggested that mathematical reasoning was a resource-intensive task requiring massive scale. Minerva, a prominent model developed by Google, achieved state-of-the-art results by leveraging 540 billion parameters. It seemed logical that to beat Minerva, one would need an even larger model. DeepSeekMath disrupts this narrative by achieving a score of 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without any external toolkits or voting ensembles. In comparison, the colossal Minerva 540B struggles to match this performance despite its immense size.

This disparity highlights a critical insight: the number of parameters is not the sole determinant of reasoning capability. The DeepSeekMath team found that starting with a code-trained base model and continuing pre-training on highly specialized mathematical data yielded superior results compared to simply scaling up a general-purpose model. Their base model, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, scored 36.2% on the MATH dataset, already outperforming Minerva 540B. This suggests that the industry may have been over-investing in compute and under-investing in the curation of training data.

## Mining Gold from the Web: The DeepSeekMath Corpus

The cornerstone of DeepSeekMath's success is its training data. The researchers constructed the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive collection comprising 120 billion math-related tokens sourced entirely from Common Crawl, the public archive of web pages. To put this in perspective, this corpus is nearly seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath, another popular open dataset.

Creating such a dataset required more than just downloading random web pages; it demanded a sophisticated engineering pipeline. The team employed an iterative approach using a fastText-based classifier to distinguish high-quality mathematical content from the noise of the internet. They began with a seed corpus of known high-quality math texts from OpenWebMath as positive examples and diverse non-math web pages as negative examples. This classifier was then used to mine additional positive instances from Common Crawl.

Crucially, the process did not stop at automatic filtering. The team organized the crawled data by domain and manually annotated URLs within domains that showed high concentrations of mathematical content, such as `mathoverflow.net`. These human-verified examples were fed back into the classifier to refine its accuracy over four iterations. By ranking pages based on their predicted scores and keeping only the top-tier content, they ensured that every token contributed meaningfully to the model's reasoning abilities. Furthermore, unlike many predecessors that focused exclusively on English, this corpus includes significant amounts of Chinese mathematical content, enabling the model to excel in multilingual benchmarks as well.

Interestingly, the study also debunked a common belief regarding data sources. While many previous efforts heavily relied on arXiv papers for mathematical training, DeepSeekMath's experiments revealed that training solely on arXiv data brought no notable improvements—and sometimes even degraded performance—on standard reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. This finding suggests that formal academic papers might lack the pedagogical structure or diversity needed to teach robust problem-solving skills compared tothe varied explanations found on educational websites and forums.

## The Power of Code and Continued Pre-training

Another pivotal decision in the DeepSeekMath strategy was initialization. Rather than starting from scratch or using a generic language model checkpoint, the team initialized their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This choice was driven by the observation that code training inherently improves logical reasoning capabilities. The experiments confirmed that models exposed to code prior to math training performed better on mathematical tasks, both with and without tool use.

The final pre-training mix consisted of 56% data from their custom DeepSeekMath Corpus, supplemented by code from GitHub (20%), arXiv papers (10%), AlgebraicStack (4%), and general natural language data (10%). This balanced diet allowed the model to retain its coding proficiency while absorbing deep mathematical knowledge. The result was a base model that didn't just memorize formulas but learned to reason step-by-step, evidenced by its strong performance on Chain-of-Thought prompting tasks across eight different benchmarks.
