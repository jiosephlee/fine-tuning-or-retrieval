# Goodbye Critic Model: Introducing GRPO, The Memory-Efficient RL Algorithm Changing the Game

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a cornerstone for pushing the boundaries of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex domains like mathematical reasoning. However, traditional RL approaches often come with a steep price tag in terms of computational resources. The standard algorithm, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), typically requires training a separate "critic" model—a value function approximator roughly the same size as the policy model itself—to estimate advantages and stabilize training. This doubles the memory footprint and significantly increases the engineering complexity required to scale these systems.

In their recent paper, *DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models*, the researchers introduce a novel solution to this bottleneck: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This new algorithm eliminates the need for a dedicated critic model entirely. Instead, it estimates baselines directly from group scores generated by the policy model itself. The result is a drastic reduction in memory usage while achieving performance that rivals closed-source giants like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra on competition-level math benchmarks.

## The Bottleneck of Traditional PPO

To understand why GRPO is such a significant departure, we must first look at how PPO operates in the context of LLMs. PPO is an actor-critic algorithm. The "actor" is the language model generating text, while the "critic" is a separate model trained to predict the expected return (value) of a given state. During training, the advantage $A_t$—which measures how much better a specific action is compared to the average—is calculated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). This calculation relies heavily on the values predicted by the critic model $V_{\psi}$.

The objective function for PPO maximizes a surrogate goal involving the ratio of probabilities between the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, scaled by this advantage $A_t$. Because the critic must be trained alongside the policy and maintained in memory, scaling PPO to large models becomes prohibitively expensive. Furthermore, in language modeling tasks where rewards are often sparse (assigned only at the end of a sequence), training a token-level value function can be unstable and inaccurate.

## How GRPO Works: Strength in Numbers

GRPO addresses these inefficiencies by rethinking how advantages are estimated. Rather than relying on a learned value function, GRPO leverages the statistical power of sampling groups of outputs. For every input question $q$, the algorithm samples a group of $G$ distinct outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$.

Instead of asking a critic model "how good is this output?", GRPO asks "how does this output compare to others generated for the same question?" The rewards for these $G$ outputs are computed using a reward model, and then normalized within the group. Specifically, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for any token in an output is derived from the normalized reward of that entire output sequence relative to the group mean and standard deviation. If an output receives a higher reward than the group average, its tokens receive positive advantages; if lower, they receive negative advantages.

This approach aligns naturally with how reward models are typically trained—on pairwise or listwise comparisons of outputs for identical prompts. By removing the critic model, GRPO simplifies the training architecture and significantly reduces GPU memory requirements. The optimization objective focuses solely on updating the policy model $\pi_\theta$ using these group-relative advantages, while regularizing via a direct KL divergence term against a reference model to prevent drift.

## Performance That Defies Expectations

The efficacy of GRPO was validated through rigorous experimentation with DeepSeekMath 7B. Starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which was already strong due to extensive supervised fine-tuning on chain-of-thought and tool-integrated data, the team applied GRPO using only a subset of English instruction tuning data related to GSM8K and MATH benchmarks.

The results were striking. After reinforcement learning with GRPO, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieved an accuracy of 88.2% on GSM8K and 51.7% on MATH using chain-of-thought reasoning alone. This performance surpasses all existing open-source models ranging from 7B to 70B parameters and even outperforms many proprietary closed-source models on these specific tasks. Remarkably, self-consistency over 64 samples pushed the MATH score to 60.9%, approaching the capabilities of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 without relying on external toolkits or massive ensemble voting techniques during inference.

Perhaps most impressively, these gains extended beyond just in-domain tasks where RL training occurred; out-of-domain benchmarks also saw improvements suggesting that GRPO helps generalize reasoning capabilities rather than simply memorizing patterns for specific datasets ($CMATH$: 84.6% $\rightarrow$ 88%).

## Why It Matters for Open Source AI

The introduction of GRPO represents more than just an incremental improvement; it lowers barrier to entry for high-performance RL fine-tuning previously reserved for well-funded labs with access to massive compute clusters. By discarding critic models entirely reduces resource consumption dramatically making sophisticated alignment techniques feasible for smaller teams working with open weights models .
