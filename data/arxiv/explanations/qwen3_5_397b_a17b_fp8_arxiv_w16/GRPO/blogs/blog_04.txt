# Beyond Memorization: How Reinforcement Learning Makes AI Reasoning More Robust

When we see an AI model jump from 46.8% to 51.7% accuracy on a difficult math benchmark like MATH after undergoing Reinforcement Learning (RL), it is tempting to assume the model has suddenly learned new mathematical theorems or forgotten its previous errors. However, the research behind DeepSeekMath suggests a more nuanced reality. The improvement isn't necessarily about teaching the model facts it didn't know; rather, it is about making the model's existing knowledge more reliable and consistent.

In this post, we dive into the findings from the DeepSeekMath paper to understand why RL is so effective even when applied to models that have already been thoroughly instruction-tuned. We will explore how RL shifts the focus from simply having a correct answer somewhere in the model's possibilities to ensuring that the correct answer is the one most likely to be generated.

## The Puzzle of Post-SFT Improvement

DeepSeekMath starts with a base model that undergoes extensive supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on high-quality mathematical data. By the time the model reaches the "DeepSeekMath-Instruct" stage, it is already quite capable, scoring 46.8% on the competition-level MATH dataset. One might expect diminishing returns at this point, assuming the model has memorized most of what it can from the training data.

Yet, when the researchers applied their novel Reinforcement Learning algorithm, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the performance jumped significantly to 51.7%. This raises a critical question: If the model was already trained on these problems during the SFT phase, what exactly did RL add? Did it learn new math, or did something else happen?

## Pass@K vs. Maj@K: Measuring Robustness

To answer this, we need to distinguish between two ways of measuring a model's capability: `Pass@K` and `Maj@K`.

`Pass@K` measures whether the model can generate *at least one* correct solution out of $K$ attempts. If you ask a model to solve a hard calculus problem 10 times and it gets it right once, its `Pass@10` is perfect, even if it failed nine times. This metric tells us about the upper bound of the model's potential—it confirms that the correct reasoning path exists somewhere within the model's parameters.

`Maj@K`, or Majority Vote at $K$, looks at consistency. It involves generating $K$ samples and selecting the answer that appears most frequently. If a model generates 10 different wrong answers and only 1 correct one, the majority vote will likely pick a wrong answer. High `Maj@K` requires not just knowing the answer, but knowing it consistently enough that it dominates the output distribution.

The DeepSeekMath team analyzed their models using these metrics and found a striking pattern. While Reinforcement Learning did not significantly improve `Pass@K`—suggesting the SFT model already "knew" how to solve many of these problems—it drastically improved `Maj@K`. This indicates that RL does not necessarily expand the set of solvable problems. Instead, it reshapes the probability landscape of the model's outputs. It suppresses erratic, incorrect reasoning paths and amplifies the correct ones, making the right answer the most probable outcome every time.

## The Misalignment Problem in Supervised Fine-Tuning

This phenomenon highlights what researchers call a "misalignment problem" in Supervised Fine-Tuning. During SFT, a model learns to mimic human-written solutions. While this teaches the model valid reasoning steps, it doesn't explicitly penalize it for occasionally drifting into plausible-looking but incorrect logic during generation. The model might assign non-trivial probability mass to wrong answers because they look syntactically similar to correct ones or because they appeared in noisy training data.

Reinforcement Learning acts as a corrective lens here. By sampling multiple outputs for a single question and comparing them against a reward signal (in this case, derived from group scores via GRPO), the model learns to differentiate between high-quality and low-quality reasoning traces. It realizes that while a specific wrong turn might look okay in isolation, consistently taking that turn leads to failure. Consequently, RL tightens the distribution around correct solutions, effectively turning a sporadic genius into a reliable tutor.

## Enter GRPO: Efficient Alignment Without a Critic

Achieving this robustness traditionally requires Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which relies on a separate "critic" model to estimate values and calculate advantages. This doubles the memory requirements and computational cost, making it expensive for large-scale experimentation.
