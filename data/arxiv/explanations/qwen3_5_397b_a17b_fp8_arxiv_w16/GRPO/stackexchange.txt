\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on arXiv papers seem ineffective for mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors state that training on arXiv papers (using corpora like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama) resulted in 'no notable improvements or even deterioration' on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. This seems counter-intuitive since arXiv contains high-level rigorous mathematics. 

Did the authors hypothesize why this happened? Was it due to the formatting of LaTeX files, the mismatch between proof-style writing and solution-style reasoning required by the benchmarks, or simply that the web-scraped data from Common Crawl was surprisingly higher quality for this specific task?
Answer:
The authors explicitly address this counter-intuitive finding in the "Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training" section, stating that training on arXiv papers resulted in "no notable improvements or even deterioration" across various mathematical benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F. While the paper does not pinpoint a single definitive cause, it strongly implies that the issue stems from a domain mismatch between the nature of arXiv content and the specific reasoning skills required by these benchmarks.

The core hypothesis supported by the text is that arXiv papers consist primarily of high-level, rigorous proof-style writing intended for experts, whereas benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH evaluate solution-style reasoning akin to competition mathematics or grade-school problems. The data in arXiv focuses on formal theorem proving and advanced theoretical exposition, which appears ineffective—or potentially detrimental—for teaching a model the step-by-step deductive logic needed to solve elementary or competition-level quantitative problems. This is evidenced by the fact that models trained exclusively on arXiv corpora (such as MathPile, which is over $85\%$ arXiv, or ArXiv-RedPajama) failed to improve performance compared to models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus.

In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus, derived from Common Crawl via a meticulous filtering pipeline, yielded superior results. The authors suggest that publicly available web data contains a higher density of the specific type of mathematical discourse found in educational contexts, forums, and solution manuals that align better with benchmark requirements. Furthermore, the paper highlights that the effectiveness of pre-training data is not just about "rigor" but about relevance to the target task distribution. The authors caution that this conclusion has limitations, noting they did not test whether arXiv data might benefit specific tasks like informal-to-formal theorem conversion or if its utility might emerge at larger model scales, but for the specific goal of improving competition math scores in $1.3\text{B}$ and $7\text{B}$ models, web-scraped data proved significantly more potent than academic literature.

### How exactly is the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ calculated in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)?
Question:
The paper introduces GRPO as a variant of PPO that removes the critic model. In standard PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) involving a learned value function $V_\psi$. 

In GRPO, the paper states they estimate the baseline from group scores. Specifically, for a question $q$, they sample a group of outputs $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$. Could someone clarify the exact formula used for $\hat{A}_{i,t}$? The text mentions normalizing rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation ($\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$). Does this normalized reward become the advantage for *every* token $t$ in that sequence (outcome supervision), or is there a different aggregation for process supervision? How does this replace the time-dependent baseline normally provided by a critic?
Answer:
In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is calculated entirely from the relative performance of a group of outputs sampled for the same question, eliminating the need for a separate learned value function (critic). The specific formula depends on whether you are using outcome supervision or process supervision, but both rely on normalizing rewards within a group of $G$ samples $\{o_1, \dots, o_G\}$ generated from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for a given query $q$.

Under **outcome supervision**, the reward model assigns a single scalar reward $r_i$ to each complete output $o_i$. These rewards are first normalized across the group to create $\widetilde{r}_i$ by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation: $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$. In this setting, this normalized score becomes the advantage for *every* token $t$ in that specific sequence. That is, $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$ for all $1 \le t \le |o_i|$. This approach treats the entire trajectory as having uniform value relative to its peers, effectively replacing the time-dependent baseline $V(s_t)$ used in standard PPO with a static, group-relative baseline derived from the mean reward of the sampled outputs.

Under **process supervision**, the mechanism is more granular. Instead of a single score per sequence, a process reward model assigns rewards to specific reasoning steps within each output. Let $\text{index}(j)$ denote the token position marking the end of the $j$-th step in output $o_i$. The raw step rewards are collected into a global set $\mathbf{R}$ across all outputs and steps in the group, then normalized similarly to get $\widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)} = \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)} - \text{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{R})}$. Here, the advantage for a specific token $t$ is defined as the sum of the normalized rewards for all subsequent steps in that trajectory: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{\text{index}(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}$. This aggregates future step-quality signals to determine the credit assignment for earlier tokens, mirroring the logic of return-to-go but using relative, normalized step rewards rather than absolute discounted returns estimated by a critic.

In both cases, GRPO replaces the explicit value function $V_\psi$ with an implicit baseline formed by the group average. By subtracting the mean reward of the group during normalization, the method ensures that outputs performing better than the group average receive positive advantages (encouraging their likelihood), while those performing worse receive negative advantages (discouraging them). This "group relative" strategy significantly reduces memory overhead since no additional critic network needs to be trained or stored, while still providing a stable variance-reduced signal for policy updates.

### What is the precise role of Code Training in the DeepSeekMath pipeline?
Question:
The abstract mentions initializing DeepSeekMath with 'DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5'. Later, in Section 4.1.1, they claim 'Code training benefits mathematical reasoning.' 

I am confused about the mechanism here. Did they find that code training helps specifically with 'Program-of-Thought' (writing Python scripts to solve math), or did it also improve pure 'Chain-of-Thought' reasoning where no code is executed? The results mention improvements in both, but how does syntactic code generation translate to better abstract mathematical logic without tool use?
Answer:
The role of code training in the DeepSeekMath pipeline is foundational, serving as a critical initialization strategy that enhances mathematical reasoning capabilities regardless of whether the final solution requires executable code. According to Section 4.1.1 and the Contributions section, the authors explicitly state that "code training improves models' ability to do mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use." This finding addresses the specific mechanism you questioned: the benefit is not limited to Program-of-Thought (PoT) scenarios where Python scripts are executed, but extends significantly to pure Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning where the model generates self-contained text solutions.

The empirical evidence for this dual benefit comes from ablation studies described in Section 4.1.1, where the researchers compared two-stage training (Code Training followed by Math Training) against controls using general tokens or math-only training. They found that an initial stage of code training alone resulted in moderate enhancements for non-tool mathematical reasoning (CoT), while also significantly boosting performance on program-aided tasks (PoT). When followed by math-specific training, this sequence yielded the best overall performance, suggesting that code training acts as an efficient primer that accelerates the acquisition of mathematical logic during subsequent training phases. The authors hypothesize that the structural rigor and logical sequencing inherent in coding syntax help the model internalize abstract reasoning patterns that transfer directly to formal mathematical deduction, even when no code interpreter is involved.

It is important to note, however, that the method of integration matters. While sequential two-stage training (Code $\rightarrow$ Math) proved optimal for reasoning, attempting to mix code and math tokens simultaneously in a single-stage training setting compromised the model's ability to perform non-tool mathematical reasoning. The authors conjecture that smaller models, such as the $1.3$B parameter model used in their ablation studies, lack the capacity to fully assimilate both distributions simultaneously without interference. Consequently, DeepSeekMath-Base $7$B was initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v$1.5$ and then subjected to continual pre-training on a mixed but carefully distributed corpus (including $20\%$ GitHub code and $4\%$ AlgebraicStack alongside math data) to maintain coding proficiency while leveraging the reasoning benefits established during the initial code-centric phase. Ultimately, the paper concludes that code training provides a unique syntactic and logical scaffold that amplifies general reasoning capabilities, making it a superior starting point for mathematical specialization compared to general language models.

### Does Reinforcement Learning actually improve fundamental capabilities or just output consistency?
Question:
In Section 4.2.2 ('Why RL Works?'), the authors present an interesting finding: RL enhances Maj@K (majority vote accuracy) but does not significantly improve Pass@K (pass rate considering any correct sample in K attempts). 

They conclude that RL makes the output distribution 'more robust' rather than enhancing 'fundamental capabilities.' If Pass@K doesn't improve, doesn't that imply the model isn't actually learning to solve harder problems it couldn't solve before, but rather just becoming more consistent at solving problems it already knew how to solve occasionally? How does this align with the significant jump in single-sample accuracy on MATH (from 46.8% to 51.7%)?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction between the significant jump in single-sample accuracy and the stagnant Pass@K metric stems from a nuanced distinction between expanding a model's *capability boundary* versus refining its *output distribution*. As detailed in Section 4.2.2 ("Why RL Works?"), the authors observe that while Reinforcement Learning (specifically their GRPO algorithm) substantially improves Maj@K (majority vote accuracy), it does not yield a commensurate improvement in Pass@K. This specific pattern indicates that the RL phase is not teaching the model to solve entirely new classes of problems that were previously unsolvable; if it were, we would expect Pass@K—the probability of generating at least one correct solution in $K$ attempts—to rise significantly as the model discovers new solution paths for hard problems.

Instead, the data suggests that the Instruction Tuned (SFT) model already possesses the latent knowledge to solve many of these difficult problems but suffers from a "misalignment" where it frequently generates incorrect or suboptimal reasoning traces despite having the underlying capability. The RL process acts as a robustness filter, reshaping the probability landscape of the model's outputs to suppress erroneous reasoning patterns and amplify the correct ones that were already present but less probable in the SFT distribution. Consequently, when you sample $K$ times, the set of unique solvable problems (Pass@K) remains roughly constant because the ceiling of what the model *can* do hasn't moved much; however, the likelihood of hitting those correct solutions in any single attempt increases dramatically. This explains the surge in single-sample accuracy (from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH) and Maj@K: the model has become far more consistent and reliable at accessing its existing knowledge, effectively converting "lucky guesses" or rare correct generations into deterministic behaviors. The authors explicitly conclude that this improvement is attributed to making the output distribution more robust rather than enhancing fundamental capabilities, implying that future gains in solving truly novel, harder problems will likely require advances in data sourcing or decoding strategies rather than just optimization of the current policy.

Why was this specific formulation chosen over adding it to the reward? Does moving it outside the clipping mechanism change how strictly the policy is constrained from deviating from the reference model compared to standard PPO?
Answer:
The specific formulation of the KL divergence in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) serves two primary purposes: simplifying the advantage calculation by decoupling the penalty from the reward signal, and providing a statistically unbiased estimate of the divergence. In standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the KL penalty is typically subtracted directly from the reward at each timestep, defined as $r_t = r_\varphi - \beta \log(\pi/\pi_{ref})$. This approach requires the advantage $A_t$ to be computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which relies on a learned value function $V_\psi$ to establish a baseline. However, GRPO eliminates the need for a separate critic model by estimating the baseline from the average reward of a group of outputs sampled for the same question. If the KL penalty were embedded within the reward signal $r_t$ as in PPO, it would contaminate the group-relative advantage calculation $\hat{A}_{i,t}$, making it difficult to isolate the relative performance of different outputs within the group purely based on the task reward. By moving the KL term outside the min/clip operation and subtracting it directly from the objective function, GRPO ensures that the advantage reflects only the relative quality of the generated responses, while the regularization acts as a global constraint on the policy update.

### How was benchmark contamination avoided given reliance on Common Crawl?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath Corpus is built entirely from Common Crawl using a fastText classifier iteratively trained on seeds like OpenWebMath. Since benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH are widely available online, how exactly did their decontamination pipeline work?

The paper mentions filtering any text segment containing a 10-gram match with benchmark substrings. Given that many websites might host solutions or discussions of these famous problems without copying them verbatim (e.g., paraphrased solutions), was there any risk of subtle leakage? Did they perform any semantic similarity checks beyond exact n-gram matching?
Answer:
The decontamination pipeline described in the paper relies exclusively on strict lexical matching rather than semantic similarity checks, meaning there is indeed a theoretical risk of subtle leakage from paraphrased solutions that do not share exact substrings with the benchmarks.

According to the "Data Collection and Decontamination" section, the authors explicitly state that they filter out web pages containing questions or answers from English benchmarks (like GSM8K and MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (like CMATH and AGIEval) by removing any text segment that contains a **10-gram string** matching exactly with any substring from the evaluation sets. For benchmark texts that are shorter than 10 grams but contain at least 3 grams, they employ **exact matching** to filter contaminated pages. The paper cites following the methodology of *DeepSeek-Coder* for this process.

Crucially, the text makes no mention of employing embedding-based semantic similarity models, fuzzy matching algorithms, or paraphrase detection tools to catch variations where the mathematical logic or solution structure is identical but the wording differs. The strategy assumes that if a webpage hosts a benchmark problem or its solution, it will likely retain a contiguous sequence of at least 10 tokens (or 3 for very short segments) identical to the official benchmark text. While this is highly effective against verbatim copies, discussions, or slightly modified reposts found on forums or educational sites, it would technically fail to detect a human-written explanation that completely rephrases the problem statement and solution without preserving those specific n-gram sequences. Consequently, while the pipeline rigorously removes direct overlaps, the possibility of "soft" contamination via semantically equivalent but lexically distinct content remains an inherent limitation of using only exact n-gram filtering.

### Understanding the 'Unified Paradigm' gradient coefficient differences between RFT and GRPO
Question:
In Section 4.2.1, the authors propose a unified paradigm where methods differ by Data Source, Reward Function, and Gradient Coefficient ($GC$). They state that Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT) uniformly reinforces all correct responses, whereas GRPO adjusts the gradient coefficient based on relative reward values.

Could someone elaborate on what happens mathematically when GRPO encounters a 'correct' answer that has a lower relative score within its group compared to other correct answers? Does GRPO effectively penalize a correct answer if another sampled answer is 'more correct' or has higher confidence, whereas RFT would treat them identically? How does this relative scaling prevent mode collapse?
Answer:
The distinction you are asking about lies precisely in how the **Gradient Coefficient ($GC$)** is constructed within the unified paradigm proposed in Section 4.2.1. In this framework, the update rule for any method is driven by a coefficient that scales the log-probability gradient, determining whether a token is reinforced, penalized, or ignored.

For **Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT)**, the reward function operates on a binary basis derived from rule-based correctness (e.g., does the final answer match the ground truth?). Consequently, the gradient coefficient acts as a switch: if a sampled response is correct, it receives a uniform positive reinforcement regardless of its specific qualities relative to other samples; if it is incorrect, it typically receives no reinforcement (or is simply discarded depending on the specific implementation variant described). Mathematically, this means every correct solution contributes equally to pushing the policy toward that trajectory, treating a "lucky" correct guess identical to a highly confident, robust derivation.

In contrast, **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** calculates the gradient coefficient based on the **relative advantage** of a response within a group of $G$ samples generated for the same question. As detailed in Section 3.1.2, GRPO computes rewards for all $G$ outputs, normalizes them by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation to obtain $\tilde{r}_i$. This normalized score becomes the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ applied to every token in the sequence. This normalization is the critical mathematical mechanism that answers your question: if GRPO encounters a 'correct' answer that has a lower relative score (perhaps due to lower confidence from the reward model, inefficiency, or being less preferred than other correct variants in the group), its normalized advantage $\tilde{r}_i$ will be lower than that of a 'better' correct answer. If a correct answer falls below the group average reward, its advantage becomes negative. In this scenario, GRPO effectively **penalizes** that specific correct response by decreasing its likelihood, whereas Online RFT would have blindly reinforced it.

This relative scaling prevents mode collapse and enhances robustness by forcing the model to distinguish not just between right and wrong, but between *degrees* of quality among right answers. Mode collapse often occurs when a model latches onto a single narrow pattern of correct reasoning and ignores other valid strategies. By using a dynamic baseline (the group mean) rather than a fixed zero baseline for correctness, GRPO ensures that only responses that are *better than average* for that specific prompt receive positive updates. This encourages the policy to explore diverse reasoning paths that yield high rewards rather than converging prematurely to a single local optimum. As noted in the "Observation about Gradient Coefficient" paragraph, this ability to differentially reinforce and penalize based on magnitude allows GRPO to surpass Online RFT, refining the output distribution to be more robust (improving Maj@K) rather than just finding one successful path (Pass@K).
