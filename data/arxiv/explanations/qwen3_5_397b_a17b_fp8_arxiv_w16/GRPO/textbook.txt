# Introduction: The State of Mathematical Reasoning in LLMs

Mathematical reasoning represents one of the most formidable frontiers in the development of artificial intelligence. Unlike general natural language processing tasks, which often rely on pattern recognition and statistical likelihoods derived from vast corpora of text, mathematics demands a rigorous adherence to logic, structure, and precise sequential deduction. For Large Language Models (LLMs), this distinction creates a unique set of challenges. While modern LLMs have revolutionized approaches to quantitative and geometric reasoning benchmarks, bridging the gap between linguistic fluency and mathematical rigor remains an open problem. This chapter explores the current landscape of mathematical reasoning in AI, contrasting the capabilities of proprietary closed-source models with the limitations of existing open-source alternatives. Central to this discussion is the introduction of DeepSeekMath, a domain-specific language model designed to push the boundaries of what is possible with open weights. By leveraging scalable pre-training on meticulously curated web data and introducing a novel reinforcement learning algorithm known as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), DeepSeekMath achieves performance levels on competition-level benchmarks that approach those of industry-leading proprietary systems like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra, all without reliance on external toolkits or ensemble voting techniques during inference.

## The Complexity of Mathematical Reasoning in AI

The inherent difficulty of mathematical reasoning for language models stems from its complex and highly structured nature. In standard conversational or narrative generation, a model can often recover from minor logical inconsistencies or factual hallucinations without completely derailing the coherence of the output. Mathematics, however, operates under a strict regime where a single error in an early step of a derivation propagates through subsequent calculations, inevitably leading to an incorrect final solution. This sensitivity requires models to maintain long-range dependencies and execute multi-step chains of thought with high precision. The challenge is not merely retrieving mathematical facts but synthesizing them into valid proofs or solutions that satisfy logical constraints.

Furthermore, mathematical problems vary wildly in complexity, ranging from elementary arithmetic to college-level calculus and abstract algebra. Successfully navigating this spectrum requires a model to possess not only broad knowledge coverage but also deep reasoning capabilities that allow it to decompose complex problems into manageable sub-tasks. Traditional language modeling objectives, which focus on predicting the next token based on local context, do not explicitly optimize for this type of structured deductive reasoning. Consequently, achieving high performance on mathematical benchmarks necessitates specialized training strategies that go beyond generic pre-training on internet text. The structured nature of math means that models must learn to generate self-contained solutions that are logically sound from start to finish, a capability that is difficult to instill without targeted data curation and advanced optimization techniques.

## Limitations of Current Open-Source Models vs. GPT-4/Gemini-Ultra

Despite the rapid advancements in the field, a significant disparity exists between the mathematical capabilities of closed-source proprietary models and those available as open-source software. State-of-the-art systems such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in solving complex mathematical problems, often serving as powerful assistants for humans tackling intricate quantitative tasks. These models have set a high bar for performance on standardized benchmarks, establishing themselves as the gold standard in AI-driven mathematical reasoning. However, their internal architectures, training data, and weight parameters remain inaccessible to the broader research community, limiting transparency and hindering independent verification or further innovation built upon their foundations.

In contrast, currently accessible open-source models considerably trail behind these proprietary giants in terms of performance. While there have been notable efforts to enhance open models through fine-tuning and domain-specific adaptation, none have consistently matched the accuracy or robustness of GPT-4 or Gemini-Ultra on rigorous academic benchmarks. This performance gap has led to a perception that achieving state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning requires massive computational resources and exclusive datasets only available to large technology corporations. The inability of open models to compete on equal footing restricts the democratization of advanced AI capabilities, leaving researchers and developers dependent on black-box APIs for high-stakes applications involving quantitative analysis. Bridging this divide is critical for fostering an open ecosystem where mathematical reasoning tools are transparent, reproducible, and accessible to all.

## Overview of DeepSeekMath: Goals and Achievements

DeepSeekMath emerges as a direct response to the limitations facing the open-source community, aiming to deliver a domain-specific language model that significantly narrows the performance gap with proprietary systems. The primary goal of DeepSeekMath is to demonstrate that open models can achieve competitive mathematical reasoning capabilities through strategic data engineering and algorithmic innovation rather than sheer scale alone. The project introduces DeepSeekMath 7B, a model initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B and subjected to continued pre-training on a massive corpus of 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl alongside natural language and code data. This approach leverages the latent potential of publicly available web data, transforming it into a high-quality resource for mathematical education and reasoning.

The achievements of DeepSeekMath are substantial and redefine expectations for 7-billion parameter models. On the competition-level MATH benchmark, DeepSeekMath 7B achieves an impressive score of 51.7% without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques such as self-consistency ensembles during the initial evaluation phase. This performance level approaches that of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4, marking a significant milestone for open-source initiatives. When employing self-consistency over 64 samples—a technique where multiple solutions are generated and the most frequent answer is selected—the model's accuracy rises further to 60.9%. These results indicate that DeepSeekMath possesses not only strong single-pass reasoning abilities but also a robust underlying distribution of correct solutions that can be exploited through sampling strategies. Furthermore, the model demonstrates versatility across languages, showing marked improvements on Chinese mathematical benchmarks due to its multilingual training corpus, thereby addressing a limitation in many previous English-centric efforts.

Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus: Data Engineering at Scale

The development of large language models capable of sophisticated mathematical reasoning relies fundamentally on the quality and scale of the data used during pre-training. While model architecture and optimization algorithms are critical, the DeepSeekMath study posits that the primary driver for achieving state-of-the-art performance in open-source models is a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline. This chapter explores the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive dataset comprising 120 billion tokens of mathematical content sourced primarily from the public web. Unlike traditional approaches that rely heavily on curated academic repositories, this work demonstrates that publicly available web data, when filtered through rigorous iterative processes, contains a vast reservoir of high-quality mathematical knowledge. The creation of this corpus involves a sophisticated interplay of machine learning classifiers, domain-based expansion strategies, human annotation, and strict decontamination protocols to ensure the integrity of evaluation benchmarks. Furthermore, the corpus is designed to be inherently multilingual, addressing a gap in previous datasets that often prioritize English to the detriment of other languages such as Chinese. By understanding the engineering behind this corpus, one gains insight into how data curation can compensate for smaller model sizes, allowing a 7-billion parameter model to compete with significantly larger counterparts.

# The Potential of Web Data vs. Academic Papers (ArXiv)

A prevailing assumption in the field of mathematical language modeling has been that academic papers, particularly those hosted on arXiv, represent the gold standard for training data. It is intuitive to believe that formal scientific literature, replete with rigorous proofs and structured arguments, would provide the most effective signal for teaching a model mathematical reasoning. Consequently, many prior efforts have constructed training corpora where arXiv papers constitute the majority of the tokens. However, the empirical findings presented in the DeepSeekMath research challenge this convention, suggesting that web-scraped data may hold untapped potential that exceeds that of academic repositories when properly harvested. The DeepSeekMath Corpus is built on the hypothesis that the Common Crawl—a massive archive of publicly accessible web pages—contains valuable mathematical information that has been largely underutilized due to noise and lack of filtering.

The study provides compelling evidence through controlled experiments comparing models trained on web-derived data versus those trained exclusively on arXiv papers. When models were trained on corpora consisting entirely of arXiv data, such as MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama, the results were counter-intuitive: there were no notable improvements in mathematical reasoning capabilities across various benchmarks. In some instances, performance even deteriorated compared to baselines. This stagnation occurred despite arXiv papers being dense with mathematical content. The researchers observed this phenomenon across different model scales, from 1.3 billion to 7 billion parameters, indicating that simply ingesting formal academic text does not automatically translate to improved problem-solving skills on standardized tests like GSM8K or MATH. This suggests that while arXiv papers are rich in content, they may lack the diversity of problem styles, explanatory depths, or pedagogical structures found in broader educational web contexts, or perhaps their specific formatting and linguistic style do not generalize well to the types of queries posed in benchmarks.

In stark contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus, derived almost entirely from Common Crawl via a specialized filtering pipeline, yielded dramatic improvements. The final corpus comprises 120 billion tokens, which is approximately seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath. Models pre-trained on this web-sourced corpus demonstrated superior performance on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks compared to models trained on arXiv-heavy datasets. The success of this approach indicates that the "long tail" of the internet—including educational forums, course notes, solution manuals, and tutorial sites—offers a richer and more varied distribution of mathematical reasoning patterns than formal publications alone. The sheer scale achievable through web mining allows for a breadth of exposure that static academic databases cannot match. Consequently, the lesson drawn from DeepSeekMath is that the key to unlocking mathematical reasoning lies not necessarily in the prestige of the source material but in the volume and diversity of high-quality examples extracted from the open web through intelligent filtering mechanisms.

# Iterative Data Selection Pipeline using fastText Classifiers

Extracting high-quality mathematical content from the chaotic expanse of Common Crawl requires more than simple keyword matching; it demands a robust classification system capable of distinguishing genuine mathematical discourse from irrelevant text containing occasional numbers or symbols. To achieve this, DeepSeekMath employs an iterative data selection pipeline centered around a fastText-based classifier. FastText was chosen for its efficiency in handling large-scale text classification tasks, allowing for rapid scoring of billions of web pages. The core philosophy behind this pipeline is recursion: the classifier is not static but evolves over multiple iterations, becoming increasingly proficient at identifying relevant content as it is exposed to more diverse and verified examples. This iterative refinement is crucial because an initial classifier trained on a limited seed set will inevitably miss valid mathematical pages that differ stylistically or topically from the seed examples.

The process begins with an initial training phase where the fastText model learns to differentiate between positive and negative examples. Positive examples are drawn from a seed corpus known to contain high-quality mathematics, while negative examples are sampled randomly from the broader Common Crawl dump to represent typical non-mathematical web content. # Pre-Training Strategy: From Code to Mathematics

The development of DeepSeekMath-Base represents a strategic departure from conventional approaches to building mathematical language models. Rather than relying solely on scaling model parameters or aggregating massive quantities of academic papers, the creators of DeepSeekMath focused on the quality and composition of the pre-training data, as well as the foundational capabilities of the initialization model. This chapter delves into the specific architectural decisions that enabled a 7-billion parameter model to compete with closed-source giants containing hundreds of billions of parameters. Central to this success is the hypothesis that code training serves as a critical catalyst for mathematical reasoning, a premise tested through rigorous ablation studies involving different training stages and data mixtures. Furthermore, the chapter examines the counter-intuitive finding that arXiv papers, traditionally considered the gold standard for mathematical pre-training, may offer diminishing returns when used in isolation compared to carefully filtered web data. By understanding these pre-training dynamics, students can appreciate how domain-specific competencies are cultivated in large language models (LLMs) not just through volume, but through the syntactic and logical structures inherent in the training corpus.

# Initialization Choice: Why Start with DeepSeek-Coder?

The foundation of DeepSeekMath-Base lies in its initialization with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a model previously trained extensively on code. This decision was not arbitrary but stemmed from empirical observations regarding the transferability of skills between computer programming and mathematical problem-solving. The underlying intuition is that both coding and mathematics require precise logical structuring, adherence to strict syntax, and the ability to decompose complex problems into executable steps. When a model learns to write code, it internalizes patterns of causality and procedural logic that are directly applicable to deriving mathematical proofs or solving quantitative word problems. The researchers posited that starting from a general-purpose language model might require the network to learn these structural constraints from scratch during mathematical pre-training, whereas a code-initialized model already possesses a latent representation of algorithmic thinking.

This choice addresses a long-standing question in the field: does code training genuinely improve reasoning abilities? While often hypothesized, concrete evidence had been scarce prior to this work. By initializing DeepSeekMath with a coder base, the authors leveraged a model that was already adept at handling formal languages and symbolic manipulation. Code involves variables, functions, loops, and conditionals, all of which map conceptually to algebraic expressions, functional relationships, and logical deductions in mathematics. Consequently, the transition from generating Python scripts to generating chain-of-thought mathematical derivations becomes smoother. The model does not need to relearn how to maintain context over long sequences of symbols or how to follow a rigid set of rules; it simply needs to adapt these existing capabilities to the semantic domain of mathematics. This strategic initialization effectively reduced the learning curve for mathematical reasoning, allowing the subsequent pre-training phase to focus on acquiring domain-specific knowledge rather than fundamental logical scaffolding.

# Experimental Analysis: Code Training as a Catalyst for Reasoning

To rigorously validate the hypothesis that code training enhances mathematical reasoning, the researchers conducted a series of controlled experiments using a 1.3 billion parameter variant of their model architecture. These experiments were designed to isolate the effects of code exposure versus general text exposure on downstream mathematical performance. The results provided compelling evidence that code training acts as a potent catalyst, improving the model's ability to solve problems both with and without external tools. In scenarios where models were permitted to use tools—specifically writing and executing Python programs to solve problems—the benefits of prior code training were immediate and substantial. A model subjected to code training demonstrated a significantly higher proficiency in generating syntactically correct and logically sound programs compared to models trained only on general text or pure mathematics from the outset.

Beyond tool use, the experiments revealed that code training also bolsters "native" mathematical reasoning—the ability to solve problems through step-by-step textual deduction without invoking an interpreter. In a two-stage training setup where models were first trained on code tokens and subsequently on math tokens, the initial code phase yielded moderate improvements in purely textual reasoning tasks. More importantly, it appeared to accelerate the efficiency of the subsequent math training phase. The model exposed to code first reached higher performance plateaus faster than its counterparts, suggesting that the logical frameworks learned during coding facilitated quicker absorption of mathematical concepts. However, the analysis also uncovered nuances regarding model capacity; when code and math tokens were mixed simultaneously in a single-stage training regime for smaller models, there was sometimes a compromise in non-tool-based reasoning performance. This suggests that while code is beneficial, smaller models may struggle to assimilate both distributions simultaneously without a sequential curriculum, reinforcing the value of the two-stage approach or careful data balancing for larger architectures like the 7B DeepSeekMath-Base.

# Two-Stage vs. One-Stage Training Regimes

The investigation into training regimes highlighted distinct trade-offs between sequential (two-stage) and simultaneous (one-stage) learning strategies. In the two-stage approach, exemplified by the configuration "Code Training for 400B Tokens $\rightarrow$ Math Training for 150B Tokens," the model undergoes a dedicated period of immersion in programming logic before shifting focus entirely to mathematics. This regimen proved highly effective for program-aided reasoning tasks. The initial saturation with code established robust pathways for symbolic manipulation, which were then refined and specialized duringthe math-only phase. This sequence avoided interference between domains during the critical early phases of learning, allowing the model to master the syntax of code before layering on the semantics of advanced mathematics. Conversely, a control experiment replacing code with general text in the first stage ("General Training $\rightarrow$ Math Training") failed to yield similar gains, confirming that it is specifically the structural nature of code, rather than just additional pre-training volume, that drives improvement.

In contrast, one-stage training involved mixing code tokens and math tokens within a single continuous stream—for instance, training on a mixture of 400B code tokens and 150B math tokens simultaneously. While this approach offered advantages in mitigating "catastrophic forgetting"—a phenomenon where learning new information causes abrupt degradation in previously acquired skills—it presented challenges for smaller models attempting to learn both domains concurrently. For deepseek-math-base 7B (the final product), an effective balance was struck not by strictly separating stages forever but by curating a specific data mixture during continual pre-training that retained enough code exposure (20% GitHub) alongside heavy math emphasis (56% DeepSeekMath Corpus). The experimental findings suggest that while one-stage mixing preserves coding fluency better than strict sequential switching which might degrade coding skills if not managed well (though here they noted mixing alleviates forgetting seen if you switch completely away), optimal performance requires acknowledging capacity limits: smaller models benefit from staging order (code first), while larger mixes require careful proportioning (as done in final DeepSeekMath Base) ensuring neither domain overwhelms representational capacity too early causing instability or poor convergence rates across either task type independently . Ultimately , these insights guided construction final recipe : initialize w/ coder -> continue w/ balanced mix heavily weighted toward curated web -based maths .

Supervised Fine-Tuning for Instruction Following

The journey from a powerful base language model to a specialized mathematical reasoning agent requires a critical intermediate step known as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). While the pre-training phase equips the model with vast amounts of world knowledge and syntactic structure, it does not inherently teach the model how to follow specific instructions or structure its output in a way that facilitates complex logical deduction. In the development of DeepSeekMath, this transition is marked by the creation of DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, a model derived from the DeepSeekMath-Base 7B through rigorous instruction tuning. This chapter details the meticulous curation of the training dataset, the integration of diverse reasoning formats such as Chain-of-Thought and Program-of-Thought, the specific hyperparameter configurations used during training, and the comprehensive evaluation strategy that positions DeepSeekMath-Instruct against both open-source and proprietary contemporaries. The goal of this stage is not merely to memorize solutions but to instill a robust capability for step-by-step reasoning and tool utilization, bridging the gap between raw linguistic probability and actionable mathematical problem-solving.

# Curating High-Quality Instruction Tuning Data

The foundation of any successful instruction-tuned model lies in the quality, diversity, and relevance of its training data. For DeepSeekMath-Instruct, the researchers constructed a specialized mathematical instruction-tuning dataset designed to cover a broad spectrum of mathematical fields and complexity levels. This dataset is bilingual, encompassing both English and Chinese problems, reflecting the multilingual nature of the underlying pre-training corpus. The total volume of this curated dataset comprises 776,000 training examples, a figure carefully selected to balance computational feasibility with the need for sufficient exposure to varied problem types.

The curation process involved aggregating data from several distinct sources to ensure comprehensive coverage. For the English portion of the dataset, the team annotated problems from established benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, specifically generating solutions that integrated tool usage. This ensures that the model learns not just abstract reasoning but also practical computation. Additionally, the dataset incorporates a subset of MathInstruct and the training set of Lila-OOD. These sources were chosen because they provide problems solved via Chain-of-Thought (CoT) or Program-of-Thought (PoT) methodologies. The resulting English collection spans diverse mathematical domains, including algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry, ensuring that the model does not become overly specialized in a single sub-field at the expense of others.

Parallel to the English efforts, a significant portion of the data was dedicated to Chinese mathematical content. This subset focuses on K-12 level mathematics, covering 76 distinct sub-topics ranging from linear equations to more advanced secondary school concepts. Crucially, the solutions for these Chinese problems were annotated in both Chain-of-Thought and tool-integrated reasoning formats. This dual-language approach distinguishes DeepSeekMath from many predecessors that rely exclusively on English-centric corpora. By including high-quality non-English data, the developers aimed to prevent the degradation of performance in non-English contexts—a common pitfall in models trained solely on English mathematical texts—and to leverage the rich educational resources available in Chinese curricula. The combination of these sources creates a heterogeneous training environment that forces the model to generalize its reasoning capabilities across languages and problem structures.

# Solution Formats: CoT, PoT, and Tool-Integrated Reasoning

A defining characteristic of the DeepSeekMath-Instruct training regimen is its emphasis on structured solution formats. Unlike standard conversational fine-tuning which might prioritize brevity or politeness, mathematical reasoning demands explicit logical progression. The dataset pairs every problem with solutions formatted in three primary styles: Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tool-Integrated Reasoning. Each format serves a unique pedagogical purpose in shaping the model's internal reasoning mechanisms.

Chain-of-Thought prompting involves breaking down a complex problem into a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer. In the context of DeepSeekMath, CoT data teaches the model to articulate its logic verbally, mimicking the way a human mathematician writes out a proof or derives a solution on paper. This format is essential for developing the model's ability to handle multi-step deductive reasoning where each step relies on the validity of the previous one. By exposing the model to thousands of examples where the path from premise to conclusion is explicitly laid out,the SFT process encourages the generation of coherent, self-contained textual solutions that do not rely on external aids.

Program-of-Thought represents a shift from purely verbal reasoning to executable logic. In PoT scenarios, the model is trained to generate Python code that encapsulates the reasoning process. Instead of describing how to calculate a value in natural language, the model writes a script using libraries such as `math` or `sympy` to perform the computation. The execution result of this program is then treated as the answer. This format is particularly effective for problems involving heavy arithmetic or symbolic manipulation where language models are prone to hallucination or calculation errors. By learning PoT, DeepSeekMath-Instruct acquires the skill to delegate precise computational tasks to an interpreter while retaining control over the logical flow of the solution through code structure.

Tool-Integrated Reasoning combines elements of both CoT and PoT into a hybrid approach. In this format, solutions interleave natural language explanations with code blocks executed by an external tool. The model learns to decide dynamically when to reason textually and when to invoke a calculator or code interpreter. For instance, it might describe a geometric setup in text, write code to compute specific coordinates or areas, parsethe output back into text, and continuethe logical derivation. This format mirrors real-world mathematical practice where humans use scratchpads or calculators alongside their theoretical work.The inclusionof tool-integrated data inthe SFT phaseis criticalfor enablingthemodelto solveproblems that exceedits inherentparametricknowledgeof arithmeticfacts,fosteringacapabilityforcomplex，tool-augmentedproblem-solvingthatpurelytext-basedmodelslack。
