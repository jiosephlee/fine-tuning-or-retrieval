# Quality Over Quantity: How DeepSeekMath Beat Giant Models with Just 7 Billion Parameters

In the race to build smarter artificial intelligence, the prevailing wisdom has often been simple: bigger is better. For years, researchers have chased performance by stacking billions of parameters onto massive neural networks, assuming that scale alone would solve complex reasoning problems. However, a new study titled "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models" challenges this assumption head-on. The authors introduce DeepSeekMath 7B, a remarkably compact model that not only outperforms open-source giants but also rivals closed-source behemoths like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 on rigorous mathematical benchmarks. What makes this achievement truly startling is that DeepSeekMath 7B accomplishes this feat with only 7 billion parameters, dwarfing the performance of Minerva 540B, a model with 77 times more parameters.

The secret to this efficiency lies not in architectural magic, but in a relentless focus on data quality. The research team demonstrates that a smaller model, when trained on a meticulously curated, high-quality dataset, can surpass much larger counterparts trained on noisier or less relevant data. This post explores how the creation of the DeepSeekMath Corpus and a novel reinforcement learning technique called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) allowed a tiny model to punch far above its weight class.

## The Myth of Parameter Scaling

For a long time, the trajectory of Large Language Model (LLM) development suggested that mathematical reasoning was a resource-intensive task requiring massive scale. Minerva, a prominent model developed by Google, achieved state-of-the-art results by leveraging 540 billion parameters. It seemed logical that to beat Minerva, one would need an even larger model. DeepSeekMath disrupts this narrative by achieving a score of 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark without any external toolkits or voting ensembles. In comparison, the colossal Minerva 540B struggles to match this performance despite its immense size.

This disparity highlights a critical insight: the number of parameters is not the sole determinant of reasoning capability. The DeepSeekMath team found that starting with a code-trained base model and continuing pre-training on highly specialized mathematical data yielded superior results compared to simply scaling up a general-purpose model. Their base model, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, scored 36.2% on the MATH dataset, already outperforming Minerva 540B. This suggests that the industry may have been over-investing in compute and under-investing in the curation of training data.

## Mining Gold from the Web: The DeepSeekMath Corpus

The cornerstone of DeepSeekMath's success is its training data. The researchers constructed the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive collection comprising 120 billion math-related tokens sourced entirely from Common Crawl, the public archive of web pages. To put this in perspective, this corpus is nearly seven times larger than the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath, another popular open dataset.

Creating such a dataset required more than just downloading random web pages; it demanded a sophisticated engineering pipeline. The team employed an iterative approach using a fastText-based classifier to distinguish high-quality mathematical content from the noise of the internet. They began with a seed corpus of known high-quality math texts from OpenWebMath as positive examples and diverse non-math web pages as negative examples. This classifier was then used to mine additional positive instances from Common Crawl.

Crucially, the process did not stop at automatic filtering. The team organized the crawled data by domain and manually annotated URLs within domains that showed high concentrations of mathematical content, such as `mathoverflow.net`. These human-verified examples were fed back into the classifier to refine its accuracy over four iterations. By ranking pages based on their predicted scores and keeping only the top-tier content, they ensured that every token contributed meaningfully to the model's reasoning abilities. Furthermore, unlike many predecessors that focused exclusively on English, this corpus includes significant amounts of Chinese mathematical content, enabling the model to excel in multilingual benchmarks as well.

Interestingly, the study also debunked a common belief regarding data sources. While many previous efforts heavily relied on arXiv papers for mathematical training, DeepSeekMath's experiments revealed that training solely on arXiv data brought no notable improvements—and sometimes even degraded performance—on standard reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. This finding suggests that formal academic papers might lack the pedagogical structure or diversity needed to teach robust problem-solving skills compared tothe varied explanations found on educational websites and forums.

## The Power of Code and Continued Pre-training

Another pivotal decision in the DeepSeekMath strategy was initialization. Rather than starting from scratch or using a generic language model checkpoint, the team initialized their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This choice was driven by the observation that code training inherently improves logical reasoning capabilities. The experiments confirmed that models exposed to code prior to math training performed better on mathematical tasks, both with and without tool use.

The final pre-training mix consisted of 56% data from their custom DeepSeekMath Corpus, supplemented by code from GitHub (20%), arXiv papers (10%), AlgebraicStack (4%), and general natural language data (10%). This balanced diet allowed the model to retain its coding proficiency while absorbing deep mathematical knowledge. The result was a base model that didn't just memorize formulas but learned to reason step-by-step, evidenced by its strong performance on Chain-of-Thought prompting tasks across eight different benchmarks.

# Goodbye Critic Model: Introducing GRPO, The Memory-Efficient RL Algorithm Changing the Game

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a cornerstone for pushing the boundaries of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in complex domains like mathematical reasoning. However, traditional RL approaches often come with a steep price tag in terms of computational resources. The standard algorithm, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), typically requires training a separate "critic" model—a value function approximator roughly the same size as the policy model itself—to estimate advantages and stabilize training. This doubles the memory footprint and significantly increases the engineering complexity required to scale these systems.

In their recent paper, *DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models*, the researchers introduce a novel solution to this bottleneck: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This new algorithm eliminates the need for a dedicated critic model entirely. Instead, it estimates baselines directly from group scores generated by the policy model itself. The result is a drastic reduction in memory usage while achieving performance that rivals closed-source giants like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra on competition-level math benchmarks.

## The Bottleneck of Traditional PPO

To understand why GRPO is such a significant departure, we must first look at how PPO operates in the context of LLMs. PPO is an actor-critic algorithm. The "actor" is the language model generating text, while the "critic" is a separate model trained to predict the expected return (value) of a given state. During training, the advantage $A_t$—which measures how much better a specific action is compared to the average—is calculated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). This calculation relies heavily on the values predicted by the critic model $V_{\psi}$.

The objective function for PPO maximizes a surrogate goal involving the ratio of probabilities between the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, scaled by this advantage $A_t$. Because the critic must be trained alongside the policy and maintained in memory, scaling PPO to large models becomes prohibitively expensive. Furthermore, in language modeling tasks where rewards are often sparse (assigned only at the end of a sequence), training a token-level value function can be unstable and inaccurate.

## How GRPO Works: Strength in Numbers

GRPO addresses these inefficiencies by rethinking how advantages are estimated. Rather than relying on a learned value function, GRPO leverages the statistical power of sampling groups of outputs. For every input question $q$, the algorithm samples a group of $G$ distinct outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$.

Instead of asking a critic model "how good is this output?", GRPO asks "how does this output compare to others generated for the same question?" The rewards for these $G$ outputs are computed using a reward model, and then normalized within the group. Specifically, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for any token in an output is derived from the normalized reward of that entire output sequence relative to the group mean and standard deviation. If an output receives a higher reward than the group average, its tokens receive positive advantages; if lower, they receive negative advantages.

This approach aligns naturally with how reward models are typically trained—on pairwise or listwise comparisons of outputs for identical prompts. By removing the critic model, GRPO simplifies the training architecture and significantly reduces GPU memory requirements. The optimization objective focuses solely on updating the policy model $\pi_\theta$ using these group-relative advantages, while regularizing via a direct KL divergence term against a reference model to prevent drift.

## Performance That Defies Expectations

The efficacy of GRPO was validated through rigorous experimentation with DeepSeekMath 7B. Starting from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which was already strong due to extensive supervised fine-tuning on chain-of-thought and tool-integrated data, the team applied GRPO using only a subset of English instruction tuning data related to GSM8K and MATH benchmarks.

The results were striking. After reinforcement learning with GRPO, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieved an accuracy of 88.2% on GSM8K and 51.7% on MATH using chain-of-thought reasoning alone. This performance surpasses all existing open-source models ranging from 7B to 70B parameters and even outperforms many proprietary closed-source models on these specific tasks. Remarkably, self-consistency over 64 samples pushed the MATH score to 60.9%, approaching the capabilities of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 without relying on external toolkits or massive ensemble voting techniques during inference.

Perhaps most impressively, these gains extended beyond just in-domain tasks where RL training occurred; out-of-domain benchmarks also saw improvements suggesting that GRPO helps generalize reasoning capabilities rather than simply memorizing patterns for specific datasets ($CMATH$: 84.6% $\rightarrow$ 88%).

## Why It Matters for Open Source AI

The introduction of GRPO represents more than just an incremental improvement; it lowers barrier to entry for high-performance RL fine-tuning previously reserved for well-funded labs with access to massive compute clusters. By discarding critic models entirely reduces resource consumption dramatically making sophisticated alignment techniques feasible for smaller teams working with open weights models .

# Code First, Math Later: Why Training on Programming Makes AI Better at Calculus

Does learning to write Python code actually help an artificial intelligence solve complex calculus problems? It sounds counter-intuitive. One might assume that to get better at math, a model simply needs more math data. However, the latest research from DeepSeek suggests a different path: mastering code first creates a stronger foundation for mathematical reasoning.

In their paper "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models," the researchers introduce DeepSeekMath 7B, a model that rivals giants like GPT-4 on competition-level math benchmarks despite having a fraction of the parameters. A key driver of this success is a specific training curriculum: code training precedes math pre-training. Furthermore, the team uncovered a surprising finding that challenges common assumptions in the field—training on massive amounts of scientific papers from arXiv provided negligible benefits compared to carefully filtered web data.

## The Power of Code Initialization

For years, a popular hypothesis in the AI community has been that code training improves general reasoning abilities, but concrete evidence has remained elusive. DeepSeekMath provides a compelling partial answer to this question, specifically within the domain of mathematics. The researchers found that initializing a model with code training significantly boosts its ability to solve mathematical problems, both when using external tools and when relying purely on internal logic.

The DeepSeekMath 7B model was not built from scratch as a general language model. Instead, it was initialized using DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This base model had already undergone extensive training on programming data. The researchers then continued pre-training this coder-focused model with 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from the web.

The results of this "code-first" approach were stark. When comparing training strategies, the team observed that models subjected to code training followed by math training outperformed those trained on general text followed by math. In experiments involving program-aided reasoning—where the model writes Python scripts to solve equations—the two-stage approach (Code then Math) yielded significant gains. Even more interestingly, this benefit extended to problems solved without tools. The initial code training stage alone provided moderate enhancements to pure mathematical reasoning, and it appeared to make the subsequent math training more efficient, leading to superior final performance.

This suggests that the structured logic, syntax precision, and algorithmic thinking required for coding create a cognitive scaffold that helps the model grasp abstract mathematical concepts more effectively later on. While mixing code and math tokens simultaneously in a single training stage helped prevent "catastrophic forgetting" of coding skills, the sequential approach of code followed by math proved optimal for maximizing mathematical prowess.

## The Surprising Ineffectiveness of arXiv Papers

If code is the secret sauce, what about the traditional staple of scientific AI training: arXiv papers? For years, it has been standard practice to include vast repositories of LaTeX-formatted scientific papers in math pre-training corpora. The assumption is that these peer-reviewed documents represent the highest quality of mathematical knowledge available.

DeepSeekMath turns this assumption on its head. The researchers conducted controlled experiments training models exclusively on arXiv corpora, including cleaned versions totaling billions of tokens. The results were disappointing. Models trained solely on arXiv data showed no notable improvements—and in some cases, even exhibited performance degradation—across various benchmarks ranging from grade-school arithmetic (GSM8K) to competition-level olympiad problems (MATH).

In contrast, the DeepSeekMath team constructed their own corpus by mining Common Crawl, the massive archive of public web pages. They did not just scrape everything; they employed a meticulous, iterative data selection pipeline. Starting with a seed set of high-quality math pages, they trained a classifier to identify and retrieve similar content from the wider web, refining their search over four iterations. This process yielded 120 billion tokens of diverse mathematical content, nearly seven times larger than the math web data used by previous state-of-the-art models like Minerva.

The takeaway is clear: quantity and source matter less than curation and diversity. The messy, varied landscape of the public web—including forums, educational sites, and documentation in multiple languages—appears to offer richer signals for learning reasoning patterns than the dense, highly specialized prose found in academic papers. Additionally, because their web-sourced corpus included significant non-English content (particularly Chinese), DeepSeekMath demonstrated superior performance on multilingual benchmarks compared to English-centric models trained on arXiv data.

## Scaling Intelligence Without Massive Parameters

The combination of code initialization and high-quality web data allowed DeepSeekMath 7B to punch well above its weight class. With only 7 billion parameters, it achieved a score of 51.7% on the MATH benchmark without using external tools or voting techniques. This performance approaches that of closed-source behemoths like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4 and surpasses Minerva 540B, a model with 77 times more parameters.

This achievement underscores a shifting paradigm in Large Language Model development. It is no longer solely about scaling up parameter counts; it is about scaling up data quality and optimizing the training trajectory. By leveraging publicly available web data through engineered pipelines and respecting the logical foundations laid by code training, researchers can build highly capable reasoning models that are efficient and accessible.

The success of DeepSeekMath invites the research community to rethink data sourcing strategies. Rather than defaulting to established academic datasets, there is immense untapped potential in the broader internet—if one knows how to filter it correctly. As AI continues to evolve, the lesson from DeepSeekMath is evident: sometimes, to teach an AI advanced calculus, you first need to teach it how to code, and you definitely shouldn't rely only on textbooks found in university archives.



# Beyond Memorization: How Reinforcement Learning Makes AI Reasoning More Robust

When we see an AI model jump from 46.8% to 51.7% accuracy on a difficult math benchmark like MATH after undergoing Reinforcement Learning (RL), it is tempting to assume the model has suddenly learned new mathematical theorems or forgotten its previous errors. However, the research behind DeepSeekMath suggests a more nuanced reality. The improvement isn't necessarily about teaching the model facts it didn't know; rather, it is about making the model's existing knowledge more reliable and consistent.

In this post, we dive into the findings from the DeepSeekMath paper to understand why RL is so effective even when applied to models that have already been thoroughly instruction-tuned. We will explore how RL shifts the focus from simply having a correct answer somewhere in the model's possibilities to ensuring that the correct answer is the one most likely to be generated.

## The Puzzle of Post-SFT Improvement

DeepSeekMath starts with a base model that undergoes extensive supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on high-quality mathematical data. By the time the model reaches the "DeepSeekMath-Instruct" stage, it is already quite capable, scoring 46.8% on the competition-level MATH dataset. One might expect diminishing returns at this point, assuming the model has memorized most of what it can from the training data.

Yet, when the researchers applied their novel Reinforcement Learning algorithm, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the performance jumped significantly to 51.7%. This raises a critical question: If the model was already trained on these problems during the SFT phase, what exactly did RL add? Did it learn new math, or did something else happen?

## Pass@K vs. Maj@K: Measuring Robustness

To answer this, we need to distinguish between two ways of measuring a model's capability: `Pass@K` and `Maj@K`.

`Pass@K` measures whether the model can generate *at least one* correct solution out of $K$ attempts. If you ask a model to solve a hard calculus problem 10 times and it gets it right once, its `Pass@10` is perfect, even if it failed nine times. This metric tells us about the upper bound of the model's potential—it confirms that the correct reasoning path exists somewhere within the model's parameters.

`Maj@K`, or Majority Vote at $K$, looks at consistency. It involves generating $K$ samples and selecting the answer that appears most frequently. If a model generates 10 different wrong answers and only 1 correct one, the majority vote will likely pick a wrong answer. High `Maj@K` requires not just knowing the answer, but knowing it consistently enough that it dominates the output distribution.

The DeepSeekMath team analyzed their models using these metrics and found a striking pattern. While Reinforcement Learning did not significantly improve `Pass@K`—suggesting the SFT model already "knew" how to solve many of these problems—it drastically improved `Maj@K`. This indicates that RL does not necessarily expand the set of solvable problems. Instead, it reshapes the probability landscape of the model's outputs. It suppresses erratic, incorrect reasoning paths and amplifies the correct ones, making the right answer the most probable outcome every time.

## The Misalignment Problem in Supervised Fine-Tuning

This phenomenon highlights what researchers call a "misalignment problem" in Supervised Fine-Tuning. During SFT, a model learns to mimic human-written solutions. While this teaches the model valid reasoning steps, it doesn't explicitly penalize it for occasionally drifting into plausible-looking but incorrect logic during generation. The model might assign non-trivial probability mass to wrong answers because they look syntactically similar to correct ones or because they appeared in noisy training data.

Reinforcement Learning acts as a corrective lens here. By sampling multiple outputs for a single question and comparing them against a reward signal (in this case, derived from group scores via GRPO), the model learns to differentiate between high-quality and low-quality reasoning traces. It realizes that while a specific wrong turn might look okay in isolation, consistently taking that turn leads to failure. Consequently, RL tightens the distribution around correct solutions, effectively turning a sporadic genius into a reliable tutor.
