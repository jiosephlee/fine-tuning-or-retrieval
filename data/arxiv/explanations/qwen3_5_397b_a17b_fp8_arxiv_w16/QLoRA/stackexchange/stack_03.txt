\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Clarification on Double Quantization: Why subtract the mean before quantizing constants?
Question:
The paper introduces Double Quantization (DQ) to save memory by quantizing the quantization constants ($c_2$). The text says: 'Since the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are positive, we subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center the values around zero and make use of symmetric quantization.'

I am confused about this step. If the original constants $c_2$ are all positive (as they represent absolute maximums or scales), subtracting their mean will result in many negative values. How does centering them around zero help with symmetric quantization if we eventually need to recover the original positive scale? Do we store the mean separately, and if so, isn't that adding memory overhead back? How is the dequantization formula modified to account for this mean subtraction?
Answer:
The confusion regarding the subtraction of the mean in Double Quantization stems from conflating the properties of the original data with the statistical distribution of the quantization constants themselves. While it is true that the first-level quantization constants ($c_2^{\text{FP32}}$), which represent absolute maximums or scales, are strictly positive values, their distribution across the many blocks of a large neural network is not uniform; rather, they tend to cluster tightly around a specific positive value with small variations. By calculating the mean of these constants and subtracting it from each individual constant, the method shifts this tight cluster so that it is centered around zero. This transformation creates a distribution containing both positive and negative values (representing deviations from the average scale) that is symmetric around zero.

This centering is crucial because it allows the second stage of quantization to utilize **symmetric quantization**. Symmetric quantization schemes are generally more efficient and simpler to implement than asymmetric ones because they do not require storing a separate "zero-point" offset to map the integer zero to the floating-point zero; in a symmetric scheme centered at zero, the integer 0 naturally represents the floating-point 0. If you were to quantize the original positive-only $c_2$ values directly without mean subtraction, you would be forced to use an asymmetric quantization strategy to efficiently cover the dynamic range, or you would waste half of your quantization bins representing negative numbers that never occur. By subtracting the mean, the authors effectively compress the dynamic range of the values needing quantization (since they are now just small residuals) and align them with a symmetric data type (like signed 8-bit integers), maximizing precision for a given bit width.

Regarding your concern about memory overhead and recovery, yes, the mean value is stored separately as a single 32-bit floating-point number ($c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ in the paper's notation for the second level, though conceptually it acts as the offset for $c_2$). However, this overhead is negligible because this single mean value applies to a large block of constants (a blocksize of 256 is used in the paper). Instead of storing 256 individual 32-bit constants, you store 256 quantized 8-bit constants plus one 32-bit mean. The dequantization formula is modified to simply reverse this operation: during inference or training, the system first dequantizes the compressed constants to get the residuals, adds the stored mean back to each residual to recover the original positive $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values, and then uses these recovered scales to dequantize the actual model weights. As shown in Equation 6 of the paper, this nested process $\text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1, c_2), \mathbf{W})$ ensures that despite intermediate subtractions and quantizations, final reconstruction remains accurate enough to preserve model performance while significantly reducing memory usage.
