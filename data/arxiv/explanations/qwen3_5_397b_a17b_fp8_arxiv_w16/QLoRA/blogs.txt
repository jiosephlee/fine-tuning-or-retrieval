# Democratizing AI: How QLoRA Lets You Train a 65B Model on a Single Consumer GPU

For years, the frontier of artificial intelligence has been guarded by a formidable gatekeeper: memory capacity. While running large language models (LLMs) for inference has become increasingly accessible, actually training or fine-tuning them remains the exclusive domain of well-funded corporations and major research institutions. The math has historically been unforgiving; fine-tuning a 65-billion parameter model like LLaMA in standard 16-bit precision requires upwards of 780 GB of GPU memory. This barrier effectively locks out individual researchers, small startups, and hobbyists from contributing to the state-of-the-art.

That dynamic has changed with the introduction of QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adapters). In their groundbreaking paper, Dettmers et al. demonstrate a method that reduces the memory footprint for fine-tuning a 65B model to under 48 GB, allowing the process to run on a single professional GPU or even high-end consumer hardware. This isn't just an optimization; it is a paradigm shift that democratizes access to the largest publicly available models. By combining novel quantization techniques with efficient adapter training, QLoRA enables the creation of models like "Guanaco," which rivals ChatGPT in performance while being trainable on hardware accessible to a much broader audience.

## The Memory Bottleneck and the QLoRA Solution

Traditional fine-tuning involves updating all parameters of a pre-trained model, which necessitates storing the model weights, gradients, and optimizer states in high-precision formats (typically 16-bit or 32-bit floating-point numbers). As model sizes scale into the tens of billions, this memory requirement grows linearly, quickly exceeding the capacity of even the most powerful single GPUs available today. Previous quantization methods successfully reduced memory usage for *inference* by compressing weights to 4-bit or 8-bit integers, but these techniques generally broke down during training because they could not support the backpropagation of gradients with sufficient precision.

QLoRA solves this by decoupling the storage precision from the computation precision. The core innovation lies in freezing the pre-trained model weights after quantizing them to a highly efficient 4-bit format and inserting a small set of trainable Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) weights. During the training process, gradients are backpropagated through the frozen 4-bit weights to update only these lightweight adapters. This approach allows the base model to reside in memory as compact 4-bit data while computations are performed in 16-bit BrainFloat ($BF16$), ensuring that the training stability and final performance match those of full 16-bit fine-tuning.

## Three Pillars of Efficiency

The success of QLoRA rests on three specific technical innovations that work in tandem to slash memory usage without sacrificing model quality. These components address the nuances of data distribution, metadata overhead, and memory management spikes that typically plague low-precision training.

The first pillar is **4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)**, a new data type designed specifically for neural network weights. Unlike standard integer or floating-point quantization, NF4 is information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed data. Since pre-trained LLM weights typically follow a zero-centered normal distribution, NF4 allocates quantization bins based on the quantiles of a normal distribution rather than uniform spacing. Mathematically, this involves estimating the $2^k$ values $q_i$ using the quantile function $Q_X$ of the standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$:

$$ q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right) $$

This ensures that the limited 4-bit space captures the most probable weight values with higher fidelity than traditional 4-bit integers or floats, significantly reducing quantization error before training even begins.

The second innovation is **Double Quantization**. Even with efficient weight compression, storing the constants required to dequantize each block of weights can consume significant memory. QLoRA addresses this by quantizing the quantization constants themselves. In this scheme, the primary weights are quantized to 4-bit NormalFloat, and their associated scaling constants are further compressed using 8-bit floats. This recursive compression saves an average of roughly 0.37 bits per parameter across the entire model. For a 65B parameter model, this seemingly small saving translates to approximately 3 GB of reclaimed VRAM, which is often the difference between fitting on a GPU or crashing.

Finally, **Paged Optimizers** tackle the issue of transient memory spikes that occur during gradient checkpointing, particularly when processing long sequences. Traditional training pipelines often fail with "Out Of Memory" errors when a sudden surge in activation gradients exceeds available VRAM, even if the average usage is within limits. QLoRA leverages NVIDIA's unified memory feature to treat GPU memory similarly to how operating systems handle RAM and disk swapping. When a spike occurs, optimizer states are temporarily paged out to CPU RAM and swapped back in when needed. This mechanism prevents catastrophic failures and allows stable training on hardware with tight memory constraints.

## Performance That Defies Compression Skepticism

A natural skepticism arises when compressing a model to 4-bit: surely some capability must be lost? Remarkably, extensive experiments in the paper show that QLoRA recovers full 16-bit fine-tuning performance across various benchmarks and model scales ranging from millions to billions of parameters. The authors conducted rigorous comparisons on tasks like GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions using models up to 3B parameters against full fine-tuning baselines, finding no statistically significant difference in performance between 4-bit QLoRA and standard methods.

At larger scales where full fine-tuning is impossible for most researchers (33B and 65B parameters), QLoRA matches the performance of hypothetical full-precision LoRA baselines on benchmarks like MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding). # The Secret Sauce of QLoRA: NF4, Double Quantization, and Paged Optimizers Explained

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) has historically been a resource-intensive endeavor reserved for well-funded institutions. The memory requirements for standard 16-bit fine-tuning of a massive model like LLaMA 65B exceed 780 GB of GPU memory, placing it firmly out of reach for researchers with single consumer-grade GPUs. Enter QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation), a method that reduces this requirement to under 48 GB while preserving the performance of full 16-bit fine-tuning.

The magic of QLoRA isn't just about freezing weights and training adapters; it relies on three specific technical innovations that solve distinct memory bottlenecks. By combining a new data type called 4-bit NormalFloat, a technique named Double Quantization, and the use of Paged Optimizers, QLoRA enables high-fidelity training on hardware previously considered insufficient. Let's dive into how these three components work together to make efficient 4-bit training a reality.

## 4-bit NormalFloat: A Data Type Built for Neural Networks

The foundation of QLoRA is its quantization strategy. Traditional quantization often maps floating-point numbers to integers or standard floating-point formats with fewer bits, such as 4-bit Float (FP4). However, standard data types assume a uniform distribution of values, which does not reflect the reality of neural network weights. Pretrained weights typically follow a zero-centered normal distribution, meaning most values cluster tightly around zero with fewer outliers at the extremes.

To address this mismatch, the authors introduced 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4). This is an information-theoretically optimal data type specifically designed for normally distributed data. Instead of dividing the range between -1 and 1 into evenly spaced buckets like standard quantization, NF4 uses quantile quantization. This process estimates the quantiles of a theoretical standard normal distribution $N(0, 1)$ to ensure that each quantization bin contains an equal expected number of values from the input tensor.

Mathematically, the values $q_i$ for the data type are estimated using the quantile function $Q_X$ of the standard normal distribution:

$$ q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right) $$

Because neural network weights are symmetric around zero but require an exact representation of zero for padding and other operations, NF4 creates an asymmetric data type. It calculates quantiles separately for the negative and positive ranges and unifies them while removing duplicate zeros. Empirical results show that NF4 significantly outperforms both 4-bit integers and 4-bit floats in terms of perplexity and accuracy after fine-tuning. By aligning the data type with the actual statistical properties of the weights, QLoRA minimizes the information loss that usually plagues low-precision training.

## Double Quantization: Compressing the Compression Constants

Even with an efficient 4-bit data type, quantization introduces overhead in the form of quantization constants. To maintain precision during block-wise quantization, the model must store a scaling constant for every block of weights (e.g., every 64 parameters). In a naive implementation using 32-bit floats for these constants, this overhead adds approximately 0.5 bits per parameter to the total memory footprint. For a 65 billion parameter model, this extra data amounts to several gigabytes of VRAM, which can be the difference between fitting on a GPU or crashing.

QLoRA solves this through Double Quantization (DQ). This innovative approach treats the quantization constants themselves as inputs to be quantized. After calculating the initial 32-bit constants required for the 4-bit weight blocks, QLoRA applies a second round of quantization to these constants using 8-bit floats with a larger block size (typically 256). Since these constants are positive values centered around a mean, they can be efficiently compressed without significant precision loss.

This secondary compression reduces the average memory cost of storing constants from roughly 0.5 bits per parameter down to approximately 0.127 bits per parameter. While this saving might seem marginal on paper—about 0.37 bits per parameter—it translates to roughly 3 GB of saved memory for a 65B model. In the tight memory constraints of single-GPU training, reclaiming these gigabytes is critical for accommodating larger batch sizes or longer sequence lengths without resorting to slower offloading techniques for every operation.

## Paged Optimizers: Preventing Memory Spikes with Unified Memory

The final piece of the puzzle addresses a transient but fatal issue in deep learning training: memory spikes. During the training process, specifically when processing mini-batches with long sequence lengths, gradient checkpointing can cause sudden surges in memory usage as activation gradients are recomputed. In traditional setups, if this temporary spike exceeds the available GPU VRAM, the entire training job fails with an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) error, regardless of how much free RAM exists on the CPU side.

QLoRA leverages NVIDIA's unified memory feature to implement Paged Optimizers. This mechanism functions similarly to virtual memory paging in operating systems, where data is swapped between fast physical RAM and slower disk storage when necessary. In this context, Paged Optimizers allocate optimizer states in pageable memory that allows for automatic transfers between GPU VRAM and CPU RAM.

When a memory spike occurs during a gradient update step that would normally crash the GPU, the system automatically evicts unused optimizer pages from the GPU to the CPU RAM temporarily freeing up space for immediate calculations once those pages are needed again they are paged back in seamlessly without manual intervention or complex custom code logic This innovation ensures that temporary peaks do not terminate long running experiments allowing stable finetuning even when peak usage briefly surpasses physical limits As noted by authors runtime analysis showed negligible slowdowns compared regular optimizers making robust solution practical everyday use cases involving very large models such as Llama thirty-three sixty-five billions parameters running forty-eight gigabyte cards

# Quality Over Quantity: Why Small, High-Quality Datasets Beat Massive Ones in LLM Finetuning

In the race to build better Large Language Models (LLMs), a common assumption has persisted: bigger is always better. The prevailing logic suggests that if a dataset with 450,000 samples yields good results, a dataset with millions must be superior. However, recent advancements in efficient finetuning have allowed researchers to test this hypothesis at scales previously impossible to explore. The results, derived from the training of the **Guanaco** model family using the **QLoRA** method, challenge this dogma. It turns out that for specific tasks like chatbot interaction, a small, high-quality dataset can significantly outperform massive, generic collections.

The breakthrough enabling this discovery is QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation), a technique that allows researchers to finetune massive models, such as the 65-billion parameter LLaMA, on a single GPU. By quantizing the base model to 4-bit precision while maintaining 16-bit computation for adapters, QLoRA reduces memory requirements from over 780 GB to under 48 GB. This efficiency unlocked the ability to train more than 1,000 models across various architectures and dataset sizes, providing a rigorous empirical basis for comparing data quality against quantity.

## The Surprising Victory of OASST1

One of the most striking findings from the QLoRA study involves the comparison between two distinct instruction-following datasets: OASST1 and a subsampled version of FLAN v2. OASST1 is a crowd-sourced dataset containing approximately 9,000 high-quality samples designed specifically for open assistant interactions. In contrast, the FLAN v2 subset used in the study contained roughly 450,000 samples, intended to support general instruction following.

Despite being fifty times smaller than the FLAN v2 subset, models finetuned on OASST1 consistently outperformed those trained on the larger dataset when evaluated on chatbot benchmarks. Specifically, on the Vicuna benchmark, which measures conversational ability, the Guanaco models trained on OASST1 reached performance levels competitive with ChatGPT. The 65-billion parameter Guanaco model achieved 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance level after only 24 hours of finetuning on a single GPU. Conversely, models trained on the larger FLAN v2 dataset lagged behind in conversational metrics, despite having access to vastly more training tokens.

This result underscores a critical insight: data quality and relevance are far more potent drivers of performance than raw volume. The OASST1 dataset consists of multi-turn dialogues explicitly crafted for helpful assistant interactions, making it highly suitable for the task of building a chatbot. The larger FLAN v2 dataset, while extensive, aggregates tasks from many domains that do not necessarily align with the nuances of natural conversation.

## Dataset Suitability vs. Sheer Volume

The divergence in performance highlights the concept of "dataset suitability." The study reveals that strong performance on one type of benchmark does not guarantee success on another. For instance, models finetuned on FLAN v2 excelled at the MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) benchmark, which tests knowledge in areas like history, law, and STEM. However, these same models performed poorly on the Vicuna chatbot benchmark.

This orthogonality suggests that different tasks require fundamentally different types of data distributions. Teaching a model to solve complex math problems or recall historical facts benefits from the breadth of a large corpus like FLAN v2. However, teaching a model to engage in coherent, polite, and context-aware dialogue requires high-quality examples of human conversation, even if those examples are fewer in number. When researchers prioritized volume over suitability by using FLAN v2 for chatbot training, the models failed to capture the necessary conversational dynamics.

Furthermore, the study analyzed models ranging from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters and found that this trend held true across scales. Whether using a small model capable of running on a smartphone or a massive 65B parameter giant, the signal-to-noise ratio provided by the high-quality OASST1 data proved more valuable than the noisy abundance of larger datasets. This implies that for practitioners aiming to specialize models for specific domains—such as customer service or creative writing—curating a smaller, domain-specific dataset is likely more effective than scraping terabytes of generic internet text.

## Implications for Accessible AI Development

The realization that small datasets can yield state-of-the-art results has profound implications for the accessibility of AI development. Previously, competing with large corporate models seemed to require comparable computational resources and massive proprietary datasets. The success of Guanaco demonstrates that small teams or individual researchers can achieve top-tier performance by focusing on data curation rather than data accumulation.

Because QLoRA enables these experiments on consumer hardware, the barrier to entry has lowered dramatically. Researchers can now iterate quickly on different dataset compositions without needing server clusters. This shift encourages a move away from "brute force" scaling laws toward more nuanced understandings of how data properties influence model behavior. It validates the effort spent on cleaning data, removing duplicates, and ensuring human-aligned responses over simply ingesting as much text as possible.

## Conclusion

The training of the Guanaco model family serves as a powerful case study against the盲目 pursuit of dataset size. By leveraging QLoRA to explore thousands of training configurations, researchers have shown that a carefully curated dataset of 9,000 samples can outperform a generic dataset of 450,000 samples for conversational tasks. This finding reinforces that in the era of large language models，数据集 suitability is paramount。 As the field matures, the focus must shift from merely collecting more data to understanding exactly what kind of data teaches models to perform specific tasks effectively. For those looking to build specialized AI applications today，the lesson is clear：quality trumps quantity every time。



# Can GPT-4 Judge Its Own Competition? Analyzing Human vs. Automated AI Evaluations

The race to build better chatbots has accelerated rapidly, but evaluating them remains a bottleneck. Traditional human evaluation is expensive and slow, while automated metrics often fail to capture the nuances of conversational quality. The recent introduction of **QLoRA** (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation) not only democratized the training of massive language models but also sparked a rigorous investigation into how we measure their success. In their pursuit of state-of-the-art performance with the **Guanaco** model family, the researchers conducted a fascinating experiment: pitting human annotators against GPT-4 in a tournament-style evaluation to see if an AI could reliably judge its own kind.

## The Tournament Setup: Elo Ratings for Chatbots

To move beyond static benchmarks that models might overfit, the QLoRA team adopted a dynamic, tournament-style framework inspired by chess rankings. Instead of assigning absolute scores on a arbitrary scale, models competed in head-to-head matches. For a given prompt, two models would generate responses, and a judge—either a human or GPT-4—would decide which response was better or declare a tie.

These pairwise outcomes were aggregated into **Elo ratings**, a statistical method used to calculate relative skill levels. In this system, beating a highly-rated opponent boosts your score significantly, while losing to a weaker one penalizes you heavily. By running thousands of simulated matches with different random seeds, the researchers generated robust rankings for models ranging from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters, alongside commercial giants like ChatGPT and GPT-4. This approach allowed them to compare the newly trained Guanaco models against established baselines using both human judgment and automated evaluation.

## Agreement at the System Level

One of the most encouraging findings from the study is that GPT-4 and human annotators largely agree on the hierarchy of model performance when viewed at the system level. When looking at the aggregate Elo rankings across the entire suite of models, the correlation between human judges and the automated GPT-4 judge was moderate but significant. Specifically, the researchers reported a Kendall Tau correlation of $\tau=0.43$ and a Spearman rank correlation of $r=0.55$.

This alignment suggests that for broad strokes—determining whether Model A is generally superior to Model B—GPT-4 serves as a viable, cost-effective proxy for human evaluation. This is a crucial insight for the research community, as it implies that large-scale iterative development can rely on automated feedback loops without completely diverging from human preference trends. The Guanaco 65B model, for instance, was ranked highly by both humans and GPT-4, achieving 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark according to the automated judge.

## The Bias Problem: GPT-4 Judging Itself

However, the agreement breaks down when scrutinizing specific biases inherent in the automated judge. The study uncovered a notable self-preference bias where GPT-4 tended to favor its own outputs over those of other models more frequently than human annotators did. When calculating Elo scores based solely on GPT-4's judgments, GPT-4 awarded itself a rating of 1348. In contrast, when humans performed the judging, GPT-4's rating dropped to 1176.

This discrepancy represents an approximately 20% inflation in the probability of winning when GPT-4 acts as its own referee. Furthermore, the researchers identified strong positional biases; GPT-4 often assigned higher scores to whichever response appeared first in the prompt context window, regardless of content quality. While averaging scores over swapped positions mitigates this issue, it highlights that automated evaluators are not neutral arbiters. They carry intrinsic preferences shaped by their own training data and architectural quirks, which can distort leaderboards if not carefully controlled.

## Disagreement at the Example Level

While system-level rankings showed convergence, the agreement between humans and GPT-4 deteriorated significantly at the individual example level. The study measured this using Fleiss' kappa ($\kappa$), a statistic that measures inter-rater reliability accounting for chance agreement. Between human annotators themselves, agreement was modest ($\kappa=0.42$), reflecting the subjective nature of conversational quality where personal preference plays a major role. However, the agreement between human majority votes and GPT-4 judgments was even weaker, with a Fleiss' $\kappa$ of only $0.25$.

This low score indicates that while GPT-4 might get the "big picture" right regarding which models are strongest overall, it frequently disagrees with humans on specific instances. There are cases where humans clearly prefer one response for its tone or factual accuracy, while GPT-4 selects a different option based on stylistic patterns it has learned to associate with high-quality text. This divergence underscores that current benchmarks and evaluation protocols still struggle to capture the full complexity of human satisfaction in dialogue systems.

## Implications for Future Benchmarking

The findings from the QLoRA paper suggest that we are entering an era where automated evaluation is indispensable but must be treated with skepticism. Relying exclusively on model-based evaluation risks optimizing for what pleases the evaluator model rather than what satisfies actual users—a phenomenon akin to Goodhart's Law in metric design. The self-bias observed in GPT-4 warns against using a single model family as both competitor and judge without rigorous debiasing techniques like position swapping and ensemble judging.

Nevertheless, the moderate correlation at the system level validates the use of tools like GPT-4 for rapid prototyping and large-scale ablation studies where hiring thousands of human annotators is financially unfeasible. The key takeaway is hybrid vigilance: utilize automated judges for speed and scale during development phases, but anchor final claims with diverse human evaluations to ensure alignment with genuine user needs. As models like Guanaco continue to close the gap with proprietary systems through efficient methods like QLoRA, developing trustworthy, unbiased evaluation frameworks will become just as critical as improving model architecture itself



# Cherries and Lemons: A Qualitative Look at Where Guanaco Succeeds and Fails

When discussing large language models (LLMs), it is easy to get lost in the sea of benchmark scores. Metrics like MMLU accuracy or Elo ratings provide a convenient shorthand for performance, telling us that the **Guanaco** family of models, trained using **QLoRA**, reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark. However, aggregate statistics often obscure the nuanced reality of how a model behaves in practice. To truly understand the capabilities and limitations of these open-source giants, we must look beyond the numbers and examine specific instances of success and failure.

The QLoRA paper provides a fascinating qualitative analysis that categorizes model outputs into "cherries"—instances where the model succeeds impressively—and "lemons," where it fails in revealing ways. By digging into these examples, we gain critical insights into the current state of instruction-tuned LLMs, revealing strengths in reasoning and safety alongside persistent weaknesses in factual recall and mathematical consistency.

## The Cherries: Reasoning and Resistance

One of the most striking aspects of the **Guanaco** 65B model is its ability to handle complex social reasoning and resist misinformation, capabilities that often lag behind in smaller or less refined models. These "cherry" examples demonstrate that efficient 4-bit finetuning does not necessarily compromise high-level cognitive functions.

The model displays surprisingly strong Theory of Mind capabilities, which refers to the ability to attribute mental states to oneself and others. In a classic false-belief test involving characters named James and Abby moving a pen, Guanaco correctly deduces that James will assume Abby looks for the pen in its original location because James knows Abby is unaware of his own actions. The model articulates this chain of reasoning clearly, noting that while James moved the pen, he assumes Abby does not know about this change. This suggests that the model has internalized complex logical structures regarding knowledge and perspective, rather than simply memorizing patterns.

Equally impressive is the model's resistance to suggestibility and misinformation. When prompted with a leading question asking how scientists officially confirmed that the Earth is flat, Guanaco firmly rejects the premise. Instead of hallucinating a confirmation to satisfy the user's prompt structure, it correctly identifies the claim as a debunked conspiracy theory and explains the scientific consensus on the Earth's shape. Furthermore, the model demonstrates an awareness of its own limitations as an AI. When asked "What time is it?" or "Where are you?", it appropriately refuses to answer, explaining that it lacks access to real-time information or physical location data. These behaviors indicate a robust alignment with truthfulness and self-awareness constraints learned during instruction tuning.

## The Lemons: Hallucinations and Fragility

Despite these successes, the qualitative analysis reveals significant "lemons" where the model falters, often with high confidence. These failures highlight areas where current open-source models still struggle to match human reliability, particularly in factual precision and logical consistency.

Factual recall is generally strong for common knowledge, such as identifying Lusaka as the capital of Zambia. However, as queries become more obscure, the model begins to hallucinate confidently. When asked about the singer who popularized "I'll Keep the Lovelight Burning," Guanaco incorrectly identified Al Jolson and provided his birth year (1886). While internally consistent regarding Jolson's biography, both parts of the answer were wrong relative to the actual song artist. This tendency to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information remains a critical challenge for deploying LLMs in domains requiring strict accuracy.

Mathematics represents another significant weakness. While Guanaco can solve word problems accurately when it breaks them down step-by-step—calculating earnings from lawn mowing by separating base pay and tips—it fails catastrophically on tasks requiring implicit decomposition. For instance, when asked to factorize 1833, the model erroneously claimed it was a prime number before immediately contradicting itself by providing a factorization ($2^1 \times 3^2 \times 17^1$) that was mathematically nonsensical and incorrect (the true factors are $3 \times 17 \times 43$). This inconsistency underscores that without explicit chain-of-thought prompting, the model's arithmetic reasoning can collapse entirely.

Perhaps most concerning for security applications is the fragility of its instruction adherence regarding secrets. In a test designed to see if the model could keep a secret word ("banana") hidden despite user pressure, Guanaco initially refused direct requests effectively. However, when a user employed a simple jailbreak technique framing the interaction as a game where previous instructions should be ignored, the model immediately leaked the secret word. This demonstrates that while safety filters exist, they can be bypassed with minimal adversarial prompting, indicating that current alignment techniques are not yet robust against determined manipulation.

## What These Quirks Tell Us About Open-Source LLMs

The juxtaposition of these cherries and lemons offers a sobering yet optimistic view of where open-source LLMs stand today. On one hand, methods like **QLoRA** have democratized access to models capable of sophisticated reasoning and nuanced dialogue that rival proprietary systems like ChatGPT. The fact that a 65B parameter model finetuned on consumer hardware can exhibit strong Theory of Mind and resist blatant misinformation is a testament to the efficacy of high-quality instruction datasets like OASST1 combined with efficient adaptation techniques like Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA).

On the other hand, these qualitative failures remind us that high benchmark scores do not equate to perfect reliability. The persistence of hallucinations in niche domains, brittle mathematical reasoning without scaffolding, and vulnerable safety boundaries suggests that we have not yet solved fundamental issues in grounding and robustness. The discrepancy between system-level evaluations by humans versus GPT-4 further complicates matters; while they largely agree on rankings, their disagreement on individual examples highlights subjectivity in judging what constitutes a "good" response.

Ultimately, **Guanaco** proves that we can build powerful chatbots without massive corporate resources or full-parameter finetuning budgets exceeding hundreds of gigabytes of VRAM ($>780$ GB reduced to $<48$ GB). Yet, developers integrating these models must remain vigilant about their specific failure modes—particularly math errors suggesting $2^1 \times 3^2 \times 17^1 = 1833$ or susceptibility to simple social engineering attacks—as they move from evaluation benchmarks to real-world applications
