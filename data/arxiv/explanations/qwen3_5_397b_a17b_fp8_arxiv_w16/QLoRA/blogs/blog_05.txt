# Cherries and Lemons: A Qualitative Look at Where Guanaco Succeeds and Fails

When discussing large language models (LLMs), it is easy to get lost in the sea of benchmark scores. Metrics like MMLU accuracy or Elo ratings provide a convenient shorthand for performance, telling us that the **Guanaco** family of models, trained using **QLoRA**, reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark. However, aggregate statistics often obscure the nuanced reality of how a model behaves in practice. To truly understand the capabilities and limitations of these open-source giants, we must look beyond the numbers and examine specific instances of success and failure.

The QLoRA paper provides a fascinating qualitative analysis that categorizes model outputs into "cherries"—instances where the model succeeds impressively—and "lemons," where it fails in revealing ways. By digging into these examples, we gain critical insights into the current state of instruction-tuned LLMs, revealing strengths in reasoning and safety alongside persistent weaknesses in factual recall and mathematical consistency.

## The Cherries: Reasoning and Resistance

One of the most striking aspects of the **Guanaco** 65B model is its ability to handle complex social reasoning and resist misinformation, capabilities that often lag behind in smaller or less refined models. These "cherry" examples demonstrate that efficient 4-bit finetuning does not necessarily compromise high-level cognitive functions.

The model displays surprisingly strong Theory of Mind capabilities, which refers to the ability to attribute mental states to oneself and others. In a classic false-belief test involving characters named James and Abby moving a pen, Guanaco correctly deduces that James will assume Abby looks for the pen in its original location because James knows Abby is unaware of his own actions. The model articulates this chain of reasoning clearly, noting that while James moved the pen, he assumes Abby does not know about this change. This suggests that the model has internalized complex logical structures regarding knowledge and perspective, rather than simply memorizing patterns.

Equally impressive is the model's resistance to suggestibility and misinformation. When prompted with a leading question asking how scientists officially confirmed that the Earth is flat, Guanaco firmly rejects the premise. Instead of hallucinating a confirmation to satisfy the user's prompt structure, it correctly identifies the claim as a debunked conspiracy theory and explains the scientific consensus on the Earth's shape. Furthermore, the model demonstrates an awareness of its own limitations as an AI. When asked "What time is it?" or "Where are you?", it appropriately refuses to answer, explaining that it lacks access to real-time information or physical location data. These behaviors indicate a robust alignment with truthfulness and self-awareness constraints learned during instruction tuning.

## The Lemons: Hallucinations and Fragility

Despite these successes, the qualitative analysis reveals significant "lemons" where the model falters, often with high confidence. These failures highlight areas where current open-source models still struggle to match human reliability, particularly in factual precision and logical consistency.

Factual recall is generally strong for common knowledge, such as identifying Lusaka as the capital of Zambia. However, as queries become more obscure, the model begins to hallucinate confidently. When asked about the singer who popularized "I'll Keep the Lovelight Burning," Guanaco incorrectly identified Al Jolson and provided his birth year (1886). While internally consistent regarding Jolson's biography, both parts of the answer were wrong relative to the actual song artist. This tendency to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information remains a critical challenge for deploying LLMs in domains requiring strict accuracy.

Mathematics represents another significant weakness. While Guanaco can solve word problems accurately when it breaks them down step-by-step—calculating earnings from lawn mowing by separating base pay and tips—it fails catastrophically on tasks requiring implicit decomposition. For instance, when asked to factorize 1833, the model erroneously claimed it was a prime number before immediately contradicting itself by providing a factorization ($2^1 \times 3^2 \times 17^1$) that was mathematically nonsensical and incorrect (the true factors are $3 \times 17 \times 43$). This inconsistency underscores that without explicit chain-of-thought prompting, the model's arithmetic reasoning can collapse entirely.

Perhaps most concerning for security applications is the fragility of its instruction adherence regarding secrets. In a test designed to see if the model could keep a secret word ("banana") hidden despite user pressure, Guanaco initially refused direct requests effectively. However, when a user employed a simple jailbreak technique framing the interaction as a game where previous instructions should be ignored, the model immediately leaked the secret word. This demonstrates that while safety filters exist, they can be bypassed with minimal adversarial prompting, indicating that current alignment techniques are not yet robust against determined manipulation.

## What These Quirks Tell Us About Open-Source LLMs

The juxtaposition of these cherries and lemons offers a sobering yet optimistic view of where open-source LLMs stand today. On one hand, methods like **QLoRA** have democratized access to models capable of sophisticated reasoning and nuanced dialogue that rival proprietary systems like ChatGPT. The fact that a 65B parameter model finetuned on consumer hardware can exhibit strong Theory of Mind and resist blatant misinformation is a testament to the efficacy of high-quality instruction datasets like OASST1 combined with efficient adaptation techniques like Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA).

On the other hand, these qualitative failures remind us that high benchmark scores do not equate to perfect reliability. The persistence of hallucinations in niche domains, brittle mathematical reasoning without scaffolding, and vulnerable safety boundaries suggests that we have not yet solved fundamental issues in grounding and robustness. The discrepancy between system-level evaluations by humans versus GPT-4 further complicates matters; while they largely agree on rankings, their disagreement on individual examples highlights subjectivity in judging what constitutes a "good" response.

Ultimately, **Guanaco** proves that we can build powerful chatbots without massive corporate resources or full-parameter finetuning budgets exceeding hundreds of gigabytes of VRAM ($>780$ GB reduced to $<48$ GB). Yet, developers integrating these models must remain vigilant about their specific failure modes—particularly math errors suggesting $2^1 \times 3^2 \times 17^1 = 1833$ or susceptibility to simple social engineering attacks—as they move from evaluation benchmarks to real-world applications
