\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why doesn't QLoRA update the 4-bit base model weights during backpropagation?
Question:
I am reading the QLoRA paper and I understand that the base model is frozen in 4-bit precision (NF4) and only the LoRA adapters are updated. However, the abstract mentions 'backpropagates gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model.'

If the weights $\mathbf{W}$ are stored as 4-bit integers (or NF4 values), how exactly does the gradient flow through them to update the LoRA matrices $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$? Does the framework dequantize $\mathbf{W}$ to BFloat16 for the forward pass, compute the loss, and then somehow propagate gradients back through the dequantization operation? Or are the gradients with respect to $\mathbf{W}$ ($\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$) simply ignored?
Answer:
The mechanism you are asking about relies on a specific separation between **storage precision** and **computation precision**, facilitated by the mathematical properties of the dequantization operation. As detailed in the "QLoRA Finetuning" section of the paper, QLoRA maintains the base model weights $\mathbf{W}$ in a 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) format strictly for storage to minimize memory usage. However, the paper explicitly states that "whenever a QLoRA weight tensor is used, we dequantize the tensor to BFloat16, and then perform a matrix multiplication in 16-bit."

During the forward pass, the framework applies a `doubleDequant` function to convert the stored 4-bit integers $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ and their associated quantization constants into high-precision BFloat16 values, denoted as $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$. The actual computation for the layer output involves this dequantized weight matrix alongside the LoRA adapters $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$, which are natively stored and computed in BFloat16. Because the forward pass operates entirely in 16-bit precision after this immediate dequantization step, the computational graph remains differentiable with respect to all inputs involved in that high-precision matmul.

When backpropagation occurs, gradients flow through this same high-precision computational path. The chain rule allows gradients to propagate from the loss function, through the 16-bit activation outputs, and back through the dequantized weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$ to reach the LoRA parameters. Crucially, while the gradient with respect to the base weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ is technically computed during this backward pass through the dequantization operation, the paper notes that "for parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ are needed." The gradients corresponding to the 4-bit base model are simply discarded and never used to update $\mathbf{W}$, keeping those weights frozen. Essentially, the dequantization acts as a fixed, non-trainable transformation that lifts the low-precision storage values into a high-precision space where standard automatic differentiation can operate to tune only the adaptable LoRA components.

### How is the 'asymmetric' NormalFloat (NF4) data type constructed to include an exact zero?
Question:
In Section 3, the paper describes constructing the NF4 data type. It states that for a symmetric k-bit quantization of a normal distribution, there is no exact representation of zero, which is problematic for padding. The authors propose creating an asymmetric data type by estimating quantiles for $2^{k-1}$ negative values and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive values, then unifying them and removing one duplicate zero.

Could someone walk through the math of how this ensures an exact zero? Specifically, if we calculate the quantiles $q_i$ using Equation 4:
$$q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$$
How do we split the ranges for negative and positive parts such that one of the resulting bin centers lands exactly on 0.0? Does this asymmetry introduce a bias in the expected value of the quantized weights?
Answer:
The construction of the asymmetric NormalFloat (NF4) data type ensures an exact zero by deliberately breaking the symmetry of the quantile distribution around the mean, rather than relying on a symmetric split which naturally misses the center point in discrete representations.

In a standard symmetric $k$-bit quantization of a normal distribution, one typically divides the $2^k$ available codes equally between negative and positive values (i.e., $2^{k-1}$ codes for each side). As noted in Section 3, when calculating bin centers using Equation 4, $q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$, this symmetric approach results in bin centers that straddle zero but never land exactly on it. This happens because the quantile function $Q_X$ for a standard normal distribution maps probabilities strictly less than 0.5 to negative numbers and strictly greater than 0.5 to positive numbers; with an even split of bins, the boundary between the negative and positive sets falls exactly at the median (probability 0.5), meaning the adjacent bin centers are equidistant from zero but non-zero themselves.

To resolve this, the authors construct NF4 by allocating the $2^k$ codes asymmetrically: they assign $2^{k-1}$ codes to the negative range and $2^{k-1} + 1$ codes to the positive range. Conceptually, you can imagine computing two separate lists of quantile-based bin centers: one list for the negative domain containing $2^{k-1}$ values, and another for the positive domain containing $2^{k-1} + 1$ values. Crucially, because the positive set includes the region starting from the median upwards, its lowest bin center calculation effectively encompasses the zero point, or more precisely, the union of these two sets creates an overlap where both the largest negative bin and the smallest positive bin would conceptually touch or include zero. The paper states that they "unify these sets... and remove one of the two zeros that occurs in both sets." By explicitly forcing a zero representation in both halves during the estimation phase and then deduplicating it upon merging, they guarantee that 0.0 is one of the final $2^k$ discrete values in the lookup table.

Regarding your concern about bias, this asymmetry does not introduce a meaningful bias in the expected value of the quantized weights for normally distributed data. The underlying distribution being modeled is zero-centered ($N(0,1)$). While the *number* of bins is asymmetric (one extra bin on the positive side), this extra bin is infinitesimally small in terms of probability mass near the peak of the Gaussian curve. The quantile estimation process ensures that each bin represents an equal amount of probability mass from the theoretical distribution. Since zero is included as an explicit code, any weight that is exactly zero (such as padding tokens) maps to this code with zero error. For non-zero weights, density is slightly higher near zero, so having a specific code for zero actually improves fidelity rather than skewing expectations. The "extra" positive code essentially absorbs what would have been a fractional assignment around zero in a symmetric scheme, ensuring that integral counts match while maintaining information-theoretic optimality for normal distributions as claimed in Section 3.

### Clarification on Double Quantization: Why subtract the mean before quantizing constants?
Question:
The paper introduces Double Quantization (DQ) to save memory by quantizing the quantization constants ($c_2$). The text says: 'Since the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are positive, we subtract the mean from $c_2$ before quantization to center the values around zero and make use of symmetric quantization.'

I am confused about this step. If the original constants $c_2$ are all positive (as they represent absolute maximums or scales), subtracting their mean will result in many negative values. How does centering them around zero help with symmetric quantization if we eventually need to recover the original positive scale? Do we store the mean separately, and if so, isn't that adding memory overhead back? How is the dequantization formula modified to account for this mean subtraction?
Answer:
The confusion regarding the subtraction of the mean in Double Quantization stems from conflating the properties of the original data with the statistical distribution of the quantization constants themselves. While it is true that the first-level quantization constants ($c_2^{\text{FP32}}$), which represent absolute maximums or scales, are strictly positive values, their distribution across the many blocks of a large neural network is not uniform; rather, they tend to cluster tightly around a specific positive value with small variations. By calculating the mean of these constants and subtracting it from each individual constant, the method shifts this tight cluster so that it is centered around zero. This transformation creates a distribution containing both positive and negative values (representing deviations from the average scale) that is symmetric around zero.

This centering is crucial because it allows the second stage of quantization to utilize **symmetric quantization**. Symmetric quantization schemes are generally more efficient and simpler to implement than asymmetric ones because they do not require storing a separate "zero-point" offset to map the integer zero to the floating-point zero; in a symmetric scheme centered at zero, the integer 0 naturally represents the floating-point 0. If you were to quantize the original positive-only $c_2$ values directly without mean subtraction, you would be forced to use an asymmetric quantization strategy to efficiently cover the dynamic range, or you would waste half of your quantization bins representing negative numbers that never occur. By subtracting the mean, the authors effectively compress the dynamic range of the values needing quantization (since they are now just small residuals) and align them with a symmetric data type (like signed 8-bit integers), maximizing precision for a given bit width.

Regarding your concern about memory overhead and recovery, yes, the mean value is stored separately as a single 32-bit floating-point number ($c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ in the paper's notation for the second level, though conceptually it acts as the offset for $c_2$). However, this overhead is negligible because this single mean value applies to a large block of constants (a blocksize of 256 is used in the paper). Instead of storing 256 individual 32-bit constants, you store 256 quantized 8-bit constants plus one 32-bit mean. The dequantization formula is modified to simply reverse this operation: during inference or training, the system first dequantizes the compressed constants to get the residuals, adds the stored mean back to each residual to recover the original positive $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values, and then uses these recovered scales to dequantize the actual model weights. As shown in Equation 6 of the paper, this nested process $\text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1, c_2), \mathbf{W})$ ensures that despite intermediate subtractions and quantizations, final reconstruction remains accurate enough to preserve model performance while significantly reducing memory usage.

### Why does applying LoRA to ALL linear layers matter more than rank $r$ for matching 16-bit performance?
Question:
In Section 4, under 'Default LoRA hyperparameters do not match 16-bit performance,' the authors state that applying LoRA only to query and value projections (the standard practice from the original LoRA paper) fails to replicate full finetuning performance on large models. They claim that using adapters on *every* linear transformer block layer is required.

This seems counter-intuitive to me because LoRA is supposed to be parameter-efficient. If adding adapters to every layer increases the parameter count significantly, doesn't that defeat some purpose of PEFT? Also, why does increasing coverage (number of layers) have a bigger impact than increasing the rank $r$ of individual adapters? Is there a theoretical reason why specific layers (like feed-forward networks) contribute more to adaptation capability in large models than others?
Answer:
The finding that applying LoRA to all linear layers is more critical than increasing the rank $r$ stems from the fundamental difference between the memory bottleneck of training and the expressive capacity required to recover 16-bit performance. While LoRA was originally designed as a Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) method to minimize trainable parameters, the QLoRA paper clarifies that in the context of Large Language Model (LLM) finetuning, the dominant memory consumption comes not from the adapter weights themselves, but from the activation gradients and optimizer states associated with the forward and backward passes. As noted in Section 3 under "Memory Requirement of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning," for a 7B model, the LoRA parameters occupy only roughly 26 MB, whereas the input gradients alone can consume hundreds of megabytes. Consequently, expanding the number of adapters from just the query and value projections to every linear layer (including key projections, output projections, and feed-forward network matrices) increases the total parameter count negligibly relative to the overall memory footprint. This allows practitioners to maximize model expressivity without hitting memory limits that would otherwise restrict batch size or sequence length.

The reason this increased coverage outweighs increasing the rank $r$ lies in the nature of how neural networks adapt to new tasks. Increasing the rank $r$ expands the dimensionality of the update within a specific layer, assuming that the bottleneck is the complexity of the transformation at that single location. However, the authors' empirical results in Section 4 suggest that for large models, the limitation is not the depth of adaptation per layer, but rather the breadth of adaptation across the architecture. By restricting LoRA to only query and value matrices, you are effectively freezing a significant portion of the model's computational graph, preventing gradients from updating vast swathes of the network's representational capacity. When you apply adapters to every linear layer, you enable gradient flow and parameter updates throughout the entire depth and width of the transformer blocks. This comprehensive coverage allows the model to make small, coordinated adjustments across all functional components—attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks alike—which collectively sum to a powerful adaptation capable of matching full 16-bit finetuning. Essentially, distributing low-rank updates everywhere provides a richer manifold for optimization than concentrating higher-rank updates in isolated parts of the network, proving that for large-scale models, architectural coverage is a more potent driver of performance than local adapter complexity.

### Is there a risk of numerical instability when dequantizing NF4 weights repeatedly during training steps?
Question:
QLoRA works by storing weights in 4-bit NF4 format but dequantizing them to BFloat16 for every forward and backward pass computation. Since this happens at every optimization step over thousands of iterations, could repeated dequantization (and potentially re-quantization if weights were being updated, though here they are frozen) lead to accumulated numerical errors or drift?

Or is the dequantization deterministic and stable enough that $\text{dequant}(\text{quant}(x)) \approx x$ within BFloat16 precision limits such that it doesn't affect gradient calculations for the LoRA adapters? I'm worried about whether treating these dequantized weights as 'true' BFloat16 values during matrix multiplication introduces subtle noise that affects convergence compared to native 16-bit training.
Answer:
There is no risk of accumulated numerical instability or drift from repeatedly dequantizing the NF4 weights in QLoRA because the base model weights are strictly frozen throughout the training process. The concern about iterative error accumulation typically arises when a value is quantized and dequantized in a loop where the output of one step becomes the input of the next; however, in QLoRA, the source of truth for the weights remains the static 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) tensor stored in memory. At every forward and backward pass, the system performs a deterministic operation defined as $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} = \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})$, mapping the fixed 4-bit representation directly to BFloat16 for computation. Since the underlying $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ never changes, the dequantized result $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$ is identical at iteration $t$ and iteration $t+1000$, preventing any possibility of drift.

The only quantization error introduced is the initial discretization error inherent in representing the original high-precision weights with 4 bits, which is minimized by using NF4, a data type designed to be information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights. The paper explicitly notes that while standard 4-bit quantization for inference can lead to performance degradation, QLoRA recovers full 16-bit performance by backpropagating gradients through these frozen quantized weights into the trainable Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA). The gradients $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ are computed using the dequantized BFloat16 values, allowing the adapters to compensate for any static precision loss from the base model without ever modifying the base weights themselves. Consequently, the "noise" you worry about is actually a fixed, bounded approximation error present from step one, not an accumulating instability, which is empirically validated by the paper's finding that 4-bit QLoRA matches 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA performance across various benchmarks.

### How do Paged Optimizers interact with Gradient Checkpointing specifically regarding memory spikes?
Question:
The paper mentions that Paged Optimizers use NVIDIA unified memory to handle memory spikes caused by gradient checkpointing when processing long sequences. I understand gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory by recomputing activations during the backward pass.

However, where exactly do these 'spikes' come from if checkpointing is supposed to reduce peak memory usage? Is it related to temporary buffers allocated during recomputation or optimizer state updates? And how does paging optimizer states to CPU RAM prevent OOM errors without drastically slowing down training due to PCIe bandwidth bottlenecks? The paper claims runtime is unaffected; is this because spikes are short-lived?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction you identified stems from distinguishing between the *average* memory reduction provided by gradient checkpointing and the *peak* memory demands that occur transiently during specific operations within the backward pass. While gradient checkpointing significantly lowers the baseline memory footprint by discarding intermediate activations during the forward pass and recomputing them on demand during the backward pass, this recomputation process itself creates temporary memory spikes. Specifically, when processing a mini-batch with a long sequence length, the model must materialize a large block of activations simultaneously to compute the gradients for that segment. This momentary allocation can exceed the available VRAM, even if the average memory usage throughout the rest of the iteration remains well within limits. Without a mechanism to handle these transient peaks, the training process would trigger an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) error despite the overall efficiency gains from checkpointing.

Paged Optimizers address this specific vulnerability by leveraging NVIDIA's unified memory feature, which allows the system to treat CPU RAM and GPU VRAM as a single contiguous address space. In this setup, optimizer states (such as momentum and variance buffers in Adam) are allocated in "paged" memory. Under normal conditions, these states reside in GPU memory for fast access. However, when a memory spike occurs during activation recomputation and free GPU space becomes insufficient, the driver automatically evicts rarely accessed pages of optimizer state to CPU RAM. Once the spike subsides and the optimizer update step requires those states again, they are paged back into GPU memory. This mechanism prevents OOM errors not by eliminating the spike, but by providing an overflow buffer in system RAM that absorbs the excess demand dynamically.

Regarding your concern about PCIe bandwidth bottlenecks slowing down training, the paper notes that runtime remains unaffected because these paging events are infrequent and short-lived. The memory spikes caused by gradient checkpointing are localized to specific moments during the backward propagation of long sequences; they do not persist throughout the entire training step. Consequently, the volume of data transferred over the PCIe bus is relatively small compared to the total computation time of the forward and backward passes. The authors explicitly mention in Section 4 ("QLoRA vs. Standard Finetuning") that empirical measurements on $65$B models with a batch size of $16$ showed identical training speeds between paged and regular optimizers. This suggests that the latency introduced by swapping pages is negligible relative to the compute-bound nature of large language model training, effectively allowing researchers to utilize longer sequence lengths or larger batches on constrained hardware without performance penalties.
