# Introduction: The Case for Tokenizer-Free Language Models

The landscape of modern artificial intelligence has been dominated by Large Language Models (LLMs) that rely heavily on a specific preprocessing step known as tokenization. In this standard paradigm, raw text data—represented fundamentally as sequences of bytes—is converted into a static set of discrete units called tokens before being fed into a neural network. This process, often utilizing algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), groups bytes into a finite vocabulary determined prior to training. While this approach has enabled the rapid scaling of models to billions of parameters, it introduces inherent structural biases. By compressing strings into fixed tokens, these models lose direct access to the underlying orthographic and character-level features of the data. This limitation manifests in several critical shortcomings: sensitivity to input noise, a lack of genuine orthographic knowledge, domain and modality fragility, and significant inequities when handling multilingual data, particularly for low-resource languages or scripts that do not align well with the predefined vocabulary. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) emerges as a novel architecture designed to dismantle this dependency on static tokenization, proposing a system that learns directly from raw bytes while matching the performance of its token-based counterparts.

The primary motivation for shifting toward byte-level modeling lies in the desire for robustness and universality. Tokenization-based LLMs treat tokens as atomic units, meaning the model has no direct visibility into the constituent characters of a word unless those specific character combinations exist as tokens in its vocabulary. Consequently, these models often struggle with tasks requiring fine-grained character manipulation, such as spelling corrections, phonological reasoning, or processing text with intentional noise like random capitalization or dropped characters. Furthermore, the static nature of the vocabulary creates a "long-tail" problem where rare words, code snippets, or non-Latin scripts are fragmented inefficiently or represented poorly, leading to multilingual inequity. BLT addresses these issues by operating directly on the byte stream, thereby inheriting a natural robustness to noise and an intrinsic understanding of sub-word structures. By eliminating the heuristic pre-processing step of tokenization, BLT aims to create models that are not only more capable in edge cases but also more equitable across diverse linguistic domains.

However, the transition from token-based to byte-based modeling is not merely an architectural tweak; it presents profound computational challenges that have historically prevented byte-level models from competing at scale. The fundamental issue is sequence length. A sentence that might comprise 20 tokens in a BPE-based model could easily expand to over 100 bytes. Since the computational cost of a Transformer architecture is heavily influenced by the sequence length processed by its attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks, training on raw bytes traditionally incurs a prohibitive cost. Previous attempts to mitigate this through efficient attention mechanisms or attention-free architectures have shown promise but generally only for small-scale models. At the scale of billions of parameters, the computational bottleneck shifts from the attention mechanism to the large feed-forward network layers that must execute for every single unit in the sequence. Therefore, simply removing the tokenizer and feeding bytes into a standard Transformer results in a model that is computationally unsustainable for large-scale training and inference.

To overcome the efficiency barrier while retaining the benefits of byte-level modeling, BLT introduces a core innovation: dynamic compute allocation via patching. Rather than treating every byte as an independent step requiring full model capacity, or grouping them into rigid static tokens, BLT segments bytes into dynamically sized "patches." These patches serve as the primary units of computation for the model's most expensive components. The segmentation strategy is driven by the entropy of the next-byte prediction; essentially, the model allocates more computational resources and creates new patch boundaries where the data is complex and unpredictable, such as at the beginning of a new word or sentence. Conversely, in regions where the data is highly predictable and low-entropy, such as the suffixes of common words or repetitive code structures, BLT groups larger sequences of bytes into single patches. This approach ensures that computational power is not wasted on trivial predictions but is instead concentrated where the information density demands it.

This dynamic patching mechanism fundamentally alters the trade-off between model capacity and inference cost. In traditional tokenization-based LLMs, every token consumes an identical amount of compute regardless of its predictive difficulty; predicting the end of a common word costs the same floating-point operations (flops) as predicting a rare technical term. BLT breaks this symmetry by allowing the model to process long stretches of predictable data with minimal overhead while invoking its full global transformer capacity only when necessary. This is achieved through a hybrid architecture consisting of lightweight local models that handle byte-to-patch encoding and decoding, and a massive global "Latent Transformer" that operates on the patch representations. By decoupling the granularity of input data (bytes) fromthe granularityofcomputation(patches),BLTachievesasignificantreductionininferenceflopswithout sacrificing modeling power. The result is an architecture that can dynamically adapt its compute expenditure based on context complexity, offering a pathway to scale byte-level models efficiently up to 8 billion parameters and beyond.

The empirical contributions of the Byte Latent Transformer demonstrate that tokenizer-free architectures are not just theoretically sound but practically viable at state-of-the-art scales. Through rigorous flop-controlled scaling studies involving up to 4 trillion training bytes, BLT has been shown to match the training performance of Llama 3, a leading tokenization-based model family. More remarkably, BLT offers flexible efficiency gains; for a fixed inference cost budget, it can achieve significantly better scaling trends than token-based models by simultaneously increasing both the model size andthe average patch size. Experiments indicate that BLT can deliver up to 50% savings in inference flops while maintaining competitive performance metrics. Beyond efficiency,the model exhibits superior robustness on downstream tasks involving noisy inputs，orthographicknowledge,andlow-resourcemachinetranslation,outperformingtoken-basedbaselinesthatlackdirectaccesstobyte-levelinformation.These findings collectively validate the thesis that dynamic patching allows byte-level models to transcend historical efficiency barriers，unlockinganewdimensionforscalinglargelanguagemodelsthatisbothmoreefficientandmorеробастthanpreviousapproaches。

# Patching Strategies: From Fixed Strides to Entropy-Based Segmentation

The fundamental innovation of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) lies in its departure from the rigid structure of traditional tokenization, replacing it with a flexible mechanism known as "patching." In conventional large language models, raw byte data is pre-processed into a static sequence of tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary. This process, while computationally convenient, imposes a fixed granularity on the model's understanding, often misaligning computational effort with the actual complexity of the data. BLT introduces patches as dynamically sized groups of bytes that serve as the primary units of computation for the model's global transformer. Unlike tokens, patches do not belong to a predefined vocabulary; instead, they are arbitrary sequences of bytes mapped to latent representations through learned encoder and decoder modules. This distinction is critical: while tokens bias how a string is compressed based on heuristics determined prior to training, patches allow the model to adaptively segment data based on contextual information density. By treating patches as variable-length containers rather than discrete symbols from a closed set, BLT decouples the model's operational resolution from a fixed dictionary, enabling a more nuanced allocation of computational resources where they are most needed.

The computational cost of processing data in transformer architectures is primarily driven by the number of steps executed by the main transformer block. In token-based models, this cost is strictly proportional to the number of tokens, regardless of whether a token represents a simple suffix or a complex, unpredictable word stem. BLT alters this dynamic by defining the cost of processing based on the number of patches required to encode a sequence. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ segments a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_i \mid i=1,\ldots, n\}$ of length $n$ into a sequence of $m$ patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j \mid j=1,\ldots,m\}$, where $m < n$. This function maps each byte $x_i$ to a binary indicator set $\{0,1\}$, where $1$ signifies the start of a new patch. The average size of these patches becomes the dominant factor in determining the floating-point operations (flops) required for both training and inference. Consequently, the strategy used to define patch boundaries directly dictates the efficiency of the model. A naive approach might result in wasted compute on predictable data or insufficient capacity for complex predictions, whereas an optimized strategy aligns the frequency of expensive global transformer calls with the inherent uncertainty of the data stream.

# Static Patching: Strided Approaches and Their Downsides

The most intuitive method for grouping bytes into patches is strided patching, where bytes are segmented into fixed-size chunks of length $k$. This approach, utilized in prior works such as MegaByte, offers significant implementation simplicity. By enforcing a rigid stride, researchers can easily control the average patch size and consequently manage the total flop count during training and inference. If one desires to halve the number of transformer steps, one simply doubles the stride $k$. However, while strided patching provides a straightforward mechanism for budget control, it fails catastrophically in terms of semantic alignment and dynamic resource allocation. The primary deficiency of static patching is its inability to distinguish between regions of high and low informational complexity. A fixed stride treats a sequence of predictable whitespace characters in source code with the same computational gravity as a dense mathematical expression or the beginning of a novel concept. This results in a gross inefficiency: the model expends valuable transformer steps predicting trivial continuations while potentially lacking sufficient capacity within a single step to resolve highly ambiguous byte sequences that span across arbitrary stride boundaries.

Furthermore, static patching introduces inconsistency in how similar linguistic units are processed. Because the segmentation is blind to content, identical words or phrases can be split differently depending on their absolute position within the byte stream relative to the stride counter. For instance, a common word might be encapsulated entirely within one patch in one occurrence but fractured across two or three patches in another simply due to an offset in the starting byte index. This lack of contextual awareness prevents the model from developing stable representations for recurring patterns, as the structural input varies unpredictably for semantically identical inputs. While strided patching serves as a useful baseline for establishing that byte-level modeling is feasible without tokenization, its rigidity makes it suboptimal for achieving state-of-the-art performance. It represents a trade-off that sacrifices intelligence in compute allocation for ease of implementation, a compromise that BLT seeks to overcome through more sophisticated segmentation strategies.

# Heuristic Patching: Space-Based Segmentation

To addressthe inconsistencies of strided patching while maintaining simplicity, researchers have explored heuristic approaches such as space-based segmentation. This method creates new patch boundaries immediately following any "space-like" byte. In this context, space-like bytes are defined broadly to include any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, with the constraint that every patch must contain at least one non-space-like character. This strategy leverages natural linguistic boundaries prevalent in many languages, effectively ensuring that words are treated as coherent units during processing. By aligning patch breaks with whitespace, space-based patching guarantees that the latent transformer executes once per word (or linguistic unit), thereby allocating computational steps to points where prediction difficulty typically spikes—specifically, at the onset of new words. # The BLT Architecture: A Hierarchical Design

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a fundamental shift in how large language models process sequential data. Traditional architectures rely heavily on tokenization, a static pre-processing step that groups bytes into fixed vocabulary items before they ever reach the neural network. While effective, this approach imposes rigid constraints on computation, forcing the model to expend the same amount of computational effort on every token regardless of its informational complexity. BLT dismantles this rigidity by operating directly on raw bytes but organizing them into dynamically sized "patches." This architectural innovation allows the model to allocate compute adaptively, dedicating more resources to complex, high-entropy regions of text while skimming over predictable sequences. To achieve this, BLT employs a sophisticated three-part hierarchy consisting of a Local Encoder, a Global Latent Transformer, and a Local Decoder. This chapter dissects these components, explaining how they interact to bridge the gap between fine-grained byte-level details and coarse-grained semantic reasoning.

At the highest level, the BLT architecture is designed to separate concerns between local signal processing and global contextual understanding. The system does not treat all bytes equally; instead, it views them through the lens of variable-length patches determined by the entropy of the data. The architecture functions as a pipeline where raw bytes enter a lightweight Local Encoder, which compresses sequences of bytes into dense latent vectors known as patch representations. These patches serve as the primary units of computation for the central engine of the model: the Global Latent Transformer. This massive autoregressive model reasons over the sequence of patches rather than individual bytes, drastically reducing the sequence length it must attend to and allowing it to focus its capacity on high-level dependencies. Finally, once the global model has updated these latent representations with contextual information, a Local Decoder expands them back into precise byte-level predictions. This hierarchical design ensures that the computationally expensive attention mechanisms of the global model are invoked only when necessary—specifically, at patch boundaries—while lighter-weight operations handle the intra-patch dynamics.

The defining characteristic of this architecture is its dynamic nature. Unlike token-based models where the segmentation of text is fixed prior to training, BLT's segmentation is driven by data complexity. Patches are formed by grouping bytes that share relatively uniform information density, often bounded by points of high uncertainty in next-byte prediction. Consequently, the number of steps the Global Latent Transformer executes varies depending on the input content. A predictable string of whitespace or repetitive code might be collapsed into a single large patch, requiring only one step of global computation. Conversely, a complex mathematical derivation or the beginning of a new sentence might result in many small patches, triggering frequent invocations of the global model. This structure fundamentally alters the trade-off between model size and inference cost, enabling BLT to scale efficiently by adjusting both the size of the global transformer and the average size of the patches it processes.

# High-Level Architectural Overview

The structural blueprint of BLT diverges sharply from standard Transformer architectures by introducing distinct modules dedicated to resolution conversion between bytes and latents. In a conventional token-based Large Language Model (LLM), the embedding layer maps discrete tokens directly to hidden states, which are then processed uniformly by a stack of identical Transformer layers. There is no distinction between "local" processing within a token and "global" processing across tokens because the token itself is an atomic unit devoid of internal structure visible to the model. BLT rejects this atomization. Instead, it explicitly acknowledges that bytes within a patch are highly correlated and can be processed locally, while relationships between patches require global attention.

The architecture comprises three primary neural networks: the Local Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$), the Global Latent Transformer ($\mathcal{G}$), and the Local Decoder ($\mathcal{D}$). The flow of data begins with a sequence of raw bytes $\pmb{x} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$. These bytes are first segmented into $m$ patches $\pmb{p} = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_m\}$ based on an external entropy signal derived from a small auxiliary language model. This segmentation dictates where computational boundaries lie. The Local Encoder then takes each patch $p_j$, which consists of a variable number of bytes, and transforms this sequence into a single latent vector representation $h_j$. This operation effectively downsamples the sequence from $n$ bytes to $m$ latents, where typically $m \ll n$.

Once the byte sequence has been compressed into patch latents, these vectors are fed into the Global Latent Transformer $\mathcal{G}$. This component is an autoregressive Transformer similar in spirit to Llama 3 but operates exclusively on the sequence of patch representations. It utilizes block-causal attention masks to ensure that each patch can only attend to itself and preceding patches within the document context. Because $\mathcal{G}$ operates on patches rather than bytes, its sequence length is significantly shorter than that of a byte-level model processing every character individually. This reduction in sequence length is critical; since the computational cost of self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length (or linearly with optimized kernels but still dependent on length), operating on patches yields substantial efficiency gains. Furthermore, because patches are dynamically sized based on entropy, $\mathcal{G}$ naturally spends more steps analyzing complex data segments where patches are short and frequent, and fewer steps on simple segments where patches are long.

After $\mathcal{G}$ has processedthe sequence and produced updated output representations $o_j$ for each patch, these latents must be converted back into actionable byte predictions. # Computational Efficiency and Scaling Laws

The transition from token-based Large Language Models (LLMs) to byte-level architectures introduces a fundamental shift in how computational resources are allocated and scaled. Traditional models rely on static tokenization, which fixes the granularity of computation regardless of the underlying information density of the text. In contrast, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) introduces a dynamic mechanism where computation is driven by "patches"—variable-length groups of bytes determined by the entropy of the data. This chapter explores the mathematical framework used to quantify these efficiencies, defines appropriate metrics for comparing byte-level and token-level models, and presents empirical scaling laws that demonstrate BLT's superiority in both training optimization and inference-time performance. By understanding these principles, one can appreciate how BLT breaks the rigid trade-offs inherent in fixed-vocabulary systems, allowing for models that scale more effectively within fixed computational budgets.

# FLOPs Estimation Framework for Hybrid Architectures

To rigorously compare the efficiency of BLT against traditional token-based models, we must establish a precise method for counting Floating Point Operations (FLOPs). In standard Transformer architectures, the computational cost is dominated by the feed-forward network (FFN) layers and the query-key-value projections within the self-attention mechanisms. However, BLT employs a hybrid architecture consisting of a large global latent transformer operating on patches, flanked by lightweight local encoder and decoder models operating on individual bytes. Therefore, the total FLOP count for BLT cannot be estimated using standard equations alone; it requires summing the costs of these distinct components while accounting for their differing sequence lengths and dimensionalities.

The estimation framework assumes that the input embedding layer acts as an efficient lookup table rather than a dense matrix multiplication, rendering it a zero-FLOP operation. The backward pass is estimated to require twice the number of FLOPs as the forward pass, consistent with standard practices in deep learning literature. For BLT specifically, the total FLOPs per byte, denoted as $\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}$, is calculated by aggregating the costs of the global latent transformer, the local encoder transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks that bridge them. The formula captures the interplay between patch size and model depth:

$$
\begin{align}
    \text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} &= \frac{\text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p,V=0)}{n_p}\\
   &+\text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) \\
   &+ \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) \\ 
   &+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times \frac{k}{n_p} \\ 
   &+ \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p) 
\end{align}
$$

In this equation, $n_{ctx}$ represents the sequence length in bytes, while $n_p$ denotes the average patch size. The term $m$ varies depending on whether the module operates on bytes or patches; for instance, the global transformer processes a sequence of length $n_{ctx}/n_p$, significantly shorter than the byte-level sequence. The variables $h$ and $l$ represent the hidden dimensionality and number of layers for the global ($\mathcal{G}$), encoder ($\mathcal{E}$), and decoder ($\mathcal{D}$) components respectively. The parameter $V$ indicates the vocabulary size for output projections, which is relevant only for the local decoder predicting bytes from a set of 256 possibilities. The ratio $k$ reflects how many local model splits concatenate to form a global representation. This granular breakdown reveals that increasing the patch size $n_p$ directly reduces the frequency with which the computationally expensive global transformer is invoked, thereby lowering the overall FLOP count per byte without necessarily sacrificing model capacity.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric

Comparing the performance of byte-level models against token-based models presents a metricological challenge. Traditionally, perplexity is used to evaluate language models, but this metric is inherently tied to the size and composition of the tokenizer's vocabulary. A model predicting tokens from a vocabulary of 32,000 items faces a different probabilistic landscape than one predicting from 128,000 items or raw bytes. Consequently, perplexity values are not directly comparable across architectures with different tokenization schemes. To address this ambiguity and ensure a fair comparison grounded in information theory rather than arbitrary vocabulary choices, we utilize Bits-Per-Byte (BPB).

BPB serves as a tokenizer-independent measure of uncertainty. It normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the total number of bytes inthe input sequence rather than the number of tokens. Mathematically, for a data sequence $\pmb{x}$ containing $n_{\text{bytes}}$ bytes, BPB is defined as:

$$
\text{BPB}(x)= \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}
$$

Here, $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})$ represents the sum of the cross-entropy loss overthe sequence. By dividing by $\ln(2)$, we convertthe natural logarithm units ofthe loss into bits. This metric effectively measures how many bits of information are required,on average,to encode each byte inthe dataset given themodel's probability distribution.Because it operates atthe fundamental unitof data storage—the byte—it allows us to directly comparethe predictive powerof BLT models against BPE-based models like Llama 3,revealing whetherthe architectural differences leadto genuine improvements in modelingthe data distribution or merely reflect artifacts oftokenization granularity.This normalization is criticalfor validatingthat BLT achieves paritywith state-of-the-art tokenizers despiteoperating ona much finer-grained input space.
