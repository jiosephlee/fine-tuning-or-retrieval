Chapter 1: # Introduction: The Case for Tokenizer-Free Language Models

The landscape of modern artificial intelligence has been dominated by Large Language Models (LLMs) that rely heavily on a specific preprocessing step known as tokenization. In this standard paradigm, raw text data—represented fundamentally as sequences of bytes—is converted into a static set of discrete units called tokens before being fed into a neural network. This process, often utilizing algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), groups bytes into a finite vocabulary determined prior to training. While this approach has enabled the rapid scaling of models to billions of parameters, it introduces inherent structural biases. By compressing strings into fixed tokens, these models lose direct access to the underlying orthographic and character-level features of the data. This limitation manifests in several critical shortcomings: sensitivity to input noise, a lack of genuine orthographic knowledge, domain and modality fragility, and significant inequities when handling multilingual data, particularly for low-resource languages or scripts that do not align well with the predefined vocabulary. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) emerges as a novel architecture designed to dismantle this dependency on static tokenization, proposing a system that learns directly from raw bytes while matching the performance of its token-based counterparts.

The primary motivation for shifting toward byte-level modeling lies in the desire for robustness and universality. Tokenization-based LLMs treat tokens as atomic units, meaning the model has no direct visibility into the constituent characters of a word unless those specific character combinations exist as tokens in its vocabulary. Consequently, these models often struggle with tasks requiring fine-grained character manipulation, such as spelling corrections, phonological reasoning, or processing text with intentional noise like random capitalization or dropped characters. Furthermore, the static nature of the vocabulary creates a "long-tail" problem where rare words, code snippets, or non-Latin scripts are fragmented inefficiently or represented poorly, leading to multilingual inequity. BLT addresses these issues by operating directly on the byte stream, thereby inheriting a natural robustness to noise and an intrinsic understanding of sub-word structures. By eliminating the heuristic pre-processing step of tokenization, BLT aims to create models that are not only more capable in edge cases but also more equitable across diverse linguistic domains.

However, the transition from token-based to byte-based modeling is not merely an architectural tweak; it presents profound computational challenges that have historically prevented byte-level models from competing at scale. The fundamental issue is sequence length. A sentence that might comprise 20 tokens in a BPE-based model could easily expand to over 100 bytes. Since the computational cost of a Transformer architecture is heavily influenced by the sequence length processed by its attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks, training on raw bytes traditionally incurs a prohibitive cost. Previous attempts to mitigate this through efficient attention mechanisms or attention-free architectures have shown promise but generally only for small-scale models. At the scale of billions of parameters, the computational bottleneck shifts from the attention mechanism to the large feed-forward network layers that must execute for every single unit in the sequence. Therefore, simply removing the tokenizer and feeding bytes into a standard Transformer results in a model that is computationally unsustainable for large-scale training and inference.

To overcome the efficiency barrier while retaining the benefits of byte-level modeling, BLT introduces a core innovation: dynamic compute allocation via patching. Rather than treating every byte as an independent step requiring full model capacity, or grouping them into rigid static tokens, BLT segments bytes into dynamically sized "patches." These patches serve as the primary units of computation for the model's most expensive components. The segmentation strategy is driven by the entropy of the next-byte prediction; essentially, the model allocates more computational resources and creates new patch boundaries where the data is complex and unpredictable, such as at the beginning of a new word or sentence. Conversely, in regions where the data is highly predictable and low-entropy, such as the suffixes of common words or repetitive code structures, BLT groups larger sequences of bytes into single patches. This approach ensures that computational power is not wasted on trivial predictions but is instead concentrated where the information density demands it.

This dynamic patching mechanism fundamentally alters the trade-off between model capacity and inference cost. In traditional tokenization-based LLMs, every token consumes an identical amount of compute regardless of its predictive difficulty; predicting the end of a common word costs the same floating-point operations (flops) as predicting a rare technical term. BLT breaks this symmetry by allowing the model to process long stretches of predictable data with minimal overhead while invoking its full global transformer capacity only when necessary. This is achieved through a hybrid architecture consisting of lightweight local models that handle byte-to-patch encoding and decoding, and a massive global "Latent Transformer" that operates on the patch representations. By decoupling the granularity of input data (bytes) fromthe granularityofcomputation(patches),BLTachievesasignificantreductionininferenceflopswithout sacrificing modeling power. The result is an architecture that can dynamically adapt its compute expenditure based on context complexity, offering a pathway to scale byte-level models efficiently up to 8 billion parameters and beyond.

The empirical contributions of the Byte Latent Transformer demonstrate that tokenizer-free architectures are not just theoretically sound but practically viable at state-of-the-art scales. Through rigorous flop-controlled scaling studies involving up to 4 trillion training bytes, BLT has been shown to match the training performance of Llama 3, a leading tokenization-based model family. More remarkably, BLT offers flexible efficiency gains; for a fixed inference cost budget, it can achieve significantly better scaling trends than token-based models by simultaneously increasing both the model size andthe average patch size. Experiments indicate that BLT can deliver up to 50% savings in inference flops while maintaining competitive performance metrics.
