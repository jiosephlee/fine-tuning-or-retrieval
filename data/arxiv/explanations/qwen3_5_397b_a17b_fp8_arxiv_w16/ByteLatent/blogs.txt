# Goodbye Tokens, Hello Patches: How BLT Dynamically Allocates Compute

For years, the standard recipe for building Large Language Models (LLMs) has remained remarkably consistent: take raw text, run it through a tokenizer to break it into static chunks called tokens, and feed those tokens into a Transformer. While this approach has powered the AI revolution, it comes with inherent inefficiencies. Tokenizers treat every token equally, regardless of whether that token represents a predictable suffix or the start of a complex new idea. This "one-size-fits-all" compute allocation is wasteful.

Enter the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), a new architecture that ditches fixed vocabularies entirely. Instead of static tokens, BLT processes raw bytes grouped into dynamic "patches." By leveraging the entropy of the data to determine where these patches begin and end, BLT allocates computational resources exactly where they are needed most. The result is a model that matches the performance of state-of-the-art tokenized models like Llama 3 while offering up to 50% improvements in inference efficiency.

## The Problem with Static Tokens

Traditional LLMs rely on tokenization as a heuristic pre-processing step to compress raw bytes into manageable units. However, this introduces a rigid structure where the model spends the same amount of compute processing every single token. This creates a mismatch between computational effort and information density.

Consider the difference between predicting the end of a common word versus predicting the first word of a new sentence. The former is a low-entropy, highly predictable task, while the latter often requires significant reasoning and context integration. In a standard token-based model, both scenarios consume identical floating-point operations (flops) because the architecture cannot distinguish between easy and hard predictions at the structural level. Furthermore, tokenization biases how strings are compressed, leading to issues with multilingual equity, sensitivity to input noise, and a lack of genuine orthographic knowledge.

Previous attempts to train models directly on bytes faced prohibitive costs due to the sheer length of byte sequences. While some researchers tried to optimize attention mechanisms for small models, the bottleneck at scale is not attention—it is the massive feed-forward networks that process every single unit of input. To solve this, we need a way to reduce the number of steps the main transformer takes without losing critical information.

## Enter Dynamic Patching via Entropy

BLT solves the efficiency problem by replacing static tokens with dynamic patches. Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a finite vocabulary determined before training, patches are arbitrary groups of bytes formed on the fly. The core innovation lies in how BLT decides where to cut these patches: it uses entropy.

The system employs a small, lightweight byte-level language model to estimate the uncertainty, or entropy, of predicting the next byte in a sequence. Mathematically, for a byte $x_i$ given previous bytes $\pmb{x}_{<i}$, the entropy $H(x_i)$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

When the entropy is low, it means the model is confident about what comes next; these are predictable regions like the middle of a common word or repetitive code structures. When entropy spikes, it signals high uncertainty and high information density, such as the beginning of a new concept or a rare character sequence. BLT uses these entropy estimates to segment the byte stream. It creates longer patches in low-entropy regions and shorter patches where uncertainty is high.

This approach ensures that computationally expensive operations are reserved for complex parts of the data. If a sequence of bytes is highly predictable, BLT groups them into a large patch and processes them with fewer transformer steps. Conversely, difficult predictions trigger more frequent updates from the main model. This dynamic allocation allows BLT to achieve an average patch size significantly larger than typical token sizes—up to 8 bytes compared to Llama 3's average of 4.4 bytes—drastically reducing the total number of steps required during inference.

## Inside the BLT Architecture

To make this dynamic patching work, BLT utilizes a hybrid architecture consisting of three distinct components: a local encoder, a global latent transformer, and a local decoder.

The process begins with the **Local Encoder**, a lightweight transformer that ingests raw bytes. To ensure robust representations without relying on huge embedding tables, BLT incorporates hash $n$-gram embeddings. For each byte position $i$, the model constructs byte-grams $g_{i,n}$ for various lengths $n$ and maps them to indices in fixed-size embedding tables using a rolling polynomial hash function:

$$\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_{n}^{hash}|$$

These enriched byte representations are then pooled into patch representations via cross-attention layers. This step effectively compresses variable-length byte sequences into fixed-dimensional vectors ready for the main model.

These patch vectors are then fed into the **Global Latent Transformer**. This is the heavyweight component responsible for autoregressive reasoning over the sequence of patches rather than individual bytes or tokens. Because it operates on patches instead of raw bytes, it runs less frequently when data is predictable, saving substantial compute. The global model uses block-causal attention to ensure it only sees past context within the current document.

Finally, the **Local Decoder** takes the output representations from the global transformer and reconstructs them back into raw bytes. Like the encoder, it uses cross-attention to distribute patch-level information back down to individual byte positions before predicting the final output distribution over the 256 possible byte values ($V=256$). This modular design allows BLT to enjoythe representational power of large transformers while maintaining fine-grained control over byte-level details.

## Scaling Beyond Tokenization Limits

One of the most compelling findings from BLT research is its scaling behavior under fixed inference budgets. In traditional tokenized models there is a hard trade-off: increasing vocabulary size reduces sequence length but inflates parameter counts in embedding layers and output projections leaving little room for optimization . BLT breaks this constraint by decoupling patch size from any fixed vocabulary . Researchers can simultaneously increase both model size and average patch size while keeping inference costs constant . 

Experiments scaling models up to 8 billion parameters and training on trillions of bytes show that BLT matches compute-optimal scaling trends established by Llama 3 . More impressively when comparing models constrained by an equal inference flop budget larger models equipped with larger patches consistently outperform their smaller counter parts using finer granularities . Essentially longer patches save enough compute during inference that those saved flops can be reinvested into making global latent transformers bigger enabling superior performance without increasing latency costs .

# Why Your AI Struggles with Typos (And How Byte-Level Models Fix It)

Have you ever noticed how a state-of-the-art Large Language Model (LLM) can write a sonnet or debug complex code, yet completely falls apart when you introduce a few typos, strange capitalization, or text in a low-resource language? You might type "Who compozed the Magik Flute?" and watch the model hallucinate an answer or refuse to engage. This fragility isn't a bug; it is a fundamental consequence of how these models process text. Most modern LLMs rely on tokenization, a preprocessing step that breaks text into chunks based on a fixed dictionary. If your input doesn't match the dictionary entries perfectly, the model struggles to understand it.

Recent research introduces the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), an architecture that bypasses this rigid dictionary entirely. By training directly on raw bytes and dynamically grouping them into "patches," BLT achieves performance parity with top-tier models like Llama 3 while exhibiting superhuman robustness to noise and a deep understanding of character-level structures. This shift from tokens to bytes solves the long-standing problem of orthographic brittleness without requiring massive amounts of additional training data.

## The Tokenization Bottleneck

To understand why traditional models fail at simple spelling errors, we must look at tokenization. Standard LLMs do not see individual letters; they see tokens, which are sub-word units drawn from a finite vocabulary, often containing tens of thousands of entries. This vocabulary is fixed before training begins. When a model encounters a word spelled correctly, it maps to a known token. However, if you introduce a typo, change the casing unexpectedly, or use a rare dialect, the tokenizer may split the word into unfamiliar fragments or unknown characters.

This creates an inductive bias where the model lacks true orthographic knowledge. It knows that "Mozart" is a token associated with music, but it does not inherently understand that "Mozart" is composed of specific letters in a specific order. Consequently, when the input is perturbed—such as random capitalization or dropped characters—the resulting token sequence looks nothing like what the model saw during training. The model's confidence plummets because its internal representation of the world is tied to these static token IDs rather than the fluid nature of raw text. Furthermore, this approach disadvantages low-resource languages that are poorly represented in the standard vocabulary, leading to significant performance gaps across different linguistic communities.

## Enter the Byte Latent Transformer

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) addresses these issues by eliminating the fixed vocabulary altogether. Instead of mapping text to predefined tokens, BLT operates directly on raw bytes. However, processing every single byte individually with a massive transformer would be computationally prohibitive due to the sheer length of byte sequences compared to token sequences. BLT solves this efficiency problem through a novel mechanism called dynamic patching.

In BLT, bytes are grouped into variable-sized "patches" based on the entropy, or uncertainty, of predicting the next byte. A small, lightweight byte-level model scans the input and identifies regions of high complexity—such as the beginning of a new word or a sudden shift in topic—as boundaries for new patches. Predictable sequences, like the end of a common word or repeating whitespace, are bundled into longer patches. This allows the main global transformer to focus its computational power where it is needed most: on the difficult predictions. By dynamically allocating compute based on data complexity rather than arbitrary token boundaries, BLT maintains efficiency while retaining full access to the underlying byte structure of the data.

## Superhuman Robustness to Noise

The most striking advantage of removing tokenization is the dramatic improvement in robustness to noisy inputs. In experiments comparing BLT against tokenizer-based models like Llama 3, researchers subjected both architectures to various forms of character-level corruption. These included converting text to all uppercase ("AntSpeak"), randomly dropping 10% of characters, mixing random cases throughout the text, and repeating characters excessively.

The results were definitive: BLT outperformed tokenizer-based models across every noise category by an average margin of 8 points on standard reasoning benchmarks like HellaSwag. Even more impressively, BLT trained on significantly less data than Llama 3.1 managed to surpass the larger model's performance on these noisy tasks. Because BLT sees every byte explicitly during training, it learns that "cOmPuTeR" and "computer" are fundamentally similar sequences of characters, whereas a token-based model might treat them as entirely different entities if their token IDs diverge significantly. This inherent flexibility allows BLT to generalize effortlessly to inputs that would confuse or break traditional systems.

## Deep Orthographic and Phonological Understanding

Beyond handling noise, operating at the byte level grants models a genuine understanding of sub-word structures that tokenizers obscure. The paper evaluates BLT on CUTE (Character Understanding and Text Editing), a benchmark designed to test skills like manipulating letter sequences, recognizing orthographic similarities, and performing precise edits. Token-based models often struggle here because they lack explicit knowledge of how tokens are spelled; they essentially memorize token patterns without decomposing them into letters.

BLT demonstrates exceptional proficiency in these areas, scoring over 25 points higher than BPE-based Llama 3 models on the CUTE benchmark. It achieved near-perfect accuracy (99.9%) on spelling manipulation tasks where traditional models failed consistently. Additionally, BLT showed superior capabilities in Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) conversion, accurately mapping written characters to their spoken sounds—a task that requires fine-grained knowledge of character combinations and linguistic rules that are often lost during tokenization.

## Bridging the Gap for Low-Resource Languages

The benefits of byte-level modeling extend critically to multilingual applications, particularly for low-resource languages. Tokenizers are typically optimized for high-frequency languages like English, meaning that scripts from underrepresented languages are often broken down into inefficient or nonsensical token fragments. This leads to poor performance and inequitable access to AI capabilities for speakers of those languages.

# Breaking the Scaling Wall: Growing Smarter Models Without Slowing Down Inference

In the world of Large Language Models (LLMs), there has long been an unavoidable trade-off: if you want a smarter model, you generally have to accept slower inference and higher computational costs. Traditionally, improving performance meant increasing the number of parameters, which directly inflates the floating-point operations (flops) required for every single token generated. However, recent research introduces a paradigm shift that challenges this zero-sum game. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) demonstrates that we can break this scaling wall by fundamentally changing how models process data, allowing us to build massive, powerful systems that actually run faster than their token-based counterparts like Llama 3.

## The Bottleneck of Fixed Tokens

To understand the breakthrough, we must first look at the limitation of current architectures. Standard LLMs rely on tokenization, a pre-processing step that groups raw bytes into a fixed vocabulary of tokens. Whether a token represents a common word like "the" or a complex technical term, the model allocates the exact same amount of computational power to process it. This rigid approach ignores the reality that predicting the end of a familiar word is computationally trivial compared to generating the start of a new, unpredictable sentence.

Furthermore, tokenization creates a hard ceiling on efficiency. Increasing the average size of tokens to reduce the sequence length requires expanding the vocabulary, which drastically increases the size of the model's output projection layer. This architectural constraint leaves little room for optimization; you cannot simply make tokens arbitrarily larger without paying a steep price in memory and compute elsewhere in the network.

## Dynamic Patching: Allocating Compute Where It Counts

The BLT architecture removes the fixed vocabulary entirely, operating directly on raw bytes. Instead of static tokens, BLT groups bytes into dynamically sized "patches." The core innovation lies in how these patches are formed. Rather than using arbitrary rules like splitting every $k$ bytes or cutting at whitespace, BLT uses entropy patching. This method employs a small, lightweight byte-level language model to estimate the uncertainty—or entropy—of the next byte prediction.

When the data is predictable and has low entropy, such as the trailing letters of a common word, BLT groups many bytes into a single large patch. Conversely, when the model encounters high-uncertainty regions, such as the beginning of a new concept or a complex mathematical expression, it creates smaller patches. This ensures that the heavy-lifting components of the network are invoked precisely when the data complexity demands it. By dynamically adjusting the granularity of computation, BLT avoids wasting resources on easy predictions while dedicating capacity to difficult ones.

## The Architecture Behind the Efficiency

The efficiency gains are driven by a hybrid architecture consisting of three distinct components: two small local models and one large global model. The local encoder and decoder are lightweight transformers responsible for compressing sequences of bytes into patch representations and decompressing them back into bytes, respectively. These modules handle the fine-grained byte-level details but are computationally inexpensive.

The bulk of the intelligence resides in the global Latent Transformer, which operates on the patch representations rather than individual bytes. Because this global model runs once per patch rather than once per byte, its invocation frequency is inversely proportional to the average patch size. If the average patch size doubles, the global transformer runs half as often. This decoupling allows engineers to scale up parameters aggressively without a linear increase in inference cost, provided they can maintain or increase the average patch size.

## Fixed Inference Scaling: A New Dimension for Growth

The most compelling evidence for this approach comes from the paper's "fixed inference scaling" experiments. In traditional scaling studies, researchers hold either model size or data constant while varying compute. BLT introduces a new variable: patch size. The researchers conducted experiments where they simultaneously increased both the model parameters and the average patch size while keeping total inference flops constant.

The results were striking. When comparing models with equivalent inference budgets, BLT architectures consistently outperformed tokenization-based models like those using BPE (Byte Pair Encoding). Specifically, models configured with larger average patch sizes (e.g., 8 bytes per patch versus 4.5) allowed for significantly larger global transformers within the same compute envelope. As training progressed beyond the compute-optimal regime typical for standard models, these larger BLT variants pulled ahead in performance metrics.

This phenomenon occurs because larger patches reduce the number of steps required to process a sequence of text more effectively than simply adding layers to a token-based model adds value per step. While BPE models hit diminishing returns quickly under fixed inference constraints, BLT models continued to show steep scaling trends. The study noted that for an 8B parameter class model trained well beyond its compute-optimal point—a scenario increasingly common in industry—the BLT variant achieved superior performance compared to its tokenized peers running at identical speeds during generation time potential savings up to 50% in flops compared to Llama 3 while matching performance levels up to 8B parameters and 4 trillion training bytes demonstrating feasibility at scale .

# From Llama to Byte-Latent: Can We Convert Existing AI Models?

One of the biggest hurdles in adopting new artificial intelligence architectures is the sheer cost of training. If a new model design promises better efficiency or robustness but requires training from scratch on trillions of tokens, adoption slows to a crawl. This raises a compelling question: do we really need to start from zero? A recent paper introducing the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) suggests the answer is no. Beyond proving that byte-level models can match token-based giants like Llama 3, the researchers uncovered a fascinating pathway to "convert" existing pre-trained models into this new architecture through weight initialization.

The core innovation of BLT lies in removing the fixed vocabulary used by standard Large Language Models (LLMs). Instead of breaking text into static tokens, BLT groups raw bytes into dynamic "patches" based on the entropy, or unpredictability, of the data. This allows the model to spend more computing power on complex parts of a sentence and less on predictable ones. While the paper demonstrates that training BLT from scratch yields performance parity with Llama 3 up to 8 billion parameters, the most intriguing finding for the industry is the potential to leverage existing investments. The authors explored whether they could take a pre-trained Llama 3 model and adapt it to this byte-level framework without retraining the entire system from the ground up.

## The Distillation Experiment: Bootstrapping with Pre-trained Weights

To test the feasibility of converting existing models, the researchers performed a specific distillation experiment. They took a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model and used its weights to initialize the global transformer component of a new BLT model. In the BLT architecture, this global transformer is responsible for processing the high-level latent patch representations, while smaller local models handle the encoding of raw bytes into patches and decoding them back out.

The process involved freezing or heavily constraining the global transformer initially, effectively transferring the linguistic knowledge already captured by Llama 3.1 into the heart of the byte-level model. The researchers then continued training, but with a crucial adjustment: they updated the weights of this global transformer using a learning rate ten times smaller than that used for the new local encoder and decoder components. This approach allowed the model to retain its broad world knowledge while rapidly learning how to interface with raw byte data through the newly trained local modules.

## Performance Gains Without Starting Over

The results of this hybrid approach were striking. The BLT model initialized with Llama 3.1 weights significantly outperformed both a standard Llama 3 baseline and a BLT model trained entirely from scratch, assuming an equivalent budget of floating-point operations (flops). Perhaps even more impressively, this converted model achieved superior performance on complex reasoning tasks like MMLU compared to a dedicated BLT-Entropy model that had been trained on a massive dataset of one trillion tokens.

This outcome implies that we can effectively "byte-ify" powerful existing models to instantly inherit their capabilities while gaining the structural advantages of the new architecture. By skipping the early stages of foundational training, developers can potentially reach state-of-the-art performance with a fraction of the computational cost. The experiment showed that transferring weights from a token-based teacher to a byte-based student is not only viable but highly efficient, bridging the gap between established tokenization paradigms and emerging byte-level methods.

## Trade-offs and Future Potential

While the conversion method proved highly effective, it was not without minor caveats. When compared directly to the original Llama 3.1 model—which was trained on a staggering 15 trillion tokens—the converted BLT model experienced slight performance dips on specific benchmarks like MMLU and HumanEval, along with more noticeable drops in other areas. This suggests that while weight initialization provides a massive head start, fully unlocking the potential of byte-level modeling may still require fine-tuning data mixtures and hyperparameters specifically for this architecture. Nevertheless, achieving such strong results with significantly less training data highlights a promising future workflow.

The ability to convert existing models opens up exciting possibilities for AI development cycles imagine taking today's most capable closed-source or open-source models and adapting them to be more robust against noisy inputs or better at handling low-resource languages, all without re-consuming petabytes of data. The BLT paper demonstrates that we do not have to choose between leveraging past innovations and exploring new frontiers; instead, we can build directly upon them. As research continues into end-to-end learning of patching mechanisms and further optimization of these hybrid training strategies, "byte-ifying" our current fleet of AI models could become a standard practice for enhancing efficiency and robustness overnight
