# Introduction to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a seismic shift with the advent of large foundation models. Systems such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, capable of performing a vast array of tasks with unprecedented proficiency. However, this surge in capability is inextricably linked to a dramatic increase in model complexity. Modern foundation models often possess hundreds of billions of parameters; for instance, GPT-3 comprises approximately $175$ billion parameters. Training such massive architectures from scratch is prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources, energy consumption, and time. Consequently, the frontier of research has moved away from training new models from the ground up and toward the efficient adaptation of existing pretrained models to specific downstream tasks. This paradigm shift necessitates methods that can leverage the rich knowledge embedded within these foundation models without incurring the colossal costs associated with full retraining.

To address the challenge of adaptation, the community has primarily converged on three distinct strategies: model fine-tuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Model fine-tuning involves keeping the original model architecture intact while updating a subset of the existing model parameters directly. Adapter tuning takes a different approach by inserting additional trainable modules, known as adapters, into the original model structure, leaving the pretrained weights frozen. Prompt tuning, conversely, attaches trainable prefix tokens to the input sequence, guiding the frozen model to produce desired outputs without altering its internal weights. Among these approaches, model fine-tuning stands out for its conceptual simplicity and practical efficiency. Crucially, unlike adapter tuning which adds computational layers during inference, standard model fine-tuning introduces no additional inference latency, making it highly attractive for deployment scenarios where speed is paramount.

The fundamental objective underlying any successful fine-tuning strategy is the preservation of the knowledge acquired during pretraining. The core principle dictates that the finetuned model must remain sufficiently similar to the pretrained model according to specific metrics, ensuring that the broad semantic understanding is not lost while adapting to narrow tasks. Historically, many fine-tuning methods have relied on ad-hoc approaches to maintain this similarity, most commonly by employing very small learning rates. This heuristic encourages only minor deviations between the initial pretrained weights and the final finetuned weights. While effective to a degree, this approach lacks a rigorous theoretical grounding regarding the structural properties of the weight matrices themselves. It treats the reduction of discrepancy as a side effect of optimization dynamics rather than a guaranteed geometric property of the transformation.

Moving beyond ad-hoc heuristics, recent research advocates for more principled approaches that explicitly preserve the relational information inherent in the weight matrices. Specifically, this involves maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons within a layer. This insight has led to the development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a framework where neurons are transformed by an orthogonal matrix. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ represents the pretrained linear layer weights, OFT modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}_d$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean distances and angles, this method provably maintains the pairwise angles between neurons throughout the finetuning process. This geometric constraint ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pretraining are structurally preserved, offering a more robust mechanism for knowledge retention than simple learning rate adjustments.

However, even principled methods like OFT face significant hurdles regarding parameter efficiency. While OFT demonstrates excellent generalizability and training stability, particularly in complex domains like text-to-image diffusion models, it suffers from a high parameter count due to the dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix theoretically requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to define fully. To mitigate this, existing implementations of OFT often impose a block-diagonal structure on $\bm{R}$, decomposing it into smaller independent orthogonal blocks $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. While this reduces the number of trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ where $b$ is the block size, it introduces rigid inductive biases. The block-diagonal assumption implies that dimensions of a neuron can be arbitrarily grouped and transformed independently, ignoring potential interactions across these groups. Furthermore, this sparsity pattern limits expressiveness; for example, a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as the Fast Fourier Transform. Thus, a critical tension exists between achieving parameter efficiency through sparsity and maintaining sufficient expressivity to capture complex task-specific adaptations without imposing detrimental structural constraints.

The central challenge is generating a dense orthogonal matrix—one that allows information flow between all input and output dimensions—using significantly fewer parameters than the naive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement. A novel perspective frames this problem not merely as matrix algebra but as an information transmission problem on a graph. By viewing matrix multiplication as a sequence of information exchanges across layers of nodes, we can derive structural constraints that enable efficient parameterization while guaranteeing dense connectivity.

# Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT): Principles and Limitations

The rapid ascent of large foundation models, such as GPT-3 with its 175 billion parameters or Stable Diffusion for image generation, has fundamentally shifted the landscape of machine learning research. While these models exhibit remarkable generalization capabilities across diverse domains, training them from scratch has become prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources and energy consumption. Consequently, the field has pivoted toward efficient adaptation strategies that allow researchers to tailor these massive pre-trained models to specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire architecture. Among the primary paradigms for efficient adaptation—adapter tuning, prompt tuning, and model fine-tuning—the latter stands out for its simplicity and lack of inference latency. Model fine-tuning operates on the premise that the pre-trained model contains valuable knowledge that should be preserved while adapting to new data. Traditional approaches often rely on heuristic methods, such as employing small learning rates to ensure the fine-tuned weights do not deviate significantly from their pre-trained values. However, a more principled approach seeks to explicitly preserve the geometric relationships inherent in the weight matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. This insight forms the bedrock of Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT), a framework designed to maintain the semantic integrity of pre-trained representations through rigorous mathematical constraints.

At its core, OFT posits that the relational information within a neural network layer is encoded in the angles between neuron vectors. By restricting the updates to orthogonal transformations, OFT ensures that these angles remain invariant throughout the fine-tuning process. Mathematically, if a pre-trained weight matrix is denoted as $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ is the input dimension and $n$ is the output dimension, standard fine-tuning might update this directly or add a perturbation. In contrast, OFT reparameterizes the updated weight matrix as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and the frozen pre-trained matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The forward pass for an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ thus changes from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, meaning $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$, it acts as a rotation or reflection in the feature space. Such transformations preserve Euclidean distances and, crucially, the cosines of angles between any pair of vectors. This geometric preservation implies that the relative semantic positioning of neurons learned during pre-training is strictly maintained, allowing the model to adapt to new tasks while mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

Despite its theoretical elegance and demonstrated success in stabilizing training for complex models like text-to-image diffusion systems, standard OFT faces a significant practical hurdle: parameter inefficiency. A full $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to specify. For modern foundation models where $d$ can be in the thousands or tens of thousands, storing and optimizing such a matrix is nearly as expensive as fine-tuning the original weights themselves. To circumvent this, existing implementations of OFT often resort to a block-diagonal structure, decomposing the large orthogonal matrix into smaller independent blocks. While this reduces the parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ where $b$ is the block size, it introduces rigid structural assumptions that limit the expressivity of the transformation. The challenge lies in constructing a dense orthogonal matrix that allows for global information flow across all dimensions while maintaining a low parameter footprint—a problem that motivates the evolution from standard OFT to more advanced factorization techniques like Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). Understanding these foundational principles and limitations is essential for grasping why new parameterizations are necessary.

# Geometric Motivation: Preserving Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The driving force behind Orthogonal Fine-Tuning is rooted in the geometry of high-dimensional vector spaces and the interpretation of neural network weights as semantic encoders. In a pre-trained foundation model, each column of a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ represents a neuron's sensitivity to specific features in the input space. The relationship between any two neurons can be characterized by the angle between their corresponding weight vectors. Research suggests that these pairwise angles encode critical relational information about how concepts are organized within the model's latent space. For instance, if two neurons respond to semantically similar inputs, their weight vectors likely form a small angle; conversely., dissimilar concepts correspond to larger angles or near-orthogonality. When fine-tuning a model on a new task, indiscriminate updates to these weights can distort these angular relationships, effectively scrambling the semantic map constructed during pre-training. This distortion is a primary mechanism behind catastrophic forgetting, where proficiency in general knowledge degrades as the model specializes in narrow downstream tasks.

Orthogonal transformations offer a unique solution to this dilemma because they are isometries; they preserve both lengths and angles. Consider two neuron vectors $\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}_j$ from the pre-trained matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The cosine of the angle $\theta$ between them is given by $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\bm{w}_i^\top \bm{w}_j}{\|\bm{w}_i\| \|\bm{w}_j\|}$. If we apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that the new weights are $\bm{w}'_i = \bm{R}\bm{w}_i$ and $\bm{w}'_j = \BM{R}\BM{w}_j$, the dot product becomes $(\BM{R}\BM{w}_i)^\top (\BM{R}\BM{w}_j) = \BM{w}_i^\top \BM{R}^\top \BM{R} \BM{w}_j$. Since $\BM{R}$ is orthogonal ($\BM{R}^\top \BM=R = I$), this simplifies back to $\BM=w_i^\top BM=w_j$. Similarly, norms are preserved because $\|\BM=R BM=w_i\|^2 = BM=w_i^\top BM=R^\top BM=R BM=w_i = \|BM=w_i\|^2$. Consequently, the cosine similarity—and thus the angle—remains exactly unchanged regardless of how much we optimize $\BM=R$. # A New Perspective: The Information Transmission Framework

The challenge of adapting large foundation models to specific downstream tasks often hinges on a delicate balance between preserving the rich knowledge encoded during pretraining and introducing sufficient flexibility to learn new patterns. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) addresses the preservation aspect by transforming weight matrices using orthogonal operations, thereby maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons. However, the traditional implementation of OFT faces a significant hurdle: constructing a dense orthogonal matrix that allows for global interaction among all neuron dimensions typically requires a prohibitive number of trainable parameters, scaling quadratically with the dimension size. Conversely, simplifying the structure to save parameters, such as using block-diagonal matrices, restricts the flow of information between different parts of the network, potentially limiting the model's expressiveness. To resolve this tension, we must fundamentally rethink how we construct these orthogonal transformations. This chapter introduces a novel theoretical lens—the information transmission framework—which reframes the algebraic problem of matrix factorization into a graphical problem of transmitting information across a network. By viewing the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as a sequence of information exchanges on a grid-structured graph, we can systematically design sparse matrix products that achieve full connectivity with remarkable parameter efficiency.

## Conceptual Shift: Matrix Multiplication as Sequential Information Flow

To understand the innovation behind the information transmission framework, one must first abandon the static view of a matrix as merely a grid of numbers and instead perceive it as a dynamic operator governing the flow of data. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning, the goal is to generate a target dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Traditionally, this matrix is treated as a single monolithic entity where every entry $R_{ij}$ represents a direct weighting factor between an input dimension $j$ and an output dimension $i$. If $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, it implies that information from the $j$-th component of the input vector contributes directly to the $i$-th component of the output vector. If $R_{ij}$ is zero, no such direct contribution exists. When $\bm{R}$ is dense, every input node influences every output node, representing a state of maximal connectivity. However, storing and optimizing such a matrix directly incurs a cost of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is unsustainable for modern high-dimensional models.

The conceptual shift proposed in this work involves decomposing this single dense matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of $m$ simpler, sparse matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. Algebraically, this is simply matrix factorization. Yet, intuitively, this decomposition transforms the operation from a single-step mapping into a multi-stage process of sequential information exchange. Instead of jumping directly from inputs to outputs in one giant leap, information traverses through intermediate stages defined by each factor $\bm{B}_i$. In this view, generating a dense matrix is equivalent to designing a communication protocol where information originating at any source node must eventually reach every destination node after passing through a series of relay stations. Each sparse matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as one such station, performing a limited set of local exchanges before passing the aggregated information to the next stage. This perspective is crucial because it decouples the requirement for global connectivity from the requirement for dense parameterization at any single step. We can achieve global reach not by connecting everyone to everyone immediately, but by orchestrating a chain of efficient local interactions that collectively propagate information everywhere.

This reframing allows us to leverage insights from fields outside of deep learning, particularly network theory and signal processing. Just as complex signals can be synthesized from simple wavelets or messages can be routed across vast distances via local hops in a computer network, a complex orthogonal transformation can be constructed from a sequence of sparse orthogonal steps. The key realization is that the "density" of the final matrix $\bm{R}$ is an emergent property of the cumulative effect of these sparse factors. Even if each individual $\bm{B}_i$ contains mostly zeros (implying very few trainable parameters), their product can fill in those zeros through indirect paths. For instance, if node $A$ connects to node $B$ in step 1, and node $B$ connects to node $C$ in step 2, then effectively node $A$ has transmitted information to node $C$, even though there was never a direct link between them in any single factor. This mechanism enables us to trade computation time (performing multiple matrix-vector multiplications) for space (drastically reducing the number of stored parameters), providing a viable pathway to parameter-efficient finetuning without sacrificing the expressive power of dense transformations.

## Graph Representation: Grid-Structured Nodes and Directed Edges

To formalize this intuition and make it actionable for designing new finetuning architectures, we map the matrix factorization problem onto a specific graph topology known as a grid-structured graph. Consider the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, where each $\bm{B}_i$ is a $d \times d$ square matrix. We visualize this process as information flowing through a grid consisting of $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns of nodes. The columns represent discrete time steps or layers in the transmission process, indexed from $0$ to $m$. The nodes in column $0$ represent the initial input dimensions (sources), while the nodes in column $m$ represent the final output dimensions (receivers). The intermediate columns $1$ through $m-1$ serve as hidden states or relay points where information is mixed and forwarded.

In this graphical representation, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ defines the connectivity pattern between column $i-1$ and column $i$. Specifically, we treat $\bm{B}_i$ as an adjacency matrix for a bipartite graph connecting the nodes at level $i-1$ to the nodes at level $i$. The element $(\bm{B}_i)_{jk}$ corresponds to a directed edge from the $k$-th node at level $i-1$ to the $j$-th node at level $i$. If $(\BM_i)_{jk}$ is non-zero, an edge exists, permitting information flow; if it is zero, no edge exists, blocking direct transmission between those specific nodes at that specific step. Consequently, sparsity in the matrix $\BM_i$ translates directly to sparsity in the graph edges between adjacent levels. A highly sparse matrix implies that each node at level $i-1\) connects to only a few neighbors at level \(i\), drastically reducing the number of edges—and thus trainable parameters—required for that step.

The power of this representation lies in its ability to visualize how indirect connectivity emerges. A path exists from a source node \(u\) in column \(0\) to a destination node \(v\) in column \(m\) if and only if there is a sequence of directed edges linking them through intermediate levels. The Butterfly Structure and Efficient Parameterization

The quest for parameter-efficient finetuning of large foundation models often hinges on a fundamental tension: the desire to maintain the rich, expressive capacity of dense matrix transformations while strictly limiting the number of trainable parameters. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), this challenge is particularly acute. While transforming weight matrices with orthogonal operators preserves crucial geometric properties like pairwise angles between neurons—thereby safeguarding pretrained knowledge—a standard dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This quadratic scaling renders direct application prohibitive for modern models with massive hidden dimensions. Previous attempts to mitigate this cost relied on block-diagonal structures, which drastically reduce parameters but impose rigid sparsity patterns that limit expressiveness and introduce undesirable inductive biases. To resolve this dilemma, we turn to a principled approach rooted in signal processing and information theory. By decomposing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse, structured matrices, we can achieve the best of both worlds: the global connectivity of a dense transform with the parameter efficiency of a sparse one. This chapter details the theoretical underpinnings and mathematical construction of this approach, centering on the butterfly structure derived from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm.

# Inspiration from Signal Processing: The Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform

The conceptual breakthrough for efficient orthogonal parameterization originates not from deep learning itself, but from the decades-old field of digital signal processing, specifically the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT revolutionized computational mathematics by demonstrating that a seemingly complex, dense linear transformation—the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)—could be computed with remarkable efficiency through recursive decomposition. At its core, the Cooley-Tukey algorithm exploits the symmetry and periodicity of sinusoidal basis functions to break down an $N$-point DFT into smaller sub-problems. It recursively reduces an $N$-point transform into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms, continuing this division until reaching trivial base cases.

This recursive strategy induces a specific computational topology known as the "butterfly graph." In the context of the FFT, this graph illustrates how data points at the input stage interact and exchange information across multiple stages to produce the final frequency domain representation. Crucially, a local change in the frequency domain corresponds to a global change in the spatial domain, implying that every input node must eventually influence every output node. This property mirrors the requirement in our finetuning framework: to generate a dense orthogonal matrix capable of arbitrary rotations, information must be able to flow from any input dimension to any output dimension. The butterfly structure provides a proven mechanism for achieving this dense connectivity efficiently. Just as the FFT allows for rapid information mixing with far fewer operations than a naive matrix-vector multiplication, the butterfly topology offers a blueprint for constructing dense orthogonal matrices using a sequence of sparse operations. By adopting this structure, we move away from ad-hoc sparsity patterns toward a design grounded in optimal information transmission principles.

# Anatomy of a Butterfly Graph: Efficient Node Exchange

To understand how the butterfly structure facilitates parameter efficiency, it is essential to visualize the generation of a dense matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. Consider the task of generating a dense $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying $m$ sparse square matrices, denoted as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. This factorization can be mapped onto a graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes arranged in levels. Each level represents the state of the data vector at a specific stage of the transformation. The matrices $\bm{B}_i$ act as connectivity maps between adjacent levels; specifically, a non-zero entry at position $(j_1, j_2)$ in matrix $\bm{B}_i$ signifies a directed edge allowing information to flow from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$. Conversely, a zero entry implies no direct connection.

For the resulting composite matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, the underlying graph must satisfy a strict connectivity criterion: every node in the initial level (level 0) must have at least one valid path to every node in the final level (level $m$). If even one pair of nodes lacks such a path, the corresponding entry in $\bm{R}$ will remain zero, resulting in a sparse rather than dense operator. A naive approach would simply connect every node at level $i$ to every node at level $i+1$, creating a fully connected bipartite graph between adjacent layers. However, this requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges per layer, offering no parameter savings over using a single dense matrix.

The elegance of the butterfly graph lies in its ability to satisfy the global connectivity requirement using a minimal number of edges. Instead of connecting everyone to everyone at each step, the butterfly topology orchestrates a series of localized exchanges that propagate information globally over multiple steps. In this structure, nodes are paired and swapped or mixed in specific patterns that shift across layers. Through $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers of these sparse interactions, information originating from any single source node diffuses to reach all destination nodes. This stands in stark contrast to block-diagonal structures used in earlier OFT variants, where information is trapped within isolated blocks and never crosses group boundaries. The butterfly graph ensures that while each individual step involves only local interactions (sparse matrices), cumulative effect across steps yields full global interaction (a dense matrix). This topological insight allows us to trade computation time (performing multiple matrix multiplications during training) for space (drastically reducing the number of stored parameters).

# Mathematical Definition: Butterfly Factors and Recursive Construction

Formalizing the intuition provided by the butterfly graph requires precise mathematical definitions involving butterfly factors and recursive matrix constructions. We begin by defining the elementary building block, known as the *butterfly factor*. Let $k \geq 2$ be an integer that is typically a power of 2. A butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is a $k \times k$ matrix defined as:

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$

where $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3,$ and $\bm{d}_4$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. The term "diag" denotes a diagonal matrix formed from these vectors. This structure essentially partitions the input into two halves and mixes them using element-wise scaling operations represented by diagonals. It captures local interactions between pairs or small groups of elements efficiently.

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Methodology and Implementation

The transition from theoretical frameworks to practical algorithms requires a precise synthesis of structural constraints, parameterization strategies, and regularization techniques. In the context of adapting large foundation models, the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method represents a culmination of the information transmission perspective on orthogonal finetuning. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on additive low-rank updates or static block-diagonal orthogonal transformations, BOFT constructs a dynamic, dense orthogonal matrix through the multiplication of sparse, structured components. This chapter details the architectural mechanics of BOFT, elucidating how butterfly factorization enables the construction of dense connectivity with logarithmic parameter complexity. We will explore the critical hyperparameters governing the depth and granularity of these transformations, demonstrate how BOFT generalizes previous orthogonal finetuning methods, and examine the specialized initialization and regularization protocols required to maintain stability in a multiplicative update regime.

# The BOFT Architecture: Product of Sparse Orthogonal Matrices

At the core of the BOFT methodology lies a fundamental shift in how orthogonal matrices are represented for finetuning. Traditional Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) often struggles with the trade-off between expressivity and parameter efficiency; a full dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is prohibitive for large layers, while block-diagonal approximations restrict information flow between disjoint sets of neurons. BOFT resolves this dichotomy by decomposing the target orthogonal matrix into a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, inspired by the butterfly structures found in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm. This factorization allows the model to generate a dense connectivity pattern—where every input dimension can influence every output dimension—while maintaining a parameter count that scales only as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$.

The architecture is built upon the concept of the "butterfly factor." For a dimension $k$ that is a power of 2, a butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as a block matrix composed of four diagonal sub-matrices:
$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$
where $\bm{d}_i$ are learnable vectors. By arranging these factors recursively, we construct a "butterfly component" $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, which acts as a block-diagonal matrix where each block is itself a butterfly factor. The full orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}(m,b)$ used in BOFT is formed by multiplying $m$ such components together. Specifically, if we denote the $i$-th butterfly component as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, the forward pass for a linear layer with pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ and input $\bm{x}$ becomes:
$$
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}, \quad \text{where} \quad \bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}.
$$
Crucially, to ensure that the resulting product remains orthogonal, every individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ must be constrained to be an orthogonal matrix. This is achieved by parameterizing the internal blocks using skew-symmetric matrices via the Cayley transform or direct rotation parameterizations for $2 \times 2$ blocks. The sparsity pattern of these components follows a specific permutation logic derived from the butterfly graph topology, ensuring that information propagates efficiently across all dimensions as it passes through successive layers of the factorization.

From an information transmission perspective, this architecture can be visualized as a grid-structured graph where nodes represent dimensions and edges represent non-zero entries in the sparse matrices. A single dense matrix would require every node in the input layer to connect directly to every node in the output layer, necessitating $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. In contrast, the BOFT architecture unrolls the matrix multiplication into a sequence of stages. In each stage, represented by a sparse butterfly component, information is exchanged locally between pairs or small groups of nodes. Through $m$ such stages, information originating from any single node diffuses across the entire graph, eventually reaching all other nodes. This sequential exchange mimics the efficiency of fast linear transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform, allowing BOFT to achieve global connectivity (a dense matrix) using only local interactions (sparse matrices), thereby drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters without sacrificing the capacity to model complex relationships between neurons.
