Chapter 1: # Introduction to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a seismic shift with the advent of large foundation models. Systems such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, capable of performing a vast array of tasks with unprecedented proficiency. However, this surge in capability is inextricably linked to a dramatic increase in model complexity. Modern foundation models often possess hundreds of billions of parameters; for instance, GPT-3 comprises approximately $175$ billion parameters. Training such massive architectures from scratch is prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources, energy consumption, and time. Consequently, the frontier of research has moved away from training new models from the ground up and toward the efficient adaptation of existing pretrained models to specific downstream tasks. This paradigm shift necessitates methods that can leverage the rich knowledge embedded within these foundation models without incurring the colossal costs associated with full retraining.

To address the challenge of adaptation, the community has primarily converged on three distinct strategies: model fine-tuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Model fine-tuning involves keeping the original model architecture intact while updating a subset of the existing model parameters directly. Adapter tuning takes a different approach by inserting additional trainable modules, known as adapters, into the original model structure, leaving the pretrained weights frozen. Prompt tuning, conversely, attaches trainable prefix tokens to the input sequence, guiding the frozen model to produce desired outputs without altering its internal weights. Among these approaches, model fine-tuning stands out for its conceptual simplicity and practical efficiency. Crucially, unlike adapter tuning which adds computational layers during inference, standard model fine-tuning introduces no additional inference latency, making it highly attractive for deployment scenarios where speed is paramount.

The fundamental objective underlying any successful fine-tuning strategy is the preservation of the knowledge acquired during pretraining. The core principle dictates that the finetuned model must remain sufficiently similar to the pretrained model according to specific metrics, ensuring that the broad semantic understanding is not lost while adapting to narrow tasks. Historically, many fine-tuning methods have relied on ad-hoc approaches to maintain this similarity, most commonly by employing very small learning rates. This heuristic encourages only minor deviations between the initial pretrained weights and the final finetuned weights. While effective to a degree, this approach lacks a rigorous theoretical grounding regarding the structural properties of the weight matrices themselves. It treats the reduction of discrepancy as a side effect of optimization dynamics rather than a guaranteed geometric property of the transformation.

Moving beyond ad-hoc heuristics, recent research advocates for more principled approaches that explicitly preserve the relational information inherent in the weight matrices. Specifically, this involves maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons within a layer. This insight has led to the development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a framework where neurons are transformed by an orthogonal matrix. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ represents the pretrained linear layer weights, OFT modifies the forward pass from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}_d$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean distances and angles, this method provably maintains the pairwise angles between neurons throughout the finetuning process. This geometric constraint ensures that the semantic relationships learned during pretraining are structurally preserved, offering a more robust mechanism for knowledge retention than simple learning rate adjustments.

However, even principled methods like OFT face significant hurdles regarding parameter efficiency. While OFT demonstrates excellent generalizability and training stability, particularly in complex domains like text-to-image diffusion models, it suffers from a high parameter count due to the dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix theoretically requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to define fully. To mitigate this, existing implementations of OFT often impose a block-diagonal structure on $\bm{R}$, decomposing it into smaller independent orthogonal blocks $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. While this reduces the number of trainable parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ where $b$ is the block size, it introduces rigid inductive biases. The block-diagonal assumption implies that dimensions of a neuron can be arbitrarily grouped and transformed independently, ignoring potential interactions across these groups. Furthermore, this sparsity pattern limits expressiveness; for example, a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms such as the Fast Fourier Transform. Thus, a critical tension exists between achieving parameter efficiency through sparsity and maintaining sufficient expressivity to capture complex task-specific adaptations without imposing detrimental structural constraints.

# An Information Transmission Framework for Orthogonal Matrices

To resolve the trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity in orthogonal finetuning, we must rethink how dense orthogonal matrices are constructed. The central challenge is generating a dense orthogonal matrix—one that allows information flow between all input and output dimensions—using significantly fewer parameters than the naive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement. A novel perspective frames this problem not merely as matrix algebra but as an information transmission problem on a graph. By viewing matrix multiplication as a sequence of information exchanges across layers of nodes, we can derive structural constraints that enable efficient parameterization while guaranteeing dense connectivity.

Consider the generation of a target dense matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ through the product of $m$ square matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. This factorization can be visualized as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph containing $d \times (m+1)$ nodes arranged in $m+1$ levels (columns), where each level contains $d$ nodes corresponding to vector dimensions. In this graphical interpretation, each intermediate matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as a connectivity map between level $i$ and level $i+1$. Specifically，the element $(\bm{B}_i)_{j_1 j_2}$ determines whether information flows from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$.
