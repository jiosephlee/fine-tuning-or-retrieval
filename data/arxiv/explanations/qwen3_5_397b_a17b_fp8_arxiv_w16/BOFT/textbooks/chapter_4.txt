\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 4: The Butterfly Structure and Efficient Parameterization

The quest for parameter-efficient finetuning of large foundation models often hinges on a fundamental tension: the desire to maintain the rich, expressive capacity of dense matrix transformations while strictly limiting the number of trainable parameters. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), this challenge is particularly acute. While transforming weight matrices with orthogonal operators preserves crucial geometric properties like pairwise angles between neurons—thereby safeguarding pretrained knowledge—a standard dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This quadratic scaling renders direct application prohibitive for modern models with massive hidden dimensions. Previous attempts to mitigate this cost relied on block-diagonal structures, which drastically reduce parameters but impose rigid sparsity patterns that limit expressiveness and introduce undesirable inductive biases. To resolve this dilemma, we turn to a principled approach rooted in signal processing and information theory. By decomposing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse, structured matrices, we can achieve the best of both worlds: the global connectivity of a dense transform with the parameter efficiency of a sparse one. This chapter details the theoretical underpinnings and mathematical construction of this approach, centering on the butterfly structure derived from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm.

# Inspiration from Signal Processing: The Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform

The conceptual breakthrough for efficient orthogonal parameterization originates not from deep learning itself, but from the decades-old field of digital signal processing, specifically the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT revolutionized computational mathematics by demonstrating that a seemingly complex, dense linear transformation—the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)—could be computed with remarkable efficiency through recursive decomposition. At its core, the Cooley-Tukey algorithm exploits the symmetry and periodicity of sinusoidal basis functions to break down an $N$-point DFT into smaller sub-problems. It recursively reduces an $N$-point transform into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms, continuing this division until reaching trivial base cases.

This recursive strategy induces a specific computational topology known as the "butterfly graph." In the context of the FFT, this graph illustrates how data points at the input stage interact and exchange information across multiple stages to produce the final frequency domain representation. Crucially, a local change in the frequency domain corresponds to a global change in the spatial domain, implying that every input node must eventually influence every output node. This property mirrors the requirement in our finetuning framework: to generate a dense orthogonal matrix capable of arbitrary rotations, information must be able to flow from any input dimension to any output dimension. The butterfly structure provides a proven mechanism for achieving this dense connectivity efficiently. Just as the FFT allows for rapid information mixing with far fewer operations than a naive matrix-vector multiplication, the butterfly topology offers a blueprint for constructing dense orthogonal matrices using a sequence of sparse operations. By adopting this structure, we move away from ad-hoc sparsity patterns toward a design grounded in optimal information transmission principles.

# Anatomy of a Butterfly Graph: Efficient Node Exchange

To understand how the butterfly structure facilitates parameter efficiency, it is essential to visualize the generation of a dense matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. Consider the task of generating a dense $d \times d$ matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying $m$ sparse square matrices, denoted as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. This factorization can be mapped onto a graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes arranged in levels. Each level represents the state of the data vector at a specific stage of the transformation. The matrices $\bm{B}_i$ act as connectivity maps between adjacent levels; specifically, a non-zero entry at position $(j_1, j_2)$ in matrix $\bm{B}_i$ signifies a directed edge allowing information to flow from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$. Conversely, a zero entry implies no direct connection.

For the resulting composite matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, the underlying graph must satisfy a strict connectivity criterion: every node in the initial level (level 0) must have at least one valid path to every node in the final level (level $m$). If even one pair of nodes lacks such a path, the corresponding entry in $\bm{R}$ will remain zero, resulting in a sparse rather than dense operator. A naive approach would simply connect every node at level $i$ to every node at level $i+1$, creating a fully connected bipartite graph between adjacent layers. However, this requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges per layer, offering no parameter savings over using a single dense matrix.

The elegance of the butterfly graph lies in its ability to satisfy the global connectivity requirement using a minimal number of edges. Instead of connecting everyone to everyone at each step, the butterfly topology orchestrates a series of localized exchanges that propagate information globally over multiple steps. In this structure, nodes are paired and swapped or mixed in specific patterns that shift across layers. Through $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers of these sparse interactions, information originating from any single source node diffuses to reach all destination nodes. This stands in stark contrast to block-diagonal structures used in earlier OFT variants, where information is trapped within isolated blocks and never crosses group boundaries. The butterfly graph ensures that while each individual step involves only local interactions (sparse matrices), cumulative effect across steps yields full global interaction (a dense matrix). This topological insight allows us to trade computation time (performing multiple matrix multiplications during training) for space (drastically reducing the number of stored parameters).

# Mathematical Definition: Butterfly Factors and Recursive Construction

Formalizing the intuition provided by the butterfly graph requires precise mathematical definitions involving butterfly factors and recursive matrix constructions. We begin by defining the elementary building block, known as the *butterfly factor*. Let $k \geq 2$ be an integer that is typically a power of 2. A butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is a $k \times k$ matrix defined as:

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$

where $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3,$ and $\bm{d}_4$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. The term "diag" denotes a diagonal matrix formed from these vectors. This structure essentially partitions the input into two halves and mixes them using element-wise scaling operations represented by diagonals. It captures local interactions between pairs or small groups of elements efficiently.
