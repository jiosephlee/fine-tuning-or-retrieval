\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why are exactly $d$ edges considered 'not trainable' in the orthogonality constraint?
Question:
The paper states in Section 3 that for a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix factor, we need at least $d$ edges to form a bijection, and claims these '$d$ edges... should not be counted into the number of edges, because these elements are not trainable (e.g., ... can only be $\pm 1$).' This confuses me. If I use the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ mentioned earlier, aren't the values continuous and dependent on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$? Why does the paper suggest that satisfying the bijection requirement forces entries to be fixed constants like $\pm 1$, rendering them non-trainable? Does this only apply to permutation matrices, or am I misunderstanding the degree of freedom in sparse orthogonal factors?
Answer:
The confusion stems from distinguishing between the *structural necessity* of having $d$ connections to maintain orthogonality and the *parametric freedom* to choose the values of those connections. In the information transmission framework presented in Section 3, the authors analyze the sparsity pattern of the factorized matrices required to form a valid orthogonal matrix. For any $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix to be full-rank (a strict requirement for orthogonality), there must be a bijection between the input nodes at level $i$ and the output nodes at level $i+1$. Graph theoretically, this necessitates at least $d$ edges; if there were fewer than $d$ edges, at least one output node would receive no information, resulting in a zero row and a singular (non-orthogonal) matrix.

The paper argues that these specific $d$ edges are "not trainable" in the context of counting *effective* parameters because, in the minimal sparse case where exactly $d$ edges exist, the orthogonality constraint $\bm{B}^\top \bm{B} = \bm{I}$ severely restricts their values. If a sparse orthogonal matrix has exactly one non-zero entry per row and column (which is the definition of a generalized permutation matrix), those non-zero entries cannot take arbitrary continuous values derived from a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ via Cayley parameterization. Instead, to satisfy the unit norm constraint of the rows and columns, each of these $d$ non-zero entries must strictly be either $+1$ or $-1$. Since these values are discrete constants determined entirely by the choice of permutation and sign rather than continuous optimization variables, they contribute zero degrees of freedom to the learning process.

Your intuition regarding the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ applies correctly to cases where there are *more* than $d$ edges (i.e., when the matrix has sufficient density to allow for continuous rotations, such as Givens rotations). In those scenarios, the extra edges beyond the mandatory $d$ provide the necessary degrees of freedom for $\bm{Q}$ to vary continuously, making them trainable. However, the paper's argument isolates the baseline cost of connectivity: the first $d$ edges are merely the "skeleton" required to keep the matrix invertible and orthogonal, acting as fixed wiring (permutations with signs) rather than learnable weights. Therefore, when calculating parameter efficiency, the authors subtract these $d$ structurally forced edges from the total count, considering only the surplus edges that allow for actual continuous variation as "trainable parameters." This distinction allows them to fairly compare different sparse topologies based on how many *extra* connections they need to achieve expressivity beyond simple signed permutations.
