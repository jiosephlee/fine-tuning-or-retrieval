\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Intuition behind Multiplicative Dropout: Why replace blocks with Identity instead of Zero?
Question:
In Section 4, the authors introduce 'Multiplicative Dropout' for BOFT. They state that conventional dropout doesn't work for multiplicative updates and propose randomly replacing selected butterfly blocks with Identity matrices. Why is replacing with Identity ($\bm{I}$) the correct analog to dropping units (setting to 0) in an additive framework like LoRA? Since the operation is multiplication ($\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$), setting a part of $\bm{R}$ to zero would destroy information entirely, whereas setting it to $\bm{I}$ preserves the input signal unchanged. Is the goal here to simulate 'skipping' a transformation layer rather than 'dropping' a feature activation?
Answer:
The intuition behind replacing blocks with Identity matrices instead of zeros lies in the fundamental difference between additive and multiplicative update rules, specifically regarding how "no change" is represented in each algebraic structure. In an additive framework like LoRA, the updated weight is $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$. Here, the neutral element that preserves the original pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$ is the zero matrix; setting $\Delta\bm{W} = \bm{0}$ ensures the output remains exactly $\bm{W}^0\bm{x}$. Consequently, standard dropout sets activations to zero to simulate removing a feature's contribution, relying on the remaining network paths to compensate.

However, BOFT operates via a multiplicative update rule defined as $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix constructed from a product of butterfly components. In this multiplicative group, the neutral element that leaves the input signal unchanged is not the zero matrix, but the Identity matrix $\bm{I}$. If you were to apply conventional dropout by setting a block of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to zero, you would effectively destroy the information flowing through those dimensions because multiplying by zero collapses the signal to null, violating the orthogonality constraint essential for preserving the pairwise angles of neurons (the core benefit of OFT). Furthermore, a matrix with zero blocks is no longer orthogonal, breaking the theoretical guarantees of the method.

Therefore, "dropping" a unit in this context must be redefined as "skipping" the learned transformation for that specific block. By randomly replacing selected butterfly blocks with $\bm{I}$, the forward pass for those blocks becomes equivalent to applying no transformation at all ($\bm{I}\bm{x} = \bm{x}$), allowing the signal to pass through untouched while maintaining the overall orthogonality of the composite matrix $\bm{R}$. This acts as a regularization technique similar to DropConnect or stochastic depth, where entire transformation layers are bypassed during training to prevent co-adaptation of features, but it does so in a way that respects the geometric constraints of the orthogonal manifold. Essentially, while additive dropout simulates removing a feature activation, multiplicative dropout simulates removing a *transformation step*, forcing the model to rely on diverse pathways through the butterfly factorization graph rather than overfitting to specific rotational adjustments in every block.
