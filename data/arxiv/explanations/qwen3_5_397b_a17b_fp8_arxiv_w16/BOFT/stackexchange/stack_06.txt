\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does BOFT compare theoretically to Kaleidoscope hierarchies for expressing arbitrary orthogonal matrices?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and mentions that a 'Kaleidoscope hierarchy' ($\bm{B}_{d-1,1}\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}\cdots$) can represent the *entire* orthogonal group. The proposed BOFT method seems to use a simpler product structure ($\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$). Does standard BOFT with finite $m < d-1$ cover a significantly smaller subset of the orthogonal group compared to Kaleidoscope? What is the practical implication of this difference: does restricting ourselves to simple products (without alternating transposes/inverses) impose an inductive bias that helps generalization, or is it purely a computational simplification?
Answer:
The distinction you raise touches on the core trade-off in BOFT between theoretical universality and practical inductive bias. While it is true that a "Kaleidoscope hierarchy"—constructed by alternating butterfly matrices and their transposes (e.g., $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}\cdots$)—can theoretically represent the *entire* orthogonal group when the depth reaches $d-1$, standard BOFT with a finite depth $m < d-1$ (typically $m = \log d$) covers a strictly smaller, structured subset of the orthogonal group. However, the paper argues that this limitation is not a deficit but rather the source of BOFT's superior generalization capabilities compared to both full orthogonal finetuning and block-diagonal OFT.

Theoretically, standard BOFT constructs the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a simple product of $m$ sparse orthogonal butterfly components, denoted as $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$. As noted in Theorem 1 and the subsequent discussion, achieving universal expressivity (covering the entire orthogonal group) would require extending this structure into a Kaleidoscope hierarchy with alternating transposes and significantly greater depth ($l=d-1$). In contrast, standard BOFT restricts itself to a shallow product without these alternating inverses. Consequently, for any finite $m$ used in practice, BOFT cannot approximate an arbitrary orthogonal matrix with zero error; it is confined to a manifold of matrices that share the specific connectivity patterns of the butterfly graph.

Practically, however, this restriction imposes a powerful **inductive bias** that aids generalization. The paper explicitly contrasts the goal of "universal expressivity" with the needs of parameter-efficient finetuning. Full finetuning possesses universal expressivity yet often suffers from overfitting and instability because the hypothesis space is too vast relative to the limited downstream data. BOFT leverages the butterfly structure—which underpins classic fast linear transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), and Hadamard transform—to create a "smoother interpolation" between the identity matrix (representing no change to the pretrained model) and complex dense transformations. By restricting the search space to this structured subset, BOFT prevents the model from learning arbitrary, potentially noisy rotations that do not align with natural signal processing priors. The authors argue that because many fundamental linear operations in nature and engineering adhere to butterfly-like sparsity patterns, constraining the orthogonal update to this subspace acts as a regularizer. This allows BOFT to achieve high expressivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—far fewer than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required for a full dense matrix or even deeper Kaleidoscope structures—while maintaining better stability than low-rank methods like LoRA or block-diagonal OFT, which lack this global connectivity.

Therefore, the difference is not merely a computational simplification to save parameters; it is a deliberate design choice to balance expressivity and regularity. While Kaleidoscope hierarchies offer a complete basis for the orthogonal group, they would likely reintroduce the overfitting risks associated with high-dimensional optimization. Standard BOFT sacrifices the ability to represent *every* possible orthogonal matrix in favor of representing *useful* ones efficiently, thereby finding a "smaller hypothesis class within the orthogonal group" that empirically yields better performance on downstream tasks across vision and language models.
