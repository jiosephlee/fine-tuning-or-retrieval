\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Clarification on Reference Policy Initialization when SFT Model is Unavailable
Question:
The 'DPO outline' subsection mentions that ideally $\pi_\text{ref}$ is initialized as the Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model ($\pi^\text{SFT}$). However, it also says: 'when $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not available, we initialize $\pi_\\text{ref}$ by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions'. 

In the Single-Turn Dialogue experiment (Section 5.3), they mention no pre-trained SFT model was available, so they trained a reference model on preferred completions. My confusion is: if we train $\\pi_\\text{ref}$ only on the *chosen* responses ($y_w$), isn't this effectively doing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)? If so, why distinguish it from an SFT model? Is there a subtle difference in how this reference model is used during DPO training versus how a standard SFT model serves as a starting point for PPO? Does initializing $\\pi_\\text{ref}$ this way introduce bias since it has never seen the rejected responses $y_l$?
Answer:
The distinction you are noticing stems from the specific role the model plays within the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective versus the standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline, specifically regarding how the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ constrains the optimization.

When the paper states that $\pi_\text{ref}$ is initialized by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions ($y_w$) when a dedicated Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model is unavailable, it is indeed performing a form of supervised fine-tuning. However, calling this model $\pi^\text{SFT}$ in the traditional sense can be misleading because of how it is utilized. In standard RLHF (like PPO), the SFT model serves two purposes: it is the initialization for the trainable policy $\pi_\theta$, and it acts as the static reference $\pi_\text{ref}$ in the KL-divergence penalty term to prevent the policy from drifting too far from a "safe" or "capable" distribution. Ideally, this reference model represents a broad distribution of high-quality responses.

In the Single-Turn Dialogue experiment described in Section 5.3, no such broad SFT model existed. If the authors had used a raw pre-trained model as $\pi_\text{ref}$, the reference distribution would likely assign extremely low probability to the specific format and style of the helpful dialogue responses found in the preference dataset. This would create a massive distributional shift. When DPO calculates the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$, a mismatched $\pi_\text{ref}$ would result in artificially inflated reward signals simply because the policy learns to speak coherently, rather than because it is learning human preferences. By training $\pi_\text{ref}$ on the chosen completions $y_w$ first, the authors ensure that the reference policy already understands the domain, tone, and formatting of the dialogue task.

The subtle but critical difference lies in what this "reference-only" SFT sees compared to a standard SFT used for initialization in other contexts. A standard SFT aims to learn a general mapping from prompts to good answers. Here, the goal is strictly to approximate the marginal distribution of the *preferred* data so that $\pi_\text{ref}$ acts as a proper baseline for the preference ratio. You asked if this introduces bias because $\pi_\text{ref}$ never sees rejected responses $y_l$. In the context of DPO, this is actually desirable and necessary. The learning signal regarding what is *bad* ($y_l$) comes entirely from the DPO loss function itself, which explicitly contrasts $y_w$ against $y_l$ via Equation 6:
$$ \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right] $$
If $\pi_\text{ref}$ were trained on rejected responses or a mix of data, it would distort this ratio. The reference model needs to represent a neutral "starting point" of competence within that specific domain so that the optimizer can correctly attribute higher implicit reward to $y_w$ relative to $y_l$. If $\pi_\text{ref}$ already knew $y_l$ was bad (by having been trained to avoid it), or if it was too distant from both $y_w$ and $y_l$,the gradient updates derived in Section 3 ("What does the DPO update do?") would not function as intended to separate the probabilities of winning and losing completions effectively.

Therefore, while procedurally similar to SFT, initializing $\pi_\text{ref}$ on $y_w$ is a pragmatic step to stabilize training by mitigating distribution shift, ensuring that the KL constraint operates within a relevant manifold of text generation. It allows DPO to focus purely on learning the *relative* ordering of responses (the preference) rather than wasting capacity learning basic instruction following or formatting from scratch during preference optimization.
