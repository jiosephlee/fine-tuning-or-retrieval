\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the DPO derivation?
Question:
I am reading the 'Direct Preference Optimization' paper and trying to follow the derivation in Section 3. The authors define the optimal policy as $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ and then rearrange it to solve for the reward: $r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. 

When they substitute this into the Bradley-Terry model probability $p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, they claim the partition function cancels out. Since $Z(x)$ depends on the prompt $x$, doesn't it remain in the equation? Could someone walk through the algebraic steps showing exactly how $\beta \log Z(x)$ disappears when taking the difference between two rewards for the same prompt?
Answer:
The partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out precisely because the Bradley-Terry model relies on the **difference** between two reward scores for the same prompt $x$, and $Z(x)$ acts as an additive constant that is identical for both responses.

To see this algebraically, let's look at the expression for the reward derived in Equation 2 of the paper:
$$r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When calculating the probability that response $y_1$ is preferred over $y_2$ given prompt $x$, the Bradley-Terry model (Equation 1) uses the sigmoid of the difference between their rewards:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$$

If we substitute the expanded reward formula into this difference term, we get:
$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right] - \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right]$$

Notice that the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears in both brackets with a positive sign. Since the partition function $Z(x)$ depends **only** on the prompt $x$ and sums over all possible outputs $y$, it remains constant regardless of which specific output ($y_1$ or $y_2$) you are evaluating for that fixed prompt. Consequently, when you subtract the reward of $y_2$ from the reward of $y_1$, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms cancel each other out perfectly:
$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}$$

This cancellation is the core insight that allows Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to bypass the need to calculate or estimate the intractable partition function. Theoretically, this works because reward functions in preference learning are only identifiable up to an additive function of the state (the prompt). As defined in Section 4.1 of the paper, two reward functions are equivalent if they differ only by a function $f(x)$. The term $\beta \log Z(x)$ is exactly such a function; it shifts the absolute scale of rewards for a given prompt but does not alter the relative ordering or the gap between any pair of responses. By formulating the loss directly on the policy ratio, DPO implicitly selects a specific representative from this equivalence class where the normalization is handled automatically, rendering the explicit calculation of $Z(x)$ unnecessary.
