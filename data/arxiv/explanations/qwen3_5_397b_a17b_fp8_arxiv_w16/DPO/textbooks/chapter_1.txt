Chapter 1: # Introduction: The Challenge of Aligning Language Models

Large-scale unsupervised language models trained on vast corpora of internet text have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, acquiring broad world knowledge and sophisticated reasoning skills. However, the very nature of their training—learning to predict the next token in sequences generated by humans with diverse goals, priorities, and skill levels—creates a fundamental misalignment between what the model knows and how it behaves. While an unsupervised model may possess the knowledge to identify a common programming error or recognize a widespread scientific misconception, it lacks the inherent mechanism to prioritize high-quality, safe, or helpful responses over mediocre or incorrect ones. For instance, a model might be capable of understanding that a specific coding pattern is buggy, yet when asked to generate code, it might reproduce that bug simply because it appears frequently in its training data. Similarly, if fifty percent of the population believes a specific misconception, an unsupervised model trained to mimic human text distribution might assert that misconception as truth in roughly half of its responses. This discrepancy highlights a critical distinction in artificial intelligence: the difference between a model's latent knowledge and abilities versus its desired responses and behavior. Selecting the latter from the former is the central challenge of alignment, essential for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable.

The primary objective in addressing this challenge is steerability—the ability to precisely control a language model's output to match human intent regarding safety, tone, and factual accuracy. While supervised fine-tuning on high-quality demonstrations offers a straightforward path to improvement, it is often limited by the scarcity of expert demonstrations and the difficulty of capturing nuanced human preferences through imitation alone. Humans find it significantly easier to judge which of two responses is better than to write a perfect response from scratch. Consequently, the field has largely coalesced around methods that leverage relative human judgments rather than absolute demonstrations. The prevailing approach involves collecting datasets where humans indicate a preference for one model generation over another. These preferences serve as a signal to guide the model away from undesirable behaviors and toward those deemed helpful and harmless. However, translating these relative preferences into a functional language model policy has traditionally required complex machinery involving reinforcement learning, introducing significant barriers to implementation and stability.

The standard methodology for achieving this alignment is known as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This pipeline is notoriously intricate, typically comprising three distinct phases: supervised fine-tuning to establish a baseline policy, training a separate reward model to quantify human preferences, and finally, using reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to optimize the language model against that reward signal. While effective in producing models with impressive conversational abilities, this process is computationally expensive and prone to instability. It requires training multiple large neural networks simultaneously and sampling from the language model during the training loop, which incurs heavy computational costs and demands extensive hyperparameter tuning. The complexity of managing the interaction between the policy, the reward model, and the reference constraints often leads to fragile training dynamics where small changes can cause the model to collapse or drift into nonsensical behavior.

In response to these challenges, a new approach called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a streamlined alternative that eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning loops. The core thesis of DPO is that the standard RLHF objective can be solved exactly using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, provided one adopts a specific parameterization of the reward function. By leveraging an analytical mapping between reward functions and optimal policies under a KL-divergence constraint, DPO allows researchers to optimize a language model directly on preference data. This method implicitly optimizes the same objective as traditional RLHF—maximizing reward while staying close to a reference policy—but does so by treating the language model itself as an implicit reward model. This conceptual shift transforms a complex reinforcement learning problem into a stable classification task, removing the need for sampling during fine-tuning and drastically reducing the engineering overhead required to align large language models with human values.

# From Pre-training to Alignment: Why unsupervised learning isn't enough

The journey of modern language models begins with unsupervised pre-training on massive datasets derived from the internet. During this phase, models learn statistical patterns of language, effectively internalizing a vast array of facts, reasoning strategies, and stylistic nuances present in human-generated text. However, this training regime is fundamentally passive; the model learns to mimic the distribution of its training data without any intrinsic understanding of quality, safety, or utility. The data sources include content written by experts alongside content filled with errors biases, toxicity, and misconceptions. Because the objective is purely next-token prediction based on likelihood, the model treats all patterns equally valid if they appear frequently enough in the corpus. Consequently, while the pre-trained model acquires "broad world knowledge," it also inherits the "wide variety of goals" and flaws inherent in humanity's digital footprint.

This inheritance creates a critical gap between capability and alignment. A pre-trained model might successfully demonstrate an awareness of common programming mistakes because such errors are prevalent in online forums where novices seek help. Yet, when tasked with acting as a coding assistant, this same awareness does not guarantee that the model will avoid generating those mistakes. Without explicit steering mechanisms biased toward high-quality outputs—which may be statistically rare in the training set—the model is just as likely to reproduce bad habits as good ones. The unsupervised nature of pre-training means the model has no concept of "desirable" versus "undesirable" behavior; it only understands "probable" versus "improbable." If low-quality code constitutes a significant portion of its training distribution regarding specific syntax structures or logic flows (e.g., Python lists vs dictionaries), it will replicate those distributions faithfully regardless of whether they constitute bugs or anti-patterns unless guided otherwise during alignment stages [1].
