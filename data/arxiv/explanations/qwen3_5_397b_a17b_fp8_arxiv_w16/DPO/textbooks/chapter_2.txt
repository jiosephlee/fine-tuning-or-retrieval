Chapter 2: # Foundations: The Traditional RLHF Framework

Before one can appreciate the elegance and efficiency of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), it is essential to rigorously understand the complex machinery it seeks to simplify. The prevailing paradigm for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This framework has been instrumental in transforming raw, unsupervised language models—which possess vast world knowledge but lack behavioral steering—into helpful, harmless, and honest assistants. However, the traditional RLHF pipeline is a multi-stage process involving distinct optimization objectives, separate neural networks, and computationally expensive reinforcement learning loops. To grasp why DPO represents a fundamental shift, we must first dissect the mathematical formalism of this standard approach, tracing the journey from supervised fine-tuning to the intricate dance of policy optimization under constraint.

The traditional RLHF pipeline, as established in foundational works and refined by subsequent research, operates in three distinct phases. It begins with grounding the model in high-quality data through supervised learning. It then moves to a critical intermediate step where a separate "reward model" is trained to mimic human judgment based on comparative data. Finally, the original language model is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize the scores provided by this reward model, while simultaneously adhering to strict constraints that prevent the model from forgetting its linguistic capabilities or collapsing into repetitive outputs. Understanding each of these components—the reference policy, the probabilistic modeling of preferences via the Bradley-Terry framework, and the KL-constrained objective—is a prerequisite for analyzing how DPO collapses this entire pipeline into a single, stable classification loss.

# Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reference Policies

The journey of alignment begins not with reinforcement learning, but with standard supervised learning. Large-scale unsupervised language models are trained on massive corpora of internet text, acquiring broad reasoning skills and factual knowledge. However, because this training data reflects the diverse and often conflicting goals of humanity, the resulting model lacks a coherent persona or specific behavioral guidelines. To address this, the first stage of RLHF involves Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). In this phase, the pre-trained model is fine-tuned on a curated dataset of high-quality demonstrations relevant to downstream tasks such as dialogue, summarization, or coding assistance. The goal is to produce a model, denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, that serves as a competent starting point for further alignment. This model has learned to generate responses that are not only grammatically correct but also demonstratively helpful according to human experts.

Crucially, this SFT model plays a dual role in the subsequent stages of the pipeline. Firstly, it acts as the initialization for the policy that will eventually be optimized via reinforcement learning. Secondly, and perhaps more importantly for the theoretical underpinnings of RLHF, it serves as the "reference policy," denoted as $\pi_\text{ref}$. In many implementations, $\pi_\text{ref}$ is identical to $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The reference policy represents the distribution of text that the system considers "safe" and linguistically diverse before any aggressive optimization for reward takes place. Throughout the reinforcement learning phase, the evolving policy $\pi_\theta$ is constantly measured against $\pi_\text{ref}$. This comparison is vital because optimizing solely for high reward can lead a language model to exploit loopholes in the reward function, generating nonsensical yet highly-scored text or collapsing into a single mode of repetition. The reference policy anchors the optimization process, ensuring that the final model retains the fluency and diversity inherent in the initial supervised training. Without this anchor, the pursuit of maximal reward could easily degrade the quality of generations to unintelligible noise that merely tricks the reward estimator.

# Modeling Human Preferences: The Bradley-Terry Model formulation

Once an SFT model is established, the next challenge is to quantify human preferences mathematically so they can be optimized. Humans rarely assign absolute numerical scores to text; instead, they naturally compare options. Consequently, RLHF relies on datasets of pairwise comparisons rather than scalar ratings. In this setup, given a prompt $x$, two candidate completions $y_1$ and $y_2$ are generated (typically sampled from $\pi^\text{SFT}$). A human labeler then indicates which response they prefer, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the winner (preferred) and $y_l$ is the loser (dispreferred). To translate these binary choices into a trainable signal, researchers employ statistical models of preference. The most widely adopted framework in this domain is the Bradley-Terry (BT) model.

The Bradley-Terry model posits that there exists a latent reward function $r^*(x, y)$ governing human preferences, even though we cannot observe this function directly. The core assumption of the BT model is that the probability of preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ follows a specific logistic form determined by the difference in their latent rewards. Mathematically, this probability distribution $p^*$ is expressed as:

$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x,y_1)\right)+\exp\left(r^*(x,y_2)\right)}.
$$

This formulation implies that if response $y_1$ has a significantly higher latent reward than $y_2$, the exponential term dominates, drivingthe probability of preference toward 1.
