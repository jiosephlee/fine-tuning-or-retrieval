Chapter 5: # Theoretical Guarantees and Stability Analysis

The introduction of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a compelling alternative to the complex Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline, but its efficacy rests on more than just empirical performance; it is grounded in rigorous theoretical guarantees. A primary concern when bypassing explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning algorithms is whether the simplified approach sacrifices expressive power or stability. This chapter addresses these concerns by providing a deep dive into the mathematical foundations that ensure DPO is not merely a heuristic approximation, but an exact solver for the standard RLHF objective under specific conditions. We will explore how DPO navigates the inherent ambiguities in preference data through the concept of equivalence classes, demonstrate that the policy reparameterization covers the entire space of valid reward functions, and analyze why traditional actor-critic methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) suffer from instabilities that DPO inherently avoids. By understanding these theoretical underpinnings, one gains confidence that optimizing a language model directly via a classification loss is mathematically equivalent to solving the constrained reinforcement learning problem, provided the correct parameterization is used.

# Equivalence Classes of Reward Functions

To understand the theoretical validity of DPO, one must first confront a fundamental property of preference-based learning: the reward function is not uniquely identifiable from preference data alone. In standard supervised learning, where we have input-output pairs, the target function is often well-defined. However, in preference learning, humans provide relative judgments—stating that response $y_1$ is better than $y_2$ given a prompt $x$—rather than absolute scalar scores. This relativity introduces an intrinsic ambiguity. Specifically, if we have a reward function $r(x, y)$ that perfectly explains a set of human preferences, adding any arbitrary function that depends solely on the prompt $x$ to the reward values will not change the relative ordering of responses. Mathematically, if $r(x, y)$ generates a specific probability distribution over preferences, then any function $r'(x, y) = r(x, y) + f(x)$ will generate the exact same distribution, because the term $f(x)$ cancels out when taking the difference between rewards for two responses to the same prompt.

This observation leads to the formal definition of an equivalence relation between reward functions. We say that two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are equivalent if and only if their difference is a function purely of the context $x$. That is, $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some function $f$. This relation partitions the vast space of all possible reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. Within any single class, every member produces identical predictions regarding which response a human would prefer over another. Consequently, from the perspective of matching human preference data, no algorithm can distinguish between members of the same equivalence class; they are statistically indistinguishable. This under-specification is a well-known characteristic of Plackett-Luce and Bradley-Terry models, which form the basis of most preference learning frameworks. Recognizing this structure is crucial because it implies that our goal in RLHF is not to recover a unique "true" reward function—which is impossible—but rather to recover *any* reward function from the correct equivalence class that induces the optimal policy.

The existence of these equivalence classes has profound implications for how we design learning algorithms. Traditional RLHF approaches often impose arbitrary identifiability constraints to force the optimization process to converge to a single point within an equivalence class, such as normalizing rewards to have zero mean across a dataset. However, these constraints are artificial and do not affect the resulting behavior of the agent, since all members of the class yield the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective. DPO leverages this insight by acknowledging that we do not need to pin down a specific numerical value for the reward; we only need to find a policy that corresponds to *some* valid representative of the optimal reward class. By shifting our focus from estimating scalar rewards to optimizing policies directly, DPO sidestepsthe difficulty of navigating this flat landscape of equivalent solutions and instead targets the policy manifold where the solution is unique and well-defined.
