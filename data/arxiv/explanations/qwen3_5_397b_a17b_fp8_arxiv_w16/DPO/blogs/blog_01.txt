# Stop Training Reward Models: Why Your Language Model Already Knows What Humans Prefer

If you have followed the recent explosion of aligned large language models, you are likely familiar with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). It is the gold standard for making models helpful, harmless, and honest. However, anyone who has tried to implement it knows the pain: RLHF is notoriously unstable, computationally expensive, and requires training multiple distinct models. You need a supervised fine-tuned model, a separate reward model, and then a complex reinforcement learning loop involving sampling and hyperparameter tuning just to get things working.

But what if the separate reward model was unnecessary? A groundbreaking paper titled "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" suggests exactly that. The authors introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning entirely. Instead of juggling multiple networks, DPO allows you to fine-tune your language model directly on human preference data using a simple classification loss.

## The Traditional RLHF Bottleneck

To understand why DPO is such a significant shift, we must first look at the traditional RLHF pipeline. Standard approaches typically involve three distinct phases. First, a pre-trained model undergoes supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on high-quality demonstrations. Second, this SFT model generates pairs of responses for various prompts, which humans rank to create a preference dataset. A separate neural network, the reward model, is then trained to predict these human rankings. Finally, the SFT model is fine-tuned again using reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to maximize the score given by the reward model while staying close to the original SFT distribution via a KL-divergence constraint.

This process is fraught with difficulties. Training the reward model introduces its own set of errors and biases. Furthermore, the RL phase requires sampling from the policy during training, which is slow and memory-intensive. The interaction between the policy and the reward model often leads to instability, requiring careful tuning of hyperparameters to prevent the model from collapsing or drifting too far from sensible behavior. Essentially, we are solving a complex optimization problem by breaking it into two harder problems: learning a reward function and then learning a policy to maximize it.

## The Mathematical Insight: Policies Encode Rewards

The core innovation of DPO lies in a mathematical realization about the relationship between reward functions and optimal policies. In standard RLHF, we seek a policy $\pi_r$ that maximizes expected reward while penalizing divergence from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The objective is usually written as maximizing $\mathbb{E}[r(x, y)] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi(y\mid x) \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]$.

It turns out that for this specific objective, there is a closed-form solution for the optimal policy. Mathematically, the optimal policy can be expressed as:

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
$$

Here, $Z(x)$ is a partition function that ensures the probabilities sum to one. While calculating $Z(x)$ is generally intractable for large language models because it requires summing over all possible sequences, this equation reveals something profound: there is a direct, analytic mapping between any reward function $r$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$.

By rearranging this equation, we can express the reward function in terms of the policy itself:

$$
r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)
$$

This reparameterization implies that any optimal policy implicitly defines a reward function. More importantly, when we plug this expression back into the Bradley-Terry model—which describes how humans choose between two options based on their underlying rewards—the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out. This happens because human preferences depend only on the *difference* in rewards between two candidates, not their absolute values. Consequently, we can express the probability of a human preferring response $y_1$ over $y_2$ solely in terms of the policy ratios:

$$
p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
$$

This derivation removes the need to ever explicitly calculate or train a separate reward model. The language model parameters themselves become sufficient statistics for the reward.

## How Direct Preference Optimization Works

With the math established, the DPO algorithm becomes remarkably straightforward. Instead of training a reward model and then running PPO, DPO treats preference learning as a simple binary classification problem directly on the language model weights. Given a dataset of preferences containing prompts $x$, preferred completions $y_w$, and dispreferred completions $y_l$, DPO minimizes a loss function that looks very similar to binary cross-entropy:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]
$$

In plain English, this loss function encourages the model to increase the relative likelihood of the preferred response compared to the dispreferred one, normalized by how likely those responses were under the reference model. The term inside sigmoid acts as an implicit reward difference. If the current policy already ranks the preferred response higher than the dispreferred one by a sufficient margin (scaled by $\beta$), the loss is small. If it gets them backwards or lacks confidence, gradients flow to adjust parameters accordingly.
