# The Comeback Kid: Why LSTMs Are Ready to Challenge Transformers Again

In the landscape of deep learning, few narratives are as dramatic as the rise and fall of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. Introduced in the 1990s, LSTMs were the undisputed champions of sequence modeling, powering the first generation of Large Language Models and solving complex tasks from handwriting recognition to machine translation. Their core innovation, the "constant error carousel," allowed them to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem that plagued earlier recurrent neural networks. However, the arrival of the Transformer architecture in 2017 marked a turning point. With its parallelizable self-attention mechanism, the Transformer quickly outpaced LSTMs at scale, becoming the foundation for modern giants like GPT and Llama.

For years, the consensus has been that LSTMs are obsolete for large-scale language modeling. But what if the limitations holding them back weren't fundamental flaws in the concept, but rather engineering hurdles that could be cleared with modern techniques? A new architecture called xLSTM (Extended LSTM) asks exactly this question. By revisiting the original LSTM design and integrating advancements from recent years, researchers have created a model that challenges the dominance of Transformers and State Space Models. This isn't just a nostalgic look back; it is a rigorous attempt to scale LSTMs to billions of parameters while fixing their three historical weaknesses: the inability to revise storage decisions, limited storage capacity, and a lack of parallelizability.

## The Three Flaws That Held LSTMs Back

To understand the significance of xLSTM, we must first understand why standard LSTMs fell behind. Despite their success in specialized domains like reinforcement learning and hydrological modeling, traditional LSTMs struggle when scaled to the demands of modern language modeling. The research identifies three specific bottlenecks.

First, standard LSTMs cannot easily revise their storage decisions. Once information is written into the cell state, modifying it based on new incoming data is difficult. This is particularly problematic in tasks requiring dynamic updates, such as finding the nearest neighbor in a sequence where a better match appears later. Second, LSTMs suffer from limited storage capacity because they compress all contextual information into scalar cell states. This compression forces the model to discard details, leading to poor performance on rare tokens or tasks requiring high-fidelity memory retention. Finally, and perhaps most critically for modern hardware, standard LSTMs lack parallelizability. Their reliance on hidden-to-hidden connections means that every time step depends on the previous one, enforcing strictly sequential processing that prevents efficient utilization of GPUs compared to the parallel nature of Transformers.

## Enter xLSTM: Exponential Gating and New Memory Structures

The xLSTM architecture addresses these limitations through two primary innovations: exponential gating and modified memory structures. These changes give birth to two distinct variants within the xLSTM family: sLSTM (scalar LSTM) and mLSTM (matrix LSTM). Together, they form building blocks that can be residually stacked to create deep architectures capable of competing with state-of-the-art models.

The first major upgrade is the introduction of exponential gating. In traditional LSTMs, gates use sigmoid activation functions bounded between 0 and 1. xLSTM replaces this with exponential activation functions for input and forget gates. This change allows the model to aggressively revise its memory content. If a new piece of information is highly relevant, the exponential gate can assign it a significantly larger weight than previous entries, effectively overwriting or updating old beliefs. To prevent numerical instability caused by large exponentials, xLSTM employs a stabilizer state that normalizes these values dynamically. This mechanism directly solves the revision problem, allowing sLSTM to handle state-tracking tasks that confuse standard RNNs and even some Transformers.

## sLSTM: Mastering State Tracking with Scalar Memory

The sLSTM variant retains the scalar memory structure of the original LSTM but enhances it with the new exponential gating and a novel memory mixing strategy. While standard LSTMs mix memory across cells using recurrent weights, sLSTM introduces a multi-head approach where memory mixing occurs within heads but not across them. This architectural tweak, combined with exponential gating, creates a powerful mechanism for tracking states over long sequences.

This capability is crucial for tasks involving formal languages or code evaluation, where keeping track of nested structures or variable assignments is essential. Experimental results show that while Transformers and State Space Models often fail at parity tasks or regular grammar checks due to their lack of explicit state tracking, sLSTM excels. By maintaining a scalar cell state $c_t$ updated via $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, but with exponentially scaled gates, sLSTM achieves a level of expressivity that bridges the gap between simple recurrence and complex attention mechanisms.

## mLSTM: Scaling Capacity with Matrix Memory

While sLSTM handles state tracking beautifully, it does not fully resolve the issues of storage capacity and parallelization. This is where mLSTM comes in. Instead of storing information in a single scalar value per cell, mLSTM expands the memory to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This shift transforms the memory update rule into a covariance update: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Here, $\mathbf{k}_t$ represents a key vector and $\mathbf{v}_t$ a value vector derived from the input, mimicking the key-value pairing found in Transformers.

This matrix memory dramatically increases storage capacity, allowing the model to retain far more distinct pieces of information without aggressive compression retrieval is performed via a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$ multiplying against this matrix ($\mathbf{h}_t \propto \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$). Crucially because mLSTM removes hidden-to-hidden connections (memory mixing), it eliminates sequential dependencies entirely regarding parameter sharing across time steps for certain operations enabling full parallelization during training similar to FlashAttention or linear transformers making it suitable for massive datasets while retaining linear computation complexity relative to sequence length unlike quadratic growth seen in classic attention mechanisms!

# Beyond Binary Gates: How 'Exponential Gating' Lets AI Change Its Mind

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network has been a cornerstone of sequence modeling. Its core innovation, the "constant error carousel," allowed information to flow through time without vanishing, enabling early successes in speech recognition and machine translation. However, traditional LSTMs suffer from a critical cognitive limitation: once they decide to store or forget information, that decision is largely final. They struggle to revise their memory when new, conflicting evidence appears later in a sequence.

The newly proposed xLSTM (Extended LSTM) architecture addresses this rigidity with a novel mechanism called **exponential gating**. By moving beyond the standard sigmoid activation functions used in classical LSTMs, xLSTM empowers neural networks to dynamically overwrite past storage decisions. This shift transforms recurrent models from static archivists into dynamic reasoners capable of solving complex state-tracking problems that previously stumped them.

## The Rigidity of Standard Sigmoid Gates

To understand the breakthrough of exponential gating, we must first look at the limitations of the original LSTM design. In a standard LSTM, the flow of information into and out of the memory cell is controlled by gates—specifically the input gate $i_t$ and the forget gate $f_t$. These gates typically use a sigmoid activation function, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+\exp(-x))$, which squashes values into a strict range between 0 and 1.

The cell state update rule in a traditional LSTM looks like this:

$$ c_t = f_t \cdot c_{t-1} + i_t \cdot z_t $$

Here, $c_t$ is the current cell state, and $z_t$ is the new candidate information. The forget gate $f_t$ determines how much of the old memory $c_{t-1}$ to retain, while the input gate $i_t$ decides how much new information to add. Because both gates are bounded by 1, the system operates on a principle of gradual decay or addition. If the model stores a piece of information early in a sequence (setting $i_t$ high), it can only remove that information slowly over many time steps by consistently applying low forget gates.

This creates a significant bottleneck for tasks requiring **state tracking**. Imagine a scenario where an AI reads a story and needs to track the location of a character. If the character moves from "Paris" to "London," a standard LSTM struggles to immediately erase "Paris" from its active memory if it was strongly encoded earlier. It cannot effectively "change its mind" about what is currently true because the sigmoid gates lack the magnitude to aggressively override previous states in a single step. This inability to revise storage decisions leads to errors in tasks like nearest neighbor search or parsing nested logical structures.

## Enter Exponential Gating

The xLSTM paper introduces a fundamental modification to this mechanism by allowing gates to utilize exponential activation functions instead of solely relying on sigmoids. In the new scalar LSTM (sLSTM) variant, the input gate $i_t$ is defined as:

$$ i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t) $$

Unlike the sigmoid function, the exponential function is unbounded. This means the input gate can produce values significantly greater than 1. When $i_t$ is large, it allows the new input $z_t$ to completely dominate the cell state update equation, effectively overwriting whatever was stored in $c_{t-1}$ regardless of the forget gate's value.

This capability grants the network the power of **revision**. If the model encounters new information that contradicts its current belief, it can instantly amplify the input gate to flush out old data and commit to the new state. This mimics a more human-like ability to update beliefs based on fresh evidence, rather than being stuck with a weighted average of everything seen so far.

However, introducing unbounded exponential values brings a risk of numerical instability and overflow. To counteract this, xLSTM employs sophisticated normalization and stabilization techniques. The authors introduce a normalizer state $n_t$ that tracks the cumulative strength of the gates:

$$ n_t = f_t \cdot n_{t-1} + i_t $$

The hidden state is then computed by dividing the cell state by this normalizer: $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. Furthermore, a stabilizer state $m_t$ is used to subtract large exponents before calculation, ensuring that while the relative magnitudes allow for aggressive updates, the actual arithmetic remains within safe floating-point limits. This ensures that the theoretical benefit of exponential gating translates into stable training dynamics.

## Solving State Tracking Problems

The practical impact of exponential gating is most evident in formal language tasks that require precise state tracking. The paper highlights experiments on tasks such as parity checks and modular arithmetic, where the model must maintain an exact count or status that flips based on incoming tokens.

Traditional Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) often fail at these tasks because they lack explicit memory mixing mechanisms that allow for such sharp revisions. Similarly, vanilla LSTMs degrade in performance as sequences grow longer because their gated updates become too diffuse to maintain exact states.

In contrast, sLSTM with exponential gating excels in these environments. By dynamically adjusting the magnitude of its updates, it can reset its internal counters or flags instantly when required by the grammar rules. For instance, in a task where the model must identify if two distant elements in a sequence match while ignoring distractors in between, sLSTM can store an initial element and then decisively overwrite it if a closer or more relevant match is found later—a feat known as revising storage decisions. This capability bridges the gap between recurrent networks and Turing-complete computation styles required for evaluating code or tracking entities in long narratives.

# From Scalars to Matrices: Supercharging AI Memory with mLSTM

For decades, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network has been a cornerstone of sequence modeling. Introduced in the 1990s, its core innovation was the "constant error carousel," a mechanism designed to prevent gradients from vanishing during training. At the heart of this design lies a simple scalar memory cell, denoted as $c_t$, which stores information as a single floating-point number per unit. While this architecture powered early successes in speech recognition and machine translation, it eventually hit a ceiling. As models scaled to billions of parameters, the traditional LSTM struggled to compete with the Transformer, primarily due to limited storage capacity and an inability to process sequences in parallel.

The recent introduction of the Extended LSTM (xLSTM) challenges the notion that recurrent networks are obsolete. Specifically, the matrix LSTM (mLSTM) variant represents a fundamental shift in how neural networks store information. By evolving from scalar memory cells to massive matrix memories governed by covariance update rules, mLSTM achieves storage capacities that rival attention mechanisms while maintaining linear computational complexity. This post explores how this architectural leap enables better recall of rare tokens and long-range associations without the prohibitive costs of standard self-attention.

## The Bottleneck of Scalar Memory

To understand the breakthrough offered by mLSTM, we must first look at the limitations of the original LSTM. In a standard setup, the cell state $c_t$ is updated additively based on input and forget gates:

$$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$$

Here, $c_t$ is a scalar value (or a vector of independent scalars). This design imposes a severe compression bottleneck. When processing long sequences or vast vocabaries, the model must compress complex semantic relationships into these limited scalar states. The research paper highlights this issue through "Rare Token Prediction" tasks. Traditional LSTMs perform poorly on infrequent tokens because their scalar memory lacks the capacity to distinctly store and retrieve specific, low-frequency patterns amidst the noise of common ones. Essentially, the memory bucket is too small to hold the nuanced details required for high-fidelity language modeling at scale.

Furthermore, standard LSTMs suffer from an inability to revise storage decisions efficiently. Once a value is written to the cell state, modifying it based on new context is difficult without erasing previous information. This rigidity makes them ill-suited for tasks requiring dynamic updates, such as nearest neighbor search within a sequence, where the model must constantly compare incoming data against stored references and update its "best match" accordingly.

## Enter mLSTM: The Matrix Memory Solution

The mLSTM addresses these capacity constraints by radically expanding the memory structure. Instead of a scalar $c_t \in \mathbb{R}$, mLSTM utilizes a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This shift transforms the memory from a simple storage register into a rich associative map capable of holding key-value pairs simultaneously.

In this framework, the model stores information using keys ($\mathbf{k}_t$) and values ($\mathbf{v}_t$), borrowing terminology from Transformer architectures. When new information arrives at time step $t$, it is not just added to a running sum; it is integrated into the matrix via an outer product operation. The update rule follows a covariance formulation:

$$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$$

Here, $f_t$ acts as a decay factor (similar to a forget gate), and $i_t$ serves as a learning rate (input gate). The term $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ creates a rank-one update that associates the value vector directly with the key vector within the matrix structure. This approach is theoretically grounded in Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs) and offers optimal separability for retrieving stored patterns. By storing interactions between dimensions rather than just independent values, the matrix memory can capture complex correlations that scalar cells simply cannot represent.

## Retrieval Without Quadratic Cost

One might wonder if expanding memory to a matrix incurs the same quadratic complexity ($O(N^2)$) that plagues Transformer self-attention over long sequences. The elegance of mLSTM lies in its retrieval mechanism, which remains efficient regardless of sequence length.

To retrieve information at a later time step $t + \tau$, the model uses a query vector $\mathbf{q}_{t+\tau}$. The retrieval is performed via a simple matrix-vector multiplication:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t}{\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|， 1)}$$

The denominator includes a normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$, which tracks the cumulative strength of the gates to ensure numerical stability. Because $\mathbf{C}_t$ aggregates all past key-value interactions into a fixed-size matrix, the cost of retrieval depends only on the feature dimension $d$, not on the length of the sequence processed so far. This allows mLSTM to access long-range dependencies with constant memory complexity relative to sequence length, avoiding the exploding memory requirements of caching every past token's key and value as Transformers do.

## Parallelization and Practical Performance

A historical drawback of recurrent networks has been their sequential nature; you must compute step $t$ before step $t+1$. However, because mLSTM removes "memory mixing" (the hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections found in standard LSTMs) and relies solely on input-driven updates to its matrix state, it is fully parallelizable during training. Just like FlashAttention optimizes Transformers for speed, mLSTM can leverage parallel hardware accelerators to process entire sequences simultaneously, despite its recurrent formulation at inference time.

Empirical results from the xLSTM paper demonstrate that this architectural change yields tangible benefits. In language modeling tasks trained on hundreds of billions of tokens, mLSTM-based architectures outperform both traditional LSTMs and competitive State Space Models like Mamba. Notably, they show superior performance in predicting rare tokens and handling long-context extrapolation up to 16k tokens. The matrix memory effectively solves the storage capacity issue, allowing the model to retain distinct representations for infrequent words and complex syntactic structures that would otherwise be lost in scalar compression.

## Conclusion

The evolution from scalar cells in traditional LSTMs to matrix memories in mLSTM marks a significant milestone in recurrent neural network design. By adopting covariance update rules, mLSTM bridges the gap between the efficiency of recurrent models and the expressive power of attention mechanisms. It proves that we do not need to sacrifice parallelizability or scalability to achieve high-capacity memory. As we continue to push towards larger language models, architectures like mLSTM offer a promising path forward—one where efficient, linear-time processing meets robust associative recall capability open new possibilities for edge deployment and real-time applications alike



# Speed Meets Efficiency: Can xLSTM Offer Linear Scaling for Edge Devices?

For over a decade, the Transformer architecture has reigned supreme in the realm of Large Language Models (LLMs). Its self-attention mechanism unlocked unprecedented capabilities in understanding and generating human language. However, this power comes with a significant cost: quadratic complexity. As the sequence length grows, the computational requirements and memory usage of Transformers explode, making them increasingly difficult to deploy on resource-constrained environments like mobile phones or IoT edge devices. A recent breakthrough paper, "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory," challenges this status quo by revisiting and radically upgrading the classic LSTM architecture to offer linear scaling without sacrificing performance.

## The Bottleneck of Quadratic Complexity

To understand the significance of xLSTM, we must first look at why Transformers struggle at the edge. The core of the Transformer is the attention mechanism, which calculates relationships between every token in a sequence and every other token. If a sequence has length $L$, the attention matrix requires $O(L^2)$ operations and memory. While techniques like FlashAttention have optimized constants, the fundamental quadratic growth remains. This means that doubling the context length quadruples the compute and memory needs, quickly hitting hardware limits on devices with finite RAM and battery life.

In contrast, traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) like LSTMs process sequences step-by-step, maintaining a fixed-size hidden state. This results in linear computation complexity $O(L)$ and constant memory usage regardless of sequence length. Historically, LSTMs fell out of favor for large-scale language modeling because they struggled to compete with Transformers in terms of accuracy and parallelizability during training. The xLSTM paper asks a pivotal question: can we fix the inherent limitations of LSTMs while retaining their efficiency advantages?

## Introducing xLSTM: A Modernized Recurrent Architecture

The authors of xLSTM propose two major innovations to modernize the LSTM: exponential gating and novel memory structures. These changes give rise to two distinct variants: sLSTM (scalar LSTM) and mLSTM (matrix LSTM). By integrating these into residual block backbones, the resulting xLSTM architectures achieve performance comparable to state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba, but with a drastically different efficiency profile.

The first innovation is exponential gating. Traditional LSTMs use sigmoid gates that output values between 0 and 1, which can limit the model's ability to revise stored information dynamically. xLSTM introduces exponential activation functions for input and forget gates, accompanied by normalization and stabilization techniques. This allows the model to aggressively update its memory state, solving issues like the inability to revise storage decisions that plagued earlier RNNs.

The second innovation addresses storage capacity through structural changes. The sLSTM retains a scalar memory cell but enhances it with new memory mixing capabilities across multiple heads. More importantly for parallelization, the mLSTM replaces the scalar cell with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. Instead of simple additive updates, mLSTM uses a covariance update rule where key-value pairs are stored via outer products: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. This matrix memory significantly boosts storage capacity, allowing the model to handle complex associative recall tasks that traditionally required attention mechanisms.

## Linear Computation and Constant Memory Footprint

The most compelling argument for xLSTM in edge computing lies in its complexity characteristics. Unlike Transformers, xLSTM networks exhibit linear computation complexity $O(L)$ with respect to sequence length. Even more critical for edge deployment is the constant memory complexity. Once an xLSTM processes a token, it updates its internal state and discards the raw input history. The memory footprint does not grow with the context window; it remains fixed based on the model dimensions.

This stands in stark contrast to Transformers, which must store growing key-value caches during inference or re-compute attention scores for long contexts. For an edge device running a real-time application—such as voice assistants, live translation, or sensor data analysis—the predictability of constant memory usage is invaluable. It ensures that latency remains stable even as conversations or data streams extend over thousands of tokens, preventing the system from crashing due to Out-Of-Memory errors.

## Parallelizability and Real-Time Performance

A historical weakness of RNNs has been their sequential nature, which prevents parallel processing during training. The xLSTM paper addresses this directly through the mLstm variant. Because mLSTM eliminates hidden-hidden recurrent connections (memory mixing) in favor of a matrix update rule that depends only on inputs and gates, it can be reformulated into a fully parallelizable operation. This allows mLSTM to leverage modern GPU accelerators just as effectively as Transformers during the training phase, utilizing techniques similar to FlashAttention or parallel scans found in SSMs.

While sLSTM retains some sequential dependencies due to its memory mixing capabilities, the authors developed highly optimized CUDA kernels that make it less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation. In inference scenarios, both variants shine. The paper reports that xLSTM models maintain low perplexity even when extrapolating to context lengths far beyond their training window (up to 16k tokens), whereas other models degrade rapidly. Furthermore, because xLSTAR does not require expanding cache sizes for longer sequences, it achieves higher throughput at larger batch sizes compared to Llama-style Transformers.

## Implications for Edge AI Deployment

The combination of Transformer-level performance and RNN-level efficiency positions xLSTM as a prime candidate for the next generation of edge AI. Industrial applications often require models that can run locally on devices with limited power budgets and strict latency requirements. The compressive nature of xLSTM's memory makes it ideal for these scenarios. Whether it is monitoring hydrological systems for flood warnings on remote sensors or powering intelligent agents on smartphones, xLSTM offers a path forward where sophisticated language understanding does not require cloud connectivity.

Moreover, the ability to scale these models to billions of parameters while maintaining linear scaling suggests that we are not forced to choose between capability and efficiency anymore. The experimental results show that xLSTM outperforms established models like Mamba and RWKV in validation perplexity across various model sizes trained on hundreds of billions of tokens. When paired with its favorable scaling laws, this indicates that future iterations could push the boundaries of what is possible on-device even further.

## Conclusion

The advent of xLSTM demonstrates that old ideas, when refined with modern insights, can rival—and sometimes surpass—current dominant technologies. By introducing exponential gating and matrix-based memory structures, xLstm overcomes the historical limitations of LSTMs regarding storage capacity and revision capabilities. Crucially for developers and engineers working on edge solutions, it delivers these gains while preserving linear computation complexity and constant memory usage. As we move toward an era where AI is ubiquitous and decentralized, architectures like xLstm provide the necessary foundation for deploying powerful language models anywhere speed meets efficiency constraints are paramount
