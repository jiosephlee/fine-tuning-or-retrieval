\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How does the normalizer state $n_t$ prevent numerical overflow in sLSTM with exponential gates?
Question:
In the sLSTM formulation (Equation 4), the input gate $i_t$ and potentially the forget gate $f_t$ use an exponential activation function ($\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$). Since exponentials can grow extremely large, I am confused about how the network remains stable. The paper introduces a 'normalizer state' $n_t$ defined as $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Later, the hidden state is calculated as $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. Could someone explain intuitively why dividing the cell state $c_t$ by this specific sum $n_t$ effectively normalizes the output and prevents explosion, even when the individual gate values become huge?
Answer:
The stability of the sLSTM despite using exponential gates relies on the fact that the normalizer state $n_t$ tracks the exact same accumulation history as the cell state $c_t$, effectively acting as a dynamic denominator that cancels out the magnitude of the exponential growth.

In the standard LSTM, gates are bounded between 0 and 1 by the sigmoid function, which naturally prevents the cell state from exploding purely due to gate magnitudes. However, the xLSTM paper introduces exponential gating ($i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$) to allow the model to aggressively revise storage decisions and handle large variations in input importance. Without compensation, multiplying the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ by a large forget gate or adding a massive input contribution would indeed cause immediate numerical overflow. The solution lies in the coupled recurrence relations defined in Equation 4 of the paper. The cell state updates as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, accumulating weighted inputs over time. Crucially, the normalizer state follows an identical structural update: $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Notice that $n_t$ sums the gates themselves ($i_t$) rather than the gated inputs ($i_t z_t$).

When you calculate the normalized hidden state $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, you are essentially computing a running weighted average of the cell inputs $z_t$. If we expand the recurrence for both states back to time step 0, $c_t$ becomes a sum of terms like $(\prod f) \cdot i \cdot z$, while $n_t$ becomes a sum of terms like $(\prod f) \cdot i$. If all gate values scale up exponentially by some factor due to large pre-activations $\tilde{i}_t$ or $\tilde{f}_t$, this scaling factor appears identically in every term of both the numerator ($c_t$) and the denominator ($n_t$). Consequently, when divided, these large common factors cancel out, leaving $\tilde{h}_t$ dependent primarily on the relative proportions of the inputs $z_t$ rather than their absolute accumulated magnitude. This ensures that even if individual gate values become huge, their ratio remains stable, preventing overflow in the final output.

The paper further notes that while this division handles the logical normalization, practical implementation still requires a "stabilizer state" $m_t$ (Equation 5) to prevent intermediate floating-point overflow during the calculation of $f_t$ and $i_t$ before they are used in the recurrence. This technique, akin to log-sum-exp tricks, shifts the exponents by a maximum value to keep numbers within representable ranges without altering the final mathematical result of the division $c_t / n_t$. Thus, the combination of the structurally identical normalizer recurrence and explicit log-space stabilization allows sLSTM to leverage the expressivity of unbounded exponential gates while maintaining numerical robustness.
