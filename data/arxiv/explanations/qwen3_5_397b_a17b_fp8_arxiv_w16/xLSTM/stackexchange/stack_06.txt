\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why does xLSTM[7:1] outperform xLSTM[1:0] on downstream reasoning tasks despite mLSTM having higher memory capacity?
Question:
Table 3 and Table 4 show that while xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) often has lower perplexity on raw language modeling (especially on diverse domains like PALOMA), xLSTEM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM blocks) tends to perform better on downstream reasoning tasks (like commonsense QA). Given that mLSTM has massive matrix memory capacity, why is adding just a few sLSTM blocks—which have smaller scalar memory but possess 'memory mixing'—so critical for reasoning? Does this suggest that 'state tracking' (enabled by sLSTM) is more important for logical reasoning than raw storage capacity (enabled by mLSTEM)?
Answer:
The observed performance gap between xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM) on downstream reasoning tasks stems from a fundamental distinction between **storage capacity** and **state tracking expressivity**, which are handled differently by the two block types. While the mLSTM excels at raw information retention due to its matrix memory, it lacks the architectural mechanism required for complex logical manipulation, which the sLSTM provides through memory mixing.

The core advantage of the mLSTM lies in its **matrix memory** $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and the associated covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. As described in the paper, this structure dramatically increases storage capacity compared to traditional scalar LSTMs, allowing the model to store a vast number of key-value pairs effectively. This explains why xLSTM[1:0] achieves superior perplexity on broad language modeling benchmarks like PALOMA; it acts as an extremely efficient associative memory, capable of recalling rare tokens and maintaining context over long sequences simply by storing more information. However, to achieve full parallelizability, the mLSTM explicitly removes **memory mixing**, meaning there are no recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections ($\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \to \mathbf{h}_t$) that allow different memory cells to interact dynamically during the sequence processing.

In contrast, reasoning tasks such as Commonsense QA often require **state tracking**—the ability to actively manipulate, update, and revise internal representations based on evolving context, rather than just passively retrieving stored facts. The paper identifies that models without memory mixing (including Transformers and State Space Models) struggle with formal languages requiring state tracking, such as parity tasks or nested dependencies. The sLSTM retains **memory mixing** via recurrent weights connecting the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the gates and cell inputs. Crucially, when combined with the new **exponential gating**, this allows the sLSTM to robustly revise storage decisions. If a piece of information needs to be updated or contradicted later in the sequence (a common requirement in logical reasoning), the exponential gates allow the model to overwrite previous states much more effectively than standard sigmoid gates.

Therefore, the superiority of xLSTM[7:1] on reasoning tasks suggests that while raw capacity (provided by the dominant mLSTM blocks) is sufficient for next-token prediction statistics, successful reasoning requires the specific computational expressivity of state tracking (provided by the sparse sLSTM blocks). The sLSTM blocks act as specialized units that can perform logical operations and maintain coherent entity states through memory mixing, while the surrounding mLSTM blocks provide the massive contextual bandwidth needed to hold the necessary facts. The addition of even a few sLSTM blocks injects this missing algorithmic capability into the architecture, enabling it to solve problems that pure storage capacity alone cannot address.
