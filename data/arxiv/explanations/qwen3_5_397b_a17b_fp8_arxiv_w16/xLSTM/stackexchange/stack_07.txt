\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What is the intuition behind using Pre-LayerNorm for mLSTEM blocks vs Post-LayerNorm for sLSTEM blocks?
Question:
In Section 3.4 ('xLSTMArchitecture'), the authors state: 'For an xLSTMEM block containing an sLTSEM, we use post up-projection block... For an xLTSEM block containing an mLTSEM, we use pre up-projection block since memory capacity becomes larger in high-dimensional space.' I am confused by this design choice. Usually, Pre-LN helps with training stability in deep networks. Why specifically does mapping to high-dimensional space *before* applying mLTSEM improve its performance? Is it because key/value/query projections need more dimensions to be effective before entering matrix-memory updates? Conversely, why does LTSEM prefer operating closer to residual stream dimensionality?
Answer:
The design choice to use **Pre-LayerNorm** (specifically described in the paper as a "pre up-projection block") for mLSTM blocks versus **Post-LayerNorm** ("post up-projection block") for sLSTM blocks stems directly from the fundamental difference in their memory capacities and the geometric properties of high-dimensional spaces, as articulated in Section 3.4 under "xLSTM Blocks."

The core intuition relies on **Cover's Theorem**, which the authors explicitly cite. This theorem posits that patterns which are not linearly separable in a low-dimensional space often become linearly separable when projected non-linearly into a sufficiently high-dimensional space. The goal of the xLSTM block is to "non-linearly summarize the past" to distinguish between different historical contexts effectively.

For the **mLSTM**, the memory structure is a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ updated via a covariance rule ($\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$). The paper argues that the "memory capacity becomes larger in high-dimensional space." By employing a pre up-projection architecture, the input is first linearly mapped into a higher-dimensional space *before* entering the recurrent mLSTM unit. This allows the matrix memory to operate on richer, higher-dimensional representations of keys and values. Since the storage capacity of this associative memory scales with the dimensionality of these vectors, expanding the dimension prior to the memory update maximizes the model's ability to store and retrieve distinct key-value pairs without interference. Essentially, you want the massive storage potential of the matrix memory to interact with the data while it is in its most expansive form.

Conversely, the **sLSTM** utilizes a scalar memory cell (or a vector of scalar cells) with explicit memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections. Its primary strength, as highlighted in the synthetic task experiments, is solving state-tracking problems rather than maximizing raw storage density through dimensionality. The authors choose a post up-projection block for sLSTM, mirroring standard Transformer feed-forward networks. In this configuration, the recurrent unit operates on the residual stream dimensionality (the "original space"), summarizing the past non-linearly within that constrained scope before projecting up for further processing. This suggests that for sLSTM, the complex dynamics of exponential gating and memory mixing are sufficient to separate histories without needing the auxiliary boost of operating inside an expanded high-dimensional manifold during the recurrence itself.

Therefore, the distinction is functional: mLSTM leverages high-dimensional geometry to maximize its matrix-based storage capacity (requiring Pre-LN/pre-projection), whereas sLSTM relies on its sophisticated gating and mixing mechanisms within the standard representation space to handle state tracking (utilizing Post-LN/post-projection).
