\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why is mLSTM fully parallelizable while sLSTM is not, despite both being recurrent?
Question:
The abstract states that mLSTM is 'fully parallelizable' while sLSTM suffers from a 'lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing.' Looking at the equations, both seem to depend on previous time steps ($t-1$). For mLSTM, we have $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. For sLSTM, we have similar recurrence. The paper mentions sLSTM has 'hidden-hidden connections' ($r_z h_{t-1}$ etc.) which mLSTM lacks (using only input-dependent gates for some parts?). Can someone clarify specifically which term in the recurrence relation breaks parallelization? Is it solely the presence of the recurrent weight matrices acting on $h_{t-1}$ in sLSTM, or does the matrix nature of $\mathbf{C}_t$ in mLSTM allow for a parallel scan algorithm (like in Mamba/SSMs) that isn't possible with scalar mixing?
Answer:
The fundamental difference lies not in the recurrence of the memory cell itself, but in whether the **gates and inputs depend on the previous hidden state** ($h_{t-1}$). While both sLSTM and mLSTM update a state variable based on $t-1$, parallelization is broken specifically by **memory mixing**, which the paper defines as "hidden-hidden connections" where recurrent weight matrices act on $h_{t-1}$ to compute the current gates or inputs.

In the standard LSTM and the proposed **sLSTM**, the computation of the cell input $z_t$ and the gates ($i_t, f_t, o_t$) explicitly includes a term like $r_z h_{t-1}$ (or $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ for multiple cells). As shown in Equation 5 of the paper, $\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{z} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{z} h_{t-1} + b_{z}$. Because the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ is derived from the cell state $c_{t-1}$ (via $h_{t-1} = o_{t-1} \psi(c_{t-1}/n_{t-1})$), you cannot compute the gates for time step $t$ without knowing the final output of time step $t-1$. This creates a strict serial dependency chain: to get $h_t$, you need $c_t$; to get $c_t$, you need $f_t$ and $i_t$; but to get those gates, you need $h_{t-1}$. This circular dependency prevents computing multiple time steps simultaneously, regardless of whether the memory is scalar or matrix-valued.

In contrast, **mLSTM** achieves full parallelizability by completely removing these hidden-hidden connections. If you examine Equations 8 through 14 in the mLSTM section, you will notice that $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, $\mathbf{q}_t$, $\mathbf{k}_t$, and $\mathbf{v}_t$ depend **only** on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and learned biases/weights (e.g., $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{i} \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$). There is no $+ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ term. Consequently, all gates and key/value/query vectors for the entire sequence can be computed in parallel in a single pass, as they do not rely on any previous temporal state. Once these values are pre-computed, the remaining recurrence—updating the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$—becomes a linear recurrence with known coefficients. This specific form allows the use of **parallel scan algorithms** (similar to those used in Mamba or associative scans), which can resolve the cumulative product and sum operations across time steps in logarithmic time ($O(\log T)$) rather than linear time ($O(T)$), effectively bypassing the sequential bottleneck.

Therefore, it is not the matrix nature of $\mathbf{C}_t$ alone that enables parallelization, nor is it strictly impossible to parallelize scalar recurrences. The dealbreaker is the **data dependency**: sLSTM mixes information from $h_{t-1}$ into the calculation of the update rules themselves, forcing sequential execution, whereas mLSTM decouples the gate/input calculation from the history, allowing the recurrence to be solved via parallel prefix scans. The paper explicitly notes in Section 2.4 that "mLSTM is parallelizable... [while] sLSTM is not parallelizable due to the memory mixing (hidden-hidden connections)."
