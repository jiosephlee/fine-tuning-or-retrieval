\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### How does 'memory mixing' in sLSTM enable solving state tracking tasks that Transformers cannot?
Question:
The paper claims that sLSTM's 'memory mixing' allows it to solve state tracking problems (like parity checks or formal languages) where Transformers and State Space Models fail. The text says: 'Memory mixing enables to solve state tracking problems... LSTMs are more expressive than State Space Models.' I understand that standard LSTMs mix information via recurrent hidden states, but how does this specifically translate to higher expressivity for formal languages? Does the ability to mix different cell states via matrices $\mathbf{R}_z, \mathbf{R}_i$, etc., allow sLSTM to simulate a finite automaton or counter machine in a way that attention mechanisms (which are content-based lookup) inherently cannot?
Answer:
The ability of sLSTM to solve state tracking tasks where Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) fail stems directly from its **memory mixing** mechanism, which allows the model to maintain and update a complex internal state that effectively simulates a finite automaton or counter machine. As noted in the "Most Related" section of the paper, models lacking memory mixing (such as Transformers and SSMs) are fundamentally less expressive than Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) for specific formal language tasks like parity checks or regular grammar recognition.

In standard Transformers and many SSMs, the mechanism for retrieving information is primarily content-based or relies on linear recurrences that do not allow different dimensions of the hidden state to interact dynamically at every time step. For instance, in a parity task (determining if the number of 1s in a sequence is even or odd), the model must strictly toggle its internal state between two distinct configurations upon seeing a specific token. Content-based attention looks up information based on similarity, which is ill-suited for this kind of rigid, algorithmic state transition. Similarly, diagonal SSMs often process dimensions independently or with fixed coupling, preventing the flexible recombination of state features required to track abstract counters or stack depths.

The sLSTM overcomes this limitation through **memory mixing**, defined in the paper as the use of recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{z}, \mathbf{R}_{i}, \mathbf{R}_{f}, \mathbf{R}_{o}$ that connect the previous hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the current cell inputs and gates. Unlike models where the hidden state is merely passed forward or scaled, these matrices allow the sLSTM to linearly mix the values across different memory cells at every time step before applying non-linear gating. Mathematically, terms like $r_{i} h_{t-1}$ in the gate equations (where $r$ represents the recurrent weights) enable the network to compute arbitrary linear combinations of its past history. When combined with the expressive power of exponential gating and the non-linearity of the sigmoid/tanh activations, this mixing allows the sLSTM to implement complex transition functions $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \rightarrow Q$ characteristic of finite automata.

Essentially, memory mixing grants the sLSTM the capacity to revise and reconfigure its entire internal representation dynamically, rather than just appending new information or decaying old information independently per channel. This structural capability aligns with theoretical findings cited in the paper (Merrill et al., Deletang et al.) showing that RNNs with such recurrent connectivity possess higher expressivity for state tracking than attention mechanisms or linear SSMs. While mLSTM sacrifices this specific mixing capability to gain full parallelizability via a matrix memory covariance update, sLSTM retains these hidden-hidden connections specifically to handle tasks requiring precise algorithmic state maintenance, making it uniquely capable among xLSTM variants for solving formal language problems that demand strict state tracking.
