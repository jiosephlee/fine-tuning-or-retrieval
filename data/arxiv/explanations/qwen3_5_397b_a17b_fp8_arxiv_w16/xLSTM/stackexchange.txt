### How does the normalizer state $n_t$ prevent numerical overflow in sLSTM with exponential gates?
Question:
In the sLSTM formulation (Equation 4), the input gate $i_t$ and potentially the forget gate $f_t$ use an exponential activation function ($\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$). Since exponentials can grow extremely large, I am confused about how the network remains stable. The paper introduces a 'normalizer state' $n_t$ defined as $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Later, the hidden state is calculated as $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. Could someone explain intuitively why dividing the cell state $c_t$ by this specific sum $n_t$ effectively normalizes the output and prevents explosion, even when the individual gate values become huge?
Answer:
The stability of the sLSTM despite using exponential gates relies on the fact that the normalizer state $n_t$ tracks the exact same accumulation history as the cell state $c_t$, effectively acting as a dynamic denominator that cancels out the magnitude of the exponential growth.

In the standard LSTM, gates are bounded between 0 and 1 by the sigmoid function, which naturally prevents the cell state from exploding purely due to gate magnitudes. However, the xLSTM paper introduces exponential gating ($i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$) to allow the model to aggressively revise storage decisions and handle large variations in input importance. Without compensation, multiplying the previous cell state $c_{t-1}$ by a large forget gate or adding a massive input contribution would indeed cause immediate numerical overflow. The solution lies in the coupled recurrence relations defined in Equation 4 of the paper. The cell state updates as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, accumulating weighted inputs over time. Crucially, the normalizer state follows an identical structural update: $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Notice that $n_t$ sums the gates themselves ($i_t$) rather than the gated inputs ($i_t z_t$).

When you calculate the normalized hidden state $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, you are essentially computing a running weighted average of the cell inputs $z_t$. If we expand the recurrence for both states back to time step 0, $c_t$ becomes a sum of terms like $(\prod f) \cdot i \cdot z$, while $n_t$ becomes a sum of terms like $(\prod f) \cdot i$. If all gate values scale up exponentially by some factor due to large pre-activations $\tilde{i}_t$ or $\tilde{f}_t$, this scaling factor appears identically in every term of both the numerator ($c_t$) and the denominator ($n_t$). Consequently, when divided, these large common factors cancel out, leaving $\tilde{h}_t$ dependent primarily on the relative proportions of the inputs $z_t$ rather than their absolute accumulated magnitude. This ensures that even if individual gate values become huge, their ratio remains stable, preventing overflow in the final output.

The paper further notes that while this division handles the logical normalization, practical implementation still requires a "stabilizer state" $m_t$ (Equation 5) to prevent intermediate floating-point overflow during the calculation of $f_t$ and $i_t$ before they are used in the recurrence. This technique, akin to log-sum-exp tricks, shifts the exponents by a maximum value to keep numbers within representable ranges without altering the final mathematical result of the division $c_t / n_t$. Thus, the combination of the structurally identical normalizer recurrence and explicit log-space stabilization allows sLSTM to leverage the expressivity of unbounded exponential gates while maintaining numerical robustness.

### What is the precise role of the stabilizer state $m_t$ in the sLSTM forward pass?
Question:
Equation 5 in the paper defines a stabilizer state $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$. The authors then define stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ by subtracting $m_t$ from the log-values before re-exponentiating. The paper claims this doesn't change the output because of the division by $n_t$. However, I'm struggling to see the algebraic cancellation. If we replace $i_t$ with $i'_t$ and $f_t$ with $f'_t$ in the cell update equation $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, doesn't that change the magnitude of $c_t$? How exactly does the interaction between the stabilizer $m_t$ and the normalizer $n_t$ ensure mathematical equivalence to the unstabilized version?
Answer:
The apparent contradiction arises from overlooking the fact that the stabilizer $m_t$ is simultaneously applied to the **normalizer state** $n_t$, creating a perfect algebraic cancellation when the final division occurs. While substituting the stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ directly into the cell state equation $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ would indeed alter the magnitude of $c_t$, the sLSTM architecture does not output $c_t$ directly. Instead, the hidden state is defined as the ratio $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. The stabilization technique ensures that both the numerator ($c_t$) and the denominator ($n_t$) are scaled by the exact same factor, leaving their quotient unchanged.

To see this explicitly, let us trace the scaling factor through both recursive equations. The stabilizer $m_t$ is defined as $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$. When we construct the stabilized gates, we effectively multiply the original gates by a decay factor determined by the difference in stabilizer states. Specifically, $i'_t = i_t e^{-m_t}$ and $f'_t = f_t e^{m_{t-1} - m_t}$. If we propagate these stabilized gates through the cell state recursion starting from $c_0=0$, every term in the expanded sum for $c_t$ accumulates a factor of $e^{-m_t}$. For instance, a contribution from an input at time step $k$ involves a product of forget gates from $k+1$ to $t$ and one input gate at $k$. In the stabilized version, the product of forget gates contributes a term $e^{m_k - m_{k+1}} \dots e^{m_{t-1} - m_t}$, which telescopes to $e^{m_k - m_t}$. Combined with the stabilized input gate factor $e^{-m_k}$, the total scaling for that specific term becomes $e^{-m_t}$. Consequently, the entire computed cell state using stabilized gates, denoted as $c'_t$, relates to the original unstabilized state by $c'_t = c_t e^{-m_t}$.

Crucially, this exact same scaling logic applies to the normalizer state $n_t$, which follows an identical recurrence structure: $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. Because $n_t$ relies on the same sequence of multiplications by $f$ and additions of $i$, substituting $f'$ and $i'$ results in a stabilized normalizer $n'_t$ that is also scaled by precisely $e^{-m_t}$ relative to the original, meaning $n'_t = n_t e^{-m_t}$. When calculating the final hidden state representation $\tilde{h}'_t$, we divide the stabilized cell state by the stabilized normalizer:
$$ \tilde{h}'_t = \frac{c'_t}{n'_t} = \frac{c_t e^{-m_t}}{n_n e^{-m_n}} $$

### Why is mLSTM fully parallelizable while sLSTM is not, despite both being recurrent?
Question:
The abstract states that mLSTM is 'fully parallelizable' while sLSTM suffers from a 'lack of parallelizability due to memory mixing.' Looking at the equations, both seem to depend on previous time steps ($t-1$). For mLSTM, we have $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. For sLSTM, we have similar recurrence. The paper mentions sLSTM has 'hidden-hidden connections' ($r_z h_{t-1}$ etc.) which mLSTM lacks (using only input-dependent gates for some parts?). Can someone clarify specifically which term in the recurrence relation breaks parallelization? Is it solely the presence of the recurrent weight matrices acting on $h_{t-1}$ in sLSTM, or does the matrix nature of $\mathbf{C}_t$ in mLSTM allow for a parallel scan algorithm (like in Mamba/SSMs) that isn't possible with scalar mixing?
Answer:
The fundamental difference lies not in the recurrence of the memory cell itself, but in whether the **gates and inputs depend on the previous hidden state** ($h_{t-1}$). While both sLSTM and mLSTM update a state variable based on $t-1$, parallelization is broken specifically by **memory mixing**, which the paper defines as "hidden-hidden connections" where recurrent weight matrices act on $h_{t-1}$ to compute the current gates or inputs.

In the standard LSTM and the proposed **sLSTM**, the computation of the cell input $z_t$ and the gates ($i_t, f_t, o_t$) explicitly includes a term like $r_z h_{t-1}$ (or $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ for multiple cells). As shown in Equation 5 of the paper, $\tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{z} \mathbf{x}_t + r_{z} h_{t-1} + b_{z}$. Because the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ is derived from the cell state $c_{t-1}$ (via $h_{t-1} = o_{t-1} \psi(c_{t-1}/n_{t-1})$), you cannot compute the gates for time step $t$ without knowing the final output of time step $t-1$. This creates a strict serial dependency chain: to get $h_t$, you need $c_t$; to get $c_t$, you need $f_t$ and $i_t$; but to get those gates, you need $h_{t-1}$. This circular dependency prevents computing multiple time steps simultaneously, regardless of whether the memory is scalar or matrix-valued.

In contrast, **mLSTM** achieves full parallelizability by completely removing these hidden-hidden connections. If you examine Equations 8 through 14 in the mLSTM section, you will notice that $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, $\mathbf{q}_t$, $\mathbf{k}_t$, and $\mathbf{v}_t$ depend **only** on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and learned biases/weights (e.g., $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_{i} \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$). There is no $+ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ term. Consequently, all gates and key/value/query vectors for the entire sequence can be computed in parallel in a single pass, as they do not rely on any previous temporal state. Once these values are pre-computed, the remaining recurrence—updating the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$—becomes a linear recurrence with known coefficients. This specific form allows the use of **parallel scan algorithms** (similar to those used in Mamba or associative scans), which can resolve the cumulative product and sum operations across time steps in logarithmic time ($O(\log T)$) rather than linear time ($O(T)$), effectively bypassing the sequential bottleneck.

Therefore, it is not the matrix nature of $\mathbf{C}_t$ alone that enables parallelization, nor is it strictly impossible to parallelize scalar recurrences. The dealbreaker is the **data dependency**: sLSTM mixes information from $h_{t-1}$ into the calculation of the update rules themselves, forcing sequential execution, whereas mLSTM decouples the gate/input calculation from the history, allowing the recurrence to be solved via parallel prefix scans. The paper explicitly notes in Section 2.4 that "mLSTM is parallelizable... [while] sLSTM is not parallelizable due to the memory mixing (hidden-hidden connections)."

### How does 'memory mixing' in sLSTM enable solving state tracking tasks that Transformers cannot?
Question:
The paper claims that sLSTM's 'memory mixing' allows it to solve state tracking problems (like parity checks or formal languages) where Transformers and State Space Models fail. The text says: 'Memory mixing enables to solve state tracking problems... LSTMs are more expressive than State Space Models.' I understand that standard LSTMs mix information via recurrent hidden states, but how does this specifically translate to higher expressivity for formal languages? Does the ability to mix different cell states via matrices $\mathbf{R}_z, \mathbf{R}_i$, etc., allow sLSTM to simulate a finite automaton or counter machine in a way that attention mechanisms (which are content-based lookup) inherently cannot?
Answer:
The ability of sLSTM to solve state tracking tasks where Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) fail stems directly from its **memory mixing** mechanism, which allows the model to maintain and update a complex internal state that effectively simulates a finite automaton or counter machine. As noted in the "Most Related" section of the paper, models lacking memory mixing (such as Transformers and SSMs) are fundamentally less expressive than Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) for specific formal language tasks like parity checks or regular grammar recognition.

In standard Transformers and many SSMs, the mechanism for retrieving information is primarily content-based or relies on linear recurrences that do not allow different dimensions of the hidden state to interact dynamically at every time step. For instance, in a parity task (determining if the number of 1s in a sequence is even or odd), the model must strictly toggle its internal state between two distinct configurations upon seeing a specific token. Content-based attention looks up information based on similarity, which is ill-suited for this kind of rigid, algorithmic state transition. Similarly, diagonal SSMs often process dimensions independently or with fixed coupling, preventing the flexible recombination of state features required to track abstract counters or stack depths.

The sLSTM overcomes this limitation through **memory mixing**, defined in the paper as the use of recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{z}, \mathbf{R}_{i}, \mathbf{R}_{f}, \mathbf{R}_{o}$ that connect the previous hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the current cell inputs and gates. Unlike models where the hidden state is merely passed forward or scaled, these matrices allow the sLSTM to linearly mix the values across different memory cells at every time step before applying non-linear gating. Mathematically, terms like $r_{i} h_{t-1}$ in the gate equations (where $r$ represents the recurrent weights) enable the network to compute arbitrary linear combinations of its past history. When combined with the expressive power of exponential gating and the non-linearity of the sigmoid/tanh activations, this mixing allows the sLSTM to implement complex transition functions $\delta: Q \times \Sigma \rightarrow Q$ characteristic of finite automata.

Essentially, memory mixing grants the sLSTM the capacity to revise and reconfigure its entire internal representation dynamically, rather than just appending new information or decaying old information independently per channel. This structural capability aligns with theoretical findings cited in the paper (Merrill et al., Deletang et al.) showing that RNNs with such recurrent connectivity possess higher expressivity for state tracking than attention mechanisms or linear SSMs. While mLSTM sacrifices this specific mixing capability to gain full parallelizability via a matrix memory covariance update, sLSTM retains these hidden-hidden connections specifically to handle tasks requiring precise algorithmic state maintenance, making it uniquely capable among xLSTM variants for solving formal language problems that demand strict state tracking.

### In mLSTM, why is retrieving memory via $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ superior to standard associative recall?
Question:
In Section 3.3, mLSTM stores information using a covariance update rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Retrieval is done by multiplying this matrix by a query vector: $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$. This looks like storing outer products and retrieving via dot products. How does this differ conceptually from the 'Retention' mechanism or linear attention? Specifically, why does expanding the memory to a full $d \times d$ matrix provide significantly better capacity for 'Rare Token Prediction' compared to a standard vector-based LSTM cell or even multi-head attention? Is it purely because we aren't compressing multiple key-value pairs into a single scalar or vector summary?
Answer:
The superiority of the mLSTM retrieval mechanism $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ over standard associative recall or vector-based LSTMs stems fundamentally from the expansion of the memory state from a scalar or vector to a full $d \times d$ matrix, which drastically increases storage capacity and prevents the destructive compression of information. In a traditional LSTM, the cell state $c_t$ is a scalar (or a vector in multi-cell variants), meaning that every new piece of information $z_t$ must be compressed into this limited space alongside all previous history. As noted in the Introduction, this leads to "limited storage capacities," causing the model to struggle with tasks like Rare Token Prediction because distinct key-value pairs interfere with one another when forced into a low-dimensional summary. The mLSTM addresses this by utilizing a covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, which stores information as an outer product of keys and values.

Conceptually, this differs from standard linear attention or retention mechanisms because the mLSTM explicitly treats the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$ as a high-capacity associative memory bank akin to Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs), rather than just a running sum of attended values. When you retrieve via $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$, you are effectively computing a weighted sum of all stored values $\mathbf{v}_\tau$, where the weights are determined by the similarity between the current query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and all past keys $\mathbf{k}_\tau$ accumulated within the matrix structure. While linear attention also approximates this operation, the mLSTM integrates it within the specific gating framework of an LSTM (with exponential input gates and stabilizers) and, crucially, operates in a higher-dimensional space facilitated by the pre-up-projection block architecture described in Section 3.4. By mapping inputs to a high-dimensional space before applying the matrix memory, the model leverages Cover's Theorem, which suggests that patterns become linearly separable in higher dimensions. This separation allows the $d \times d$ matrix to store many more orthogonal key-value pairs without them collapsing into noise, a feat impossible for a scalar or narrow vector state.

The significant gain in "Rare Token Prediction" performance is therefore not merely due to avoiding scalar compression, but because the matrix memory allows for maximal separability of retrieved vectors. The paper cites that the covariance update rule is optimal for maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio when retrieving binary vectors, allowing the model to distinguish between rare, infrequent tokens that might otherwise be overwritten or blurred in a standard LSTM's constant error carousel. Essentially, while a standard LSTM or even some linear RNNs must overwrite or average out old information to make room for new inputs within a fixed vector size, the mLSTM's matrix structure provides enough degrees of freedom ($d^2$ vs $d$) to retain distinct associations for a much larger vocabulary of keys, enabling precise retrieval even for tokens that appear rarely in the training sequence.

### Why does xLSTM[7:1] outperform xLSTM[1:0] on downstream reasoning tasks despite mLSTM having higher memory capacity?
Question:
Table 3 and Table 4 show that while xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) often has lower perplexity on raw language modeling (especially on diverse domains like PALOMA), xLSTEM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM blocks) tends to perform better on downstream reasoning tasks (like commonsense QA). Given that mLSTM has massive matrix memory capacity, why is adding just a few sLSTM blocks—which have smaller scalar memory but possess 'memory mixing'—so critical for reasoning? Does this suggest that 'state tracking' (enabled by sLSTM) is more important for logical reasoning than raw storage capacity (enabled by mLSTEM)?
Answer:
The observed performance gap between xLSTM[1:0] (pure mLSTM) and xLSTM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM with some sLSTM) on downstream reasoning tasks stems from a fundamental distinction between **storage capacity** and **state tracking expressivity**, which are handled differently by the two block types. While the mLSTM excels at raw information retention due to its matrix memory, it lacks the architectural mechanism required for complex logical manipulation, which the sLSTM provides through memory mixing.

The core advantage of the mLSTM lies in its **matrix memory** $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and the associated covariance update rule $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. As described in the paper, this structure dramatically increases storage capacity compared to traditional scalar LSTMs, allowing the model to store a vast number of key-value pairs effectively. This explains why xLSTM[1:0] achieves superior perplexity on broad language modeling benchmarks like PALOMA; it acts as an extremely efficient associative memory, capable of recalling rare tokens and maintaining context over long sequences simply by storing more information. However, to achieve full parallelizability, the mLSTM explicitly removes **memory mixing**, meaning there are no recurrent hidden-to-hidden connections ($\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \to \mathbf{h}_t$) that allow different memory cells to interact dynamically during the sequence processing.

In contrast, reasoning tasks such as Commonsense QA often require **state tracking**—the ability to actively manipulate, update, and revise internal representations based on evolving context, rather than just passively retrieving stored facts. The paper identifies that models without memory mixing (including Transformers and State Space Models) struggle with formal languages requiring state tracking, such as parity tasks or nested dependencies. The sLSTM retains **memory mixing** via recurrent weights connecting the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ to the gates and cell inputs. Crucially, when combined with the new **exponential gating**, this allows the sLSTM to robustly revise storage decisions. If a piece of information needs to be updated or contradicted later in the sequence (a common requirement in logical reasoning), the exponential gates allow the model to overwrite previous states much more effectively than standard sigmoid gates.

Therefore, the superiority of xLSTM[7:1] on reasoning tasks suggests that while raw capacity (provided by the dominant mLSTM blocks) is sufficient for next-token prediction statistics, successful reasoning requires the specific computational expressivity of state tracking (provided by the sparse sLSTM blocks). The sLSTM blocks act as specialized units that can perform logical operations and maintain coherent entity states through memory mixing, while the surrounding mLSTM blocks provide the massive contextual bandwidth needed to hold the necessary facts. The addition of even a few sLSTM blocks injects this missing algorithmic capability into the architecture, enabling it to solve problems that pure storage capacity alone cannot address.

### What is the intuition behind using Pre-LayerNorm for mLSTEM blocks vs Post-LayerNorm for sLSTEM blocks?
Question:
In Section 3.4 ('xLSTMArchitecture'), the authors state: 'For an xLSTMEM block containing an sLTSEM, we use post up-projection block... For an xLTSEM block containing an mLTSEM, we use pre up-projection block since memory capacity becomes larger in high-dimensional space.' I am confused by this design choice. Usually, Pre-LN helps with training stability in deep networks. Why specifically does mapping to high-dimensional space *before* applying mLTSEM improve its performance? Is it because key/value/query projections need more dimensions to be effective before entering matrix-memory updates? Conversely, why does LTSEM prefer operating closer to residual stream dimensionality?
Answer:
The design choice to use **Pre-LayerNorm** (specifically described in the paper as a "pre up-projection block") for mLSTM blocks versus **Post-LayerNorm** ("post up-projection block") for sLSTM blocks stems directly from the fundamental difference in their memory capacities and the geometric properties of high-dimensional spaces, as articulated in Section 3.4 under "xLSTM Blocks."

The core intuition relies on **Cover's Theorem**, which the authors explicitly cite. This theorem posits that patterns which are not linearly separable in a low-dimensional space often become linearly separable when projected non-linearly into a sufficiently high-dimensional space. The goal of the xLSTM block is to "non-linearly summarize the past" to distinguish between different historical contexts effectively.

For the **mLSTM**, the memory structure is a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ updated via a covariance rule ($\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$). The paper argues that the "memory capacity becomes larger in high-dimensional space." By employing a pre up-projection architecture, the input is first linearly mapped into a higher-dimensional space *before* entering the recurrent mLSTM unit. This allows the matrix memory to operate on richer, higher-dimensional representations of keys and values. Since the storage capacity of this associative memory scales with the dimensionality of these vectors, expanding the dimension prior to the memory update maximizes the model's ability to store and retrieve distinct key-value pairs without interference. Essentially, you want the massive storage potential of the matrix memory to interact with the data while it is in its most expansive form.

Conversely, the **sLSTM** utilizes a scalar memory cell (or a vector of scalar cells) with explicit memory mixing via hidden-to-hidden recurrent connections. Its primary strength, as highlighted in the synthetic task experiments, is solving state-tracking problems rather than maximizing raw storage density through dimensionality. The authors choose a post up-projection block for sLSTM, mirroring standard Transformer feed-forward networks. In this configuration, the recurrent unit operates on the residual stream dimensionality (the "original space"), summarizing the past non-linearly within that constrained scope before projecting up for further processing. This suggests that for sLSTM, the complex dynamics of exponential gating and memory mixing are sufficient to separate histories without needing the auxiliary boost of operating inside an expanded high-dimensional manifold during the recurrence itself.

Therefore, the distinction is functional: mLSTM leverages high-dimensional geometry to maximize its matrix-based storage capacity (requiring Pre-LN/pre-projection), whereas sLSTM relies on its sophisticated gating and mixing mechanisms within the standard representation space to handle state tracking (utilizing Post-LN/post-projection).
