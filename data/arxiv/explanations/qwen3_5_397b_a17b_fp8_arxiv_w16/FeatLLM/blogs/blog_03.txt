\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

# The Power of Priors: Why Your Spreadsheet Needs Common Sense More Than Big Data

In the world of machine learning, data is often king. We are taught that bigger datasets lead to better models, and that with enough examples, an algorithm can learn anything. But what happens when you don't have thousands of labeled rows? What if you are working in healthcare, where patient records are private and scarce, or in finance, where labeling a single transaction requires hours of expert review? In these "few-shot" scenarios, traditional algorithms often struggle, latching onto random noise rather than genuine patterns.

A new framework called \textsf{FeatLLM} proposes a radical shift in how we handle tabular data under these constraints. Instead of forcing a model to learn everything from scratch using limited data, \textsf{FeatLLM} injects common sense directly into the feature engineering process. By leveraging the vast prior knowledge stored in Large Language Models (LLMs), this approach allows spreadsheets to "understand" the context of their columns, filtering out spurious correlations and identifying meaningful rules that standard algorithms miss.

## The Limitation of Pure Data-Driven Learning

Traditional tabular learning methods, such as Random Forests or XGBoost, are powerful but fundamentally blind to semantics. To these models, a column named "age" and a column named "random_id_42" are mathematically identical; they are just vectors of numbers. When training data is abundant, the sheer volume of examples helps the model distinguish signal from noise. However, in few-shot regimes—where we might only have 4 or 8 labeled examples per class—these models are prone to overfitting. They easily memorize coincidental patterns, known as spurious correlations, that do not generalize to new data.

For instance, imagine a tiny dataset predicting heart disease where every patient in the training set happens to live on "Maple Street." A traditional algorithm might incorrectly conclude that living on Maple Street causes heart disease simply because it perfectly predicts the training labels. Without external knowledge to contradict this, the model fails miserably on new data from "Oak Avenue."

## Injecting Common Sense via Feature Engineering

\textsf{FeatLLM} addresses this vulnerability by changing the role of the LLM. Rather than using an LLM to predict the final outcome for every single row—a computationally expensive process that hits token limits on large tables—\textsf{FeatLLM} uses the LLM as an expert feature engineer. The system asks the LLM to act as a domain expert who understands the relationships between features and targets based on general world knowledge.

The process begins by presenting the LLM with feature names, definitions, and a handful of examples. The framework guides the model through a structured reasoning path. First, it asks the LLM to infer causal relationships based purely on prior knowledge, ignoring the specific training examples initially. For a medical task, the LLM knows inherently that advanced age correlates with higher disease risk, regardless of what the tiny training set says. Second, it combines this prior knowledge with the few available examples to derive explicit rules.

These rules are not vague suggestions; they are precise logical conditions generated in natural language, such as "If age is greater than 65 AND cholesterol is above 240." The system then parses these rules into executable code to transform the original dataset into a new set of binary features. Each new feature represents whether a specific row satisfies a specific rule derived from common sense and data patterns.

## Filtering Noise and Spurious Correlations

The true power of this approach lies in its ability to ignore irrelevant data. Because the LLM grounds its rule generation in semantic understanding, it naturally filters out columns that make no logical sense for the task at hand. In experiments involving spurious correlations, researchers tested \textsf{FeatLLM} by attaching irrelevant columns from unrelated datasets to a heart disease prediction task. While baseline models saw their performance degrade significantly as more noise was added, \textsf{FeatLLM} remained robust.

This resilience occurs because the LLM refuses to generate rules based on nonsensical connections. When prompted to find criteria for heart failure, an LLM will not prioritize a random ID number or an unrelated demographic statistic if it contradicts medical logic. The resulting binary features are therefore highly informative by design. Experiments showed that even before any statistical tuning, the majority of generated rules had an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) score greater than 0.5 against the target labels, confirming their intrinsic relevance.

Furthermore, \textsf{FeatLLM} employs a simple downstream linear model to weigh these generated features. This step acts as a secondary filter; rules that happen to be slightly off-base receive near-zero weights during training on the few-shot data, while logically sound rules are amplified. This combination of semantic filtering by the LLM and statistical weighting by the linear model creates a defense mechanism against overfitting that pure data-driven methods lack.

## Efficiency and Real-World Applicability

Beyond accuracy, \textsf{FeatLLM} solves critical infrastructure problems associated with using LLMs for tabular data. Many existing approaches require sending every single test sample through an LLM API for inference. This is slow, expensive, and often impossible when tables contain hundreds of columns that exceed the model's context window limit.
