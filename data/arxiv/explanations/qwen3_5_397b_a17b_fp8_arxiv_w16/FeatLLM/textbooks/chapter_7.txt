\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 7: Chapter 7: Experimental Evaluation and Results Analysis

The empirical validation of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework serves as the critical bridge between theoretical design and practical utility. To rigorously assess the efficacy of using Large Language Models (LLMs) as automated feature engineers, the authors conducted a comprehensive series of experiments spanning thirteen distinct tabular datasets. These evaluations were designed not only to measure raw predictive accuracy but also to probe the framework's robustness, computational efficiency, and scalability in low-data regimes. The experimental setup contrasts \textsf{FeatLLM} against a diverse array of nine baseline methods, ranging from classical statistical models to state-of-the-art deep learning and LLM-based approaches. By dissecting the results across various dimensions—including dataset characteristics, shot counts, and computational overhead—this chapter elucidates why \textsf{FeatLLM} represents a significant advancement in few-shot tabular learning, particularly in its ability to leverage prior knowledge while maintaining the inference speed of traditional machine learning models.

# Dataset diversity:Benchmarking on real-world,synthetic,and temporal-shifted data

A cornerstone of the experimental evaluation is the deliberate selection of a heterogeneous collection of thirteen datasets, ensuring that the findings are not artifacts of specific data distributions or domains familiar to the LLM during its pre-training phase. The benchmark suite includes well-established real-world datasets covering a wide spectrum of applications. For instance, the "Adult" dataset is utilized for predicting annual income levels exceeding \$50,000, while the "Bank" dataset focuses on term deposit subscription predictions. Medical and health-related tasks are represented by datasets such as "Blood," which predicts donor return rates, "Diabetes" for disease presence, "Heart" for coronary artery disease, and "Myocardial" for chronic heart failure complications. Other domains include automotive quality assessment ("Car"), regional crime rate prediction ("Communities"), and credit risk evaluation ("Credit-g"). These datasets vary significantly in complexity; notably, "Communities" and "Myocardial" contain over 100 attributes each, presenting a stringent test for methods constrained by context window limits.

Crucially, to verify that the performance gains stem from genuine reasoning capabilities rather than memorization of pre-training data, the authors incorporated datasets that are temporally shifted or entirely synthetic. Two recent additions to the UCI Machine Learning Repository, "Cultivars" (predicting soybean grain yield) and "NHANES" (predicting age groups), were donated after September 2023. Since the LLM backbone used in the study, GPT-3.5-turbo-0613, was trained on data preceding this date, these datasets effectively serve as out-of-distribution tests that the model could not have encountered during pre-training. Furthermore, two synthetic datasets, "Sequence-type" and "Solution-mix," were constructed specifically to eliminate any possibility of memorization. "Sequence-type" involves classifying numerical sequences into arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, or Collatz types, while "Solution-mix" requires predicting whether a mixture concentration exceeds a specific threshold. This rigorous curation ensures that any success achieved by \textsf{FeatLLM} is attributable to its ability to synthesize rules from provided feature descriptions and few-shot examples rather than recalling latent patterns from its training corpus.

# Performance comparison:AUC metrics against LogReg,XGBoost,and LLM-baselines

The primary metric for evaluating predictive performance across these diverse tasks is the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), averaged over three independent runs with different random splits of training and test sets. \textsf{FeatLLM} was pitted against nine baselines categorized into three groups: conventional tabular learners, methods leveraging unlabeled data or synthetic pre-training, and existing LLM-based approaches. The conventional baselines included Logistic Regression (LogReg), XGBoost, and Random Forest, representing standard industry practices. Methods utilizing auxiliary information included SCARF and STUNT, which rely on large quantities of unlabeled data for semi-supervised learning—a condition often infeasible in real-world few-shot scenarios—and TabPFN, which uses synthetic data distributions for pre-training a Bayesian neural network. The LLM-based competitors comprised direct In-context learning, TABLET (which augments prompts with external classifier hints), and TabLLM (which employs parameter-efficient fine-tuning).

The results demonstrate that \textsf{FeatLLM} consistently achieves state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art performance across nearly all datasets. In head-to-head comparisons, \textsf{FeatLLM} frequently secured the top rank or placed second among all competing methods. While certain baselines exhibited sporadic strengths—for example, STUNT and TabPFN showed notable improvements on specific datasets—their aggregate performance uplift over conventional machine learning models was not substantial across the board. In contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} delivered consistent superiority. A critical distinction emerged regarding scalability with feature dimensionality: LLM-based baselines such as direct In-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM often failed completely when applied to datasets with a large number of features due to prompt length limitations or architectural constraints. \textsf{FeatLLM}, however, maintained satisfactory performance even on high-dimensional datasets like "Communities." This resilience is attributed to its integration of bagging and ensemble techniques during the feature generation phase, which allows it to circumvent context window bottlenecks that cripple end-to-end LLM prediction methods.

# The efficiency gap:Few-shot performance(4 shots)vs.traditional methods(32 shots)

One of the most compelling findings from the experimental analysis is the exceptional data efficiency exhibited by \textsf{FeatLLM}. The framework was evaluated in extreme low-shot regimes where only a handful of labeled examples are available for training. Specifically, experiments highlighted that \textsf{FeatLLM}, when operating with merely 4 labeled shots (examples), achieves predictive performance comparable to that of conventional tabular baselines trained on 32 shots. This eight-fold reduction in data requirements underscores the potency of injecting prior knowledge via LLM-generated rules. Traditional methods like Logistic Regression or XGBoost typically struggle to generalize effectively from such sparse data without overfitting to noise or spurious correlations. By contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} leverages the semantic understanding encoded in the LLM to construct meaningful features that capture causal relationships or strong tendencies inherent to the task domain, effectively regularizing the learning process even with minimal supervision.

This efficiency gap highlights a fundamental shift in how few-shot learning can be approached. Conventional few-shot strategies often rely on complex meta-learning architectures or massive amounts of unlabeled data to learn generalizable representations before adapting to a specific task. \textsf{FeatLLM} bypasses these requirements by utilizing the LLM's intrinsic world knowledge to act as an expert feature engineer. The ablation studies further reinforce this point: removing components that facilitate knowledge injection, such as feature descriptions or structured reasoning instructions caused significant performance drops in low-shot settings.
