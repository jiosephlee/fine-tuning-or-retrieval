To truly understand the efficacy of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework, it is insufficient to merely observe its aggregate performance metrics against baselines. One must dissect the internal mechanics of the system to isolate the specific contributions of its architectural components, prompt design elements, and hyperparameters. This chapter provides a rigorous diagnostic analysis of \textsf{FeatLLM}, moving beyond the "black box" perception often associated with Large Language Model (LLM) applications. By systematically removing or altering key components—a process known as ablation—we can quantify the value added by each stage of the pipeline. Furthermore, we examine the quality of the features generated by the LLM, assess the model's sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, and test its robustness against spurious correlations. These insights not only validate the design choices made in constructing \textsf{FeatLLM} but also offer guidance for practitioners aiming to deploy similar systems in resource-constrained, few-shot environments.

# Impact of Weight Tuning vs. Simple Counting Mechanisms

A central hypothesis in the design of \textsf{FeatLLM} is that not all rules generated by an LLM possess equal predictive power. While the LLM leverages vast prior knowledge to propose conditions that might distinguish between classes, some rules may be redundant, weak, or occasionally misleading when applied to a specific dataset. To address this, the framework employs a lightweight linear model with trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ to estimate class likelihoods, rather than relying on a naive aggregation of rule satisfactions. To evaluate the necessity of this tuning step, we conduct an ablation study designated as "\textsf{-Tuning}," wherein the weight optimization process is omitted. In this variant, the logit for a given class is computed simply by summing the binary features—effectively counting how many rules a sample satisfies—without assigning differential importance to individual rules.

The results of this ablation reveal that removing weight tuning leads to a consistent degradation in performance across all tested datasets. This finding underscores that while the LLM is adept at generating plausible rules, it cannot perfectly calibrate their relative significance for every specific task without observing data. The trainable weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ serve as a critical mechanism for filtering noise and emphasizing high-signal features. Interestingly, the magnitude of performance loss attributed to omitting tuning varies depending on the number of available shots. As the number of labeled training samples increases, the benefit of weight tuning becomes more pronounced. With more data, the linear model can more accurately estimate the true importance of each rule, distinguishing between those that are genuinely predictive and those that are coincidental. Conversely, in extremely low-shot regimes where data is scarce, the advantage of tuning is still present but slightly less dominant compared to other factors like prompt quality. This dynamic illustrates that \textsf{FeatLLM} effectively bridges the gap between static prior knowledge and data-driven adaptation; the LLM provides the candidate features, but the simple downstream learner refines their utility based on empirical evidence.

# The Necessity of Ensemble Methods for Robustness

Another pivotal component of \textsf{FeatLLM} is its reliance on ensemble methods combined with feature bagging. Given the stochastic nature of LLM decoding, a single query may yield a suboptimal set of rules due to sampling variance or momentary lapses in reasoning consistency. To mitigate this, \textsf{FeatLLM} repeats the feature generation process multiple times ($T$ trials), introducing randomness via temperature settings and shuffling the order of few-shot demonstrations. These diverse sets of rules are then aggregated through an ensemble predictor. We evaluate the criticality of this approach through an ablation labeled "\textsf{-Ensemble}," which restricts the framework to a single trial without bagging or averaging.

The removal of the ensemble mechanism results in a noticeable drop in Area Under Curve (AUC) scores across all experimental settings. This degradation confirms that ensembling is not merely a marginal improvement but a structural necessity for achieving state-of-the-art robustness. The ensemble approach leverages the principle of self-consistency: rules that are inferred correctly tend to appear across multiple independent trials, whereas erroneous or hallucinated rules are likely to be idiosyncratic to a single run. By averaging predictions over $T$ trials, \textsf{FeatLLM} amplifies the signal from consistent, high-quality rules while canceling out the noise from outlier generations. Furthermore, ensembling plays a dual role in overcoming technical limitations regarding context window sizes. Through feature bagging, where subsets of features or instances are selected for each trial, the framework can handle datasets with hundreds of attributes that would otherwise exceed the LLM's input token limits. A single monolithic attempt to process all features might fail or suffer from attention dilution, but an ensemble of models trained on manageable subsets ensures comprehensive coverage of the feature space without violating prompt constraints. Thus, ensembling transforms potential weaknesses in individual LLM outputs into a collective strength, stabilizing performance even when individual rule sets are imperfect.

# Value of Feature Descriptions and Reasoning Instructions in Few-Shot Settings

The prompt engineering strategy within \textsf{FeatLLM} is meticulously structured into three parts: basic information description, reasoning instructions, and response instructions. Two specific elements within this structure warrant close examination: the inclusion of natural language feature descriptions and the two-step reasoning instruction protocol. We isolate their contributions through two distinct ablations: "\textsf{-Description}," which removes definitions and value type explanations for features, and "\textsf{-Reasoning}," which omits Step 1 ofthe reasoning process wherethe LLM infers causal relationships before seeing examples.

The absence of feature descriptions leads to significant performance drops, particularly when working with very few shots (e.g., 2 or 4 samples).
