\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to LLMs in Tabular Learning

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as transformative tools in artificial intelligence, demonstrating a remarkable capacity to solve complex reasoning problems that they have never encountered before. Trained on vast and diverse text corpora, these models possess rich prior knowledge that can effectively substitute for expert domain expertise in various fields. This capability allows LLMs to automate tasks ranging from dialogue analysis to program repair without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. By simply incorporating a task description and a few examples into a prompt, LLMs can understand the context of a problem and attend to critical features, showcasing an ability to analyze data that suggests they can function as expert feature engineers. While initially celebrated for their prowess in natural language processing, the application of LLMs has expanded into the realm of tabular learning, where structured data is paramount for real-world decision-making in sectors like healthcare and finance.

The integration of LLMs into tabular learning leverages their inherent world knowledge to improve predictions, particularly in scenarios where data is scarce. For instance, an LLM's internal knowledge regarding the correlation between advanced age and increased disease risk can be directly applied to medical diagnosis tasks without explicit training on that specific dataset. Traditional approaches to this problem involve defining task and feature descriptions in natural language, serializing the tabular data into text formats, and feeding this information directly into the LLM. These methods often rely on in-context learning or parameter-efficient fine-tuning to adapt the model to the specific tabular task. While these strategies have proven effective in low-shot regimes—outperforming conventional baselines by utilizing the LLM's priors—they fundamentally treat the LLM as an end-to-end predictor, querying the model for every single inference.

Despite the promise of these early approaches, significant barriers remain that hinder their deployment in practical, industrial settings. The primary limitation is computational inefficiency; end-to-end prediction requires at least one LLM inference call per sample, making the process prohibitively expensive and slow for large-scale applications. Furthermore, many high-performing modern LLMs are only accessible via inference APIs, lacking the open-source codebases necessary for fine-tuning, which renders parameter-efficient adaptation methods infeasible for these powerful models. Additionally, tabular datasets often contain a large number of features, leading to serialized text lengths that exceed the context window limits of current LLMs. When prompts become too lengthy due to extensive feature lists, performance degrades or the approach becomes entirely unworkable. These constraints highlight a critical need for a new paradigm that retains the benefits of LLM reasoning while eliminating the bottlenecks of direct inference.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed shifting the role of the LLM from a direct predictor to an automated feature engineer. This paradigm shift is embodied in the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework, which posits that LLMs are best utilized not to output final class predictions for every row of data, but rather to infer the underlying "criteria" or rules that govern the relationship between features and targets. In this view, the LLM acts as an expert analyst that examines a few examples and generates a set of logical rules—for example, determining exactly which combinations of patient vitals indicate a high risk of heart failure. These rules are then translated into new binary features that indicate whether a given data sample satisfies specific conditions. Once these high-quality features are engineered by the LLM, a simple, lightweight downstream machine learning model, such as linear regression, can be trained to estimate class likelihoods.

The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework represents a novel in-context learning approach designed specifically to overcome the latency, cost, and context limitations of previous methods. By decoupling feature generation from inference, \textsf{FeatLLM} eliminates the need to query the LLM for every test sample; instead, the LLM is queried only during the initial feature engineering phase to discover robust rules. The resulting system relies solely on a simple predictive model at inference time, drastically reducing latency and computational cost. Moreover, because the heavy lifting of reasoning is confined to rule extraction rather than processing full datasets at once, \textsf{FeatLLM} can employ techniques like feature bagging to handle datasets with hundreds of columns, effectively bypassing prompt size limitations. Empirical evaluations across thirteen diverse tabular datasets demonstrate that this approach not only resolves practical deployment issues but also achieves superior accuracy, outperforming existing LLM-based baselines by an average of 10\% in few-shot settings.

## The rise of Large Language Models and their reasoning capabilities

The advent of Large Language Models marks a significant milestone in machine learning, characterized by their ability to generalize to unseen tasks without explicit retraining. Unlike traditional models that require massive amounts of labeled data specific to a target domain, LLMs leverage patterns learned from extensive pre-training on internet-scale text corpora. This exposure grants them a form of "common sense" or prior knowledge about the world, encompassing facts about biology economics social dynamics and more Crucially this knowledge allows them to perform reasoning tasks where they must connect disparate pieces of information to reach a conclusion When presented with a new problem through a prompt containing instructions and examples known as in-context learning LLMs can dynamically adjust their attention mechanisms to focus on relevant aspects of the input mirroring how a human expert might approach a novel challenge

This reasoning capability is particularly potent when applied to domains requiring synthesis of background knowledge with specific observations In scientific and professional fields such as genomics or clinical medicine LLMs have shown they can interpret complex scenarios by drawing upon their latent understanding of cause-and-effect relationships For example an LLM can infer that certain symptoms combined with demographic factors likely point to a specific pathology even if it has not seen that exact combination during training This ability stems from the model's architecture which facilitates deep contextual understanding allowing it to weigh evidence and deduce outcomes based on logical consistency rather than mere statistical co-occurrence in a narrow dataset Consequently researchers have begun exploring how these reasoning faculties can be harnessed beyond text generation specifically targeting structured data analysis where domain expertise is traditionally required to identify meaningful patterns

In the context of data analysis the reasoning power of LLMs enables them to act as interpreters between raw numbers and semantic meaning Tabular data often lacks explicit context column headers may be abbreviated and relationships between variables are implicit An LLM however can read feature names and definitions understand their types such as categorical versus numerical and hypothesize about their potential influence on a target variable By framing the analysis task in natural language users can guide the LLM to simulate an expert's thought process asking it to consider why certain features matter or how they interact This transforms the LLM from a passive pattern matcher into an active reasoner capable of generating hypotheses about data structure which is precisely what is needed for effective feature engineering in low-data regimes
