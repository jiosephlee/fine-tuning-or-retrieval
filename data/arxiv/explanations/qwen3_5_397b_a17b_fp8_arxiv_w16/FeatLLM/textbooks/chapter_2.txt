\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 2: Chapter 2: Foundations and Related Work

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has fundamentally altered the landscape of machine learning, offering unprecedented capabilities in reasoning and generalization across unseen tasks. However, applying these models to tabular data—a staple of real-world applications in finance, healthcare, and industry—presents unique challenges distinct from text or image domains. To fully appreciate the innovation behind the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework, one must first understand the evolutionary trajectory of few-shot learning for tabular data. This chapter establishes the theoretical groundwork by examining traditional strategies that rely on data augmentation and semi-supervised objectives, before transitioning to more advanced transfer learning paradigms that leverage pre-training on diverse datasets. We then delve into the emergence of language-interfaced tabular learning, where LLMs are tasked with interpreting serialized table data. A critical distinction is drawn between parameter-efficient fine-tuning and in-context learning, highlighting the operational constraints of current methodologies. Finally, we provide a rigorous analysis of existing baselines such as TabLLM, STUNT, and TabPFN, elucidating the specific limitations regarding computational cost, API accessibility, and prompt size that motivate the feature-engineering approach proposed in this work.

# Traditional few-shot learning strategies: Semi-supervised learning and data augmentation

Few-shot learning aims to extract generalizable representations from data even when labeled examples are scarce, a scenario that inherently risks the model learning spurious correlations rather than true causal relationships. In the context of tabular data, early attempts to mitigate this risk focused heavily on leveraging large quantities of unlabeled data through semi-supervised learning techniques. The core intuition here is that while labeled data may be expensive or difficult to obtain, unlabeled records are often abundant. Methods such as VIME and SubTab operate under the premise that by imposing consistency constraints or reconstructing masked portions of the input data, a model can learn robust latent representations of the feature space without needing explicit labels. For instance, some approaches involve masking or corrupting parts of the tabular entries and training the model to reconstruct the original values, effectively forcing the network to understand the underlying statistical structure of the columns. Others employ contrastive learning objectives, where data augmentation techniques are used to create positive pairs of similar instances, pushing their representations closer together in the embedding space while distancing dissimilar ones.

Despite the theoretical appeal of these unsupervised objectives, their practical efficacy in few-shot tabular settings has been limited. The primary obstacle lies in the heterogeneous nature of tabular data itself. Unlike images, where transformations like rotation or cropping preserve semantic meaning, or text where synonym replacement maintains context, tabular features vary wildly in type and distribution. A numerical column representing age cannot be augmented in the same way as a categorical column representing city names without violating physical or logical constraints. Consequently, finding universally applicable augmentation strategies that do not introduce noise or distort the data distribution proves exceptionally difficult. Empirical evidence suggests that while these semi-supervised and augmentation-based strategies show promise in data-rich environments, they fail to deliver notable performance improvements when restricted to the low-shot regimes where labeled data is minimal. The inability to define safe and meaningful augmentations often leads to models that overfit to the few available labels or learn artifacts of the augmentation process rather than genuine predictive signals.

# Transfer learning in tabular domains: Pre-training on diverse datasets

Recognizing the limitations of augmentation within a single dataset, research shifted toward transfer learning approaches that seek to imbue models with broader structural knowledge by pre-training on a vast array of diverse tabular datasets. The hypothesis driving this direction is that by exposing a model to thousands of tables with varying schemas and domains during a pre-training phase, it can learn universal semantic relationships between column names and values that generalize to new, unseen tasks. A prominent example of this philosophy is TransTab, which utilizes transformer-based architectures trained not just on cell values but explicitly on the semantic relationships implied by column headers. By learning how different types of columns interact across disparate contexts—for example, understanding how "income" relates to "loan approval" in one dataset and "credit score" in another—the model acquires an inductive bias that facilitates zero-shot or few-shot inference on target tasks without requiring task-specific retraining from scratch.

Another sophisticated iteration of this paradigm is TabPFN, which takes a Bayesian approach to pre-training. Instead of training on real-world datasets alone, TabPFN generates massive amounts of synthetic data by mixing different types of distributions and functional relationships. This synthetic pre-training allows the model to internalize a prior over possible tabular functions essentially teaching it how to perform Bayesian inference directly during the forward pass. When presented with a new few-shot task, TabPFN processes both the training support set and the test samples simultaneously, outputting predictions based on its learned prior without any gradient-based updates or fine-tuning. These methods have demonstrated significant success, often surpassing traditional tree-based approaches like Random Forests or XGBoost in low-data regimes. However, while effective, these approaches still require specialized model architectures and substantial computational resources for pre-training, and they operate as black boxes that do not easily incorporate external natural language knowledge or allow for interpretable rule extraction.

# Language-Interfaced Tabular Learning: Serializing data and prompting strategies

The integration of Large Language Models into tabular learning marks a paradigm shift from purely numerical processing to language-interfaced reasoning. Since LLMs are natively designed to process sequences of tokens rather than structured matrices, a fundamental prerequisite for this approach is the serialization of tabular data into natural language text. This process involves converting rows and columns into descriptive sentences or structured prompts that an LLM can comprehend. Typical serialization formats describe each feature-value pair explicitly, such as stating "$f_1$ is $x_1$, $f_2$ is $x_2$," followed by a task description and potentially feature definitions derived from domain knowledge. By framing the prediction task as a natural language query—for instance asking "Does this patient's data indicate heart failure?"—researchers can leverage the vast prior knowledge encoded within LLMs during their pre-training on internet-scale corpora. This allows the model to apply common sense reasoning and world knowledge (e.g., knowing that older age correlates with higher disease risk) to inform its predictions, something traditional numerical models cannot do without explicit feature engineering.

Once serialized, there are two primary strategies for eliciting predictions from the LLM: in-context learning and fine-tuning. In-context learning relies on constructing prompts that include a task description alongside a few labeled examples (few-shot demonstrations). The LLM attends to these examples to infer patterns and generate predictions for new queries without any modification to its internal weights.
