# Foundations of Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a critical paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models (LLMs), enabling them to solve sophisticated problems in domains such as competitive mathematics and computer programming. As models grow in size and complexity, particularly with the adoption of architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), the stability and efficiency of the underlying RL algorithms become paramount. Before introducing advanced optimizations, it is essential to establish a rigorous understanding of the mathematical foundations governing autoregressive policies, reward structures, and the evolution from classic policy optimization methods to modern group-based approaches. This chapter delineates the formal notation used to describe language model interactions, explores the mechanics of reward verification, and provides a deep dive into Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Special attention is paid to the structural nuances of token-level importance sampling, which forms the basis for identifying limitations in current state-of-the-art algorithms when applied to massive-scale training.

# Mathematical Notation: Policies, Queries, Responses, and Likelihoods

To rigorously analyze reinforcement learning algorithms for language models, we must first define the probabilistic framework in which these agents operate. An autoregressive language model is formally treated as a stochastic policy parameterized by a vector $\theta$, denoted as $\pi_\theta$. This policy governs the probability distribution over sequences of tokens given an input prompt. We utilize $x$ to represent a specific query drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$. Upon receiving a query $x$, the policy generates a response $y$, which consists of a sequence of discrete tokens. The generative process is sequential; the probability of generating the entire response $y$ conditioned on the query $x$ is decomposed into the product of conditional probabilities for each token in the sequence. Mathematically, if $|y|$ denotes the total number of tokens in the response $y$, the likelihood of the response under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. Here, $y_t$ represents the token at position $t$, and $y_{<t}$ signifies all preceding tokens in the sequence up to step $t-1$. This formulation underscores the Markovian property inherent in autoregressive generation, where the prediction of the next token depends solely on the current context comprising the original query and all previously generated tokens.

The interaction between the agent and its environment is encapsulated within a query-response pair $(x, y)$. # The Stability Crisis: Diagnosing Failures in Token-Level Optimization

As reinforcement learning (RL) becomes the primary paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex mathematical proofs and generate sophisticated code, the stability of the underlying training algorithms has become the critical bottleneck. While earlier iterations of RL were successful on smaller scales, the transition to gigantic models with billions of parameters, sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, and extremely long reasoning chains has exposed fatal flaws in existing methodologies. Specifically, algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which rely on token-level optimization objectives, exhibit severe instability that often culminates in catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This chapter dissects the theoretical and practical roots of this crisis, demonstrating how the misapplication of importance sampling at the token level generates high-variance noise that accumulates destructively over time, rendering traditional approaches unsuitable for the next generation of AI systems.

### The Off-Policy Challenge in Large Batch Training

To understand the origin of instability in modern RL training, one must first appreciate the operational constraints imposed by the sheer scale of contemporary language models. As models grow in size and sparsity, and as the required response lengths extend to accommodate deep reasoning processes, maximizing hardware utilization demands enormous rollout batch sizes. It is computationally prohibitive to perform a gradient update immediately after generating every single batch of data. Consequently, standard practice involves collecting a vast amount of trajectory data from an older version of the policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, storing it, and then partitioning this data into multiple mini-batches for successive gradient updates targeting the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

This separation between data collection and optimization inherently creates an off-policy learning setting. The samples used to calculate gradients are drawn from a distribution that differs from the one currently being optimized. If unchecked, this distributional shift can lead to arbitrarily large updates that destabilize the model. Historically, algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its successor GRPO have relied on clipping mechanisms to mitigate this risk. By restricting the influence of samples where the probability ratio between the new and old policies exceeds a certain threshold $\varepsilon$, these methods attempt to keep the updated policy within a "proximal" region of the old policy. However, while clipping acts as a safety valve, it addresses merely symptoms rather than causes if itself operates correctly but suffers from fundamental errors elsewhere. # Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical methodology for enhancing the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex reasoning tasks in mathematics and programming. However, as models grow larger and generate longer responses, traditional algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) encounter severe stability issues, often leading to catastrophic model collapse. The root cause of this instability lies in a fundamental mismatch between how the algorithm calculates importance weights and how rewards are assigned. This chapter introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a novel algorithm designed to resolve these deficiencies by shifting the granularity of optimization from the token level to the sequence level. By rigorously adhering to the principles of importance sampling and aligning the units of optimization with the units of reward, GSPO offers a stable, efficient, and theoretically sound framework for training modern large language models, including sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures.

# Core Principle: Aligning Optimization Units with Reward Units

To understand the necessity of GSPO, one must first recognize the structural flaw inherent in preceding token-level approaches like GRPO when applied to long-sequence generation. In standard reinforcement learning setups for language models, a verifier assigns a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ to an entire response sequence $y$ given a query $x$. This reward reflects the quality of the complete thought process or solution provided by the model. However, algorithms such as GRPO attempt to optimize the policy by calculating importance ratios and advantages at every individual token position $t$ within that sequence. This creates a conceptual dissonance: the feedback signal (the reward) is global and pertains to the whole sequence, yet the correction mechanism (the importance weight) is local and applied per token.

The theoretical justification for importance sampling requires estimating the expectation of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. Crucially, this statistical validity relies on averaging over multiple samples to correct for distributional mismatches. In the context of autoregressive language generation, however, we typically possess only a single sample trajectory—the specific sequence generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. When GRPO applies an importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ at each token step based on this single sample, it fails to perform genuine distribution correction. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. Because language generation involves hundreds or thousands of sequential steps, this noise accumulates multiplicatively along the chain. As training progresses, particularly with longer responses required for difficult reasoning tasks, this accumulated variance destabilizes the learning dynamics, eventually causing the model to collapse irreversibly.

GSPO addresses this by enforcing a strict alignment between the unit of optimization and the unit of reward. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence $y$, the off-policy correction must also operate at the sequence level. The core principle posits that if we cannot reliably estimate distribution shifts at every microscopic token step due to single-sample limitations and accumulating noise, we should instead evaluate how likely the *entire* generated response is under the new policy versus the old policy. This shift moves away from fragile token-by-token corrections toward a holistic assessment of the response trajectory. By treating the sequence as indivisible regarding its contribution to loss calculation—matching it exactly with how it receives credit via rewards—we eliminate sources of internal inconsistency that plague token-level methods. This alignment ensures that if a response is deemed valuable by verifier metrics contributing positively to parameters proportionally based on coherent logic behind why it worked together uniformly good outcomes were rewarded similarly bad ones penalised cohesively thus preventing erratic updates caused conflicting signals across timesteps . # Mathematical Analysis: Gradient Dynamics and Variants

Understanding the internal mechanics of reinforcement learning algorithms requires moving beyond surface-level objective functions to examine the underlying gradient dynamics that drive parameter updates. While Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have served as foundational tools for aligning language models, their application to massive scales reveals subtle yet critical flaws in how they weight gradient contributions. This chapter provides a rigorous mathematical dissection of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), contrasting its gradient flow with that of GRPO. By deriving the exact form of the gradients for both sequence-based and token-based objectives, we expose the source of instability in prior methods: the accumulation of high-variance noise caused by unequal token weighting. Furthermore, we introduce GSPO-token, a specialized variant that retains the stability benefits of sequence-level importance sampling while permitting fine-grained advantage adjustments necessary for complex tasks like multi-turn dialogue. Through this analysis, we establish why GSPO offers a theoretically sounder path for scaling reinforcement learning in large language models.

### Deriving Gradients for Sequence-Based Objectives

To appreciate the novelty of GSPO, one must first rigorously derive its gradient update rule starting from its proposed objective function. Unlike traditional approaches that sum losses over individual tokens before applying importance weights, GSPO operates on the premise that the unit of reward is the entire sequence. Consequently, the importance ratio used to correct for off-policy data must also be defined at the sequence level. The GSPO objective, denoted as $\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta)$, seeks to maximize the expected return over a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ generated from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Ignoring the clipping mechanism momentarily to isolate the core gradient structure, the objective simplifies to an expectation involving the product of a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and a group-normalized advantage $\widehat{A}_i$.

The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is constructed by taking the geometric mean of the token-level probability ratios across the entire response length $|y_i|$. Mathematically, this is expressed as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. Utilizing properties of logarithms and exponentials, this can be rewritten as an exponential of the average log-probability differences: $s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$. When we differentiate this objective with respect to the model parameters $\theta$, we apply the chain rule. The derivative of an exponential function $e^{u}$ is $e^u \cdot u'$, meaning the gradient of the importance ratio itself pulls down a factor proportional to the sum of the gradients of the log-likelihoods of every token in the sequence.

Carrying out this derivation explicitly yields a remarkably clean result. The gradient of the GSPO objective becomes an expectation over queries and response groups where each term consists of three components multiplied together: the scalar advantage $\widehat{A}_i$, the scalar sequence importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, and crucially, an arithmetic mean of token-level score functions. specifically, $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$.
