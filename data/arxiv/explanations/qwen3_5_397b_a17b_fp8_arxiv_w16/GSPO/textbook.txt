# Chapter 1: Introduction to Scaling Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has rapidly evolved from a niche optimization technique into a pivotal paradigm for scaling the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). As the field pushes toward ever-larger models, RL has become the primary mechanism through which these systems develop the ability to tackle sophisticated, high-stakes problems. Specifically, through large-scale RL training, language models acquire the capacity to engage in deeper and longer reasoning processes required for competition-level mathematics, advanced programming tasks, and complex logical deduction. This shift represents a fundamental transition in how we train AI; rather than merely predicting the next token based on static datasets, models are now learning to optimize their output trajectories to achieve specific goals verified by external reward signals. The success of recent breakthroughs in solving difficult mathematical proofs and generating functional code underscores that RL is not just an additive improvement but a necessary component for unlocking the highest tiers of machine intelligence.

However, the path to scaling RL with greater computational investment is fraught with significant engineering and algorithmic hurdles. The foremost prerequisite for successfully expanding RL training regimes is maintaining stable and robust training dynamics throughout the optimization process. As models grow in parameter count, adopt sparse architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), and generate increasingly lengthy responses, the margin for error narrows considerably. Stability is no longer merely a desirable trait but a strict requirement; without it, the massive computational resources invested in training can yield diminishing returns or, worse, complete failure. The community has observed that current state-of-the-art algorithms, while effective on smaller scales, often exhibit severe instabilities when applied to gigantic language models. These instabilities manifest as erratic loss landscapes and divergent policy updates, preventing researchers from pushing the capability boundaries of language models through continued training iterations.

The most critical manifestation of these stability issues is the phenomenon of catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. When training enormous models using existing algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), practitioners frequently encounter scenarios where the model's performance degrades abruptly and permanently. This collapse is characterized by a breakdown in the model's ability to generate coherent text or solve problems, rendering further training futile. Empirical evidence suggests that once this collapse occurs, standard recovery methods—such as reverting to previous checkpoints, meticulously retuning hyperparameters like clipping ranges, extending generation lengths, or swapping out query sets—are entirely unavailing. This fragility poses a hard ceiling on the potential of RL scaling; if an algorithm cannot guarantee stability as model size and complexity increase, it cannot serve as the foundation for future generations of intelligent systems. Consequently, identifying the root causes of this instability has become a central focus of contemporary research in deep reinforcement learning.

Investigation into these failures reveals that the instability inherent in algorithms like GRPO stems from a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling weights within their algorithmic design. Traditional approaches rely on token-level importance ratios to correct for the discrepancy between the policy currently being optimized and the older policy used to generate the data. However, in the context of long sequences generated by massive models, this token-level approach introduces high-variance training noise. This noise does not remain isolated; rather, it progressively accumulates over the length of the response and is further amplified by the clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize updates. The compounding effect of this variance eventually precipitates model collapse. The core issue lies in treating each token as an independent unit for off-policy correction when the reward signal itself is assigned to the entire sequence. This mismatch between the granularity of the reward and the granularity of the optimization objective creates an ill-posed problem that undermines training stability at scale.

To address these profound limitations, this text introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm specifically engineered for training large language models. GSPO represents a paradigmatic shift away from token-centric optimization toward a theoretically grounded sequence-level framework. The key innovation of GSPO lies in its redefinition of the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood rather than individual token probabilities. By aligning the importance sampling mechanism with the basic principles of probability theory regarding whole sequences, GSPO ensures that off-policy corrections are performed consistently with how rewards are assigned. Furthermore, GSPO computes normalized rewards as advantages across a group of responses to a single query, ensuring perfect alignment between sequence-level rewarding and sequence-level optimization. This structural coherence eliminates the high-variance noise sources that plague previous methods, offering a stable pathway for scaling RL efforts.

The implications of adopting GSPO extend beyond mere theoretical elegance; they offer tangible improvements in stability, efficiency, and architectural flexibility. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency and final performance compared to GRPO, enabling continuous capability gains simply by increasing compute budgets and refining query sets. Perhaps most significantly, GSPO inherently resolves the notorious stability challenges associated with training large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE architectures, expert activation patterns can volatilely shift between updates, causing token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly and invalidate learning signals under traditional algorithms like GRPO unless complex workarounds are employed at great cost?

# Chapter 2: Preliminaries and Existing Algorithms

To understand the advancements introduced by modern reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, one must first establish a rigorous understanding of the underlying mathematical framework and the evolutionary path of policy optimization methods. This chapter lays the necessary groundwork by defining the formal notation used to describe autoregressive language models as policies, the mechanisms for scoring their outputs, and the specific objectives of two pivotal algorithms: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). By dissecting the mechanics of these existing approaches, particularly their handling of importance sampling and advantage estimation, we prepare the ground for identifying the structural limitations that motivate newer developments. The discussion focuses intently on how these algorithms navigate the trade-off between exploration, stability, and computational feasibility, with special attention paid to the granular level—token versus sequence—at which optimization occurs.

## Mathematical Notation: Policies, Queries, Responses, and Likelihoods

In the context of reinforcement learning for language models, we treat an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a stochastic policy, denoted as $\pi_\theta$. This policy governs the probability distribution over sequences of tokens given an input prompt. We define $x$ as a query drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$, representing the initial context or instruction provided to the model. Upon receiving $x$, the policy generates a response $y$, which is a sequence of tokens. The structure of language generation implies that the probability of generating the entire sequence $y$ is decomposable into the product of conditional probabilities for each individual token. Specifically, if $|y|$ denotes the total number of tokens in the response $y$, the likelihood of generating $y$ given $x$ under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. Here, $y_t$ represents the token at position $t$, and $y_{<t}$ signifies all preceding tokens in the sequence up to but not including $t$. This factorization is central to how likelihoods are computed and subsequently manipulated during policy updates.

The interaction between the model and its environment is framed around query-response pairs $(x, y)$. In an off-policy learning setting, which is standard practice to improve sample efficiency, we distinguish between two versions of the policy: the "old" policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, which was used to generate the data currently being processed, and the "current" policy $\pi_\theta$, which is being optimized via gradient descent. The divergence between these two distributions is a critical factor in algorithm design. When responses are sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but evaluated to update $\pi_\theta$, we encounter a distributional mismatch. Correcting for this mismatch requires careful application of importance sampling principles, where the ratio of probabilities assigned by the new policy versus the old policy plays a decisive role in weighting gradients. Understanding this dynamic at both the token level ($t$) and the sequence level ($y$) is essential for grasping why certain algorithms succeed or fail as model sizes and response lengths increase.

Chapter 3: Diagnosing Instability in Token-Level Optimization

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical paradigm for enhancing the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex problems in mathematics and programming through extended reasoning chains. However, as models grow larger and responses become longer, the stability of training dynamics has emerged as a primary bottleneck. State-of-the-art algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which operate by optimizing policies at the token level, frequently suffer from catastrophic model collapse when applied to giant architectures. This chapter provides a rigorous diagnosis of these failures, arguing that the root cause lies in a fundamental misalignment between the algorithmic design of token-level importance sampling and the reality of sequence-level rewards. By dissecting the theoretical flaws in how distribution shifts are corrected and analyzing the compounding effects of variance over long horizons, we reveal why current methods struggle. Furthermore, we examine how these instabilities are uniquely exacerbated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models due to expert-activation volatility, rendering standard workarounds like "Routing Replay" insufficient for true scalability.

# The Off-Policy Setting in Mini-Batch Updates

To understand the origins of instability in modern RL algorithms for language models, one must first appreciate the operational constraints imposed by hardware and data efficiency. As language models increase in size and sparsity, maximizing hardware utilization during the roll-out phase requires generating massive batches of response data. However, processing this entire batch simultaneously for gradient updates is often computationally prohibitive due to memory limitations. Consequently, it is standard practice to partition a large batch of roll-out data into several smaller mini-batches. These mini-batches are then used sequentially to update the model parameters.

This partitioning strategy inadvertently creates an off-policy learning environment. When the first mini-batch is processed, the gradients are computed based on data sampled from the old policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. However, by the time subsequent mini-batches are processed, the model parameters $\theta$ have already been updated slightly by the preceding batches. Therefore, the later mini-batches consist of samples drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but they are being used to optimize a current policy $\pi_\theta$ that has drifted away from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This discrepancy means that the data distribution no longer perfectly matches the policy currently being trained.

The existence of this off-policy gap necessitates mechanisms to prevent the model from overfitting to outdated data or making excessively large updates based on samples that are no longer representative of the current policy's behavior. Algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and GRPO employ clipping mechanisms specifically to manage this divergence. The clipping acts as a guardrail, preventing importance ratios—the weights used to correct for the distribution mismatch—from becoming too extreme. While these mechanisms are designed to stabilize training within a proximal region, they assume that the underlying method of calculating importance weights is sound. As we shall see, the instability arises not merely because an off-policy setting exists, but because the method used to correct for it in GRPO is theoretically ill-posed for the task at hand.

# The Misapplication of Importance Sampling Principles

The core theoretical flaw in GRPO stems from a misunderstanding or misapplication of the principles of importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate the expected value of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ by using samples drawn from a different behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Mathematically, this relationship is expressed as:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right].
$$
For this equality to hold and for the estimator to be reliable, a crucial condition must be met: the expectation on the right-hand side must be approximated by averaging over a sufficiently large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) drawn from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The importance weight $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) } { \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$ serves to re-weight these multiple samples so that their aggregate contribution accurately reflects what would have been obtained had they been drawn directly from $\pi_\text{tar}$.

In the context of GRPO, however, this fundamental requirement is violated. GRPO attempts to apply importance sampling corrections at every single token position $t$ within a generated sequence. For a specific token $y_{i,t}$ at position $t$ in response $i$, the algorithm calculates an importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ defined as:
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Chapter 4: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

# Core Philosophy: Matching the Unit of Optimization to the Unit of Reward

The fundamental instability plaguing previous reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), arises from a conceptual mismatch between the granularity of the reward signal and the granularity of the optimization objective. In the context of training language models, the verification process typically assigns a scalar reward score $r(x, y)$ to an entire query-response pair $(x, y)$. This reward reflects the quality of the complete sequence $y$ generated in response to query $x$. However, traditional algorithms like GRPO attempt to optimize the policy by applying importance sampling corrections and gradient updates at the individual token level. This misalignment creates an ill-posed objective function that becomes increasingly unstable as model sizes grow and response lengths extend.

To understand why this mismatch is detrimental, one must examine the statistical foundations of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling dictates that to estimate the expectation of a function under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, one must re-weight the samples by the ratio of their probabilities. Crucially, this theoretical guarantee relies on averaging over a sufficient number of samples to correct for the distributional shift. In GRPO, the importance weight is calculated for every single token $y_t$ based on the conditional probability $\pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})$. Because each token represents a single sample from its respective next-token distribution, there is no ensemble averaging occurring at the token level to stabilize the weight. Consequently, these token-level weights act as sources of high-variance noise rather than effective corrective factors.

This variance is not merely a minor inconvenience; it compounds catastrophically over long sequences. As a model generates a response comprising hundreds or thousands of tokens, the accumulation of noisy gradients from invalid token-level importance weights destabilizes the training dynamics. This instability is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanisms designed to constrain policy updates. When applied to noisy token-level ratios, clipping can amplify errors rather than suppress them, leading to what researchers observe as irreversible model collapse. Once a model collapses under these conditions, recovery is often impossible regardless of hyperparameter tuning or dataset modifications. Therefore, the core philosophical pivot required to resolve these issues is to recognize that since the reward is assigned at the sequence level, the optimization objective—including off-policy correction and clipping—must also operate at the sequence level. By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, we eliminate the structural source of variance inherent in token-level approaches.

# Defining Sequence-Level Importance Ratios via Likelihood

Having established that optimization must occur at the sequence level, we turn to defining a mathematically rigorous importance ratio that operates over entire responses rather than isolated tokens. In Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the importance ratio is derived directly from the likelihood of generating the full sequence $y_i$ given a query $x$. For an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$, the likelihood of a sequence is defined as the product of conditional probabilities for each token: $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. The raw sequence-level importance ratio would naturally be expressed as $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y | x)}$. However, utilizing this raw ratio presents practical challenges related to numerical stability and variance control, particularly because sequence likelihoods decay exponentially with length.

To address these challenges, GSPO introduces a length-normalized definition for the sequence-level importance ratio, denoted as $s_{i}(\theta)$. Instead of using the raw product ratio, GSPO defines $s_{i}(\theta)$ as geometric mean of per-token probability ratios raised to an inverse length power:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
This expression can equivalently be written in terms logarithms and exponentials for clearer interpretation:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right) $$
By raising joint likelihoods—the products—to an exponent inversely proportional to their respective lengths ($1/|yi|$)—GSPO effectively transforms multiplicative chains into additive averages inside exponential form thus keeping values bounded within manageable numeric ranges regardless output span variation preventing extreme outliers caused solely differences sentence structure complexity duration etcetera .
