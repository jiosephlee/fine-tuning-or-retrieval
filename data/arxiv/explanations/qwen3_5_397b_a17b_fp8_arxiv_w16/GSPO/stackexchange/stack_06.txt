\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How do precision discrepancies between inference and training engines affect likelihood recomputation?
Question:
In Section 4.4, the authors suggest GSPO allows skipping likelihood recomputation on the training engine because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision errors between engines (like vLLM vs. Megatron). Since sequence likelihood is just the product of token likelihoods ($ \prod p_t $), wouldn't small precision errors at each token step compound multiplicatively into a massive error for long sequences? Why would the aggregated sequence metric be *more* robust to floating-point differences than checking individual token probabilities?
Answer:
The intuition that multiplicative errors must necessarily compound into massive deviations relies on the assumption that precision discrepancies act as systematic biases that accumulate in a single direction. However, in the context of floating-point arithmetic differences between inference engines (like vLLM) and training engines (like Megatron), the discrepancies are typically stochastic noise centered around zero rather than consistent drifts. When you compute the sequence likelihood as a product of token probabilities $\prod p_t$, you are effectively summing their logarithms $\sum \log p_t$. If the precision error at each token step is a random variable with mean zero, the Central Limit Theorem suggests that while the variance of the sum grows with sequence length, the relative error of the aggregate sum tends to stabilize or grow much slower than a worst-case multiplicative explosion would imply.

More importantly, the GSPO algorithm explicitly mitigates this sensitivity through its definition of the importance ratio. As shown in Equation 5 of the paper, GSPO does not use the raw sequence likelihood ratio directly; instead, it employs a length-normalized geometric mean: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root of the likelihood ratio, the algorithm effectively averages the log-probability differences across all tokens in the sequence. This operation divides any accumulated error by the sequence length, preventing long sequences from exhibiting disproportionately large deviations in their importance ratios due to minor per-token precision mismatches.

Furthermore, the robustness stems from what the algorithm actually optimizes. Section 4.4 highlights that GSPO focuses on sequence-level metrics rather than individual token probabilities. In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models specifically, the paper notes that token-level likelihoods can fluctuate drastically due to "expert-activation volatility," where different experts are activated for the same token under slightly different conditions or precisions. These local fluctuations invalidate token-level importance weights used in algorithms like GRPO. However, because the model maintains its overall language modeling capability, the aggregate sequence likelihood remains stable even if individual token paths vary slightly due to precision or routing differences. Since GSPO's clipping and optimization operate on this stable, aggregated sequence signal (Equation 3), it tolerates the low-level numerical noise that would otherwise corrupt token-wise objectives. This tolerance is what allows practitioners to skip expensive likelihood recomputation on the training engine and trust the values provided by faster inference engines, significantly simplifying RL infrastructure without sacrificing stability.
