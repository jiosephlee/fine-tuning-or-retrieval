\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why are the optimal clipping ranges for GSPO ($3e^{-4}$) so much smaller than for GRPO ($0.2$)?
Question:
The experimental setup specifies clipping ranges of roughly $0.0003$ for GSPO versus $0.2$ for GRPO. Given that both use a min/clip objective structure, why is there such a vast difference in scale? Is this solely because $s_i(\theta)$ is a geometric mean (which stays close to 1) whereas token ratios can vary wildly? If I were to tune GSPO on a new dataset, should I expect these hyperparameters to remain in the $10^{-4}$ range regardless of model size, or does this scale with something else?
Answer:
The vast difference in optimal clipping ranges between GSPO ($3 \times 10^{-4}$) and GRPO ($0.2$) stems directly from the mathematical definition of their respective importance ratios and the statistical effect of length normalization. Your intuition that the geometric mean keeps the ratio close to 1 is correct, but the underlying reason involves how probability products behave over long sequences compared to individual token probabilities.

In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated at the token level as $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. Since this compares two conditional probabilities for a single token, the ratio can naturally fluctuate significantly depending on how much the policy has shifted for that specific next-token distribution. A shift where the new policy assigns double the probability to a token compared to the old policy results in a ratio of $2.0$, while halving it results in $0.5$. Consequently, a clipping range of $\varepsilon = 0.2$ (creating a window of $[0.8, 1.2]$) is necessary to allow for reasonable policy updates while preventing extreme outliers.

In contrast, GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as the geometric mean of the token-level ratios across the entire sequence: $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$. The paper explicitly states that this length normalization is adopted to "reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." Mathematically, taking the $|y_i|$-th root of a product (or averaging the logs) acts as a powerful variance reducer via the Law of Large Numbers. Even if individual token ratios vary wildly, their geometric mean over a long response tends to converge very tightly toward 1 unless the *entire* sequence distribution has shifted coherently. A deviation of $0.2$ in this geometric mean would imply a massive, exponential divergence in the total sequence likelihood, which represents a catastrophic policy shift rather than a standard update step. Therefore, the "signal" for a meaningful update exists in a much narrower band around 1, necessitating a clipping range orders of magnitude smaller ($3 \times 10^{-4}$) to effectively distinguish between normal noise and significant off-policy deviations.

Regarding your question on tuning for new datasets or model sizes, you should generally expect these hyperparameters to remain in the $10^{-4}$ range provided you are dealing with typical response lengths found in LLM reasoning tasks. The scale of $s_i(\theta)$ is intrinsically tied to the sequence length $|y_i|$; as the paper notes, without length normalization, "importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." Because GSPO includes the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ term, it normalizes this effect, making the metric relatively stable across different context lengths compared to raw likelihood ratios. However, if you were to train on extremely short sequences (e.g., single-token outputs), the variance reduction benefit of the geometric mean would diminish, and you might need to increase $\varepsilon$. Conversely, for very long reasoning chains, the ratio becomes even more concentrated near 1, potentially validating even tighter clipping. The key takeaway from the paper is that GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a "more reliable and effective learning signal" precisely because it filters out the high-frequency noise inherent in token-level updates, allowing stable training even when discarding a larger fraction of samples via clipping.
