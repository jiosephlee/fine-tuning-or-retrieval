\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How exactly does 'expert-activation volatility' invalidate token-level importance weights in MoE models?
Question:
The paper mentions that for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models trained with GRPO, roughly 10% of activated experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$ for the same token. The authors claim this makes the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }$ fluctuate drastically. Could someone explain the mechanism here? If an expert changes, doesn't the new expert simply output a probability for that token? Why does swapping the underlying network component cause the *ratio* to become unstable or meaningless compared to the dense model case?
Answer:
The instability arises because the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ in GRPO acts as a direct multiplier for the gradient of individual tokens, making it hypersensitive to discrete structural changes in the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture that do not necessarily reflect a meaningful shift in the model's overall policy. In an MoE model, the probability of a token $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | \dots)$ is determined by a specific subset of active experts selected by a routing mechanism. The paper notes that between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the updated policy $\pi_{\theta}$, approximately 10% of these activated experts change for the same token. When an expert swaps out, the underlying neural network component computing that probability changes entirely. Even if the new expert produces a probability value reasonably close to the old one, the sudden discontinuity in the function approximator causes the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }$ to fluctuate drastically and unpredictably.

This volatility invalidates the importance weight because, in the context of Reinforcement Learning with off-policy data, these weights are intended to correct for distributional shifts smoothly. However, when driven by expert swapping rather than gradual parameter updates, the ratio ceases to represent a stable measure of "how much the policy has changed" and instead becomes high-variance noise. As described in the gradient analysis section of the paper, GRPO weighs the gradient of each token's log-likelihood by this volatile $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Consequently, tokens associated with swapped experts receive erratic gradient signals—either vanishing or exploding—which accumulate over long sequences and destabilize training, often leading to model collapse. This forces practitioners to use complex workarounds like "Routing Replay," which artificially freezes the routing decisions of $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ during the calculation for $\pi_{\theta}$ to ensure the same experts are used, thereby masking the root issue.

In contrast, GSPO resolves this by shifting the unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level. Instead of relying on unstable individual ratios, GSPO utilizes a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ derived from the geometric mean of token probabilities (or equivalently, the ratio of sequence likelihoods raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$). While individual expert activations may flip and cause local turbulence in token probabilities, the aggregate sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ remains robust because the model maintains its overall language modeling capability across different expert combinations. By averaging these effects over the entire sequence before applying clipping and weighting, GSPO smooths out the noise introduced by expert-activation volatility. This allows the algorithm to converge stably without needing to enforce consistent routing paths, effectively decoupling training stability from the sparsity-induced discontinuities inherent in MoE architectures.
