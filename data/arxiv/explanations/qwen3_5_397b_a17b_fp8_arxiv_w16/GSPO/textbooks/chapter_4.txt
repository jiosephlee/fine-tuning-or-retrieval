# Core Philosophy: Matching the Unit of Optimization to the Unit of Reward

The fundamental instability plaguing previous reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), arises from a conceptual mismatch between the granularity of the reward signal and the granularity of the optimization objective. In the context of training language models, the verification process typically assigns a scalar reward score $r(x, y)$ to an entire query-response pair $(x, y)$. This reward reflects the quality of the complete sequence $y$ generated in response to query $x$. However, traditional algorithms like GRPO attempt to optimize the policy by applying importance sampling corrections and gradient updates at the individual token level. This misalignment creates an ill-posed objective function that becomes increasingly unstable as model sizes grow and response lengths extend.

To understand why this mismatch is detrimental, one must examine the statistical foundations of importance sampling. The principle of importance sampling dictates that to estimate the expectation of a function under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, one must re-weight the samples by the ratio of their probabilities. Crucially, this theoretical guarantee relies on averaging over a sufficient number of samples to correct for the distributional shift. In GRPO, the importance weight is calculated for every single token $y_t$ based on the conditional probability $\pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})$. Because each token represents a single sample from its respective next-token distribution, there is no ensemble averaging occurring at the token level to stabilize the weight. Consequently, these token-level weights act as sources of high-variance noise rather than effective corrective factors.

This variance is not merely a minor inconvenience; it compounds catastrophically over long sequences. As a model generates a response comprising hundreds or thousands of tokens, the accumulation of noisy gradients from invalid token-level importance weights destabilizes the training dynamics. This instability is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanisms designed to constrain policy updates. When applied to noisy token-level ratios, clipping can amplify errors rather than suppress them, leading to what researchers observe as irreversible model collapse. Once a model collapses under these conditions, recovery is often impossible regardless of hyperparameter tuning or dataset modifications. Therefore, the core philosophical pivot required to resolve these issues is to recognize that since the reward is assigned at the sequence level, the optimization objective—including off-policy correction and clipping—must also operate at the sequence level. By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, we eliminate the structural source of variance inherent in token-level approaches.

# Defining Sequence-Level Importance Ratios via Likelihood

Having established that optimization must occur at the sequence level, we turn to defining a mathematically rigorous importance ratio that operates over entire responses rather than isolated tokens. In Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the importance ratio is derived directly from the likelihood of generating the full sequence $y_i$ given a query $x$. For an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$, the likelihood of a sequence is defined as the product of conditional probabilities for each token: $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. The raw sequence-level importance ratio would naturally be expressed as $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y | x)}$. However, utilizing this raw ratio presents practical challenges related to numerical stability and variance control, particularly because sequence likelihoods decay exponentially with length.

To address these challenges, GSPO introduces a length-normalized definition for the sequence-level importance ratio, denoted as $s_{i}(\theta)$. Instead of using the raw product ratio, GSPO defines $s_{i}(\theta)$ as geometric mean of per-token probability ratios raised to an inverse length power:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
This expression can equivalently be written in terms logarithms and exponentials for clearer interpretation:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right) $$
