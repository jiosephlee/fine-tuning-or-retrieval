\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Scaling Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has rapidly evolved from a niche optimization technique into a pivotal paradigm for scaling the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). As the field pushes toward ever-larger models, RL has become the primary mechanism through which these systems develop the ability to tackle sophisticated, high-stakes problems. Specifically, through large-scale RL training, language models acquire the capacity to engage in deeper and longer reasoning processes required for competition-level mathematics, advanced programming tasks, and complex logical deduction. This shift represents a fundamental transition in how we train AI; rather than merely predicting the next token based on static datasets, models are now learning to optimize their output trajectories to achieve specific goals verified by external reward signals. The success of recent breakthroughs in solving difficult mathematical proofs and generating functional code underscores that RL is not just an additive improvement but a necessary component for unlocking the highest tiers of machine intelligence.

However, the path to scaling RL with greater computational investment is fraught with significant engineering and algorithmic hurdles. The foremost prerequisite for successfully expanding RL training regimes is maintaining stable and robust training dynamics throughout the optimization process. As models grow in parameter count, adopt sparse architectures such as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), and generate increasingly lengthy responses, the margin for error narrows considerably. Stability is no longer merely a desirable trait but a strict requirement; without it, the massive computational resources invested in training can yield diminishing returns or, worse, complete failure. The community has observed that current state-of-the-art algorithms, while effective on smaller scales, often exhibit severe instabilities when applied to gigantic language models. These instabilities manifest as erratic loss landscapes and divergent policy updates, preventing researchers from pushing the capability boundaries of language models through continued training iterations.

The most critical manifestation of these stability issues is the phenomenon of catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. When training enormous models using existing algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), practitioners frequently encounter scenarios where the model's performance degrades abruptly and permanently. This collapse is characterized by a breakdown in the model's ability to generate coherent text or solve problems, rendering further training futile. Empirical evidence suggests that once this collapse occurs, standard recovery methods—such as reverting to previous checkpoints, meticulously retuning hyperparameters like clipping ranges, extending generation lengths, or swapping out query sets—are entirely unavailing. This fragility poses a hard ceiling on the potential of RL scaling; if an algorithm cannot guarantee stability as model size and complexity increase, it cannot serve as the foundation for future generations of intelligent systems. Consequently, identifying the root causes of this instability has become a central focus of contemporary research in deep reinforcement learning.

Investigation into these failures reveals that the instability inherent in algorithms like GRPO stems from a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling weights within their algorithmic design. Traditional approaches rely on token-level importance ratios to correct for the discrepancy between the policy currently being optimized and the older policy used to generate the data. However, in the context of long sequences generated by massive models, this token-level approach introduces high-variance training noise. This noise does not remain isolated; rather, it progressively accumulates over the length of the response and is further amplified by the clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize updates. The compounding effect of this variance eventually precipitates model collapse. The core issue lies in treating each token as an independent unit for off-policy correction when the reward signal itself is assigned to the entire sequence. This mismatch between the granularity of the reward and the granularity of the optimization objective creates an ill-posed problem that undermines training stability at scale.

To address these profound limitations, this text introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm specifically engineered for training large language models. GSPO represents a paradigmatic shift away from token-centric optimization toward a theoretically grounded sequence-level framework. The key innovation of GSPO lies in its redefinition of the importance ratio based on sequence likelihood rather than individual token probabilities. By aligning the importance sampling mechanism with the basic principles of probability theory regarding whole sequences, GSPO ensures that off-policy corrections are performed consistently with how rewards are assigned. Furthermore, GSPO computes normalized rewards as advantages across a group of responses to a single query, ensuring perfect alignment between sequence-level rewarding and sequence-level optimization. This structural coherence eliminates the high-variance noise sources that plague previous methods, offering a stable pathway for scaling RL efforts.

The implications of adopting GSPO extend beyond mere theoretical elegance; they offer tangible improvements in stability, efficiency, and architectural flexibility. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency and final performance compared to GRPO, enabling continuous capability gains simply by increasing compute budgets and refining query sets. Perhaps most significantly, GSPO inherently resolves the notorious stability challenges associated with training large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE architectures, expert activation patterns can volatilely shift between updates, causing token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly and invalidate learning signals under traditional algorithms like GRPO unless complex workarounds are employed at great cost?
