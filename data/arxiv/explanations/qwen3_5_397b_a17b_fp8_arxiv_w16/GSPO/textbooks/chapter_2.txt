Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Preliminaries and Existing Algorithms

To understand the advancements introduced by modern reinforcement learning algorithms for large language models, one must first establish a rigorous understanding of the underlying mathematical framework and the evolutionary path of policy optimization methods. This chapter lays the necessary groundwork by defining the formal notation used to describe autoregressive language models as policies, the mechanisms for scoring their outputs, and the specific objectives of two pivotal algorithms: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). By dissecting the mechanics of these existing approaches, particularly their handling of importance sampling and advantage estimation, we prepare the ground for identifying the structural limitations that motivate newer developments. The discussion focuses intently on how these algorithms navigate the trade-off between exploration, stability, and computational feasibility, with special attention paid to the granular level—token versus sequence—at which optimization occurs.

## Mathematical Notation: Policies, Queries, Responses, and Likelihoods

In the context of reinforcement learning for language models, we treat an autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ as a stochastic policy, denoted as $\pi_\theta$. This policy governs the probability distribution over sequences of tokens given an input prompt. We define $x$ as a query drawn from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$, representing the initial context or instruction provided to the model. Upon receiving $x$, the policy generates a response $y$, which is a sequence of tokens. The structure of language generation implies that the probability of generating the entire sequence $y$ is decomposable into the product of conditional probabilities for each individual token. Specifically, if $|y|$ denotes the total number of tokens in the response $y$, the likelihood of generating $y$ given $x$ under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as $\pi_\theta (y | x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t} )$. Here, $y_t$ represents the token at position $t$, and $y_{<t}$ signifies all preceding tokens in the sequence up to but not including $t$. This factorization is central to how likelihoods are computed and subsequently manipulated during policy updates.

The interaction between the model and its environment is framed around query-response pairs $(x, y)$. In an off-policy learning setting, which is standard practice to improve sample efficiency, we distinguish between two versions of the policy: the "old" policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, which was used to generate the data currently being processed, and the "current" policy $\pi_\theta$, which is being optimized via gradient descent. The divergence between these two distributions is a critical factor in algorithm design. When responses are sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but evaluated to update $\pi_\theta$, we encounter a distributional mismatch. Correcting for this mismatch requires careful application of importance sampling principles, where the ratio of probabilities assigned by the new policy versus the old policy plays a decisive role in weighting gradients. Understanding this dynamic at both the token level ($t$) and the sequence level ($y$) is essential for grasping why certain algorithms succeed or fail as model sizes and response lengths increase.
