Chapter 2: # The Stability Crisis: Diagnosing Failures in Token-Level Optimization

As reinforcement learning (RL) becomes the primary paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex mathematical proofs and generate sophisticated code, the stability of the underlying training algorithms has become the critical bottleneck. While earlier iterations of RL were successful on smaller scales, the transition to gigantic models with billions of parameters, sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, and extremely long reasoning chains has exposed fatal flaws in existing methodologies. Specifically, algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which rely on token-level optimization objectives, exhibit severe instability that often culminates in catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. This chapter dissects the theoretical and practical roots of this crisis, demonstrating how the misapplication of importance sampling at the token level generates high-variance noise that accumulates destructively over time, rendering traditional approaches unsuitable for the next generation of AI systems.

### The Off-Policy Challenge in Large Batch Training

To understand the origin of instability in modern RL training, one must first appreciate the operational constraints imposed by the sheer scale of contemporary language models. As models grow in size and sparsity, and as the required response lengths extend to accommodate deep reasoning processes, maximizing hardware utilization demands enormous rollout batch sizes. It is computationally prohibitive to perform a gradient update immediately after generating every single batch of data. Consequently, standard practice involves collecting a vast amount of trajectory data from an older version of the policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, storing it, and then partitioning this data into multiple mini-batches for successive gradient updates targeting the current policy $\pi_\theta$.

This separation between data collection and optimization inherently creates an off-policy learning setting. The samples used to calculate gradients are drawn from a distribution that differs from the one currently being optimized. If unchecked, this distributional shift can lead to arbitrarily large updates that destabilize the model. Historically, algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its successor GRPO have relied on clipping mechanisms to mitigate this risk. By restricting the influence of samples where the probability ratio between the new and old policies exceeds a certain threshold $\varepsilon$, these methods attempt to keep the updated policy within a "proximal" region of the old policy. However, while clipping acts as a safety valve, it addresses merely symptoms rather than causes if itself operates correctly but suffers from fundamental errors elsewhere.
