Chapter 3: Chapter 3: Diagnosing Instability in Token-Level Optimization

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical paradigm for enhancing the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex problems in mathematics and programming through extended reasoning chains. However, as models grow larger and responses become longer, the stability of training dynamics has emerged as a primary bottleneck. State-of-the-art algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which operate by optimizing policies at the token level, frequently suffer from catastrophic model collapse when applied to giant architectures. This chapter provides a rigorous diagnosis of these failures, arguing that the root cause lies in a fundamental misalignment between the algorithmic design of token-level importance sampling and the reality of sequence-level rewards. By dissecting the theoretical flaws in how distribution shifts are corrected and analyzing the compounding effects of variance over long horizons, we reveal why current methods struggle. Furthermore, we examine how these instabilities are uniquely exacerbated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models due to expert-activation volatility, rendering standard workarounds like "Routing Replay" insufficient for true scalability.

# The Off-Policy Setting in Mini-Batch Updates

To understand the origins of instability in modern RL algorithms for language models, one must first appreciate the operational constraints imposed by hardware and data efficiency. As language models increase in size and sparsity, maximizing hardware utilization during the roll-out phase requires generating massive batches of response data. However, processing this entire batch simultaneously for gradient updates is often computationally prohibitive due to memory limitations. Consequently, it is standard practice to partition a large batch of roll-out data into several smaller mini-batches. These mini-batches are then used sequentially to update the model parameters.

This partitioning strategy inadvertently creates an off-policy learning environment. When the first mini-batch is processed, the gradients are computed based on data sampled from the old policy, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. However, by the time subsequent mini-batches are processed, the model parameters $\theta$ have already been updated slightly by the preceding batches. Therefore, the later mini-batches consist of samples drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but they are being used to optimize a current policy $\pi_\theta$ that has drifted away from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. This discrepancy means that the data distribution no longer perfectly matches the policy currently being trained.

The existence of this off-policy gap necessitates mechanisms to prevent the model from overfitting to outdated data or making excessively large updates based on samples that are no longer representative of the current policy's behavior. Algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and GRPO employ clipping mechanisms specifically to manage this divergence. The clipping acts as a guardrail, preventing importance ratios—the weights used to correct for the distribution mismatch—from becoming too extreme. While these mechanisms are designed to stabilize training within a proximal region, they assume that the underlying method of calculating importance weights is sound. As we shall see, the instability arises not merely because an off-policy setting exists, but because the method used to correct for it in GRPO is theoretically ill-posed for the task at hand.

# The Misapplication of Importance Sampling Principles

The core theoretical flaw in GRPO stems from a misunderstanding or misapplication of the principles of importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate the expected value of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ by using samples drawn from a different behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. Mathematically, this relationship is expressed as:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right].
$$
For this equality to hold and for the estimator to be reliable, a crucial condition must be met: the expectation on the right-hand side must be approximated by averaging over a sufficiently large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) drawn from the behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The importance weight $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) } { \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$ serves to re-weight these multiple samples so that their aggregate contribution accurately reflects what would have been obtained had they been drawn directly from $\pi_\text{tar}$.

In the context of GRPO, however, this fundamental requirement is violated. GRPO attempts to apply importance sampling corrections at every single token position $t$ within a generated sequence. For a specific token $y_{i,t}$ at position $t$ in response $i$, the algorithm calculates an importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ defined as:
$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$
