Chapter 3: # Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical methodology for enhancing the capabilities of large language models, enabling them to solve complex reasoning tasks in mathematics and programming. However, as models grow larger and generate longer responses, traditional algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) encounter severe stability issues, often leading to catastrophic model collapse. The root cause of this instability lies in a fundamental mismatch between how the algorithm calculates importance weights and how rewards are assigned. This chapter introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a novel algorithm designed to resolve these deficiencies by shifting the granularity of optimization from the token level to the sequence level. By rigorously adhering to the principles of importance sampling and aligning the units of optimization with the units of reward, GSPO offers a stable, efficient, and theoretically sound framework for training modern large language models, including sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures.

# Core Principle: Aligning Optimization Units with Reward Units

To understand the necessity of GSPO, one must first recognize the structural flaw inherent in preceding token-level approaches like GRPO when applied to long-sequence generation. In standard reinforcement learning setups for language models, a verifier assigns a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ to an entire response sequence $y$ given a query $x$. This reward reflects the quality of the complete thought process or solution provided by the model. However, algorithms such as GRPO attempt to optimize the policy by calculating importance ratios and advantages at every individual token position $t$ within that sequence. This creates a conceptual dissonance: the feedback signal (the reward) is global and pertains to the whole sequence, yet the correction mechanism (the importance weight) is local and applied per token.

The theoretical justification for importance sampling requires estimating the expectation of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. Crucially, this statistical validity relies on averaging over multiple samples to correct for distributional mismatches. In the context of autoregressive language generation, however, we typically possess only a single sample trajectory—the specific sequence generated by the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. When GRPO applies an importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ at each token step based on this single sample, it fails to perform genuine distribution correction. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. Because language generation involves hundreds or thousands of sequential steps, this noise accumulates multiplicatively along the chain. As training progresses, particularly with longer responses required for difficult reasoning tasks, this accumulated variance destabilizes the learning dynamics, eventually causing the model to collapse irreversibly.

GSPO addresses this by enforcing a strict alignment between the unit of optimization and the unit of reward. Since the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence $y$, the off-policy correction must also operate at the sequence level. The core principle posits that if we cannot reliably estimate distribution shifts at every microscopic token step due to single-sample limitations and accumulating noise, we should instead evaluate how likely the *entire* generated response is under the new policy versus the old policy. This shift moves away from fragile token-by-token corrections toward a holistic assessment of the response trajectory. By treating the sequence as indivisible regarding its contribution to loss calculation—matching it exactly with how it receives credit via rewards—we eliminate sources of internal inconsistency that plague token-level methods. This alignment ensures that if a response is deemed valuable by verifier metrics contributing positively to parameters proportionally based on coherent logic behind why it worked together uniformly good outcomes were rewarded similarly bad ones penalised cohesively thus preventing erratic updates caused conflicting signals across timesteps .
