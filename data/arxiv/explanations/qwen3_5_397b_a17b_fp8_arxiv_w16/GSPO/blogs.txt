# Why Your LLM Training Collapses: The Hidden Flaw in Token-Level Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the secret sauce for pushing large language models (LLMs) to solve complex math problems and write sophisticated code. However, anyone who has tried to scale RL training on massive models knows the pain: sudden, catastrophic model collapse. One moment your model is improving; the next, it stops making sense entirely, and no amount of hyperparameter tuning can bring it back.

Recent research identifies the root cause of this instability in popular algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The culprit is a fundamental misunderstanding of how to apply importance sampling at the token level. By shifting the focus from individual tokens to entire sequences, a new algorithm called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers a stable, efficient path forward. This post breaks down why token-level optimization fails and how sequence-level thinking fixes it.

## The Ill-Posed Nature of Token-Level Importance Sampling

To understand the problem, we first need to look at how current algorithms handle off-policy learning. When training large models, we often generate a batch of responses using an "old" policy and then update the model several times using that same data. Because the model parameters change during these updates, the data becomes "off-policy." To correct for this distribution shift, algorithms use importance sampling weights.

The principle of importance sampling relies on averaging over many samples to correctly re-weight expectations. Mathematically, if we want to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$, we rely on the ratio $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$. This works well when we have sufficient samples to smooth out variance.

However, algorithms like GRPO apply this logic at every single token position $t$ within a generated response. They calculate an importance ratio for each token:

$$ w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} $$

Here lies the flaw. In autoregressive generation, each token $y_{i,t}$ is a single sample drawn from the next-token distribution. Applying an importance weight based on a single sample fails to perform the necessary distribution correction. Instead of correcting the bias, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimates.

This noise is not static; it accumulates over the length of the response. In long reasoning chains common in modern LLMs, these small variances compound exponentially. Furthermore, the clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize training often exacerbate the issue by interacting unpredictably with these noisy weights. The result is a training dynamic that eventually spirals into irreversible model collapse.

## The Solution: Aligning Rewards and Optimization Units

The core insight driving GSPO is simple yet profound: the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward. In most RL setups for LLMs, the verifier assigns a single reward score to an entire completed sequence, not to individual tokens. Therefore, applying off-policy corrections at the token level creates a mismatch that destabilizes training.

GSPO resolves this by moving the importance weighting and clipping operations to the sequence level. Instead of calculating a ratio for every token, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio for the entire response $y_i$ based on sequence likelihood:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

Notice the length normalization term $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$. This is crucial because raw sequence likelihoods can vary wildly depending on how long the response is. By taking the geometric mean (via the exponent), GSPO keeps the importance ratios within a manageable numerical range regardless of sequence length.

The optimization objective then becomes:

$$ \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x,\, \{y_i\}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i},  \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right] $$

In this formulation, clipping is applied to the entire response rather than individual tokens. If a whole sequence is deemed too "off-policy," it is down-weighted uniformly. This approach eliminates the accumulation of token-level noise and aligns perfectly with the sequence-level reward signal.

## Gradient Stability and Efficiency

When we analyze the gradients produced by these two approaches, the difference becomes stark. In GRPO, every token in a sentence contributes to the gradient with a different weight based on its specific importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Since these ratios are noisy estimates derived from single samples, they create an uneven and unstable landscape for optimization. Some tokens might be overweighted while others are underweighted purely due to stochastic variance, leading to erratic updates.

In contrast, GSPO weights all tokens within a specific response equally during the gradient calculation. The sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a global scaler for that specific trajectory's contribution to the loss. This removes the internal inconsistency found in token-level methods. Empirically, this leads to surprising results: GSPO often clips a significantly larger fraction of tokens compared to GRPO—sometimes two orders of magnitude more—yet it achieves higher training efficiency and better final performance. This counter-intuitive finding suggests that GRPO was wasting computation on noisy gradient signals from tokens that shouldn't have been trusted in isolation, whereas GSPO focuses only on reliable, coherent learning signals.

## Unlocking Stable MoE Training

One of the most significant practical victories for GSPO is its ability to stabilize training for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without complex workarounds. MoE models activate only a subset of parameters (experts) for any given input. # Supercharging MoE Models: How GSPO Eliminates the Need for Complex Workarounds

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a cornerstone of modern large language model architecture, offering impressive capabilities by activating only a subset of parameters for any given input. However, when it comes to scaling these models using Reinforcement Learning (RL), engineers have hit a significant wall. The very sparsity that makes MoE models efficient also introduces a phenomenon known as "expert-activation volatility," which often leads to catastrophic training instability. Until recently, mitigating this required complex, resource-heavy engineering hacks.

A new algorithm called Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) changes the game entirely. By shifting the focus from token-level calculations to sequence-level optimization, GSPO naturally resolves the stability issues plaguing MoE RL training. This approach not only eliminates the need for cumbersome workarounds like "Routing Replay" but also served as a critical component in achieving the remarkable performance gains seen in the latest Qwen3 models.

## The Instability Problem in MoE RL Training

To understand why GSPO is such a breakthrough, we first need to look at why standard RL algorithms struggle with MoE architectures. Popular methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) rely on calculating importance ratios at every single token position. Mathematically, this involves comparing the probability of generating a specific token $y_t$ under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ versus an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The ratio is defined as $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$.

In dense models, this calculation is relatively stable. However, in MoE models, the set of active experts can shift dramatically between gradient updates. Research shows that for deep MoE models, roughly 10% of the experts activated for the same response can change after just one update step. Because different experts have different parameters, this shift causes the token-level probabilities—and consequently the importance ratios—to fluctuate wildly. These high-variance noises accumulate over long sequences, eventually causing the model to collapse irreversibly.

Previously, the industry solution to this problem was a strategy called "Routing Replay." This technique involved caching which experts were activated under the old policy and forcing the new policy to use those exact same experts when calculating importance ratios. While effective at stabilizing training, Routing Replay adds significant memory and communication overhead. More importantly, it artificially constrains the model, preventing it from leveraging its full dynamic capacity during optimization.

## The GSPO Solution: Thinking in Sequences

GSPO addresses this root cause by fundamentally changing the unit of optimization. Instead of treating every token as an independent sample for importance weighting, GSPO recognizes that rewards in language tasks are assigned to entire sequences. Therefore, the importance ratio should also be calculated at the sequence level.

The core innovation of GSPO is defining the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on the geometric mean of token likelihoods across the whole response $y_i$:

$$
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
$$

This formula essentially averages out the noise inherent in individual token predictions. While specific experts might swap in and out causing local volatility at certain token positions $t$, the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)$ remains remarkably stable because the model retains its general language modeling capability regardless of which specific path through the experts was taken.

By applying clipping and optimization to this sequence-level ratio rather than individual tokens, GSPO filters out outliers where the new policy has drifted too far from the old one, without being misled by transient expert-switching noise. This alignment between the unit of reward (the sequence) and the unit of optimization ensures that gradient updates are robust and meaningful.

## Eliminating Routing Replay

The practical implications of this theoretical shift are profound. Because GSPO relies on aggregate sequence likelihoods rather than fragile token-level ratios, it renders expert-activation volatility irrelevant. The algorithm converges normally even when different sets of experts are activated between steps, completely removing the need for Routing Replay.

Removing Routing Replay offers three distinct advantages:
*   **Simplified Infrastructure:** Engineers no longer need to implement complex caching mechanisms to track and replay routing decisions across distributed systems.
*   **Reduced Overhead:** The elimination of routing synchronization reduces both memory consumption and communication costs during training.
*   **Full Model Capacity:** Without artificial constraints forcing specific expert pathways, the MoE model can optimize freely, utilizing its full architectural potential to learn complex reasoning patterns.

Empirical results confirm that GSPO achieves superior training efficiency and stability compared to GRPO, even while clipping a significantly larger fraction of tokens. This counter-intuitive finding highlights that GRPO's token-level signals were often too noisy to be useful, whereas GSPO's sequence-level signals provide a cleaner guide for optimization.

# Less Computation, Better Results: The Counter-Intuitive Efficiency of GSPO

In the world of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Large Language Models (LLMs), conventional wisdom suggests that maximizing data usage is paramount. The logic seems sound: if you generate thousands of tokens during a rollout, you should utilize every single one of them to update your model weights. Discarding data feels wasteful, akin to throwing away valuable training signal. However, recent research into Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) reveals a startling contradiction to this belief. It turns out that ignoring a massive portion of your data—specifically, clipping entire sequences rather than individual tokens—can actually lead to faster training, better performance, and significantly more stable models.

This counter-intuitive finding sits at the heart of GSPO, a new algorithm designed to address the critical instability issues plaguing current state-of-the-art methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). By shifting the focus from noisy token-level calculations to robust sequence-level metrics, GSPO not only stabilizes the training of gigantic models but also simplifies the underlying infrastructure required to run them.

## The Problem with Token-Level Noise

To understand why discarding data helps, we must first look at why keeping it all hurts. Current algorithms like GRPO rely on token-level importance sampling. When updating a policy, these methods calculate an importance ratio for every single token $y_t$ in a generated response. Mathematically, this ratio is defined as $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$. Ideally, this weight corrects for the fact that the data was generated by an older version of the model.

However, in practice, applying this correction at the token level is fundamentally flawed when dealing with long sequences and large batches. Because each weight is derived from a single sample rather than an average over many samples, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. As the response length increases, this noise accumulates. When combined with the standard clipping mechanism used to prevent drastic updates, this noise doesn't just persist; it amplifies. The result is often catastrophic model collapse, where the model's capabilities degrade irreversibly, rendering further training useless regardless of hyperparameter tuning.

## The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Stability

GSPO addresses this by realigning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. In most RL setups for language models, the reward is assigned to the entire sequence $y$, not to individual tokens. Therefore, it makes theoretical sense to perform importance sampling and clipping at the sequence level as well.

Instead of calculating a unique weight for every token, GSPO defines a single importance ratio for the entire response based on sequence likelihood:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

Here, the ratio represents how much the probability of generating the whole sequence has changed under the new policy compared to the old one. The exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ serves as a length normalization factor, ensuring that the metric remains stable regardless of whether the response is short or long. By using this sequence-level ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ for clipping and weighting, GSPO treats all tokens within a valid sequence equally during the gradient update. This eliminates the erratic weighting found in GRPO, where some tokens might be heavily up-weighted while others are down-weighted based on noisy local estimates.

## The Paradox of Clipping More to Learn Faster

The most striking experimental result from the GSPO paper is what happens when we compare the fraction of data utilized during training. In traditional GRPO implementations, clipping occurs at the token level. If a specific token's importance ratio falls outside a narrow range (e.g., $[0.8, 1.2]$), only that specific token's contribution to the loss is clipped or ignored, while the rest of the sentence continues to influence the update.

In contrast, GSPO applies clipping to the entire sequence. If the sequence-level ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ falls outside its designated range (which is typically much tighter, around $1 \pm 4\times10^{-4}$), the *entire* response is excluded from the gradient calculation. One might expect this aggressive filtering to starve the model of data and slow down convergence dramatically.

The reality is exactly the opposite. Experiments show that GSPO clips approximately two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO. Despite discarding vastly more data points per iteration, GSPO achieves superior training efficiency and reaches higher benchmark scores on tasks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench in less time. This paradox confirms that the token-level signals discarded by GSPO were not useful learning signals at all; they were primarily noise that hindered optimization. By filtering out these unreliable sequences entirely, GSPO provides a cleaner, more consistent gradient direction, allowing the model to learn effectively from fewer but higher-quality updates.
