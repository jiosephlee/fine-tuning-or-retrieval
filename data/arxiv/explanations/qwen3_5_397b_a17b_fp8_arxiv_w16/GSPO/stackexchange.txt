\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ include a $1/|y_i|$ exponent?
Question:
In the GSPO algorithm section, the importance ratio is defined as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. I understand that $\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)$ is the product of token probabilities, which gets extremely small for long sequences. However, why do we take the geometric mean (via the $1/|y_i|$ exponent) instead of just using the raw sequence likelihood ratio? Is this purely to prevent numerical underflow, or does it serve a specific purpose in controlling the variance of the estimator relative to sequence length?
Answer:
The inclusion of the $1/|y_i|$ exponent in the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ serves a critical dual purpose: it transforms the raw likelihood ratio into a geometric mean to stabilize the numerical range of the estimator, and it explicitly reduces the variance of the training signal that would otherwise explode with sequence length. As noted in Section 3.1 of the paper, if one were to use the raw sequence likelihood ratio $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)}$ without normalization, the metric would be dominated by the cumulative product of token probabilities. Since autoregressive likelihoods are products of values less than one, this raw ratio becomes extremely sensitive to sequence length; even minor deviations in token probabilities accumulate multiplicatively over long responses, causing the ratio to fluctuate dramatically or vanish entirely. This would render a fixed clipping range $\varepsilon$ ineffective, as responses of different lengths would inherently occupy vastly different numerical scales, requiring length-specific hyperparameters.

By raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO effectively computes the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios, as shown by the identity $s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$. This operation ensures that $s_i(\theta)$ represents an average per-token deviation rather than a total sequence deviation, keeping the importance ratios within a unified and manageable numerical range regardless of whether the response is short or long. The paper explicitly states that this length normalization is adopted "to reduce the variance and to control $s_{i}(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range," preventing situations where likelihood changes in just a few tokens cause disproportionate swings in the global ratio. Furthermore, this formulation aligns with the gradient structure derived in Section 3.2; when computing $\nabla_{\theta} \log s_{i}(\theta)$, the $1/|y_i|$ factor naturally emerges to weight all tokens in the response equally. This stands in stark contrast to GRPO, where unequal token-level weights accumulate unpredictably, a flaw that GSPO corrects by ensuring the optimization signal remains stable and consistent across varying sequence lengths.

### How exactly does 'expert-activation volatility' invalidate token-level importance weights in MoE models?
Question:
The paper mentions that for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models trained with GRPO, roughly 10% of activated experts change between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$ for the same token. The authors claim this makes the token-level ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }$ fluctuate drastically. Could someone explain the mechanism here? If an expert changes, doesn't the new expert simply output a probability for that token? Why does swapping the underlying network component cause the *ratio* to become unstable or meaningless compared to the dense model case?
Answer:
The instability arises because the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ in GRPO acts as a direct multiplier for the gradient of individual tokens, making it hypersensitive to discrete structural changes in the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture that do not necessarily reflect a meaningful shift in the model's overall policy. In an MoE model, the probability of a token $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | \dots)$ is determined by a specific subset of active experts selected by a routing mechanism. The paper notes that between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the updated policy $\pi_{\theta}$, approximately 10% of these activated experts change for the same token. When an expert swaps out, the underlying neural network component computing that probability changes entirely. Even if the new expert produces a probability value reasonably close to the old one, the sudden discontinuity in the function approximator causes the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | \dots) }$ to fluctuate drastically and unpredictably.

This volatility invalidates the importance weight because, in the context of Reinforcement Learning with off-policy data, these weights are intended to correct for distributional shifts smoothly. However, when driven by expert swapping rather than gradual parameter updates, the ratio ceases to represent a stable measure of "how much the policy has changed" and instead becomes high-variance noise. As described in the gradient analysis section of the paper, GRPO weighs the gradient of each token's log-likelihood by this volatile $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. Consequently, tokens associated with swapped experts receive erratic gradient signals—either vanishing or exploding—which accumulate over long sequences and destabilize training, often leading to model collapse. This forces practitioners to use complex workarounds like "Routing Replay," which artificially freezes the routing decisions of $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ during the calculation for $\pi_{\theta}$ to ensure the same experts are used, thereby masking the root issue.

In contrast, GSPO resolves this by shifting the unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level. Instead of relying on unstable individual ratios, GSPO utilizes a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ derived from the geometric mean of token probabilities (or equivalently, the ratio of sequence likelihoods raised to the power of $1/|y_i|$). While individual expert activations may flip and cause local turbulence in token probabilities, the aggregate sequence likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x)$ remains robust because the model maintains its overall language modeling capability across different expert combinations. By averaging these effects over the entire sequence before applying clipping and weighting, GSPO smooths out the noise introduced by expert-activation volatility. This allows the algorithm to converge stably without needing to enforce consistent routing paths, effectively decoupling training stability from the sparsity-induced discontinuities inherent in MoE architectures.

### Is GSPO-token mathematically equivalent to GSPO when advantages are uniform?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the paper introduces GSPO-token with a modified importance weight $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t}|\dots) }{ \mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t}|\dots)] }$. The text states this term is numerically equal to 1, making $s_{i,t}$ equal to $s_i$. However, looking at the gradient derivation in Eq. 9, it seems the stop-gradient operations are crucial. If I implement this in PyTorch, does `sg[s_i] * (prob / sg[prob])` truly result in the exact same computational graph and gradient flow as the original GSPO objective when $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$? I am worried I might be missing a subtle difference in how the autograd engine handles the division by a detached value.
Answer:
Yes, your intuition is correct: when the advantages are uniform across all tokens in a sequence (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), GSPO-token is mathematically equivalent to GSPO in terms of both the objective value and the resulting gradients. The specific construction of the importance weight $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ using stop-gradient operations is a clever implementation trick designed to decouple the scalar magnitude of the sequence-level ratio from the token-level probability distribution during backpropagation, ensuring the gradient flows exactly as intended without altering the numerical loss.

To understand why this works, consider the definition provided in Equation 14: $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t}|\dots) }{ \mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t}|\dots)] }$. Numerically, the term $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} }{ \mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}] }$ evaluates to exactly $1$ because the numerator and denominator hold identical floating-point values at the time of computation; the stop-gradient operator (`sg` or `detach`) only affects the computational graph, not the forward pass value. Consequently, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically identical to $s_i(\theta)$, meaning the loss computed in Equation 13 is indistinguishable from the GSPO loss in Equation 7 when advantages are constant.

The crucial distinction lies in how automatic differentiation engines like PyTorch handle the gradient of this product. When you compute the gradient $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)$, the product rule applies. However, because the second factor involves a division by a detached term, its derivative with respect to $\theta$ simplifies significantly. Specifically, treating $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ as a constant $C$ and $\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}]$ as a constant $D$, the term becomes $C \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta}{D}$. The gradient with respect to $\pi_\theta$ is simply $\frac{C}{D}$. Since $C = s_i(\theta)$ and $D = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)$ (numerically), this ratio $\frac{s_i(\theta)}{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|\dots)}$ acts as a scaling factor for the gradient of the token probability.

When you substitute this back into the full gradient derivation shown in Equation 16, this scaling factor combines with the derivative of the log-probability inherent in policy gradient methods. The math works out such that $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta = \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta}{\pi_\theta}$. The construction effectively cancels out the explicit dependence on the individual token probability in the weighting term, leaving only the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ multiplying the sum of token-level log-gradients. As explicitly stated below Equation 16 in Section 3.3, this results in a gradient expression identical to that of GSPO (Equation 9) under uniform advantages.

Therefore, implementing `sg[s_i] * (prob / sg[prob])` in PyTorch does indeed reproduce the exact same computational graph behavior as the original GSPO objective for this specific case. The stop-gradient on the denominator prevents gradients from flowing through the "normalization" part of that fraction, while stop-gradient on $s_i$ ensures that variations in individual token probabilities do not retroactively alter the sequence-level scalar weight during that specific update step. This formulation essentially hard-codes the mathematical property that "all tokens share one sequence-level weight" directly into the autograd graph, allowing GSPO-token to serve as a generalized framework that collapses perfectly back to GSPO when token-specific advantage customization is not required.

### Why does clipping entire sequences (discarding more data) lead to higher training efficiency?
Question:
Section 4.2 notes a 'curious observation': GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO because it discards an entire sequence if *any* part of it falls outside the clip range. Intuitively, throwing away more data should slow down convergence or require more samples. Yet, the paper claims GSPO is *more* efficient. Does this imply that the gradients from the 'partially clipped' sequences in GRPO are actually harmful (high variance noise) rather than just less useful? How does removing these noisy signals accelerate learning enough to offset the loss of sample count?
Answer:
The counter-intuitive observation that discarding significantly more data leads to higher training efficiency stems from the fact that the "extra" data retained by GRPO is not merely less useful, but actively detrimental due to high-variance noise introduced by its token-level importance weighting. As detailed in Section 4.2 ("Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions") and Section 3 ("Motivation"), GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens because it discards an entire sequence if its global sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside the clipping range, whereas GRPO only clips individual tokens where the local ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ exceeds the threshold.

The core issue lies in the theoretical validity of the importance sampling weights used for off-policy correction. Section 3 explains that valid importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples to correct for distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies weights based on single token samples $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. This misapplication fails to perform proper distribution correction and instead injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. Crucially, Section 3.2 ("Gradient Analysis") demonstrates that in GRPO, every token in a sequence is weighted differently by these unstable factors, causing errors to accumulate over long sequences and leading to unpredictable gradient directions. In contrast, GSPO utilizes a sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$, which effectively weights all tokens within a response equally. This eliminates the intra-sequence variance caused by fluctuating token-level ratios.

Consequently, the gradients computed from the "partially clipped" sequences in GRPO are indeed harmful rather than just inefficient. The paper notes in Section 4.2 that this noise is so severe that it can cause catastrophic and irreversible model collapse, particularly in large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models where expert activation volatility further destabilizes token-level probabilities (Section 4.3). By aggressively filtering out any sequence that has drifted too far from the behavior policy (indicated by the sequence-level ratio), GSPO ensures that every gradient update is derived from a statistically reliable signal. Therefore, although GSPO processes fewer total tokens per batch, the quality of each update is sufficiently higher that the model converges faster and more stably, offsetting the reduction in sample count. Essentially, GSPO trades quantity for fidelity, avoiding the accumulation of noise that plagues GRPO's token-level approach.

### What is the theoretical justification for claiming GRPO's token-level weighting is 'ill-posed'?
Question:
The Motivation section argues that GRPO is ill-posed because importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution to correct distributional mismatch, but GRPO applies weights to single token samples ($N=1$ per position). Standard PPO also uses token-level weights on single samples without collapsing immediately. What is the specific mathematical breakdown that occurs in GRPO's formulation due to this 'single sample' issue that doesn't occur in standard PPO? Is it strictly because GRPO lacks a value function baseline to reduce variance, or is there a fundamental bias introduced by applying sequence-level rewards to token-level weights?
Answer:
The claim that GRPO's token-level weighting is "ill-posed" stems from a fundamental violation of the statistical requirements for importance sampling when applied to single-sample estimates at the token level, rather than simply a lack of a value function baseline. As detailed in the Motivation section of the paper, the mathematical principle of importance sampling relies on estimating the expectation of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. Crucially, this correction is theoretically valid only when averaging over multiple samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution to accurately represent the distributional mismatch.

The specific breakdown in GRPO occurs because it applies an importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ to every single token position $t$ based on exactly one sample ($N=1$) drawn from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Since there is no ensemble of samples at that specific timestep to average over, the weight fails to perform its intended role of correcting the distribution. Instead of reducing bias, this single-sample ratio acts as high-variance noise. The paper argues that while standard PPO also uses token-level weights, it mitigates this through a learned value function baseline that stabilizes the advantage estimate; GRPO removes this baseline and replaces it with a group-relative advantage, leaving the noisy token-level weights unchecked.

This noise is not static; it accumulates multiplicatively over the length of the sequence. In long-response tasks, these erratic weights compound, leading to unstable gradients that are further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism intended to constrain updates. The Gradient Analysis section illustrates this by contrasting the gradient formulations: GRPO weights individual token log-likelihood gradients by their respective volatile ratios, whereas GSPO utilizes a sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ that effectively averages the log-probabilities before exponentiation. This sequence-level approach ensures that all tokens in a response contribute equally to the gradient direction, weighted only by the overall quality of the entire sequence relative to the old policy. Consequently, the "ill-posed" nature refers to the mismatch between the unit of reward (the whole sequence) and the unit of off-policy correction (individual tokens), which introduces irreducible variance that leads to the catastrophic model collapse observed in large-scale training, particularly in Mixture-of-Experts models where expert activation volatility makes token-level ratios even more unreliable.

### How do precision discrepancies between inference and training engines affect likelihood recomputation?
Question:
In Section 4.4, the authors suggest GSPO allows skipping likelihood recomputation on the training engine because sequence-level likelihoods are more tolerant of precision errors between engines (like vLLM vs. Megatron). Since sequence likelihood is just the product of token likelihoods ($ \prod p_t $), wouldn't small precision errors at each token step compound multiplicatively into a massive error for long sequences? Why would the aggregated sequence metric be *more* robust to floating-point differences than checking individual token probabilities?
Answer:
The intuition that multiplicative errors must necessarily compound into massive deviations relies on the assumption that precision discrepancies act as systematic biases that accumulate in a single direction. However, in the context of floating-point arithmetic differences between inference engines (like vLLM) and training engines (like Megatron), the discrepancies are typically stochastic noise centered around zero rather than consistent drifts. When you compute the sequence likelihood as a product of token probabilities $\prod p_t$, you are effectively summing their logarithms $\sum \log p_t$. If the precision error at each token step is a random variable with mean zero, the Central Limit Theorem suggests that while the variance of the sum grows with sequence length, the relative error of the aggregate sum tends to stabilize or grow much slower than a worst-case multiplicative explosion would imply.

More importantly, the GSPO algorithm explicitly mitigates this sensitivity through its definition of the importance ratio. As shown in Equation 5 of the paper, GSPO does not use the raw sequence likelihood ratio directly; instead, it employs a length-normalized geometric mean: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root of the likelihood ratio, the algorithm effectively averages the log-probability differences across all tokens in the sequence. This operation divides any accumulated error by the sequence length, preventing long sequences from exhibiting disproportionately large deviations in their importance ratios due to minor per-token precision mismatches.

Furthermore, the robustness stems from what the algorithm actually optimizes. Section 4.4 highlights that GSPO focuses on sequence-level metrics rather than individual token probabilities. In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models specifically, the paper notes that token-level likelihoods can fluctuate drastically due to "expert-activation volatility," where different experts are activated for the same token under slightly different conditions or precisions. These local fluctuations invalidate token-level importance weights used in algorithms like GRPO. However, because the model maintains its overall language modeling capability, the aggregate sequence likelihood remains stable even if individual token paths vary slightly due to precision or routing differences. Since GSPO's clipping and optimization operate on this stable, aggregated sequence signal (Equation 3), it tolerates the low-level numerical noise that would otherwise corrupt token-wise objectives. This tolerance is what allows practitioners to skip expensive likelihood recomputation on the training engine and trust the values provided by faster inference engines, significantly simplifying RL infrastructure without sacrificing stability.

### Why are the optimal clipping ranges for GSPO ($3e^{-4}$) so much smaller than for GRPO ($0.2$)?
Question:
The experimental setup specifies clipping ranges of roughly $0.0003$ for GSPO versus $0.2$ for GRPO. Given that both use a min/clip objective structure, why is there such a vast difference in scale? Is this solely because $s_i(\theta)$ is a geometric mean (which stays close to 1) whereas token ratios can vary wildly? If I were to tune GSPO on a new dataset, should I expect these hyperparameters to remain in the $10^{-4}$ range regardless of model size, or does this scale with something else?
Answer:
The vast difference in optimal clipping ranges between GSPO ($3 \times 10^{-4}$) and GRPO ($0.2$) stems directly from the mathematical definition of their respective importance ratios and the statistical effect of length normalization. Your intuition that the geometric mean keeps the ratio close to 1 is correct, but the underlying reason involves how probability products behave over long sequences compared to individual token probabilities.

In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated at the token level as $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. Since this compares two conditional probabilities for a single token, the ratio can naturally fluctuate significantly depending on how much the policy has shifted for that specific next-token distribution. A shift where the new policy assigns double the probability to a token compared to the old policy results in a ratio of $2.0$, while halving it results in $0.5$. Consequently, a clipping range of $\varepsilon = 0.2$ (creating a window of $[0.8, 1.2]$) is necessary to allow for reasonable policy updates while preventing extreme outliers.

In contrast, GSPO defines the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as the geometric mean of the token-level ratios across the entire sequence: $s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$. The paper explicitly states that this length normalization is adopted to "reduce the variance and to control $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range." Mathematically, taking the $|y_i|$-th root of a product (or averaging the logs) acts as a powerful variance reducer via the Law of Large Numbers. Even if individual token ratios vary wildly, their geometric mean over a long response tends to converge very tightly toward 1 unless the *entire* sequence distribution has shifted coherently. A deviation of $0.2$ in this geometric mean would imply a massive, exponential divergence in the total sequence likelihood, which represents a catastrophic policy shift rather than a standard update step. Therefore, the "signal" for a meaningful update exists in a much narrower band around 1, necessitating a clipping range orders of magnitude smaller ($3 \times 10^{-4}$) to effectively distinguish between normal noise and significant off-policy deviations.

Regarding your question on tuning for new datasets or model sizes, you should generally expect these hyperparameters to remain in the $10^{-4}$ range provided you are dealing with typical response lengths found in LLM reasoning tasks. The scale of $s_i(\theta)$ is intrinsically tied to the sequence length $|y_i|$; as the paper notes, without length normalization, "importance ratios of responses with different lengths will require varying clipping ranges." Because GSPO includes the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ term, it normalizes this effect, making the metric relatively stable across different context lengths compared to raw likelihood ratios. However, if you were to train on extremely short sequences (e.g., single-token outputs), the variance reduction benefit of the geometric mean would diminish, and you might need to increase $\varepsilon$. Conversely, for very long reasoning chains, the ratio becomes even more concentrated near 1, potentially validating even tighter clipping. The key takeaway from the paper is that GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a "more reliable and effective learning signal" precisely because it filters out the high-frequency noise inherent in token-level updates, allowing stable training even when discarding a larger fraction of samples via clipping.
