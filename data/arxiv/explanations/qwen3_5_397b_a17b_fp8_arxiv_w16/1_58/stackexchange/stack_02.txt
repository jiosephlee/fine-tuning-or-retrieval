\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does the absmean quantization function use a scalar $\gamma$ instead of per-channel scaling?
Question:
In Equation (3), the scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as the average absolute value of the entire weight matrix ($\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$). Most modern quantization techniques (like per-channel quantization in LLaMA) use a separate scale for each output channel to preserve accuracy. Why does BitNet b1.58 choose a single global scalar for the whole matrix? Doesn't this risk losing significant information in channels with vastly different magnitudes compared to the global average?
Answer:
The choice of a single global scalar $\gamma$ in BitNet b1.58, rather than per-channel scaling, is a deliberate architectural decision driven by the goal of maximizing hardware efficiency and simplifying the computation paradigm for 1-bit models. While per-channel quantization is indeed standard in post-training quantization methods (like those used in LLaMA variants) to preserve accuracy by adapting to local weight distributions, BitNet b1.58 operates under a different constraint: it is trained from scratch with ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ specifically to enable a multiplication-free matrix multiplication process.

The core motivation lies in the definition of the quantization function presented in Equation (3), where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. By using a single scalar for the entire weight matrix $W$, the quantized operation $\widetilde{W}X$ can be mathematically restructured to eliminate multiplications entirely during inference. If per-channel scales were used, the inference engine would need to perform a multiplication between the activation values and a unique scale factor for every output channel, effectively reintroducing the floating-point or fixed-point multiplication costs that BitNet aims to eradicate. With a global $\gamma$, the scaling factor can be absorbed or applied uniformly, allowing the heavy lifting of the matrix multiplication to rely solely on integer additions and bit-wise operations, which corresponds to the paper's claim of saving "orders of energy cost."

Furthermore, the paper suggests that because BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch rather than quantized after training, the network learns to adapt its weight distribution to this global normalization constraint. The inclusion of the $0$ value in the ternary set provides an explicit mechanism for feature filtering, which compensates for the lack of granularity in scaling. The empirical results shown in the paper support this trade-off; despite using a coarse global scalar, BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and end-task performance of full-precision FP16 baselines starting at the 3B parameter scale. This indicates that within the context of a model designed specifically for 1.58-bit representation, the representational capacity lost by not using per-channel scales is recovered through the training dynamics and the sparsity introduced by the zero weights, ultimately yielding a Pareto improvement in latency, memory, and energy without sacrificing final model quality.
