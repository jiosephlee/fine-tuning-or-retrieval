\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is the precise relationship between model size and the "crossover point" where b1.58 outperforms FP16?
Question:
The Results section claims that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting from a 3B model size, and even outperforms a 3B FP16 model at 3.9B parameters. However, for smaller models (implied <3B), there seems to be a gap. What drives this scaling behavior? Is it that larger matrices allow the statistical approximation of $\gamma$ (the absmean scaler) to become more accurate, thereby mitigating quantization noise? Or does the capacity of larger models simply absorb the information loss better?
Answer:
The crossover point where BitNet b1.58 matches and eventually surpasses FP16 performance around the 3B parameter mark is driven primarily by the model's capacity to absorb quantization noise rather than a statistical refinement of the scaling factor $\gamma$. While your intuition regarding the `absmean` scaler is logical, the paper indicates that $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|$ is a deterministic aggregation over the entire weight matrix; even in smaller models, this average is statistically robust enough to provide a stable scaling baseline. The bottleneck for smaller architectures is not the accuracy of this scalar estimation, but rather the sheer representational limitation imposed by constraining every weight to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

In smaller models (those under 3B parameters), the network lacks sufficient degrees of freedom to compensate for the information loss inherent in extreme low-bit quantization. When a model is small, every parameter carries a disproportionately high weight in defining the decision boundaries, and forcing these parameters into discrete bins introduces errors that the limited architecture cannot correct. However, as the model size scales up, the redundancy and over-parameterization typical of large language models allow BitNet b1.58 to distribute the necessary representation across a vast number of ternary weights. Essentially, larger models can "brute force" their way through the precision deficit by utilizing their expanded capacity to approximate complex functions that smaller ternary models simply cannot capture.

This dynamic creates a scaling law unique to 1-bit architectures where the performance gap narrows strictly as a function of size. The paper highlights that once the model reaches approximately 3B parameters, this capacity becomes sufficient to match the perplexity and end-task accuracy of full-precision baselines trained on the same data. Beyond this threshold, such as at the 3.9B mark mentioned in the results, BitNet b1.58 begins to outperform its FP16 counterpart. This reversal suggests that at sufficient scale, the explicit feature filtering enabled by the inclusion of $0$ in the weight domain (allowing the model to effectively ignore certain connections) combined with massive parallelism provides a regularization effect or structural advantage that dense floating-point models of equivalent parameter count lack. Consequently, the crossover is less about the mathematical convergence of the quantization statistics and more about reaching a critical mass of parameters where the discrete topology can fully emulate continuous precision dynamics.
