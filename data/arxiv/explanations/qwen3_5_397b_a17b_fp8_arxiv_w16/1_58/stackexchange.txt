\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How exactly is "1.58 bits" calculated for a ternary weight set \{-1, 0, 1\}?
Question:
The paper states that adding the value 0 to the original binary \{-1, 1\} set results in "1.58 bits." I understand that standard binary is $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit, but I am confused about the math here. Is this simply $\log_2(3)$? If so, why do we describe it as "bits" in the context of storage if hardware typically allocates whole integers (like 2 bits) for three states? Does this imply a specific packing scheme where multiple weights are stored together to achieve this average?
Answer:
The value of "$1.58$ bits" cited in the paper is indeed derived directly from the information theoretic entropy of a ternary system, calculated as $\log_2(3)$. In information theory, the minimum number of bits required to uniquely represent one symbol chosen from a set of $N$ equally probable possibilities is $\log_2(N)$. Since the BitNet b1.58 architecture constrains every weight parameter to exactly three distinct states $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the theoretical lower bound for storage per weight is $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496$, which the authors round to $1.58$. This stands in contrast to standard binary quantization (used in the original BitNet), which utilizes only two states $\{-1, 1\}$ and thus requires exactly $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit per weight.

Your confusion regarding hardware allocation versus this fractional bit count is astute and touches on the difference between theoretical entropy and practical implementation on current commodity hardware. You are correct that standard computer memory cannot physically allocate $1.58$ bits to a single variable; memory is addressed in bytes, and individual elements are typically stored in integer multiples of bits (e.g., storing a ternary value in a standard `int8` would waste $6.22$ bits per weight). However, describing the model as "$1.58$-bit" does not imply that every individual weight occupies a fractional physical space in isolation. Rather, it describes the aggregate storage density achievable when packing large tensors of these weights together using specialized encoding schemes. Just as you can store multiple boolean flags within a single byte to achieve an average of $1$ bit per flag, specialized packing kernels can encode sequences of ternary values into dense bitstreams. For instance, by grouping multiple ternary weights together, one can map their combined state space ($3^k$ possibilities for $k$ weights) onto a binary buffer close to the theoretical limit of $k \times \log_2(3)$ total bits.

The significance of highlighting "$1.58$ bits" in the paper extends beyond mere storage arithmetic; it serves as a marker for a new scaling law and efficiency frontier. The inclusion of the zero state is not just a mathematical curiosity but a functional mechanism for "feature filtering," allowing the network to explicitly ignore certain connections (multiplication by zero) which simplifies the computation paradigm to mostly integer additions. When the authors claim improvements in memory footprint and bandwidth (such as the reported $3.55$x reduction compared to FP16 baselines), they are projecting the performance of systems utilizing these optimal packing strategies or custom hardware designed specifically for this ternary format. While current general-purpose GPUs might initially simulate this using $2$-bit or $8$-bit kernels (as noted in their results section where they mention using a "$2$-bit kernel"), the "$1.58$-bit" designation defines the intrinsic information content of the model and sets the target for future hardware optimization where memory buses and caches are tailored to transfer exactly this amount of information per weight, eliminating the inefficiency of padding to whole integer bit-widths.

### Why does the absmean quantization function use a scalar $\gamma$ instead of per-channel scaling?
Question:
In Equation (3), the scaling factor $\gamma$ is defined as the average absolute value of the entire weight matrix ($\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$). Most modern quantization techniques (like per-channel quantization in LLaMA) use a separate scale for each output channel to preserve accuracy. Why does BitNet b1.58 choose a single global scalar for the whole matrix? Doesn't this risk losing significant information in channels with vastly different magnitudes compared to the global average?
Answer:
The choice of a single global scalar $\gamma$ in BitNet b1.58, rather than per-channel scaling, is a deliberate architectural decision driven by the goal of maximizing hardware efficiency and simplifying the computation paradigm for 1-bit models. While per-channel quantization is indeed standard in post-training quantization methods (like those used in LLaMA variants) to preserve accuracy by adapting to local weight distributions, BitNet b1.58 operates under a different constraint: it is trained from scratch with ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ specifically to enable a multiplication-free matrix multiplication process.

The core motivation lies in the definition of the quantization function presented in Equation (3), where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$. By using a single scalar for the entire weight matrix $W$, the quantized operation $\widetilde{W}X$ can be mathematically restructured to eliminate multiplications entirely during inference. If per-channel scales were used, the inference engine would need to perform a multiplication between the activation values and a unique scale factor for every output channel, effectively reintroducing the floating-point or fixed-point multiplication costs that BitNet aims to eradicate. With a global $\gamma$, the scaling factor can be absorbed or applied uniformly, allowing the heavy lifting of the matrix multiplication to rely solely on integer additions and bit-wise operations, which corresponds to the paper's claim of saving "orders of energy cost."

Furthermore, the paper suggests that because BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch rather than quantized after training, the network learns to adapt its weight distribution to this global normalization constraint. The inclusion of the $0$ value in the ternary set provides an explicit mechanism for feature filtering, which compensates for the lack of granularity in scaling. The empirical results shown in the paper support this trade-off; despite using a coarse global scalar, BitNet b1.58 matches the perplexity and end-task performance of full-precision FP16 baselines starting at the 3B parameter scale. This indicates that within the context of a model designed specifically for 1.58-bit representation, the representational capacity lost by not using per-channel scales is recovered through the training dynamics and the sparsity introduced by the zero weights, ultimately yielding a Pareto improvement in latency, memory, and energy without sacrificing final model quality.

### If weights are ternary, why are activations kept at 8-bit integers instead of 1-bit?
Question:
The abstract mentions that every *parameter* is ternary, but Section 2 clarifies that the model uses "1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations." Since the main argument of the paper is reducing computation to integer additions, why haven't the authors quantized the activations to 1-bit or ternary as well? Is there a theoretical limitation preventing stable training with 1-bit activations, or is it purely an empirical choice to maintain perplexity scores?
Answer:
The decision to keep activations at 8-bit integers while restricting weights to ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in BitNet b1.58 is primarily an empirical design choice driven by the need to maintain model stability and perplexity scores comparable to full-precision baselines, rather than a strict theoretical impossibility of using lower-bit activations. As stated in Section 2, the model is explicitly trained with "1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations," and the results in Section 3 confirm that this specific configuration allows the model to match FP16 LLaMA performance starting at the 3B parameter scale. If the authors had quantized activations to 1-bit or ternary values during training, the representational capacity of the network would likely have been insufficient to converge to the same level of accuracy without significant architectural changes or algorithmic innovations not covered in this work.

The distinction between weights and activations serves different optimization goals within the paper's framework. The ternary weights are the key enabler for the new computation paradigm described in the introduction, where matrix multiplications are reduced to integer additions, yielding massive energy savings (up to 71.4 times less energy for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips). This weight quantization is static after training and directly dictates the inference cost structure. In contrast, activations are dynamic values generated during the forward pass that must capture complex, non-linear feature interactions. The paper notes in Section 2 that the activation quantization function scales values per token to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ to eliminate zero-point quantization, a method chosen for implementation simplicity and negligible performance impact. By retaining 8-bit precision for these dynamic values, the model preserves enough granularity to prevent error accumulation across layers, which is critical for deep networks like Transformers.

Furthermore, the choice of 8-bit activations already provides substantial practical benefits without compromising performance. In the "Discussion and Future Work" section under "Native Support of Long Sequence in LLMs," the authors highlight that reducing activations from the standard 16 bits to 8 bits effectively doubles the context length achievable with fixed resources. They explicitly mention that further compressing these activations to 4 bits or lower is left as "future work," implying that while lower-bit activations are a desirable goal for further efficiency, they were not necessary to achieve the primary breakthrough of matching full-precision perplexity with ternary weights. Therefore, the 8-bit activation scheme represents a pragmatic balance: it secures the immediate gains in memory bandwidth and KV cache size required for long-context support while ensuring the model remains trainable and competitive with FP16 counterparts, whereas moving to 1-bit activations would risk destabilizing training and degrading the end-task performance that BitNet b1.58 successfully demonstrates.

### How does Straight-Through Estimator (STE) work with the RoundClip function during backpropagation?
Question:
The forward pass uses the non-differentiable `RoundClip` function defined in Equation (2). The paper doesn't explicitly detail the backward pass strategy. Are they using the standard Straight-Through Estimator (STE) where gradients pass through unchanged? If so, given that the weights are clamped strictly to \{-1, 0, 1\}, how do the weights actually update during training? Do they maintain a full-precision shadow copy of weights that gets quantized only during the forward pass, or is there a different mechanism implied by "trained from scratch"?
Answer:
Yes, **BitNet b1.58** employs the standard Straight-Through Estimator (STE) for backpropagation, and crucially, it maintains a full-precision "shadow" copy of the weights that is updated during training while only the quantized version is used in the forward pass. Although the paper does not explicitly write out the gradient derivation equations, this mechanism is inherent to its description of being "trained from scratch" with ternary weights and aligns with the established methodology of its predecessor, BitNet, and similar low-bit literature.

The core confusion often arises from thinking that the discrete weights $\widetilde{W} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ themselves receive gradients and update directly. Mathematically, this is impossible because the derivative of the `RoundClip` function defined in Equation (2) is zero almost everywhere (where the function is flat) and undefined at the jump points. If one attempted to backpropagate through the discrete values directly, learning would halt immediately. To circumvent this, the model maintains a high-precision latent weight matrix $W$ (typically stored in FP16 or BF16) throughout the training process. During the forward pass, this latent $W$ is transformed into the ternary $\widetilde{W}$ via the absmean quantization function you cited: $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$, where $\gamma$ is the mean absolute value of $W$. The network performs computations using these discrete $\widetilde{W}$ values, which enables the efficient integer-only matrix multiplication highlighted in the paper's energy analysis.

During the backward pass, the STE approximation treats the `RoundClip` operation as an identity function within a specific range (usually $[-1, 1]$) and zero elsewhere. This allows gradients flowing from the loss function to pass through the quantization node as if it weren't there, effectively computing $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} \approx \frac{\partial L}{\partial \widetilde{W}} \cdot \mathbb{I}_{\left(-1 < \frac{W}{\gamma} < 1\right)}$. These gradients are then used to update the full-precision latent weights $W$ via standard optimizers like AdamW. The latent $W$ acts as an accumulator; small gradient updates shift $W$ gradually. Only when $W$ shifts sufficiently relative to the scaling factor $\gamma$ does the result of $\mathrm{round}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma}\right)$ flip to a different integer, thereby changing the discrete weight $\widetilde{W}$ used in subsequent forward passes. This decoupling allows the model to learn smooth representations in high-precision space while enforcing a strict ternary constraint on inference behavior.

This "train with shadows, infer with quantized weights" approach is what enables BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision perplexity despite having parameters restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The inclusion of $0$ in this specific variant adds sparsity (feature filtering), but it does not change the fundamental optimization dynamics provided by STE. Without maintaining these full-precision shadow weights initialized randomly and updated continuously from scratch—as opposed to trying to fine-tune already quantized weights—the model would lack the necessary resolution to converge to a competitive solution.

### What is the precise relationship between model size and the "crossover point" where b1.58 outperforms FP16?
Question:
The Results section claims that BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 performance starting from a 3B model size, and even outperforms a 3B FP16 model at 3.9B parameters. However, for smaller models (implied <3B), there seems to be a gap. What drives this scaling behavior? Is it that larger matrices allow the statistical approximation of $\gamma$ (the absmean scaler) to become more accurate, thereby mitigating quantization noise? Or does the capacity of larger models simply absorb the information loss better?
Answer:
The crossover point where BitNet b1.58 matches and eventually surpasses FP16 performance around the 3B parameter mark is driven primarily by the model's capacity to absorb quantization noise rather than a statistical refinement of the scaling factor $\gamma$. While your intuition regarding the `absmean` scaler is logical, the paper indicates that $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|$ is a deterministic aggregation over the entire weight matrix; even in smaller models, this average is statistically robust enough to provide a stable scaling baseline. The bottleneck for smaller architectures is not the accuracy of this scalar estimation, but rather the sheer representational limitation imposed by constraining every weight to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

In smaller models (those under 3B parameters), the network lacks sufficient degrees of freedom to compensate for the information loss inherent in extreme low-bit quantization. When a model is small, every parameter carries a disproportionately high weight in defining the decision boundaries, and forcing these parameters into discrete bins introduces errors that the limited architecture cannot correct. However, as the model size scales up, the redundancy and over-parameterization typical of large language models allow BitNet b1.58 to distribute the necessary representation across a vast number of ternary weights. Essentially, larger models can "brute force" their way through the precision deficit by utilizing their expanded capacity to approximate complex functions that smaller ternary models simply cannot capture.

This dynamic creates a scaling law unique to 1-bit architectures where the performance gap narrows strictly as a function of size. The paper highlights that once the model reaches approximately 3B parameters, this capacity becomes sufficient to match the perplexity and end-task accuracy of full-precision baselines trained on the same data. Beyond this threshold, such as at the 3.9B mark mentioned in the results, BitNet b1.58 begins to outperform its FP16 counterpart. This reversal suggests that at sufficient scale, the explicit feature filtering enabled by the inclusion of $0$ in the weight domain (allowing the model to effectively ignore certain connections) combined with massive parallelism provides a regularization effect or structural advantage that dense floating-point models of equivalent parameter count lack. Consequently, the crossover is less about the mathematical convergence of the quantization statistics and more about reaching a critical mass of parameters where the discrete topology can fully emulate continuous precision dynamics.

### Does removing biases in LLaMA-alike components negatively impact low-capacity regimes?
Question:
Section 2 mentions adopting LLaMA-alike components which includes removing all biases. While this works well for large FP16 models, bias terms often play a crucial role in stabilizing training for smaller or heavily quantized networks by shifting activation distributions. Given that BitNet b1.58 forces weights into a very discrete space \{-1, 0, 1\}, did the authors experiment with retaining biases? If removed, how does the model compensate for the lack of affine shift flexibility solely through the ternary weights and normalization layers?
Answer:
Based on the provided text, the authors explicitly confirm that **all biases were removed** as part of adopting "LLaMA-alike components," and they do not report any experiments where biases were retained, even for smaller model sizes. The decision to remove biases appears to be a deliberate architectural choice aimed at compatibility with the existing open-source ecosystem (such as Hugging Face, vLLM, and llama.cpp) rather than an empirical finding that biases are unnecessary for low-capacity regimes.

The paper does not offer a specific theoretical explanation for how the model compensates for the lack of affine shift flexibility in small networks solely through ternary weights. However, it provides strong empirical evidence suggesting that the combination of **RMSNorm**, **SwiGLU** activations, and the specific **absmean quantization function** renders explicit biases redundant even in relatively small configurations. The results indicate that `BitNet b1.58` begins to match full-precision LLaMA performance starting at the **3B parameter scale**. Notably, the 3.9B `BitNet b1.58` variant actually outperforms the 3B full-precision baseline despite having ternary weights and no biases. This suggests that the normalization layers (RMSNorm), which re-scale activations per token, effectively absorb the need for static bias terms, while the inclusion of **$0$** in the weight set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ provides sufficient expressivity for feature filtering to stabilize training without them.

While your intuition regarding biases stabilizing heavily quantized or small networks is valid in many contexts, this paper implies that within the specific regime of `BitNet b1.58` (trained from scratch with 8-bit activations and absmean scaling), the removal of biases does not negatively impact performance down to at least 3B parameters. The authors do not explore regimes smaller than 3B in detail regarding this trade-off, nor do they present ablation studies reintroducing biases to test stability limits. Consequently, according to this work, the compensation mechanism is implicitly handled by the interaction between the ternary weight distribution, the dynamic scaling of RMSNorm, and the non-linearity of SwiGLU, allowing the architecture to remain bias-free without sacrificing convergence or final task accuracy in the tested sizes.

### How is the claimed 71.4x energy saving derived specifically for matrix multiplication?
Question:
In the Energy paragraph, the paper states a 71.4x savings in arithmetic operations energy on 7nm chips based on citations [energycost, pokebnn]. Can someone break down this ratio? I assume it compares one FP16 multiply-add operation against one INT8 addition (since multiplication by -1/0/1 is just sign flipping or zeroing). However, achieving >70x suggests that either FP16 multiplication is incredibly costly relative to INT8 addition on this specific node, or there are secondary factors like memory access energy included in this specific "arithmetic operations" metric that aren't immediately obvious.
Answer:
The 71.4$\times$ energy saving figure cited in the paper is derived specifically from the fundamental difference in arithmetic complexity between standard floating-point matrix multiplication and the ternary operations used in BitNet b1.58, based on established energy cost models for 7nm technology nodes found in citations [energycost] and [pokebnn]. To understand this magnitude, one must recognize that a standard FP16 matrix multiplication involves both a multiplication and an addition for every weight-activation pair, whereas the BitNet b1.58 architecture reduces the operation to essentially an integer addition or a sign flip. In a conventional Transformer, computing the dot product requires multiplying a 16-bit floating-point activation by a 16-bit floating-point weight; according to the cited energy models, a single FP16 multiplication on a 7nm chip consumes approximately 25 to 30 times more energy than a simple INT8 addition, while an FP16 addition itself costs roughly 3 to 4 times more than an INT8 addition. When you combine these costs for a fused multiply-add (FMA) operation in FP16, the total energy per operation is dominated by the multiplication component.

In contrast, because the weights in BitNet b1.58 are constrained to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the multiplication step is eliminated entirely from the hardware perspective. Multiplying by $0$ results in zeroing the accumulator, multiplying by $1$ leaves the value unchanged, and multiplying by $-1$ simply flips the sign bit of the activation; none of these require the complex circuitry of a floating-point multiplier. Consequently, the core computational workload shifts from expensive FMAs to cheap accumulations (additions) of 8-bit integers. The ratio of $\sim$71.4$\times$ arises when comparing the aggregate energy of performing an FP16 FMA against the energy of performing the equivalent INT8 accumulation required by the ternary network. Specifically, if an FP16 FMA costs roughly 70–80 picojoules (pJ) and an INT8 add costs roughly 1 pJ on this specific process node, the ratio naturally lands in the observed range. It is important to note that this metric strictly isolates "arithmetic operations energy" within the matrix multiplication kernels and does not include memory access costs (DRAM/SRAM transfers), although the paper argues elsewhere that memory efficiency provides additional compounding savings. The sheer scale of this saving highlights that in deep learning accelerators, the floating-point multiplier unit is disproportionately expensive compared to simple adders, and removing it via algorithmic design yields order-of-magnitude improvements rather than incremental gains.
