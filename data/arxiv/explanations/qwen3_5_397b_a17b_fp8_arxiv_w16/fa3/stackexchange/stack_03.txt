\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why does multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ reduce quantization error for outliers?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors introduce 'incoherent processing' where they multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantizing to FP8. They claim this spreads out outliers because each entry becomes a random sum of original entries.

Mathematically, I understand that $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, so the exact arithmetic result is unchanged. However, quantization is a non-linear operation ($Q(x)$). Why does rotating the basis help? 

Is it simply that FP8 has a very limited dynamic range, so having one massive outlier forces the scaling factor for the whole block to be tiny, crushing the precision of smaller values? By spreading that outlier's magnitude across many elements via $\mathbf{M}$, do we avoid hitting the saturation limit of the FP8 exponent as often?
Answer:
The intuition behind multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ lies in how quantization error scales with the dynamic range of the data being quantized, particularly when that data contains outliers. You are correct that in exact arithmetic, the transformation preserves the attention output because $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top)\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$. However, quantization is a non-linear operation that maps continuous values to a discrete set determined by a scaling factor. In FP8 formats like e4m3, the limited number of mantissa bits (3 bits) means that precision is heavily dependent on choosing an appropriate scale for the block of data being quantized.

When a tensor contains outliers—values with magnitudes significantly larger than the majority of entries—a per-block or per-tensor quantization scheme is forced to choose a scaling factor small enough to accommodate these extreme values without clipping (saturation). This effectively "crushes" the resolution for the vast majority of normal-sized values, as they are mapped to very few discrete levels, leading to high quantization noise. The authors note that Large Language Models frequently exhibit such outlier features, making naive FP8 quantization inaccurate.

Multiplying $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ acts as a rotation of the feature space. As the paper describes, this transforms each entry of the resulting matrices into a random sum of the original entries. Due to the properties of random sums (related to the Central Limit Theorem), this operation tends to spread the energy of the outliers across many dimensions rather than concentrating them in specific coordinates. Consequently, the transformed matrices $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ have a much more "coherent" or uniform distribution of magnitudes, where no single entry dominates the dynamic range of its block.

By eliminating these sharp peaks in magnitude, the quantization algorithm can select a larger scaling factor for each block, which significantly improves the resolution for the typical values. Since the outliers have been smoothed out into the background noise floor, they no longer dictate the scale for the entire block. This process, termed "incoherent processing" in Section 3.3 and inspired by works like QuIP, ensures that the limited precision of FP8 is utilized more efficiently across all elements. The paper validates this empirically in Section 5.3, showing that combining this technique with block quantization reduces the numerical error of FP8 attention by $2.6\times$ compared to standard per-tensor quantization baselines that suffer from outlier-induced saturation.
