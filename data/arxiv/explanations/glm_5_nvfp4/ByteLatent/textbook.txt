# Introduction: The Case for Byte-Level Modeling

Modern large language models have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of tasks, from natural language understanding to code generation. Yet beneath these impressive capabilities lies a fundamental architectural choice that has remained largely unchallenged: the use of tokenization as a pre-processing step. This chapter examines the standard tokenization paradigm, its limitations, and the motivation for moving toward byte-level modeling through the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture.

## The Standard Pipeline: Tokenization and Its Role in Modern LLMs

The prevailing approach to training large language models follows a well-established pipeline. Raw text data, which exists fundamentally as a sequence of bytes, is first processed through a tokenization algorithm before being fed into the neural network. Tokenization is a heuristic pre-processing step that groups bytes into a static set of tokens drawn from a fixed vocabulary. This vocabulary is determined prior to training, typically using algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), which iteratively merges frequent byte sequences to create a compressed representation.

In this paradigm, tokens serve as the atomic units of computation. The transformer model processes sequences of these tokens, learning to predict the next token in an autoregressive manner. The tokenizer acts as an interface between the raw byte-level data and the model's internal representations. For instance, the Llama 3 model uses a BPE tokenizer with a vocabulary size of 128K tokens, achieving an average token size of approximately 4.4 bytes. This means that a sequence of raw bytes is compressed into a shorter sequence of tokens before model processing.

The tokenization step has been considered essential for practical reasons. By reducing sequence length, tokenization decreases the computational cost of the attention mechanism, which scales quadratically with sequence length in standard transformers. Additionally, tokens provide a form of compression that aligns with linguistic intuition—words and subword units often carry meaning that individual bytes lack. The success of models like GPT-4, Llama, and other prominent LLMs has cemented tokenization as a seemingly indispensable component of the modern LLM architecture.

However, this tokenization step represents a significant departure from the end-to-end learning principle that has driven much of deep learning's success. The tokenizer is trained separately on a corpus, often using simple frequency-based algorithms, and then frozen during the main model training. This creates a disconnect between the model's objectives and how it processes input data.

## Limitations of Tokenization: Domain Sensitivity, Multilingual Inequity, and Noise Vulnerability

While tokenization has enabled the scaling of large language models, it introduces several fundamental limitations that become increasingly problematic as models are deployed in diverse real-world settings.

**Domain and Modality Sensitivity.** Tokenizers are typically trained on specific data distributions, often web text or book corpora. When applied to different domains or modalities, the tokenizer's efficiency degrades significantly. A tokenizer optimized for English prose may perform poorly on code, mathematical notation, or scientific text. This domain sensitivity creates a bias in how strings are compressed, potentially favoring certain types of content over others. The fixed vocabulary cannot adapt to the statistical properties of new domains, leading to suboptimal compression and potentially degraded model performance.

**Multilingual Inequity.** Perhaps one of the most consequential limitations of tokenization is its treatment of different languages. Tokenizers trained predominantly on English text produce efficient tokenizations for English but often fragment text in other languages into many more tokens. This phenomenon, documented in research on multilingual language models, creates an inherent inequity: models require more computational resources to process the same amount of information in non-English languages, and the representation quality may suffer as meaningful linguistic units are split across multiple tokens. For morphologically rich languages or those with non-Latin scripts, this problem is particularly acute.

**Sensitivity to Input Noise.** Tokenization-based models exhibit brittleness when confronted with noisy or adversarially modified inputs. Small changes at the character level—such as typos, character insertions, or unusual capitalization—can cause a token to fall outside the vocabulary, resulting in suboptimal tokenization through fallback mechanisms like byte-level encoding. This creates a disconnect between the model's behavior on clean versus noisy data, a significant concern for real-world deployment where inputs may not match the pristine conditions of training data.

**Lack of Orthographic Knowledge.** Because tokenization abstracts away from individual characters, models trained on tokens often struggle with tasks requiring character-level understanding. This includes spelling correction, character manipulation, understanding the composition of words, and processing novel word forms. The model learns to recognize tokens as opaque symbols rather than compositions of characters, limiting its ability to generalize orthographic patterns and making it difficult to handle out-of-vocabulary words gracefully.

These limitations stem from a fundamental mismatch: tokenization imposes a fixed, pre-determined structure on data that may not align with the true complexity or structure of the information being processed. The model has no direct access to the raw bytes, only to the tokens produced by this pre-processing step.

## The Promise of Byte-Level Models: Eliminating the Pre-processing Bias

An alternative approach eliminates tokenization entirely, training models directly on raw bytes. Bytes represent the fundamental unit of digital text—a sequence of 8-bit values that can represent any character in any language, as well as binary data, code, and other content. By operating directly on bytes, models gain several potential advantages.

First, byte-level models have access to the complete information in the input. There is no pre-processing step that might discard or obscure character-level patterns. The model can learn to recognize and process any byte sequence, whether it represents English text, Chinese characters, mathematical notation, or even binary data formats.

Second, byte-level models eliminate the vocabulary problem entirely. There is no out-of-vocabulary issue because the vocabulary is fixed at 256 possible byte values. Every possible input can be represented without fallback mechanisms or special handling. This universality is particularly valuable for multilingual applications and for handling diverse data modalities.

Third, byte-level models can potentially develop richer representations. By processing sequences at the byte level, the model must learn to compose meaningful units from raw data, potentially developing more robust and generalizable internal representations. This could lead to better handling of morphological variations, novel word forms, and character-level patterns that token-based models struggle with.

The promise of byte-level modeling has been recognized since the early days of neural language models. Character-level RNNs demonstrated the feasibility of learning directly from raw character sequences. More recently, byte-level transformer models have been explored, showing improved robustness to noise and better handling of multilingual text. However, these approaches have faced a significant barrier: computational cost.

## The Computational Challenge: Long Sequence Lengths and the Cost of Processing Raw Bytes

The primary obstacle to scaling byte-level models has been computational efficiency. Consider a text that tokenizes to $n$ tokens. The same text represented as bytes would typically contain approximately $4n$ to $5n$ bytes, given that average token sizes in modern tokenizers range from about 3.7 to 4.4 bytes. This four-to-five-fold increase in sequence length has profound implications for computational cost.

In standard transformer architectures, computational cost comes from two main sources: the attention mechanism and the feed-forward network layers. The attention mechanism scales as $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ where $n$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the hidden dimension, while the feed-forward networks scale as $O(n \cdot d^2)$. While attention costs grow quadratically with sequence length, at scale, the feed-forward network layers dominate the computational budget. This means that processing five times more bytes would require approximately five times more computation, even with efficient attention implementations.

Previous attempts to make byte-level models practical have focused primarily on reducing attention costs. Approaches have included more efficient self-attention mechanisms, such as sliding window attention or sparse attention patterns, and even attention-free architectures. While these innovations help reduce the quadratic scaling of attention, they primarily benefit smaller models. At scale, the dominant cost remains the feed-forward network computations that must run on every single byte in the sequence.

This computational barrier explains why tokenization has persisted despite its limitations. The tokenization step serves as an efficiency mechanism, reducing the number of computational steps by compressing sequences. The question becomes: can we achieve similar efficiency while retaining the benefits of byte-level modeling?

## Introduction to the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) Paradigm

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a paradigm shift in how we approach the efficiency-robustness tradeoff in language modeling. Rather than relying on a fixed tokenization scheme, BLT introduces a dynamic, learnable method for grouping bytes into "patches"—variable-sized units of computation that adapt to the complexity of the data.

The key insight behind BLT is that not all positions in a sequence require equal computational investment. Consider predicting the next element in a sequence: predicting the first character of a new word is typically much harder than predicting the remaining characters once the first is known. For example, given the question "Who composed the Magic Flute?", predicting the first character of the answer is a high-entropy decision with many possible continuations. Once the model predicts "M," the completion "Mozart" becomes comparatively easy to predict—the entropy drops significantly.

BLT exploits this observation by allocating more compute to high-entropy positions and less to low-entropy ones. It segments bytes into patches based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction. Positions with high entropy—where the model is uncertain and the prediction is difficult—become patch boundaries, triggering more computational investment. Positions with low entropy can be grouped together, allowing the model to process multiple bytes with a single computational step.

The architecture comprises three main components:

1. **A Local Encoder**: A lightweight transformer that processes raw bytes and produces patch representations using cross-attention mechanisms.

2. **A Global Latent Transformer**: A large autoregressive transformer that operates on the patch representations, consuming the bulk of computational resources.

3. **A Local Decoder**: A lightweight transformer that decodes patch representations back into bytes.

The local encoder and decoder are relatively small, allowing efficient processing at the byte level. The global transformer, which is much larger, runs less frequently—only once per patch rather than once per byte. This architectural choice means that the expensive computational steps are reserved for positions where they matter most.

Crucially, BLT has no fixed vocabulary for patches. Unlike tokenization, where the set of possible tokens is determined before training, BLT can form arbitrary groups of bytes into patches. The patching is determined dynamically based on the local entropy, creating contextualized groupings with relatively uniform information density within each patch.

## Key Contributions: Dynamic Compute Allocation and Scaling Without Tokenization

The BLT architecture makes several significant contributions that advance the state of byte-level language modeling.

**Dynamic Compute Allocation.** The central innovation of BLT is its ability to allocate computational resources dynamically based on data complexity. Tokenization-based models apply the same computational budget to every token, regardless of whether the prediction is easy or difficult. BLT instead segments data based on entropy estimates, creating patches that align with information density. This means that predictable data—such as repetitive patterns or common word endings—is processed efficiently with large patches, while complex or unpredictable data receives more computational attention through smaller patches.

**First Flop-Controlled Scaling Study of Byte-Level Models.** The paper presents the first comprehensive scaling study of byte-level models up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes. This scale of experimentation demonstrates that byte-level models can be trained end-to-end without fixed-vocabulary tokenization while maintaining computational efficiency. The results show that BLT matches the training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference.

**Parity with Tokenization-Based Models at Scale.** Previous byte-level models have lagged behind their tokenized counterparts, particularly at larger scales. BLT is the first byte-level transformer architecture to achieve matching scaling trends with BPE-based models in compute-optimal regimes. This validates the fundamental premise that tokenizer-free architectures can scale effectively.

**A New Dimension for Scaling.** BLT unlocks a previously unavailable scaling dimension. With tokenization-based models, increasing vocabulary size (and thus average token size) requires increasing the output embedding dimension, creating a tradeoff that limits flexibility. BLT can independently increase both model size and patch size while maintaining the same inference flop budget. Longer patch sizes save compute, which can be reallocated to grow the global transformer. Experiments show significantly better scaling trends for BLT in inference-flop-controlled settings.

**Improved Robustness and Long-Tail Generalization.** By working directly with raw bytes, BLT models demonstrate enhanced robustness to noisy inputs and better character-level understanding. Evaluations show significant improvements on tasks requiring orthographic knowledge, phonology understanding, and low-resource machine translation—areas where tokenization-based models typically struggle.

**Efficiency Gains.** During both training and inference, BLT dynamically selects long patches when data is predictable. This leads to substantial efficiency gains without sacrificing performance. The architecture demonstrates that it is possible to match Llama 3's performance while significantly reducing inference costs.

The BLT paradigm redefines the relationship between efficiency and representation in language models. By removing the fixed tokenization bottleneck, it opens new possibilities for scaling and robustness while maintaining computational efficiency. The following chapters will explore the technical details of how BLT achieves these goals, including the mechanics of patching, the architecture of the local and global transformers, and the empirical results that validate this approach.

# From Bytes to Patches: Dynamic Segmentation Strategies

Understanding how raw bytes are transformed into meaningful units of computation is fundamental to grasping the Byte Latent Transformer architecture. This chapter explores the concept of "patching"—the mechanism by which BLT groups individual bytes into larger units called patches. Unlike traditional tokenization, which relies on predetermined vocabularies, patching introduces a dynamic, context-aware approach to segmentation that fundamentally changes how computational resources are allocated during language modeling.

#

### Defining Patches vs. Tokens: Variable Groups Without a Fixed Vocabulary

The distinction between patches and tokens represents a paradigm shift in how we think about processing text in language models. In conventional large language models, tokens are the fundamental units of computation. These tokens are drawn from a fixed vocabulary determined before training begins, typically using algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). For instance, the Llama 3 tokenizer has a vocabulary of 128,000 tokens, meaning that any sequence of bytes must be mapped to one of these 128,000 predefined symbols. This creates a rigid mapping: every input must be compressed into this finite set of tokens, regardless of the actual structure or complexity of the data.

Patches, in contrast, represent a fundamentally different approach. A patch is a group of bytes that is determined dynamically based on the content itself, without being constrained by any fixed vocabulary. The BLT architecture can work with arbitrary groups of bytes—whether a patch contains 2 bytes or 20 bytes depends entirely on the context and the chosen patching strategy. This flexibility means that the same byte sequence might be segmented differently depending on where it appears and what surrounds it.

The implications of this distinction are profound. When using a fixed vocabulary, the model has no direct access to the underlying byte features once bytes are merged into tokens. The tokenization process acts as a information bottleneck, compressing potentially important byte-level details into abstract symbols. With patches, however, the model maintains a connection to the raw byte level through learned encoder and decoder modules. These modules map arbitrary groups of bytes to and from latent patch representations, preserving the ability to reason about individual bytes when necessary.

This architectural choice redefines the traditional trade-off between vocabulary size and computational cost. In standard tokenization-based models, increasing the vocabulary size leads to larger tokens on average, which means fewer computational steps for processing a given sequence. However, this comes at the cost of a larger output dimension for the final projection layer of the model. This trade-off leaves little room for significant variations. For example, Llama 3 increased its average token size from 3.7 bytes to 4.4 bytes compared to Llama 2, but this required increasing the size of its embedding table by four times. With patches, this constraint is removed entirely—the model can work with patches of any size without being limited by vocabulary considerations.

#

### The Patching Function and Its Impact on Computational Cost

The mathematical formalization of patching centers on what is called the patching function. Formally, a patching function $f_p$ segments a sequence of bytes $\mathbf{x}=\{x_i\}|i=1,\ldots,n\}$ of length $n$ into a sequence of $m < n$ patches $\mathbf{p}=\{p_j|j=1,\ldots,m\}$. The function accomplishes this by mapping each byte position $x_i$ to a binary value in the set $\{0,1\}$, where a value of 1 indicates the start of a new patch. In other words, the patching function acts as a boundary detector, identifying which bytes mark the beginning of new computational units.

Understanding the relationship between patching and computational cost is crucial. For both token-based and patch-based models, the computational cost of processing data is primarily determined by the number of steps executed by the main Transformer. In the BLT architecture, this is the Latent Global Transformer, which operates on patch representations rather than individual bytes. Therefore, the number of patches needed to encode a given sequence directly determines how much computation is required.

This leads to a key insight: the average size of a patch—often simply referred to as the "patch size"—is the main factor determining the computational cost during both training and inference. If the average patch size is 4 bytes, then a sequence of 400 bytes will require approximately 100 steps through the global transformer. If the average patch size increases to 8 bytes, the same sequence would only require about 50 steps, effectively halving the computational cost. This relationship between patch size and computational efficiency forms the foundation for BLT's dynamic compute allocation strategy.

The Flops (Floating Point Operations) estimation for BLT models reflects this relationship. The total computational cost combines the flops for the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, and the local decoder transformer, along with the cross-attention blocks. The formula accounts for the patch size $n_p$ in determining how the global transformer's computation scales. When patches are larger, the global transformer—which consumes the bulk of the computation—is invoked fewer times, leading to significant efficiency gains.

#

### Strided Patching: Fixed-Size Grouping and Its Limitations

The most straightforward approach to grouping bytes into patches is strided patching, where bytes are grouped into patches of fixed size $k$. This method, employed by the MegaByte architecture, creates patches by taking every $k$ consecutive bytes and treating them as a single unit. For example, with $k=4$, the byte sequence "Hello World" would be divided into patches like "Hell", "o Wo", "rld"—each patch containing exactly 4 bytes.

Strided patching offers several practical advantages. It is straightforward to implement for both training and inference, providing a clear and predictable mechanism for grouping bytes. The patch size directly controls the computational cost—doubling $k$ roughly halves the number of patches and thus the computation required. This makes it easy to reason about and control the flop cost of processing data.

However, this simplicity comes with significant downsides that limit its effectiveness. The most critical limitation is that computation is not dynamically allocated to where it is needed most. Consider processing code: many lines might consist primarily of whitespace or predictable patterns, yet strided patching would allocate the same computational resources to these easy predictions as it would to complex mathematical expressions or dense logic. Conversely, when processing mathematically dense content where bytes carry substantial information, the fixed patch size might not provide sufficient computational capacity.

Another significant problem is the inconsistency of patching similar byte sequences. The same word appearing in different positions might be split across patch boundaries differently depending on where it falls in the sequence. For instance, the word "information" might be split as "info" + "rmat" + "ion" in one context but as "infor" + "matio" + "n" in another. This non-contextual patching means that the model cannot build reliable representations for words or sub-word units, as their internal representations vary arbitrarily based on their position in the sequence.

#

### Space Patching: Using Whitespace as a Natural Boundary

Space patching represents a significant improvement over strided patching by using whitespace and similar characters as natural boundaries for segmentation. This approach, proposed in SpaceByte, creates new patches after any "space-like" bytes, which are defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte. Importantly, each patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte, ensuring that patches are never empty or meaningless.

The intuition behind space patching is elegant: whitespace characters often mark the boundaries of linguistic units in many languages. In English and similar languages, words are typically separated by spaces, making these characters natural points for segmentation. By allocating a latent transformer step to model every word, space patching ensures that words are patched consistently across different sequences—the word "information" will always be segmented as a single unit regardless of its position.

More importantly, space patching aligns computational allocation with prediction difficulty. Consider the question "Who composed the Magic Flute?____" The most difficult prediction is determining the first character of the answer. Once we predict "M", the completion "Mozart" becomes comparatively easy because the first character significantly reduces the number of likely choices. Space patching naturally allocates more compute (a new transformer step) precisely at these hard prediction points—the beginnings of words—where they are most needed.

However, space patching has limitations that prevent it from being a complete solution. First, it cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains. Many languages, such as Chinese or Japanese, do not use spaces between words, rendering this strategy ineffective. Similarly, in domains like programming or mathematical notation, whitespace may not correspond to meaningful boundaries. Most critically, space patching cannot vary the patch size—all words, regardless of their complexity, are treated as single patches. This means that simple, predictable words like "the" or "and" receive the same computational attention as complex, information-dense words.

#

### Entropy-Based Patching: Allocating Compute Based on Prediction Uncertainty

Entropy-based patching represents a paradigm shift in how we think about segmentation. Rather than relying on rule-based heuristics like whitespace, this approach uses a data-driven method to identify points of high uncertainty in the byte sequence. The fundamental insight is that the model should dynamically allocate more computation precisely where predictions are harder, and less computation where predictions are easier.

The intuition can be understood through a simple example. In English text, predicting the ending of most words is relatively easy—once you see "informat", the probability that "ion" follows is very high. However, predicting the first character of a new word is significantly harder because there are many more possibilities. The entropy of the next-byte prediction captures this difference: high entropy indicates high uncertainty (many equally likely possibilities), while low entropy indicates low uncertainty (the outcome is predictable).

This creates a natural mechanism for patch segmentation. We want to begin new patches at points of high entropy—where the next byte is hard to predict—because these are the points where we need more computational resources. Conversely, we want to continue existing patches through low-entropy regions where predictions are easy and fewer computational resources are needed. This approach ensures that compute is allocated proportionally to the actual complexity of the data, rather than arbitrary boundaries.

The practical benefits of this approach are substantial. During inference, when the model encounters predictable data—such as repeated patterns, common phrases, or obvious continuations—it can use longer patches and save computation. When encountering unpredictable data—novel concepts, rare words, or complex reasoning—the model automatically creates shorter patches, allocating more compute where it's needed. This dynamic allocation leads to significant efficiency improvements: the paper demonstrates up to 50% fewer flops at inference compared to tokenization-based models while maintaining comparable performance.

#

### Calculating Next-Byte Entropy Using a Small Byte-Level Language Model

The implementation of entropy-based patching requires a method for estimating the entropy of next-byte predictions. The BLT approach uses a small byte-level autoregressive language model trained on the same distribution as the full model. This entropy model is trained once and then used to compute entropies for the training data as a preprocessing step.

The entropy calculation follows standard information-theoretic principles. Given a small byte-level language model with distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$, the entropy of the next-byte prediction at position $i$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\mathbf{x}_{<i})$$

This formula computes the expected surprise or uncertainty about the next byte, conditioned on all previous bytes in the sequence. When the entropy is high, the model is uncertain about what byte comes next; when it is low, the model has a strong belief about the continuation.

In the experimental setup described in the paper, the entropy model is a transformer with approximately 100 million parameters, 14 layers, and a hidden dimensionality of 512. It uses sliding window attention over 512 bytes, providing sufficient context to make meaningful predictions about byte-level entropy. The model is trained on the same training distribution as the full BLT model, ensuring that its entropy estimates reflect the actual difficulty of predictions in the target domain.

An important practical consideration is that patch boundaries are identified during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during data loading. This means the entropy model's predictions are computed before training begins, rather than being learned end-to-end with the main model. While the paper notes that learning patching end-to-end could be an interesting direction for future work, the preprocessing approach is efficient and effective. When the entropy model's receptive field is small enough, it can even be encoded in an efficient lookup table, further reducing computational overhead.

#

### Global vs. Approximate Monotonic Constraints for Boundary Identification

Once next-byte entropies are computed, the system needs to decide which positions should serve as patch boundaries. The paper explores two methods for making this determination, each with different characteristics.

The first method uses a global entropy threshold. A patch boundary is created whenever the entropy exceeds a fixed threshold $\theta_g$:

$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$

This approach is straightforward: any position where prediction uncertainty exceeds the threshold marks the start of a new patch. By adjusting $\theta_g$, we can control the average patch size—a lower threshold creates more patches (smaller average size), while a higher threshold creates fewer patches (larger average size). This provides a direct mechanism for trading off between computational efficiency and modeling capacity.

The second method uses an approximate monotonicity constraint, which identifies points that are high entropy relative to the previous byte:

$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$

This approach can be interpreted as identifying points that break the approximate monotonic decrease in entropy within a patch. The intuition is that entropy tends to decrease as we progress through a word—the first character has high uncertainty, but each subsequent character constrains the possibilities. When entropy suddenly increases (or doesn't decrease as expected), it signals a potential boundary.

The approximate monotonic constraint has practical advantages in certain scenarios. The paper notes that entropy patching can yield progressively larger patches in structured content like multiple choice tasks, which often contain very repetitive content. These variations occur because the entropy model's predictions change based on its context—repeated content leads to lower entropy estimates. The monotonicity constraint is less susceptible to this "entropy drift" because it compares relative entropy changes rather than absolute values.

For large-scale experiments, the paper uses the approximate monotonicity constraint and resets the entropy model context with newlines. This modification addresses the context-dependent nature of entropy estimates while maintaining the benefits of dynamic patching. Importantly, this only affects how entropies are computed—the procedure for identifying the entropy threshold remains the same.

#

### Incremental Patching: Constraints for Autoreressive Generation

A critical requirement for patching in generative models is that it must be incremental. During generation, the model needs to decide whether the current position in the byte sequence is at a patch boundary without knowing what bytes will follow. This is because the rest of the sequence has not yet been generated. Therefore, patching cannot assume access to future bytes when deciding how to segment the sequence.

Formally, a patching scheme $f_p$ satisfies the incremental patching property if:

$$f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}$$

This equation states that the patching decision for the prefix up to position $i$ must be identical whether we're patching just that prefix or the entire sequence. In other words, the patching decisions made at each step must be final and cannot be revised based on future information.

This constraint has important implications. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), the most common tokenization method in modern LLMs, does not satisfy this property. The same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on what follows it. For example, the prefix "inf" might be tokenized differently if it's followed by "ormation" versus "erior" versus any other continuation. BPE's greedy merging algorithm looks at the entire sequence to determine the optimal tokenization, which is not possible during autoregressive generation when future bytes are unknown.

This distinction highlights another advantage of patches over tokens. Entropy-based patching naturally satisfies the incremental property because entropy at position $i$ depends only on bytes $x_{<i}$. The entropy model's prediction for $x_i$ conditions only on previous bytes, so patch boundary decisions can be made without knowing future bytes. Space patching also satisfies this property because the decision to start a new patch after a space character can be made when that space is encountered, without needing to see what follows.

The paper notes that it is technically possible to make BPE incremental by using a special delimiter token to indicate patch boundaries, but this approach increases the byte-sequence length and defeats the efficiency benefits of tokenization. The elegance of entropy-based patching is that it naturally satisfies the incremental constraint while also providing compute allocation proportional to prediction difficulty—a dual benefit that tokenization-based approaches cannot achieve.

# The Byte Latent Transformer Architecture

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a fundamental departure from traditional tokenization-based language models by operating directly on raw bytes while maintaining computational efficiency. At its core, BLT employs a hierarchical architecture that strategically allocates compute resources based on the complexity of the data being processed. This chapter provides a detailed examination of the BLT architecture, explaining how information flows from raw bytes through latent patch representations and back to byte-level outputs.

# Architectural Overview: The Global Transformer, Local Encoder, and Local Decoder

The BLT architecture consists of three distinct transformer components working in concert: a large global autoregressive transformer that operates on patch representations, and two smaller local models that handle the translation between bytes and patches. This hierarchical design enables BLT to process raw bytes efficiently by avoiding the need to run expensive transformer computations on every single byte.

The Latent Global Transformer serves as the primary computational engine of the architecture. This large autoregressive model processes sequences of latent patch representations, performing the heavy lifting of language modeling at the patch level. Because patches can contain multiple bytes, the global transformer executes fewer forward passes than a comparable byte-level model, significantly reducing computational cost. The global transformer uses a block-causal attention mask, which restricts attention to the current patch and all preceding patches within the same document, enabling autoregressive modeling while maintaining efficiency.

The Local Encoder is a lightweight transformer that efficiently transforms raw input bytes into expressive patch representations. With significantly fewer layers than the global transformer ($l_{\mathcal{E}} << l_{\mathcal{G}}$), the encoder's primary role is to compress byte-level information into a form suitable for the global transformer. The encoder employs local block causal attention, where each byte attends to a fixed window of preceding bytes. This local attention mechanism allows the encoder to capture byte-level patterns without the computational expense of full attention over long sequences. Crucially, the encoder includes cross-attention layers that pool byte representations into patch representations, a mechanism we will explore in detail later.

The Local Decoder performs the inverse operation of the encoder: it takes the output patch representations from the global transformer and decodes them back into raw bytes. Like the encoder, the decoder is lightweight compared to the global transformer ($l_{\mathcal{D}} << l_{\mathcal{G}}$). The decoder must generate byte sequences autoregressively, conditioned on both the patch representations from the global model and previously decoded bytes. This is achieved through cross-attention layers that attend to patch representations, followed by transformer layers that process the resulting byte representations.

The separation of concerns among these three components is fundamental to BLT's efficiency. The expensive global transformer runs only once per patch, while the lightweight local models handle byte-level processing. This means that when data is predictable and patches are long, the global transformer runs fewer times, saving computation. Conversely, when data is complex and patches are short, more compute is allocated appropriately.

# The Latent Global Transformer: Autoregressive Modeling on Patch Representations

The Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$, is the centerpiece of the BLT architecture. It is an autoregressive transformer model with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers that processes sequences of latent patch representations. The global transformer takes as input a sequence of patch representations $\{p_j\}$ and produces a sequence of output patch representations $\{o_j\}$, where the subscript $j$ denotes patch-level indexing throughout the architecture.

The global transformer employs a block-causal attention mask, a key design choice that enables proper autoregressive modeling on patch sequences. This masking strategy restricts attention such that each patch can only attend to itself and all preceding patches within the current document. In other words, when processing patch $p_j$, the model can utilize information from patches $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_j$, but not from any future patches. This ensures that the model learns to predict patch representations based solely on past context, which is essential for autoregressive language modeling.

The computational cost of the global transformer dominates both pre-training and inference in BLT. This is by design: by concentrating the bulk of the model's parameters in the global transformer, BLT ensures that the most expensive computations are invoked sparingly—only once per patch. The decision of when to invoke the global transformer is determined by the patching scheme, which segments bytes into patches based on entropy or other criteria. This creates a direct mechanism for controlling compute allocation: more predictable data leads to longer patches and fewer global transformer invocations, while complex data triggers more frequent execution of the global model.

The input patch representations fed to the global transformer are not simply embeddings of bytes, but rather learned latent representations produced by the local encoder. Similarly, the output patch representations from the global transformer are not directly interpretable as bytes; they must be decoded by the local decoder. This abstraction allows the global transformer to operate in a learned latent space where patches, regardless of their byte composition, are represented as dense vectors of dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$.

# The Local Encoder: Transforming Byte Sequences into Patch Representations

The Local Encoder, denoted as $\mathcal{E}$, is responsible for converting raw byte sequences into the patch representations that the global transformer processes. This component is designed to be computationally efficient, with $l_{\mathcal{E}}$ layers where $l_{\mathcal{E}} << l_{\mathcal{G}}$. The encoder's architecture combines standard transformer layers with specialized cross-attention mechanisms that enable pooling of byte representations into patch representations.

The encoding process begins with byte embeddings. Input bytes $b_i$ are embedded using a matrix of dimension $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$, which maps each of the 256 possible byte values to a dense vector of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$. This embedding matrix is learned during training and provides the initial representations that are subsequently refined through the encoder's layers. These byte embeddings can be augmented with additional information, particularly hash n-gram embeddings that capture local context.

After the initial byte embedding, the encoder applies a series of alternating transformer layers and cross-attention layers. Each transformer layer processes the byte representations using a local block causal attention mask. This masking strategy defines a fixed attention window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes, meaning each byte can attend to a bounded local context. Importantly, this attention window can cross dynamic patch boundaries but cannot cross document boundaries. The local attention mechanism allows the encoder to capture byte-level patterns and dependencies without the quadratic cost of full attention over long byte sequences.

Following each transformer layer, a cross-attention layer pools information from the byte representations into patch representations. This is a crucial architectural innovation that enables the transition from byte-level to patch-level representations. The cross-attention mechanism creates one query vector for each patch, initialized from the byte representations belonging to that patch, and attends only to the bytes within the patch. This produces a fixed-dimensional patch representation regardless of how many bytes the patch contains.

The final output of the local encoder is a sequence of patch representations $\{p_j\}$, each of which is a dense vector that encodes the information from all bytes in the corresponding patch. These patch representations are dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, matching the hidden dimension of the global transformer. This dimensional compatibility allows the patch representations to be directly fed into the global transformer for further processing.

# Encoder Hash n-gram Embeddings: Incorporating Local Context via Hashing

A critical challenge in byte-level modeling is capturing local patterns and dependencies that span multiple bytes. While individual bytes provide the raw building blocks of text, meaningful linguistic units—morphemes, words, phrases—span multiple consecutive bytes. To enable the model to recognize and leverage these multi-byte patterns, BLT incorporates hash n-gram embeddings into the encoder.

The key insight behind hash n-gram embeddings is that the model should consider not only the current byte $b_i$ but also the preceding bytes that form an n-gram ending at position $i$. For each byte position $i$ and each n-gram size $n \in \{3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}$, the encoder constructs a byte-gram:

$$g_{i,n} = \{b_{i-n+1}, \ldots, b_i\}$$

These n-grams capture local context of varying lengths, allowing the model to recognize patterns like common character sequences, word fragments, or punctuation patterns.

Since the number of possible n-grams grows exponentially with $n$, directly embedding all possible n-grams would be infeasible. Instead, BLT uses a hashing trick: each n-gram is mapped via a hash function to an index in a fixed-size embedding table $E_n^{hash}$. The hash function used is RollPolyHash, defined as:

$$\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_n^{hash}|$$

The resulting embedding is then added to the byte embedding and normalized:

$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n=3,\ldots,8} E_n^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$

where $x_i$ is the base embedding of byte $b_i$. The final embedding $e_i$ is normalized by dividing by the number of n-gram sizes plus one (accounting for the base byte embedding).

This hashing approach provides several advantages. First, it allows the model to capture n-gram information without requiring an exponentially large embedding table. Second, the fixed table size means memory usage remains bounded regardless of the training data. Third, the hash function ensures that similar n-grams tend to map to nearby indices, preserving some locality in the embedding space. The embedding tables for different n-gram sizes are separate, allowing the model to learn distinct representations for different context lengths.

# Encoder Cross-Attention: Pooling Byte Representations into Patches

The cross-attention mechanism in the encoder is responsible for pooling variable-length byte sequences into fixed-dimensional patch representations. This mechanism draws inspiration from the Perceiver architecture but adapts it for the specific requirements of BLT, where latent representations correspond to dynamically sized patches rather than a fixed set of latents.

The cross-attention module operates after each transformer layer in the encoder. Each patch $p_j$ is associated with a query vector that attends to the byte representations belonging to that patch. The query vector is initialized by pooling the byte embeddings corresponding to patch $p_j$, followed by a linear projection:

$$P_{0,j} = \mathcal{E}_C(f_\text{bytes}(p_j))$$

where $f_\text{bytes}(p_j)$ denotes the sequence of bytes belonging to patch $p_j$, and $\mathcal{E}_C$ is a linear projection with output dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}} \times U_{\mathcal{E}}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the number of encoder cross-attention heads.

The cross-attention computation follows the standard attention mechanism but with a key difference in masking. The attention is computed as:

$$P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$

where the query $Q_j$ is a projection of the patch representation, and keys $K_i$ and values $V_i$ are projections of the byte representations from the previous layer:

$$Q_j = W_q(P_{l-1,j}), \quad K_i = W_k(h_{l-1,i}), \quad V_i = W_v(h_{l-1,i})$$

The masking strategy is specific to patching: each query $Q_j$ only attends to keys and values that correspond to bytes in patch $j$. This ensures that the patch representation only incorporates information from its constituent bytes, maintaining the integrity of the patch boundaries.

Since patch representations have larger dimension ($h_{\mathcal{G}}$) than byte representations ($h_{\mathcal{E}}$), the multi-headed attention maintains $P_l$ as multiple heads of dimension $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, which are then concatenated to form the final $h_{\mathcal{G}}$-dimensional patch representation. The module uses pre-LayerNorm on queries, keys, and values, and notably does not use positional embeddings within the cross-attention, as position information is already encoded in the byte representations from the preceding transformer layer. A residual connection wraps around the cross-attention block to facilitate gradient flow.

# The Local Decoder: Generating Bytes from Patch Representations

The Local Decoder, denoted as $\mathcal{D}$, performs the essential task of converting the output patch representations from the global transformer back into raw bytes. This component enables BLT to generate text at the byte level while leveraging the efficiency of patch-level processing in the global transformer.

Like the encoder, the decoder is designed to be lightweight, with $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ layers where $l_{\mathcal{D}} << l_{\mathcal{G}}$. The decoder takes as input the output patch representations $\{o_j\}$ from the global transformer and produces a sequence of bytes $\{y_i\}$. Importantly, the decoder also conditions on previously decoded bytes, enabling autoregressive generation at the byte level.

The decoder's architecture mirrors the encoder in structure but with key differences in the cross-attention mechanism. The decoder applies alternating layers of cross-attention followed by transformer layers. The order is significant: cross-attention is applied first to create byte representations from patch representations, and then the transformer layer operates on the resulting byte sequence.

The initial byte representations for the decoder are derived from the encoder's final layer. Specifically, the decoder starts with the hidden representations $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$ from the last encoder layer. This connection between encoder and decoder allows the decoder to leverage the byte-level representations computed during encoding, providing additional signal for generating the output bytes.

The decoder transformer layers use the same local block causal attention as the encoder, maintaining consistency in how byte-level information is processed. The output vocabulary is the 256 possible byte values, and the decoder produces probability distributions over this vocabulary at each byte position.

# Decoder Cross-Attention: Attending to Patch Representations to Predict Bytes

The cross-attention mechanism in the decoder performs the opposite operation of the encoder cross-attention: instead of pooling bytes into patches, it expands patch representations into byte representations. This reversal is reflected in the roles of queries and key/values being interchanged compared to the encoder.

In decoder cross-attention, the byte representations serve as queries, while the patch representations serve as keys and values. This allows each byte position to attend to the relevant patch representation to inform its generation. The initial byte representations $D_0$ are set to the encoder's final hidden representations:

$$D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$$

The cross-attention computation proceeds as:

$$B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$

where:

$$Q_i = W_q(d_{l-1,i}), \quad K_j = W_k(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j)), \quad V_j = W_v(\mathcal{D}_C(o_j))$$

Here, $d_{l-1,i}$ is the byte representation at position $i$ from the previous decoder transformer layer (or from the encoder for the first layer), and $\mathcal{D}_C$ is a linear transformation and split operation applied to the patch representations $o_j$ from the global model.

The keys and values are projected from patch representations, while queries are projected from byte representations. This allows each byte position $i$ to attend across all patch representations, extracting the information needed to predict that byte. The output $B_l$ has dimension $h_{\mathcal{D}} \times n_b$, where $n_b$ is the number of output bytes.

Following the cross-attention, the decoder transformer layer operates on $B_l$ to produce $D_l$:

$$D_l = \text{Decoder-Transformer-layer}_l(B_l)$$

As in the encoder cross-attention, the decoder uses multiple attention heads, pre-LayerNorm normalization, no positional embeddings within the cross-attention module, and a residual connection around the cross-attention block. The final layer's byte representations are projected to the 256-dimensional output vocabulary to produce the next-byte predictions.

# Attention Masking Strategies: Block Causal and Local Block Causal Masks

Attention masking is crucial for controlling information flow in autoregressive language models. BLT employs two distinct masking strategies for different components of the architecture: block causal masking for the global transformer and local block causal masking for the local models.

The block causal attention mask is used by the Latent Global Transformer. This masking strategy implements autoregressive modeling at the patch level by ensuring that each patch can only attend to itself and all preceding patches within the same document. When processing patch $p_j$, the attention mechanism has access to patches $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_j$ but not patches $p_{j+1}, p_{j+2}, \ldots$. This causal structure is essential for autoregressive language modeling, as it prevents information leakage from future tokens during training and enables proper causal generation during inference.

The block causal mask also respects document boundaries: attention cannot cross from one document to another. This prevents the model from using information from subsequent documents when processing the current document, which is important for training efficiency and proper modeling of document-level structure.

The local block causal attention mask is used by the Local Encoder and Local Decoder. This masking strategy implements a fixed-window attention where each byte can attend to a bounded context of preceding bytes. Specifically, each byte position $i$ can attend to bytes in the window $[i - w + 1, i]$, where $w$ is the window size (typically 512 bytes in BLT). This window can extend across dynamic patch boundaries, allowing the local models to capture dependencies that span patch boundaries, but it cannot cross document boundaries.

The local attention mechanism provides several benefits. First, it dramatically reduces the computational cost compared to full attention, as the attention complexity becomes linear in sequence length rather than quadratic. Second, it still allows each byte to access a meaningful local context, capturing patterns like word formation, common character sequences, and local syntactic structure. Third, because patches are formed dynamically based on entropy, important boundaries may not align with natural linguistic units; local attention that crosses patch boundaries ensures that byte-level patterns are not artificially segmented.

For the cross-attention modules, additional patch-specific masking is applied. In the encoder cross-attention, each patch query only attends to bytes within that patch, ensuring clean separation between patches. In the decoder cross-attention, each byte query can attend to all patch representations, as the patches provide the global context needed for generation.

BLT leverages efficient implementations for these attention patterns. For fixed-standard attention masks like block causal and fixed-window block causal, the architecture uses Flash Attention. For the dynamic patch-dependent masks in cross-attention layers, BLT uses Flex Attention, which provides fused implementations that significantly speed up training by efficiently handling the variable masking patterns that arise from dynamic patching.

# Experimental Setup and Training Methodology

The experimental design underlying the Byte Latent Transformer represents a carefully constructed framework for comparing byte-level architectures against established tokenization-based models. This section details the methodological choices that enable fair and meaningful comparisons, from dataset curation to computational cost estimation.

## Pre-training Datasets

The empirical validation of BLT relies on two distinct pre-training datasets, each serving different experimental purposes. The first dataset derives from the Llama 2 pre-training corpus, which comprises approximately 2 trillion tokens collected from diverse publicly available sources. This corpus undergoes substantial cleaning and filtering procedures to enhance data quality, ensuring that the training signal remains strong and consistent throughout the optimization process.

The second dataset, designated BLT-1T, represents a newly curated collection of approximately 1 trillion tokens. This dataset aggregates content from various public sources and incorporates a substantial subset of the pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM, a benchmark designed for training data quality assessment. The inclusion of Datacomp-LM data within BLT-1T reflects the growing understanding that data quality and diversity play crucial roles in achieving strong downstream task performance.

The division of labor between these two datasets follows a deliberate strategy. The Llama 2 dataset serves primarily for scaling law experiments, where the goal is to determine the optimal architectural configurations for BLT. The well-characterized nature of this dataset, combined with the established scaling laws from prior work, makes it an ideal substrate for controlled experiments that isolate the effects of architectural modifications. In contrast, BLT-1T supports the complete pre-training runs that enable direct comparison with Llama 3 on downstream evaluation benchmarks. Neither dataset incorporates any information gathered from Meta products or services, ensuring that the experimental results remain reproducible and that the data composition reflects publicly accessible sources.

A critical methodological choice concerns the tokenization baseline. For all baseline experiments involving tokenizer-based models, the Llama 3 tokenizer with its vocabulary of 128,000 tokens serves as the primary point of comparison. This choice reflects empirical findings that the Llama 3 tokenizer produces stronger baseline performance than its predecessor, the Llama 2 tokenizer, which operates with a smaller vocabulary of 32,000 tokens. The larger vocabulary of Llama 3 yields an average token size of approximately 4.4 bytes, compared to 3.7 bytes for Llama 2, representing a meaningful shift in how byte sequences map to computational steps.

## Baseline Models and Architectural Comparisons

The experimental framework positions BLT against two generations of established architectures: Llama 2 and Llama 3. These baselines represent the current state of the art in tokenization-based large language models, providing a rigorous standard against which to evaluate the viability of byte-level modeling at scale. The comparison encompasses multiple model scales—400 million, 1 billion, 2 billion, 4 billion, and 8 billion parameters—enabling a comprehensive assessment of how architectural advantages scale with model capacity.

The tokenization-based baselines employ Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), a subword tokenization algorithm that iteratively merges the most frequent byte pairs to construct a fixed vocabulary. The Llama 3 tokenizer, in particular, represents a substantial investment in vocabulary engineering, with its 128,000-token vocabulary achieving compression rates that necessitate fewer computational steps per byte sequence. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of increased embedding table dimensions, as larger vocabularies require proportionally larger output projection layers. The trade-off between vocabulary size and computational efficiency effectively constrains how much tokenization-based approaches can vary their average token size without incurring prohibitive parameter overhead.

For instance, the transition from Llama 2 to Llama 3 increased the average token size from 3.7 to 4.4 bytes, but this relatively modest 19% improvement required a fourfold expansion of the embedding table. BLT fundamentally circumvents this constraint by eliminating the fixed vocabulary entirely, allowing patch sizes to scale without corresponding increases in output projection dimensions. This architectural difference unlocks new possibilities for balancing model capacity against inference cost that remain inaccessible to tokenization-based approaches.

The experimental methodology ensures that comparisons remain fair by controlling for both training compute and context length. When comparing models with different patch sizes or token sizes, the number of bytes in each training batch remains constant in expectation. This precaution prevents confounding factors from the ability to process larger contexts, which could artificially advantage models with longer average units. Similarly, the architectural hyperparameters for BLT models at each scale are designed such that the latent global transformer matches the size and structure of the corresponding baseline transformer, isolating the effects of the byte-level processing and dynamic patching mechanisms.

## Entropy Model Configuration and Context Handling

The entropy-based patching scheme requires a separate model to estimate next-byte prediction uncertainty across the training corpus. This entropy model takes the form of a byte-level autoregressive language model trained on the same distribution as the full BLT model, ensuring that its entropy estimates reflect the actual difficulty of predicting each byte in context.

The default entropy model configuration employs a transformer architecture with 100 million parameters distributed across 14 layers, with a hidden dimensionality of 512. The model uses sliding window attention with a context window of 512 bytes, meaning that entropy estimates at each position incorporate information from the preceding 512 bytes. This context window represents a balance between computational efficiency—the entropy model must process every byte during training data preparation—and the need for sufficient contextual information to make meaningful entropy estimates.

The entropy model processes the entire training corpus as a preprocessing step, computing the entropy $H(x_i)$ at each byte position $i$ according to:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

where $p_e$ denotes the entropy model's predicted distribution over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. These entropy values then guide patch boundary decisions through threshold comparisons, as detailed in the patching methodology.

An important practical consideration concerns the handling of the entropy model's context during inference. Empirical observations revealed that entropy patching tends to produce progressively larger patches in highly structured content, such as multiple-choice question formats, where repetitive patterns lead to progressively lower entropy estimates. This phenomenon, termed "entropy drift," arises because the entropy model's predictions become increasingly confident when processing repetitive content within its context window.

To mitigate this effect in large-scale experiments, the methodology introduces two modifications. First, the entropy context is reset upon encountering newline characters, preventing the accumulation of contextual information across unrelated segments of text. Second, the approximate monotonicity constraint—identifying patch boundaries when $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$—proves more robust to entropy drift than the global threshold approach, as it identifies local increases in uncertainty rather than absolute entropy levels that may shift with context length.

For smaller entropy models with sufficiently limited receptive fields, the entire model can be encoded as an efficient lookup table, substantially accelerating the patching process during both training data preparation and inference.

## Evaluation Metrics

Comparing models that operate on fundamentally different units—bytes versus tokens—requires careful consideration of evaluation metrics. The standard metric of perplexity, defined as the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood per token, becomes problematic when models differ in their tokenization schemes. Perplexity measures the uncertainty per prediction step, but the number of steps required to process a given text varies with the tokenization strategy. A model operating on characters makes many more predictions than a model operating on subword tokens, yet this difference in granularity does not necessarily reflect a difference in modeling quality.

To address this incompatibility, the experimental framework adopts Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as the primary metric for comparing byte-level and token-level models. BPB normalizes the cross-entropy loss by the total number of bytes rather than the number of prediction steps, providing a tokenizer-independent measure of modeling quality. The formal definition is:

$$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})$ denotes the cross-entropy loss over the sequence $\pmb{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ represents the total byte count. The denominator includes the natural logarithm of 2 to convert from natural logarithm units (nats) to binary logarithm units (bits), maintaining consistency with information-theoretic conventions.

This normalization enables direct comparison: if one model achieves a lower BPB than another on the same text, it has learned a more efficient compression of that text, regardless of whether it processes that text byte-by-byte, token-by-token, or in variable-sized patches. The metric treats all models as competing to solve the same fundamental problem—predicting raw byte sequences—and evaluates them on equal terms.

Beyond language modeling metrics, downstream task evaluation encompasses a suite of classification and generation benchmarks. Classification tasks include ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, and MMLU, evaluated using prompt-scoring methods that calculate likelihood over choice characters. Coding tasks include MBPP and HumanEval, where pass@1 scores measure the ability to generate functional Python code.

## FLOPs Estimation

Quantifying computational cost requires a systematic methodology for counting floating-point operations (FLOPs). The experimental framework largely follows the conventions established by Chinchilla scaling laws, which decompose transformer computation into several components: feed-forward network operations, query-key-value-output (QKVO) projections in attention layers, attention score computation, and output projections.

A notable methodological choice concerns the input embedding layer. In many transformer implementations, the embedding lookup can be implemented as an efficient table access rather than a dense matrix multiplication, rendering it effectively a zero-FLOP operation. This assumption aligns with standard practice in scaling law calculations and prevents the embedding operation—which scales with vocabulary size—from artificially inflating FLOP counts for models with large vocabularies.

The backward pass through the network approximately doubles the FLOP count of the forward pass, reflecting the need to compute gradients with respect to all parameters. This 2:1 ratio between backward and forward passes is a standard assumption in transformer FLOP estimation.

For BLT architectures, the FLOP calculation must account for the multiple components of the model: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks in both encoder and decoder. The total FLOPs per byte are computed as:

$$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$$

where $h$ denotes hidden dimension, $l$ denotes number of layers, $n_{ctx}$ is the byte sequence length, $n_p$ is the patch size, $w$ is the local attention window, and $V$ is the vocabulary size for output projection (256 for bytes in the decoder, 0 elsewhere where not applicable). The parameter $k$ represents the ratio of patch dimension to byte dimension, and $r$ is the ratio of queries to key-value pairs in cross-attention.

The context length $m$ varies across components: the global transformer processes patches with context $n_{ctx}/n_p$, while local models process bytes within windows of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ or $w_{\mathcal{D}}$. This decomposition enables precise comparison of computational costs across architectures with different structural configurations.

## Hyperparameters and Optimization

The transformer blocks within BLT largely inherit architectural conventions from the Llama family. Feed-forward layers employ the SwiGLU activation function, which combines element-wise multiplication with a gated linear unit structure, providing improved training dynamics compared to standard ReLU or GELU activations. Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) encode position information through rotation matrices applied to query and key vectors, with a base frequency $\theta = 500,000$ that determines the frequency spectrum of positional encodings. Layer normalization uses RMSNorm, which normalizes by the root mean square of activations rather than computing mean and variance separately.

Self-attention layers use Flash Attention for efficient implementation of standard attention masks—block causal masks for the global transformer and fixed-window block causal masks for local models. The window size of 512 bytes represents a balance between computational efficiency and the ability to capture local dependencies. For cross-attention layers, which require dynamic masking determined by patch boundaries, the implementation uses Flex Attention to produce fused kernels that significantly accelerate training.

The optimization procedure uses the AdamW optimizer with momentum parameters $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and numerical stability constant $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. Weight decay of 0.1 provides regularization, and global gradient clipping at a threshold of 1.0 prevents gradient explosion during training. The learning rate schedule consists of a linear warm-up over 2,000 steps followed by cosine decay to zero, a configuration that has proven effective for training large language models across diverse architectures.

All BLT models use a learning rate of $4 \times 10^{-4}$, determined through hyperparameter search comparing rates between $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ at the 400M and 1B scales. This search revealed that the optimal learning rate coincides with standard settings for tokenization-based models, suggesting that the learning dynamics of BLT do not require substantial deviation from established practice.

The hash-based n-gram embeddings use 500,000 hash buckets with a single hash function, processing n-grams of sizes three through eight. These embeddings capture multi-byte patterns without requiring explicit storage of all possible n-grams, which would be computationally infeasible. The first cross-attention layer in the local encoder uses max-pooling to initialize query vectors from byte representations, providing a deterministic starting point for the attention computation.

The batch configuration maintains approximately 16 million bytes per batch on average, with byte sequences padded or truncated to 12,000 bytes for Llama 2 experiments and 24,000 bytes for BLT-1T experiments. These limits prevent memory spikes from sequences with unusually large patches while ensuring that the more expensive latent transformer processes full batches without excessive padding.

# Scaling Trends: Efficiency and Performance

Understanding how neural language models scale with increased computational resources is fundamental to modern machine learning research. The Byte Latent Transformer introduces a paradigm shift in this landscape by decoupling the traditional relationship between model size and inference cost. This chapter explores the scaling properties of BLT, demonstrating how it achieves computational efficiency while maintaining—and in some cases exceeding—the performance of state-of-the-art tokenization-based models. We will examine the theoretical foundations of these scaling behaviors, the empirical results across different model sizes, and the implications for future language model development.

# Parameter-Matched Compute Optimal Scaling Trends

The concept of compute-optimal training represents a cornerstone principle in modern language model development. When researchers speak of compute-optimal scaling, they refer to the theoretically ideal ratio between model parameters and training data that maximizes performance for a given computational budget. This framework, established through foundational work like Chinchilla, provides a principled way to compare different architectures on equal footing.

To understand BLT's scaling behavior, the researchers designed rigorous experiments comparing BLT against tokenizer-based models using the Llama 2 dataset. The experimental setup trains models across four different parameter scales: 1B, 2B, 4B, and 8B parameters. For each scale, both BPE-based models and BLT models are trained using the optimal ratio of model parameters to training data as determined by Llama 3. This approach ensures that the comparison reflects true architectural differences rather than artifacts of suboptimal training configurations.

The primary metric for comparison is bits-per-byte (BPB), which measures the average information content required to encode each byte of data under the model's predictions. This metric serves as a tokenizer-independent measure of language modeling quality, allowing fair comparison between byte-level and token-level models. Formally, BPB is defined as:

$$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ represents the cross-entropy loss over the data sequence $\pmb{x}$, and $n_{\text{bytes}}$ denotes the total number of bytes. This normalization by the natural logarithm of 2 converts the cross-entropy from nats to bits, providing an interpretable measure of model uncertainty.

The results reveal that BLT models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across all tested scales. This finding represents a significant breakthrough: prior to BLT, no byte-level Transformer architecture had achieved matching scaling trends with BPE-based models in compute-optimal regimes. The significance of this achievement cannot be overstated, as it validates the fundamental hypothesis that tokenization is not a necessary component for competitive language model performance.

The scaling curves demonstrate that both architectural innovations and dynamic patching contribute to this success. When examining space-patching-based models without the full BLT architectural enhancements, a substantial gap remains between these simplified variants and Llama 3. This ablation reveals that the combination of cross-attention mechanisms, hash n-gram embeddings, and entropy-based dynamic patching all play crucial roles in achieving parity with tokenizer-based models.

# Comparison with Llama 2 and Llama 3 Baselines at Different Scales

The choice of baseline tokenizer significantly impacts model performance, and understanding this relationship provides important context for interpreting BLT's achievements. The Llama 2 tokenizer operates with a vocabulary of 32,000 tokens and achieves an average token size of approximately 3.7 bytes. In contrast, the Llama 3 tokenizer expands this vocabulary to 128,000 tokens, resulting in an average token size of about 4.4 bytes.

This difference in vocabulary size reflects different design philosophies about the trade-off between vocabulary size and computational efficiency. Larger vocabularies mean fewer tokens are needed to encode the same text, potentially reducing the number of forward passes required during inference. However, larger vocabularies also require larger embedding tables, which increase the parameter count of the model's input and output layers. The Llama 3 tokenizer represents a more aggressive position on this trade-off, quadrupling the vocabulary size compared to Llama 2.

The empirical results clearly demonstrate that models trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer outperform those using the Llama 2 tokenizer on identical training data. This finding establishes an important baseline: any byte-level architecture must not only match Llama 2's performance but also compete with the stronger Llama 3 baseline to claim true parity with state-of-the-art systems.

BLT achieves this goal while offering additional flexibility that fixed-vocabulary tokenizers cannot provide. While Llama 2 and Llama 3 have fixed average token sizes determined by their vocabulary construction, BLT can operate with arbitrary patch sizes. The experiments show that BLT achieves similar scaling trends to Llama 3 even with an average patch size of 6 bytes, and maintains competitive performance with patch sizes up to 8 bytes.

The relationship between patch size and inference efficiency follows a straightforward principle: inference FLOPs are inversely proportional to the average patch size. A model operating with 8-byte patches requires approximately half the inference computation compared to a model with 4-byte patches, since the expensive global transformer is invoked half as often. This means BLT with larger patch sizes can theoretically achieve up to 50% inference FLOP savings compared to BPE-based models while maintaining competitive performance.

Interestingly, the scaling behavior of BLT models with larger patch sizes shows favorable properties as model scale increases. At the 1B parameter scale, BLT with an 8-byte patch size starts at a significantly worse position compared to BPE Llama 2. However, by the 7B scale, the same configuration ends up performing better than BPE baselines. This convergence suggests that larger patch sizes become more viable as model capacity increases, possibly because the global transformer becomes better at extracting useful information from longer byte sequences.

# The Impact of Patch Size on Scaling Behavior

The patch size parameter represents one of BLT's most distinctive features, introducing a new degree of freedom in model design that tokenizer-based architectures fundamentally lack. Understanding how patch size affects scaling behavior requires examining both the computational and modeling implications of this choice.

From a computational perspective, patch size directly determines the frequency with which the expensive global transformer is invoked. The global latent transformer consumes the bulk of FLOPs during both pre-training and inference. When bytes are grouped into larger patches, the global transformer processes fewer total patches for the same amount of data, directly reducing computational cost.

However, larger patches present modeling challenges. Each patch representation must encode more information about a longer sequence of bytes. The local encoder must compress more byte-level information into a single patch representation, and the local decoder must generate more bytes from each patch representation output by the global transformer. Intuitively, this increases the burden on both the encoder and decoder to maintain fine-grained information while the global transformer operates at a coarser granularity.

The experimental results reveal that BLT's architecture handles these challenges effectively. The combination of cross-attention mechanisms, which allow information to flow between byte-level and patch-level representations, and hash n-gram embeddings, which provide the model with explicit information about byte sequences, enables effective operation even at larger patch sizes.

The scaling curves show an important trend: larger patch sizes perform relatively better at larger model scales. This phenomenon has significant implications for future model development. As models scale to billions and potentially trillions of parameters, the relative contribution of the local encoder and decoder to total FLOPs decreases. When growing total parameters by a factor of 20 (from 400M to 8B), BLT only roughly doubles the local model parameters. This means that the efficiency gains from larger patches—which primarily affect the global transformer's computational load—become proportionally more significant at larger scales.

The practical implications of this scaling behavior are substantial. A practitioner developing an 8B parameter model has the option to use larger patches without sacrificing performance, directly translating to reduced inference costs. This flexibility does not exist in tokenizer-based models, where vocabulary size and average token size are fixed by the tokenizer construction process.

# Fixed-Inference Scaling: Increasing Model Size Without Increasing Inference Cost

Traditional scaling approaches for language models face a fundamental constraint: larger models require more computation during inference. This relationship creates a practical ceiling on model deployment, as the cost of serving predictions scales linearly (or worse) with model size. BLT introduces a novel scaling paradigm that breaks this constraint by simultaneously increasing both model size and patch size while maintaining a fixed inference budget.

The key insight enabling this approach is that inference FLOPs depend on both the model size and the number of patches processed. Larger patch sizes reduce the number of patches, which reduces the number of times the global transformer is invoked. This saved computation can then be reinvested into making the global transformer larger. In essence, the model takes fewer steps but each step involves a more powerful transformer.

To validate this scaling paradigm, the researchers conducted a fixed-inference scaling study. Starting from baseline models at 400M and 3.6B parameters with the Llama 2 tokenizer, they identified flop-equivalent models using both the Llama 3 tokenizer and BLT-Entropy models with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes. Each configuration was trained for various training FLOP budgets to understand how performance evolves with increased training computation.

The results demonstrate that BLT models achieve better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures within fixed inference FLOP classes. Interestingly, BPE models perform better with small training budgets, but BLT quickly surpasses them beyond the compute-optimal regime. The crossover point—where BLT begins to outperform token-based models—occurs at approximately 3 times the compute-optimal budget for smaller models and shifts to approximately 2.5 times for larger models.

This crossover pattern has practical significance for real-world model deployment. Many production models, including Llama 3.1, are trained well beyond the compute-optimal point—sometimes by orders of magnitude—to achieve better inference-time performance. Llama 3.1, for example, is trained on approximately two orders of magnitude more data than what is compute-optimal for its 8B parameter size. In this regime, BLT's advantages become more pronounced.

The scaling study also reveals that larger patch sizes exhibit steeper scaling trends, meaning performance improves more rapidly with additional training computation. The BLT model with 8-byte patches, which showed competitive performance at smaller scales, overtakes other configurations sooner when scaled to larger training budgets. This behavior suggests that the efficiency gains from larger patches become more valuable as models are pushed into over-trained regimes.

# Analysis of How Larger Patches Allow for Larger Global Transformers

The relationship between patch size and global transformer capacity represents a fundamental architectural innovation in BLT. To understand this relationship quantitatively, we must examine how FLOPs are distributed across BLT's components and how patch size affects this distribution.

The total FLOPs per byte for a BLT model can be decomposed into contributions from several components: the local encoder transformer, the global latent transformer, the local decoder transformer, and the cross-attention blocks in both encoder and decoder. Formally, this is expressed as:

$$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$$

where $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, and $h_{\mathcal{D}}$ denote the hidden dimensions of the global transformer, encoder, and decoder respectively; $l_{\mathcal{G}}$, $l_{\mathcal{E}}$, and $l_{\mathcal{D}}$ denote their layer counts; $n_p$ is the patch size; and $k$ is the ratio of patch-dimension to byte-dimension.

The critical observation is that the global transformer FLOPs scale inversely with patch size, while the local encoder and decoder FLOPs are largely independent of patch size. This creates an opportunity for reallocation: increasing patch size reduces global transformer FLOPs, creating headroom to increase the global transformer's size while maintaining the same total inference budget.

For example, consider the transition from 400M to 8B total parameters. In this scaling, the global transformer grows substantially (approximately 20x in terms of FLOPs), while the local models only roughly double in size. This asymmetric scaling means that at larger model sizes, the efficiency gains from larger patches—which primarily affect the global transformer—become proportionally more significant.

The practical consequence is that BLT enables a new scaling dimension. Rather than being limited to scaling model size (which directly increases inference cost), practitioners can now scale along a diagonal, simultaneously increasing model capacity and patch size. This flexibility is particularly valuable in production settings where inference cost often represents the dominant expense of model deployment.

The patch size 8 model provides a concrete illustration of this principle. When moving from the smaller to larger FLOP class, this model's size increased from 1.6x to 1.7x of the Llama 2 baseline model size. This increase occurs because larger patch sizes mean the global transformer runs less frequently, allowing it to be larger without increasing total inference FLOPs.

# Performance Trade-offs: Balancing Evaluation Metrics with FLOP Reduction

The relationship between model performance and computational efficiency represents a fundamental trade-off in language model design. BLT's architecture provides practitioners with explicit control over this trade-off through the patch size parameter, enabling fine-grained optimization for specific deployment requirements.

The downstream task evaluations illuminate this trade-off clearly. When comparing BLT-Entropy (with a 4.5-byte average patch size), BLT-Space (with a 6-byte average patch size), and Llama 3 tokenizer-based models on standard benchmarks, distinct patterns emerge. The BLT-Entropy model outperforms the Llama 3 model on 4 out of 7 tasks while being trained on the same number of bytes. This improvement likely stems from both the dynamic allocation of compute through entropy-based patching and the direct modeling of byte-level information.

The BLT-Space model presents a different optimization point. With its larger average patch size of 6 bytes, it underperforms the Llama 3 tokenizer on most tasks but achieves a significant reduction in inference FLOPs. The larger patch size allows the model to cover 30% more data with the same training budget, though this pushes the model further from the compute-optimal point. This configuration represents a valid choice for applications where inference efficiency is paramount and some performance degradation is acceptable.

The benchmark tasks span diverse capabilities: classification tasks including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, and MMLU; and code generation tasks including MBPP and HumanEval. The BLT-Entropy model demonstrates particular strength on MMLU, outperforming both the Llama 3 baseline and even matching models trained on significantly more data. This suggests that the entropy-based patching mechanism effectively identifies and allocates computation to conceptually difficult predictions.

Beyond standard benchmarks, BLT demonstrates significant advantages in robustness and character-level understanding. On noised versions of classification tasks, BLT outperforms tokenizer-based models by an average of 8 points on HellaSwag, even improving over Llama 3.1 trained on a much larger dataset. The CUTE benchmark, which tests character-level manipulation abilities, reveals an even larger gap: BLT-Entropy outperforms BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points, achieving near-perfect scores on spelling tasks. These improvements highlight that direct byte modeling provides capabilities that are difficult to achieve with tokenization-based approaches.

The low-resource machine translation results further validate BLT's advantages for modeling the long-tail of data. BLT outperforms Llama 3 on numerous language pairs within lower-resource language families, with a 2-point overall advantage in translating into English. This finding underscores the effectiveness of byte modeling for generalizing to sequences that appear infrequently in training data.

In summary, BLT redefines the efficiency-performance landscape for language models. The architecture achieves training FLOP-controlled parity with Llama 3 up to 8B scale while offering the option to trade minor losses in evaluation metrics for up to 50% reductions in inference FLOPs. More fundamentally, BLT unlocks a new dimension for scaling, enabling simultaneous increases in model and patch size within fixed inference budgets. These capabilities position BLT as a compelling alternative to traditional tokenization-based approaches for applications ranging from large-scale deployment to specialized tasks requiring robustness and character-level understanding.

# Robustness and Long-Tail Generalization

# Robustness to Input Noise: Handling Character-Level Perturbations

One of the most compelling advantages of byte-level modeling is the inherent robustness to input noise. Tokenization-based models face significant challenges when confronted with character-level perturbations because their tokenizers were designed around clean, standardized text. When noise disrupts the expected byte patterns, tokenizers can produce unexpected or malformed tokens, leading to degraded model performance. The Byte Latent Transformer, by contrast, operates directly on raw bytes and therefore maintains its ability to process input regardless of surface-level variations.

To systematically evaluate this robustness, the researchers created noised versions of standard benchmark classification tasks. They employed five distinct character-level noising strategies, each designed to stress different aspects of a model's ability to handle input variation. The first strategy, called \textit{AntSpeak}, converts all text into uppercase with space-separated characters, transforming a phrase like ``hello world'' into ``H E L L O W O R L D.'' This tests whether a model can still understand content when the fundamental character composition is dramatically altered. The second strategy, \textit{Drop}, randomly removes 10\% of the characters from the text, forcing the model to infer missing information from context. The third strategy, \textit{RandomCase}, converts 50\% of characters to uppercase and 50\% to lowercase randomly throughout the text, creating visual inconsistency that can confuse tokenizers. The fourth strategy, \textit{Repeat}, repeats 20\% of characters up to four times, introducing stuttering artifacts. The fifth strategy, \textit{UpperCase}, simply transforms all characters to uppercase.

The experimental results on noised HellaSwag demonstrate that BLT significantly outperforms tokenizer-based models across all noise types. Compared to a model trained on the same data, BLT achieves an average advantage of 8 points in accuracy. Remarkably, BLT even outperforms the Llama 3.1 model, despite that model being trained on a much larger dataset of 15 trillion tokens. This finding is particularly striking because it suggests that the architectural advantage of byte-level modeling can compensate for substantial differences in training data scale. The evaluation methodology applied each noising strategy to either the prompt, the completion, or both, treating these as separate tasks and reporting average scores to ensure comprehensive coverage of robustness capabilities.

The underlying reason for this robustness lies in the fundamental difference between how BLT and tokenization-based models process text. When a tokenizer encounters noisy input, it may produce out-of-vocabulary tokens or fragment words in unexpected ways. For example, a tokenizer trained on lowercase text might not have appropriate tokens for uppercase variants, or repeated characters might cause the tokenizer to split tokens differently than expected during training. BLT, however, has no fixed vocabulary and processes each byte individually. Whether a byte appears as lowercase ``a'' or uppercase ``A,'' the model processes it through the same embedding table and can leverage its learned understanding of character-level patterns. This direct access to the byte sequence means that noise affects only the surface representation, not the model's fundamental ability to extract meaning.

# Character-Level Understanding: Performance on the CUTE Benchmark

Beyond robustness to noise, byte-level modeling provides significant advantages in understanding the internal structure of words and characters. Tokenization-based models, by abstracting away from individual characters to subword tokens, often struggle with tasks that require fine-grained character manipulation or orthographic awareness. To evaluate these capabilities, the researchers employed the CUTE benchmark, which comprises several tasks broadly classified into three categories: understanding composition, understanding orthographic similarity, and the ability to manipulate sequences.

The CUTE benchmark poses a significant challenge for most tokenizer-based models. While these models appear to possess knowledge of their tokens' spellings, they struggle to effectively utilize this information when required to manipulate text at the character level. This disconnect between knowing and doing reveals a fundamental limitation of tokenization: the model learns about character sequences only indirectly, through the patterns they form in training data, rather than through direct manipulation and understanding.

The results demonstrate a dramatic advantage for BLT on character-level tasks. BLT-Entropy outperforms both BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on the CUTE benchmark. This is despite BLT having been trained on 16 times less data than Llama 3.1, indicating that character-level information is particularly difficult for BPE models to learn. The most striking performance comes on character manipulation tasks, where BLT achieves 99.9\% accuracy on both spelling tasks. This near-perfect performance suggests that when a model is trained directly on bytes, it develops an intimate understanding of character composition that enables it to manipulate text with precision.

The qualitative examples provided in the paper illustrate scenarios where the Llama 3 tokenizer model struggles while BLT performs well. These include tasks that require understanding how characters combine to form words, how words relate to each other orthographically, and how to manipulate character sequences to achieve specific goals. The only tasks where BPE performs better are word deletion and insertion. These word manipulation tasks might not be straightforward for a byte-level model because the concept of a ``word'' is not explicitly marked in the byte stream. However, the gap is not large, and as the authors note, building from characters to words may be easier than the reverse process of understanding characters when starting from tokens.

The implications of these results extend beyond simple benchmark performance. Character-level understanding is crucial for many real-world applications, including spelling correction, text normalization, handling of proper names and technical terms, and processing of user-generated content that may contain typos or non-standard spellings. By demonstrating superior performance on CUTE, BLT shows that byte-level modeling provides a stronger foundation for these applications than tokenization-based approaches.

# Phonological Awareness: Grapheme-to-Phoneme Tasks

Language is not merely a visual medium; it is fundamentally a spoken phenomenon. The relationship between written characters (graphemes) and spoken sounds (phonemes) is therefore a critical aspect of language understanding. The Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) task evaluates a model's capability to map a sequence of graphemes, which are the characters representing a word, into a transcription of that word's pronunciation using phonemes.

This task is challenging for several reasons. First, the mapping from graphemes to phonemes is not one-to-one; the same letter can represent different sounds in different contexts, and the same sound can be represented by different letters. Consider the letter sequence ``ough'' in English, which is pronounced differently in ``though,'' ``through,'' ``rough,'' ``cough,'' and ``bough.'' Second, pronunciation depends on word origin, with words borrowed from other languages often retaining pronunciation patterns from their source language. Third, pronunciation varies by dialect, meaning that there is often no single correct answer.

The researchers assessed G2P capability using Phonology Bench in a 5-shot setting. In this evaluation setup, the model is provided with five examples of correctly transcribed words before being asked to transcribe a new word. This few-shot approach tests whether the model can learn the grapheme-to-phoneme mapping pattern from context and apply it to new cases, rather than relying solely on memorization.

The results show that BLT outperforms the baseline Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model on this task. This advantage likely stems from BLT's direct exposure to character-level patterns during training. While a tokenizer-based model might see ``phonetics'' as a single token or a small number of subword tokens, BLT processes each character individually and must learn the relationships between character sequences and their pronunciations. This forces the model to develop a more detailed understanding of orthographic patterns and their phonological correlates.

The practical importance of phonological awareness extends to speech recognition, text-to-speech synthesis, and language learning applications. Models that understand the relationship between written and spoken forms can better assist with pronunciation guides, detect potential spelling errors based on phonetic similarity, and support language learners in understanding how written words should be pronounced. BLT's superior performance on G2P tasks suggests that byte-level modeling provides a stronger foundation for these applications.

# Long-Tail Generalization: Low-Resource Machine Translation

One of the most significant challenges in natural language processing is generalization to the long tail of the data distribution. The long tail refers to the vast array of rare words, phrases, and language patterns that appear infrequently in training data but are nonetheless important for comprehensive language understanding. Tokenization-based models are particularly vulnerable to long-tail challenges because their fixed vocabularies are typically optimized for frequent patterns. Rare words or sequences may be broken into uninformative subword pieces or mapped to unknown tokens, limiting the model's ability to process them effectively.

Machine translation provides an excellent testbed for long-tail generalization because it requires handling diverse vocabulary across language pairs, including many lower-resource languages that may not be well-represented in training data. The researchers evaluated BLT on translating into and out of six popular language families and twenty-one lower-resource languages with various scripts, using the FLORES-101 benchmark. Translation quality was measured using SentencePiece BLEU scores.

The results demonstrate that BLT outperforms the model trained with the Llama 3 tokenizer, achieving a 2-point overall advantage in translating into English and a 0.5-point advantage in translating from English. In popular language pairs, BLT performs comparably to or slightly better than Llama 3, suggesting that byte-level modeling does not sacrifice performance on common cases. However, BLT particularly excels on numerous language pairs within lower-resource language families. This performance advantage underscores the effectiveness of byte modeling for generalizing to long-tail byte sequences.

The reason for this advantage lies in how byte-level modeling handles rare sequences. Rather than relying on a fixed vocabulary that may not include adequate representations for rare words or scripts, BLT processes all text as bytes. A word that appears only a handful of times in training data can still be processed character by character, allowing the model to leverage its understanding of character composition and its knowledge of similar words. This is particularly valuable for languages with different scripts, where a tokenizer trained primarily on one script may have very few dedicated tokens for another script.

Furthermore, the multilingual advantage of byte-level modeling addresses concerns about ``multilingual inequity'' that have been raised about tokenization-based models. When a tokenizer is trained primarily on high-resource languages, it may not efficiently encode other languages, leading to longer sequences and less effective processing. Byte-level models treat all languages equivalently at the input level, potentially providing more equitable processing across the language spectrum.

# Distillation and Initialization: 'Byte-ifying' Pre-Trained Llama 3 Models

Training large language models from scratch requires enormous computational resources, which raises the question of whether existing tokenization-based models can be converted to byte-level models. The researchers explored a workflow where BLT models leverage existing pre-trained tokenizer-based models for better and faster training convergence. This process, which can be termed ``byte-ifying,'' involves initializing the global transformer parameters of BLT with those of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model.

The approach works as follows. The global transformer in BLT serves the same fundamental role as the main transformer in a tokenization-based model: it processes sequences of representations (patches for BLT, tokens for standard models) through multiple transformer layers. Because this global transformer has the same architecture as a standard transformer, its weights can be initialized from a pre-trained model. After initialization, the training process updates the weights of the global transformer using one-tenth the learning rate employed for the local encoder and local decoder models. This differential learning rate allows the pre-trained knowledge to be preserved while still adapting to the new task of processing byte-level inputs through patches.

The results demonstrate that BLT initialized from Llama 3.1 significantly outperforms both the Llama 3 baseline and the BLT baseline trained from scratch, even though all models were trained with the same number of flops. When compared to the BLT-Entropy model trained on the larger 1T token dataset, the byte-ified model still achieves superior performance on the MMLU task. This suggests that distillation from pre-trained models can be an effective approach to significantly reduce training flops while achieving competitive or superior performance.

However, when compared to the original LLaMA 3.1 model trained on 15 trillion tokens, the byte-ified model experiences a slight performance decline on MMLU and HumanEval, with a more significant drop on other tasks. This indicates that further work is needed to fully leverage the pre-trained model and improve upon its performance. The authors suggest that optimizing data mixtures and hyperparameters may help close this gap.

This distillation approach can also be viewed as a method for transforming tokenizer-based models into tokenizer-free ones. Rather than discarding the enormous investment in training models like Llama 3.1, this technique allows the knowledge encoded in those models to be transferred to a byte-level architecture. Future work in this direction may uncover methods that not only retain the benefits of byte-level modeling but also push performance beyond tokenizer-based models without requiring training from scratch.

# Ablation Studies and Architectural Validation

Understanding why an architecture works is often as important as demonstrating that it works. In the case of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT), numerous design decisions contribute to its ability to match and sometimes exceed the performance of tokenization-based models. This chapter systematically investigates the necessity of specific design choices through ablation studies, which involve removing or modifying individual components to measure their isolated contribution to overall performance. These experiments provide crucial insights for future architecture design, revealing which components are essential, which are optional, and how different hyperparameters interact to determine model quality.

The ablation studies presented here were conducted primarily using models trained on the Llama 2 dataset, with scaling experiments at the 400M and 1B parameter scales. By carefully controlling for confounding factors such as training compute and context length, these experiments isolate the effects of specific architectural choices and hyperparameter settings. The findings illuminate the trade-offs involved in designing byte-level language models and offer guidance for practitioners seeking to optimize BLT-style architectures for their own applications.

# Impact of Entropy Model Size and Context Window Length

The entropy model serves as the cornerstone of BLT's dynamic patching mechanism. Recall that entropy patching uses a small byte-level language model to estimate the uncertainty of next-byte predictions, creating patch boundaries at points of high entropy. The quality of these entropy estimates directly determines how effectively compute is allocated across the byte sequence. This raises a fundamental question: how sophisticated must the entropy model be to provide useful patch boundaries?

To answer this question, researchers conducted extensive experiments varying two key dimensions of the entropy model: its parameter count and its context window length. The parameter count determines the model's capacity to capture complex patterns in the byte distribution, while the context window length determines how many preceding bytes the model considers when estimating entropy at each position. Both dimensions influence the quality of patch boundary predictions, but potentially in different ways.

The experiments trained byte-level entropy transformer models across a wide range of sizes, from 1M parameters at the smallest scale to 100M parameters at the largest. Similarly, context window lengths varied from 64 bytes to 512 bytes. Each entropy model configuration was then used to guide the training of full BLT models at the 400M and 1B parameter scales. The resulting bits-per-byte (BPB) versus training flop scaling curves reveal how entropy model quality affects overall BLT performance.

The results demonstrate a clear positive correlation between scaling performance and both dimensions of the entropy model. Larger entropy models with more parameters provide better patch boundaries, leading to more efficient allocation of compute in the main BLT model. Similarly, entropy models with longer context windows capture more distant dependencies, which improves their ability to identify genuinely unpredictable byte positions versus positions that merely appear unpredictable due to limited context.

However, the relationship is not linear. The experiments reveal diminishing returns when the entropy model scales beyond approximately 50M parameters. Beyond this point, further increases in entropy model size yield progressively smaller improvements in the final BLT model's performance. This suggests that the entropy estimation task, while important, does not require the full sophistication of a large language model. The entropy model need only capture enough structure to distinguish reliably between predictable and unpredictable byte positions.

The default configuration used in the main experiments reflects these findings: a transformer with 100M parameters, 14 layers, and a hidden dimensionality of 512. While this slightly exceeds the 50M parameter threshold where diminishing returns begin, it provides a comfortable margin of performance without being computationally burdensome. The sliding window attention mechanism operates over 512 bytes of context, allowing the entropy model to consider a substantial window of preceding bytes when making its predictions.

An interesting practical consideration emerges when the entropy model's receptive field becomes small enough: the entire model can be encoded in an efficient lookup table. This optimization eliminates the need to run a neural network during the preprocessing step, further reducing the computational overhead of entropy-based patching. The trade-off between entropy model sophistication and preprocessing cost depends on the specific deployment scenario, with smaller entropy models offering faster patching at the cost of somewhat less optimal compute allocation.

# Comparative Analysis of Different Patching Schemes on Downstream Tasks

The BLT architecture's most distinctive feature is its approach to grouping bytes into patches for efficient processing. However, entropy patching is not the only viable approach. Understanding how different patching schemes compare provides crucial insight into the value of dynamic, learned patching versus simpler alternatives.

Four distinct patching schemes were systematically compared: strided patching with fixed stride values of 4 and 6 bytes, space-based patching that creates boundaries at whitespace characters, BPE tokenizer-based patching using the Llama 3 vocabulary, and entropy-based patching using a small byte language model. Each scheme represents a different philosophy for determining patch boundaries, from purely mechanical (strided) to heuristic (space-based) to data-driven (entropy-based).

A critical experimental consideration is controlling for context length. Different patching schemes produce different numbers of patches for the same byte sequence, which means the main transformer processes different effective sequence lengths. To ensure fair comparisons, all models were configured to see the same number of bytes in each sequence during training and inference. This control prevents any patching scheme from gaining an unfair advantage simply by providing longer contexts to the model.

The scaling experiments reveal a clear hierarchy among patching schemes. All alternatives significantly outperform static strided patching, confirming that some form of intelligent boundary selection is essential for competitive performance. Strided patching's weakness stems from its inability to adapt to the actual complexity of different byte sequences. A fixed stride of 4 or 6 bytes treats a sequence of whitespace characters the same as a complex mathematical formula, wasting compute on predictable regions while potentially under-allocating compute for difficult predictions.

Space patching emerges as a surprisingly competitive alternative to entropy-based patching. By creating patch boundaries at whitespace characters, this approach naturally aligns patches with linguistic units in many languages. The first byte of a word is often the most difficult to predict, as it significantly constrains the possible completions, while subsequent bytes in the same word are typically easier to predict given the context. Space patching naturally allocates one transformer step per word, which often aligns compute with prediction difficulty.

However, entropy-based patching maintains consistent advantages over space patching in the experimental results. While space patching is simpler to implement and does not require running an entropy model during preprocessing, the gains observed with entropy-based patching on scaling trends carry forward to downstream benchmark tasks. The entropy model captures more nuanced patterns of prediction difficulty than the simple heuristic of whitespace boundaries, allowing for more fine-grained and accurate allocation of compute.

The comparison with BPE tokenizer-based patching is particularly instructive. When using the Llama 3 tokenizer to determine patch boundaries (essentially treating each token as a patch), performance falls between the extremes. This hybrid approach captures some benefits of learned boundary selection without the full flexibility of entropy-based patching. The results suggest that the boundary between tokens and patches is not merely semantic: the ability to create arbitrary patch boundaries without a fixed vocabulary provides genuine advantages for compute allocation.

Perhaps most importantly, the experiments demonstrate that space patching, while simpler, remains far from matching Llama 3's performance when implemented without additional architectural improvements like n-gram embeddings and cross-attention. This highlights the synergistic relationship between patching strategy and overall architecture. The full BLT architecture, combining entropy-based patching with cross-attention and n-gram embeddings, achieves parity with state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models—something that no single component achieves in isolation.

# The Role of Cross-Attention Layers in the Encoder and Decoder

Cross-attention mechanisms form a crucial bridge between the byte-level local models and the patch-level global transformer in BLT. Understanding where and how cross-attention contributes to performance helps clarify the information flow through the architecture and guides decisions about where to invest computational resources.

The local encoder uses cross-attention to aggregate byte representations into patch representations, while the local decoder uses cross-attention to decode patch representations back into bytes. In both cases, the cross-attention mechanism allows information to flow between different levels of abstraction: from bytes to patches in the encoder, and from patches to bytes in the decoder.

Ablation experiments tested various configurations of cross-attention in both encoder and decoder. For the encoder cross-attention, three different query initialization strategies were compared: using the same learned embedding for every global state, using a hash embedding of the bytes in the patch, and using a pooling of the encoder hidden representation of the patch bytes at the given encoder layer. Each initialization strategy represents a different assumption about what information should guide the aggregation of bytes into patches.

The results reveal a striking asymmetry between encoder and decoder. Cross-attention in the decoder proves most effective for overall performance. This finding aligns with intuition about the model's task: the decoder must convert abstract patch representations back into specific bytes, and cross-attention provides a direct mechanism for the decoder to attend to the appropriate patch representation when predicting each byte. Without this cross-attention, the decoder would have to rely solely on the information passed through the encoder's final hidden states.

In the encoder, cross-attention provides more modest improvements. The benefit appears only when queries are initialized with pooling of the encoder hidden representations. This pooling initialization allows the cross-attention to build upon the byte representations that have already been processed through the transformer layers, creating a more informed aggregation than a learned embedding or hash embedding could provide. The relatively small improvement suggests that the encoder's primary function—transforming byte embeddings into representations suitable for the global transformer—can be largely achieved through the local transformer layers, with cross-attention providing a helpful but not essential refinement.

An important finding relates to the interaction between cross-attention and patch size. Cross-attention helps particularly on Common-Crawl data and especially with larger patch sizes. With larger patches, more bytes must be aggregated into a single patch representation, making the quality of that aggregation more critical. Cross-attention provides a learned, context-sensitive mechanism for this aggregation, which becomes increasingly valuable as the number of bytes per patch increases.

The encoder cross-attention follows the Perceiver architecture's input cross-attention module, with a key modification: the latent representations correspond to variable patch representations rather than a fixed set of latents. Each query vector attends only to the keys and values corresponding to bytes within its patch, ensuring that patch representations are built solely from relevant byte information. This patch-specific masking is essential for maintaining the correspondence between patches and their constituent bytes.

The decoder cross-attention reverses the roles of queries and key-values. Byte representations serve as queries, attending to patch representations as key-values. This allows each byte prediction to draw upon the appropriate patch representation, with the masking ensuring that each byte attends only to the patch it belongs to. The initial byte representations for cross-attention come from the final encoder layer, establishing a direct path from input bytes through encoder processing to decoder predictions.

# Sensitivity to n-gram Hash Embedding Vocabulary Sizes

The n-gram hash embeddings represent one of BLT's key innovations for capturing local byte patterns. Understanding how the vocabulary size for these embeddings affects performance reveals important trade-offs between representational capacity and computational efficiency.

Hash embeddings work by mapping byte n-grams through a hash function to indices in a fixed-size embedding table. The vocabulary size determines how many distinct embeddings are available for the potentially infinite set of possible n-grams. With a small vocabulary, hash collisions become more frequent, meaning different n-grams will map to the same embedding. With a large vocabulary, more n-grams receive distinct embeddings, but at the cost of larger embedding tables and more parameters.

The ablation experiments tested vocabulary sizes of 0 (no hash embeddings), 100K, 200K, and 400K n-gram hash embeddings. The results reveal that hash embeddings help across all domains, but with varying magnitudes of benefit. Wikipedia and Github data show particularly strong improvements—approximately 0.04 bits-per-byte difference compared to 0.01 bpb difference on other domains after 15k steps at 8B scale. This domain-dependent benefit suggests that certain types of text benefit more from n-gram information than others.

The Wikipedia benefit likely stems from the structured, encyclopedic nature of the text, where certain byte sequences (like common words or formatting patterns) appear frequently. Hash embeddings allow the model to recognize these patterns without having to learn them from scratch through the transformer layers. Similarly, code in Github repositories often contains repeated patterns like variable names and syntactic constructs that hash embeddings can capture efficiently.

At the 8B scale, experiments tested vocabularies from 300K to 500K hashes, finding that the performance difference was minimal—approximately 0.001 bpb after 15k steps. This indicates diminishing returns beyond approximately 300K hashes. The embedding table is large enough that most important n-grams receive distinct embeddings, with collisions affecting only rare combinations. This threshold provides practical guidance for architecture design: vocabularies of 300K to 500K hashes are sufficient for models at this scale.

The default configuration uses 500,000 hashes with n-gram sizes ranging from 3 to 8. This range captures patterns from trigrams up to 8-grams, providing a multi-scale representation of local context. For each byte position, the model constructs n-grams of each size, hashes each separately, and sums the corresponding embeddings before adding to the base byte embedding and normalizing. This creates an augmented embedding that encodes both the current byte and its recent context.

Importantly, the gains from hash embeddings appear largely complementary with those from cross-attention. These components provide improvements on different datasets, suggesting they capture different aspects of byte-level information. Hash embeddings excel at capturing local pattern statistics, while cross-attention enables dynamic, context-sensitive aggregation. The combination provides more benefit than either alone.

The hash function used, RollPolyHash, provides a computationally efficient way to map n-grams to indices. The rolling property means that computing the hash for an n-gram at position $i$ can build upon the hash computation for the previous position, avoiding redundant computation. This efficiency is important during both training and inference, as hash computations must be performed for every byte position.

# Optimizing Local Model Depth and Layer Configuration

The local encoder and decoder models form BLT's interface with the raw byte stream. Their depth—the number of transformer layers—determines how much local processing occurs before information enters the global latent transformer or after it exits. Optimizing this configuration involves balancing computational cost against representational power.

The fundamental design principle underlying BLT is that the global latent transformer should handle the most computationally demanding aspects of language modeling, while the local models handle byte-level details. This division suggests that local models should be lightweight, minimizing the computational overhead of processing each byte while still providing sufficient expressiveness for the byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte conversions.

Ablation experiments tested various configurations for the number of layers in the local encoder and decoder. The key finding is that BLT works well with an extremely lightweight encoder—just one layer—when paired with hash n-gram embeddings. The hash embeddings provide the encoder with rich local context without requiring deep transformer layers to extract that information. A single layer can then focus on the essential transformation needed to prepare byte representations for the global transformer.

The decoder, in contrast, benefits from a heavier configuration. More decoder layers provide additional capacity for converting patch representations into byte predictions. This asymmetry makes sense given the different functions of encoder and decoder. The encoder's task is primarily aggregation: combining multiple byte representations into a single patch representation. The decoder's task is primarily expansion: taking a patch representation and generating multiple byte predictions. The expansion task may simply require more processing to maintain precision across multiple outputs.

The interaction between local model depth and other architectural components reveals important synergies. Hash embeddings reduce the need for deep encoder layers by providing pre-computed local context. Cross-attention in the decoder increases the value of decoder layers by providing a direct mechanism for incorporating patch information into byte predictions. These interactions suggest that architectural decisions should be considered holistically rather than optimized component by component.

When patch sizes increase, additional encoder layers become more beneficial. Experiments with patch size 8 used 3 encoder layers instead of 1. With larger patches, more bytes must be aggregated into each patch representation, increasing the complexity of the aggregation task. Additional encoder layers provide more capacity for this complex aggregation. This scaling of encoder depth with patch size ensures that the quality of patch representations does not degrade as patches grow larger.

The relationship between local and global model sizes follows a consistent pattern as BLT scales up. When growing total parameters from 400M to 8B—a 20x increase—the local model parameters only roughly double. This disproportionate scaling reflects the architectural philosophy: as models grow larger, most additional capacity should go to the global latent transformer, which handles the core language modeling task. The local models need only grow enough to maintain adequate byte-level processing as the overall architecture scales.

This scaling pattern has important implications for inference efficiency. Since local models process every byte while the global transformer processes only patches, the relative cost of local models increases with patch size. Keeping local models lightweight while growing the global transformer allows BLT to scale efficiently, with more parameters focused on fewer patch steps rather than being distributed across many byte steps.

The optimal configuration depends on the specific patch size and model scale. For standard BLT models with moderate patch sizes (around 4-5 bytes), a single encoder layer paired with a multi-layer decoder provides good performance with minimal overhead. For larger patch sizes, additional encoder layers help maintain patch representation quality. These findings provide a starting point for architecture optimization, though the exact optimal configuration may vary depending on the target domain and computational budget.

# Conclusion and Future Directions

# Summary of Contributions: Efficiency, Robustness, and Scaling

The Byte Latent Transformer represents a significant advance in language modeling architecture, demonstrating that tokenizer-free models can achieve competitive performance with traditional tokenization-based approaches while offering substantial advantages in efficiency and robustness. The contributions of this work span several interconnected dimensions that collectively establish a new paradigm for training large language models directly on raw byte data.

The first major contribution lies in the development of a dynamic compute allocation mechanism that fundamentally reimagines how processing resources are distributed across input sequences. Unlike tokenization-based models, which allocate identical computational resources to every token regardless of the complexity of the prediction task, the BLT architecture adaptively concentrates computation where it matters most. This is achieved through entropy-based patching, which identifies high-uncertainty byte predictions and allocates additional transformer steps to these challenging regions. The practical implications of this innovation are substantial: the BLT achieves training flop-controlled parity with Llama 3 at scales up to 8 billion parameters while offering the option to trade minor evaluation metric losses for inference flop reductions of up to 50 percent. This efficiency gain stems from the observation that many predictions in language modeling are comparatively easy—predicting the continuation of a word given its first character requires far less computational investment than predicting that first character—and the BLT architecture exploits this asymmetry directly.

The second contribution concerns the scaling properties of byte-level models. Prior to this work, the computational expense of processing raw bytes at scale presented a seemingly insurmountable barrier, with sequence lengths that were prohibitive for practical training. The BLT overcomes this limitation through its patching mechanism, which reduces the effective sequence length processed by the computationally expensive global transformer. Crucially, the architecture unlocks a new dimension for scaling: the ability to simultaneously increase model size and patch size while maintaining a fixed inference budget. Because longer patch sizes reduce the number of global transformer steps, the saved computation can be reallocated to increase the size of the latent transformer itself. This represents a fundamental departure from tokenization-based models, where increasing vocabulary size (and thus average token length) necessitates larger embedding tables, creating a trade-off that severely limits flexibility. The BLT decouples these considerations, demonstrating in fixed inference scaling experiments that larger models taking fewer steps on larger patches can outperform smaller models taking more steps.

The third contribution addresses the robustness and sub-word understanding capabilities of language models. By operating directly on bytes, the BLT gains access to fine-grained character-level information that is typically obscured by tokenization. This proves particularly valuable for handling noisy inputs, where the BLT substantially outperforms tokenization-based models, as well as for tasks requiring character manipulation, spelling, and phonological understanding. On the CUTE benchmark, which evaluates character-level understanding across tasks involving composition, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation, the BLT-Entropy model outperforms BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points, demonstrating near-perfect performance on spelling tasks. Furthermore, the byte-level approach yields improvements in low-resource machine translation, particularly for language pairs involving scripts that are underrepresented in standard tokenizers, highlighting the equity benefits of removing vocabulary-based biases from the modeling pipeline.

# Historical Context: From Character RNNs to Byte-level Transformers

The development of byte-level language modeling traces a fascinating trajectory through the history of neural language models, reflecting evolving architectural paradigms and computational capabilities. Understanding this progression provides essential context for appreciating the significance of the BLT architecture and the challenges it addresses.

The earliest successes in character-level language modeling emerged alongside the development of recurrent neural networks in the early 2010s. Researchers recognized that modeling characters directly offered compelling advantages: the ability to handle out-of-vocabulary words organically, improved handling of morphologically rich languages, and freedom from the heuristic decisions inherent in tokenization schemes. Character language modeling gained popularity with foundational work by researchers including Sutskever, who demonstrated generative character-level models, and Mikolov, who explored subword-level modeling. Graves extended this line of research with generative sequence models that could learn complex patterns in character sequences. These early approaches demonstrated the conceptual viability of character-level modeling while revealing the computational challenges that would motivate subsequent architectural innovations.

The development of character-level processing architectures progressed along multiple technical pathways. Kim and colleagues proposed models that processed characters using convolutional and highway networks, with outputs feeding into LSTM-based recurrent networks. This hybrid approach achieved performance competitive with word-level models of the era, with particular advantages for morphologically rich languages where character-level morphology carries substantial information. Kenter demonstrated that byte-level LSTM models could outperform word-level models for machine comprehension in morphologically rich languages such as Turkish and Russian. Zhang explored character-based convolutional models for classification tasks, finding advantages in certain domains. These works collectively established that character and byte-level representations offered genuine benefits, but their computational requirements limited their scalability relative to word-level alternatives.

The hierarchical modeling approach emerged as one strategy for managing the computational burden of character-level processing. Chung developed hierarchical LSTM models that used boundary detectors at each level to discover latent hierarchical structure in text, improving character-level language modeling performance. ByteNet, introduced by Kalchbrenner, employed convolutional layers on characters rather than attention mechanisms for machine translation, representing an alternative architectural approach to managing sequence length. These hierarchical approaches foreshadowed the patching strategies that would later prove central to scalable byte-level transformers.

The emergence of the transformer architecture, with its attention mechanism, combined with subword tokenization techniques such as Byte-Pair Encoding, dramatically improved neural language modeling performance. However, the implicit assumption that models should operate at the word or subword level represented a significant inductive bias. Researchers interested in combining transformer architectures with character-level processing initially faced disappointing results: Al-Rfou and colleagues used very deep transformers with auxiliary losses to train character-level models that outperformed previous LSTM-based approaches but still lagged behind word-level models. GPT-2 similarly found that byte-level language models were not competitive with word-level models on large-scale benchmarks. Choe demonstrated that byte-level transformer models could theoretically outperform subword models with comparable parameters, but at the cost of substantially greater computation and training time.

The development of encoder-only models for transfer learning prompted renewed investigation of character-level processing. CharacterBERT (later called CharFormer) built word representations by applying convolutions on character embeddings, demonstrating improvements in specialized domains such as medical text, though again at substantial computational cost. CANINE, developed by Clark and colleagues, operated directly on character sequences using a deep transformer stack combined with local transformers and strided convolutions for downsampling, outperforming multilingual BERT on downstream multilingual tasks at the 150 million parameter scale. ByT5 explored encoder-decoder models operating directly on bytes without patching operations. While ByT5 exhibited improved robustness to noise and competitive performance with substantially less training data, the lack of patching necessitated expensive attention computations over every byte, rendering the approach computationally intensive at scale.

# Relation to Prior Work: MegaByte, SpaceByte, and ByT5

The BLT architecture builds directly upon several influential predecessors in the byte-level modeling literature, refining and extending their insights to achieve new levels of performance and scalability. Understanding the relationship between BLT and these prior approaches illuminates both the conceptual progression of the field and the specific innovations that enable BLT's advances.

ByT5, developed by Xue and colleagues, represented a significant exploration of byte-level encoder-decoder models that deliberately avoided any form of patching or downsampling. The rationale for this design choice was to maintain complete access to byte-level information throughout the model, ensuring robustness and flexibility. ByT5 indeed demonstrated compelling properties: improved robustness to input noise, competitive performance with tokenizer-based models trained on four times more data, and natural handling of arbitrary byte sequences. However, the computational demands of computing attention over every byte in long sequences proved prohibitive for scaling. The attention mechanism, which computes pairwise interactions between all positions in a sequence, scales quadratically in sequence length, meaning that processing byte sequences (which are typically four to five times longer than token sequences) dramatically increased computational requirements. While ByT5 demonstrated the potential of byte-level modeling, it also highlighted the necessity of efficiency improvements for practical deployment at scale.

MegaByte, introduced by Yu and colleagues, addressed the efficiency challenge through static patching with a fixed stride. The architecture employed a decoder-only causal language model design, using fixed-size patches combined with concatenation of representations to convert bytes to patch representations, and a local model on the decoder side to convert patches back to bytes. MegaByte demonstrated that patching could reduce computational requirements to levels that enabled meaningful scale, achieving performance competitive with tokenizer-based models at the 1 billion parameter scale on approximately 400 billion bytes of training data. This represented an important proof of concept for patch-based byte modeling. However, the BLT experiments revealed significant limitations of the static patching approach. Fixed stride patching fails to dynamically allocate computation to where it is needed most, potentially wasting transformer steps on predictable regions while under-allocating compute to information-dense content. Furthermore, static patching leads to inconsistent and non-contextual segmentation of similar byte sequences, with the same word potentially being split differently across different contexts. The BLT ablations demonstrate that static patching lags substantially behind current state-of-the-art compute-optimally trained tokenizer-based models in flop-controlled settings, motivating the search for more sophisticated patching strategies.

SpaceByte, developed by Slagle, extended the static patching approach by segmenting on whitespace and other space-like bytes, which provide natural linguistic boundaries in many languages. This modification ensured that transformer steps were allocated to model each word, guaranteeing consistent patching of words across contexts and focusing computation on predictions that often follow spaces—which tend to be harder, given the broader uncertainty about what word will begin. SpaceByte also added a local encoder model, improving the representation of byte sequences within each patch. The approach demonstrated improvements over tokenized transformer models in compute-controlled settings on specific domains such as GitHub code and arXiv scientific documents at the 1 billion parameter scale. However, space patching cannot gracefully handle all languages and domains—whitespace is not a universal linguistic boundary—and critically, cannot vary the patch size dynamically based on content complexity. The BLT ablations show that while SpaceByte improves substantially over MegaByte, further architectural innovations are necessary to match current state-of-the-art token-based models such as Llama 3 at scale.

The BLT architecture synthesizes and extends these prior approaches in several key ways. First, the entropy-based dynamic patching mechanism transcends the fixed-vocabulary limitations of tokenization while providing the content-adaptive allocation that static and space patching lack. Second, the architectural innovations—particularly the cross-attention modules for encoding bytes to patches and decoding patches to bytes, combined with hash n-gram embeddings—provide more expressive byte-to-patch and patch-to-byte mappings than the concatenation approaches used in MegaByte. Third, the careful scaling experiments demonstrate for the first time that byte-level models can match the training flop-controlled performance of state-of-the-art tokenizer-based models at scales up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes. The experimental results demonstrate that both the architectural improvements and the dynamic patching mechanism are crucial: BLT with space patching but without n-gram embeddings and cross-attention underperforms substantially, while the full BLT architecture with entropy patching achieves parity with Llama 3.

# Current Limitations: Wall-Clock Time and Specific Scaling Laws

Despite the substantial advances demonstrated by the BLT architecture, several limitations constrain its practical deployment and point toward areas requiring further investigation. These limitations span methodological considerations regarding scaling laws, practical concerns about implementation efficiency, and open questions about architectural choices at extreme scales.

A fundamental limitation concerns the scaling laws used to determine optimal training configurations. The experiments presented in this work adopt the optimal ratio of model parameters to training data as determined for Llama 3, which was calculated specifically for BPE-level transformers. While the results demonstrate that this configuration yields competitive performance for BLT models, there is no guarantee that these scaling laws are optimal for the byte-level architecture. The dynamics of training on patches rather than tokens, the interplay between local encoder/decoder size and global transformer size, and the relationship between patch size and optimal training configuration may differ substantially from tokenization-based models. The computation of dedicated scaling laws specifically for BLT architectures represents an important direction for future work, potentially revealing more favorable scaling trends and optimal training configurations that are not apparent when borrowing hyperparameters from tokenizer-based models.

The experimental scale of many architectural decisions presents another limitation. Many of the architectural ablations and hyperparameter searches were conducted at scales up to 1 billion parameters, with the full architecture then applied at 8 billion scale. While this approach is computationally necessary, it is possible that optimal architectural choices change as model scale increases. The interaction between patch size, model size, and training data size may exhibit emergent properties at larger scales that are not predictable from smaller-scale experiments. The results hint at such effects: larger patch sizes appear to perform relatively better at larger model scales, with BLT using patch size 8 starting from a substantially worse position than Llama 2 at 1 billion parameters but ending up better at 7 billion scale. This suggests that architectural choices validated at one scale may require reconsideration as models grow, and that certain configurations may become more favorable at extreme scales.

From a practical deployment perspective, wall-clock time remains a concern despite favorable flop comparisons. Existing transformer libraries and codebases have been heavily optimized over years of development for tokenizer-based architectures, benefiting from specialized kernels, memory-efficient attention implementations, and hardware-aware optimizations. The BLT architecture introduces several components that deviate from standard transformer designs: the cross-attention modules with dynamic patch-dependent masks, the hash n-gram embedding lookup, and the interplay between local and global models. While the theoretical flop analysis demonstrates computational advantages, and implementations using technologies such as FlexAttention for handling custom attention masks help bridge the gap, the current implementations may not yet achieve wall-clock time parity with highly optimized tokenizer-based models. This represents an engineering challenge rather than a fundamental limitation—byte-level architectures have received far less optimization effort than their tokenization-based counterparts—but it constitutes a practical barrier to immediate deployment.

The separate training of the entropy model for patching introduces additional complexity to the training pipeline. While the entropy model is lightweight compared to the main BLT model, requiring preprocessing to compute next-byte entropies over the training data, this additional step complicates the training workflow and introduces a potential dependency on the quality of the entropy model. The relationship between entropy model quality and downstream BLT performance adds another hyperparameter dimension that must be considered during model development.

# Future Work: End-to-End Patch Learning and Optimized Implementation

The limitations and open questions raised by the BLT architecture outline several promising directions for future research, spanning methodological innovations, engineering optimizations, and novel training paradigms.

The calculation of scaling laws specifically for BLT architectures represents perhaps the most impactful methodological extension. Dedicated scaling law studies that systematically vary model size, training data, patch size, and the relative sizes of local and global transformer components could reveal optimal training configurations that are substantially different from those borrowed from tokenizer-based models. Given that BLT introduces new architectural degrees of freedom—patch size can be varied independently of model size, and the ratio of local to global model parameters provides another optimization axis—the scaling law formulation itself may require extension beyond the traditional parameter-count and data-volume variables. Such studies could demonstrate even more favorable scaling trends than those already observed, potentially revealing regimes where BLT architectures dominate tokenization-based approaches.

The development of end-to-end patch learning mechanisms would address the current dependency on separately trained entropy models. Currently, BLT uses a pretrained byte-level language model to estimate next-byte entropies, which are then thresholded to determine patch boundaries. While this approach is effective and computationally tractable—the entropy model can be encoded as a lookup table when its receptive field is sufficiently small—learning the patching decisions jointly with the language modeling objective could potentially yield more optimal patch boundaries. The challenge lies in the discrete nature of patch boundary decisions and the need for differentiable training. Approaches using reinforcement learning, Gumbel-Softmax relaxations, or auxiliary boundary prediction objectives could potentially enable end-to-end learning while maintaining the content-adaptive allocation that makes entropy-based patching effective.

Engineering optimizations for wall-clock efficiency represent an important practical direction. The development of specialized kernels for cross-attention with dynamic masks, memory-efficient implementations that minimize the overhead of the local encoder and decoder, and hardware-aware optimizations for the hash embedding lookups could substantially reduce the gap between theoretical flop savings and realized wall-clock improvements. As byte-level architectures receive more attention from the research community, the collective optimization effort that has benefited tokenization-based models over years of development could be accelerated for BLT-style architectures.

The exploration of larger model scales beyond 8 billion parameters represents a natural extension that could reveal emergent properties of the architecture. The scaling trends observed in the flop-controlled experiments suggest that larger patch sizes become more competitive at larger model scales, hinting that the advantages of dynamic compute allocation may become more pronounced as models grow. Training BLT models at scales matching current frontier models—tens or hundreds of billions of parameters—could demonstrate decisive advantages over tokenization-based approaches.

Perhaps most intriguingly, the "byte-ifying" approach demonstrated in the distillation experiments opens novel research directions for leveraging existing pretrained models. The experiments show that initializing the BLT global transformer with weights from a pretrained Llama 3.1 model, then fine-tuning with reduced learning rate for the global transformer while training the local encoder and decoder from scratch, yields models that substantially outperform both the original Llama 3.1 and a BLT trained from scratch with equivalent flops. This suggests a pathway for converting the massive investment in pretrained tokenizer-based models into tokenizer-free architectures, potentially capturing the robustness and efficiency benefits of byte-level modeling without requiring training from scratch. Further investigation of this approach could reveal methods that not only retain the benefits of byte-level modeling but push performance beyond what tokenizer-based models can achieve, using the pretrained models as initialization rather than as endpoints.
