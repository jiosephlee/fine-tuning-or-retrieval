\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### What is the fundamental difference between patches in BLT and tokens in traditional LLMs?
Question:
I'm reading the BLT paper and I'm confused about the distinction between "patches" and "tokens". The paper states that patches are "dynamically grouped sequences without a fixed vocabulary" whereas tokens come from a finite vocabulary determined before training.

But practically speaking, both seem to be groups of bytes that get fed into a transformer. What exactly makes them fundamentally different? Does the model actually process them differently, or is it just about how they're determined?
Answer:
The fundamental difference lies in **how the groupings are determined and what information is preserved**, not just how they're processed.

**Fixed vs. Dynamic Vocabulary**

Tokens come from a fixed vocabulary determined before training begins. A BPE tokenizer has a finite set of tokens (e.g., Llama 3's 128K vocabulary), and each token is a static mapping from a byte sequence to an index. Patches, by contrast, have no fixed vocabulary at all—they are "dynamically grouped sequences" where arbitrary byte groupings can be created on the fly based on the content.

**Access to Underlying Bytes**

This is perhaps the most critical distinction. As the paper states:

> "A critical difference between patches and tokens is that with tokens, the model has no direct access to the underlying byte features."

In tokenization-based models, once bytes are tokenized, the model only sees token IDs—it loses direct access to the character-level information. BLT's architecture explicitly preserves this: the local encoder and decoder models process raw bytes and use cross-attention to create patch representations, so the model maintains awareness of the byte-level composition throughout.

**Incrementality**

Patches satisfy what the paper calls the **incremental patching property**:

$$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}$$

This means the patching of a prefix is consistent regardless of what comes after. BPE tokenization does *not* satisfy this property—the same prefix can be tokenized differently depending on the continuation, which creates problems for autoregressive generation. Patches must be determinable from past context only.

**Dynamic Compute Allocation**

Tokens allocate uniform compute regardless of prediction difficulty. Patches in BLT are segmented based on next-byte entropy, allowing the model to "allocate more compute and model capacity where increased data complexity demands it." Predicting the end of "Mozart" is easier than predicting what follows "The composer of the Magic Flute was ___"—entropy patching naturally creates more patches (more transformer steps) for the latter.

So while both are groups of bytes fed into a transformer, patches fundamentally decouple the model from a pre-determined vocabulary while maintaining byte-level grounding and enabling dynamic compute allocation.
