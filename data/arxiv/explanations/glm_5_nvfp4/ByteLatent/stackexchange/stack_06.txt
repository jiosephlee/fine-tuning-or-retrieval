\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### What is the purpose of hash n-gram embeddings in BLT?
Question:
The BLT paper introduces hash n-gram embeddings that map byte n-grams to embeddings via a hash function:

$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...,8}E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$

I understand this incorporates information about preceding bytes, but why use a hash function instead of just learning embeddings directly? Why use n-grams from size 3 to 8 specifically? What would happen if we removed this component?
Answer:
The hash $n$-gram embeddings serve a crucial purpose in BLT: they provide the local encoder with information about byte sequences, not just individual bytes, while keeping memory requirements bounded.

## Why hashing instead of direct embeddings?

The core issue is the combinatorial explosion of possible $n$-grams. With a 256-byte vocabulary, the number of possible $n$-grams grows exponentially:

- 3-grams: $256^3 \approx 16.8$ million combinations
- 8-grams: $256^8 \approx 2.8 \times 10^{19}$ combinations

Learning direct embeddings for all possible $n$-grams is computationally infeasible. Hashing provides an elegant solution by mapping $n$-grams to a fixed-size embedding table via $\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_{n}^{hash}|$. This allows the model to:

1. Maintain a bounded memory footprint regardless of vocabulary size
2. Handle arbitrarily rare or unseen $n$-grams naturally (no OOV issues)
3. Share statistical strength across $n$-grams that hash to the same bucket

## Why $n$-grams from size 3 to 8?

This range captures meaningful subword patterns. Smaller $n$-grams (1-grams, 2-grams) provide limited contextual information, while larger $n$-grams would add computational overhead with diminishing returns. The paper notes that hash embeddings are particularly beneficial for domains like Wikipedia and GitHub code, where capturing character-level patterns matters.

## What happens if removed?

The ablation study in Table 6 shows that removing or reducing hash embeddings significantly degrades performance. The paper states: "hashes are vital to bringing the performance of BLT to match those of tokenizer based models." Without them, BLT would fail to match tokenization-based baselines, particularly on tasks requiring character-level understanding. The gains from hash embeddings are complementary to cross-attention, improving performance on different datasets.

In essence, hash $n$-gram embeddings give BLT the benefits of subword awareness that tokenizers provide naturally, but without the fixed vocabulary constraints.
