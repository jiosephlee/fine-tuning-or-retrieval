\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why is there a trade-off between MMLU and chatbot benchmark performance?
Question:
The QLoRA paper shows an interesting finding: finetuning on FLAN v2 performs well on MMLU but poorly on Vicuna chatbot benchmark, while finetuning on OASST1 does the opposite. The paper concludes that "strong MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance and vice versa."

I understand that different datasets optimize for different things, but I'm confused about why there's an *inverse* relationship. If FLAN v2 is similar to MMLU (both test "knowledge"), wouldn't a model trained on FLAN v2 still be decent at chat, since it presumably learned good representations? 

Is the trade-off because:
1. The model overfits to one task type and loses generalization?
2. Chat and knowledge tasks require fundamentally different behavioral patterns?
3. Something about catastrophic forgetting during finetuning?

The paper calls this "partial orthogonality" but I don't fully grasp what that means mechanistically.
Answer:
The trade-off you've identified between MMLU and chatbot benchmark performance stems from a fundamental insight in the paper: **benchmark performance depends heavily on how similar your finetuning data is to the evaluation benchmark**.

The paper explicitly addresses this in the limitations section: "From the evidence presented, it appears that the performance of these benchmarks likely depend how similar the finetuning data is to the benchmark dataset. For example, FLAN v2 is similar to MMLU, but dissimilar to chatbot benchmarks and vice versa for the Chip2 dataset."

Let me break down what's happening:

**Dataset-Benchmark Alignment.** FLAN v2 is a large corpus of academic-style instructional tasks—precisely the kind of multi-task knowledge and reasoning that MMLU tests with its 57 subjects spanning mathematics, history, computer science, and law. When you finetune on FLAN v2, you're optimizing for the distribution of tasks that look like MMLU. Conversely, OASST1 consists of crowd-sourced multilingual multiturn conversations, which aligns naturally with what chatbot benchmarks (Vicuna, OA) actually measure: conversational fluency, instruction-following in dialogue contexts, and multi-turn coherence.

**What "Partial Orthogonality" Means Mechanistically.** When the paper describes these benchmarks as "partially orthogonal," they mean the evaluation axes measure somewhat independent capabilities. Strong MMLU performance requires factual knowledge recall and structured reasoning in question-answering formats. Strong chatbot performance requires conversational dynamics, handling user intent across turns, and generating contextually appropriate responses. These aren't opposing skills, but they're also not strongly correlated—the training signal from one doesn't automatically transfer to the other.

**Why the Inverse Relationship?** This isn't about catastrophic forgetting or overfitting in the traditional sense. Rather, with limited finetuning data and compute, you're effectively making an implicit choice about which capability distribution to optimize for. A model finetuned on FLAN v2 learns better calibrated responses for knowledge-intensive queries but may not develop the conversational "style" that chatbot benchmarks reward. Similarly, OASST1-trained models learn good dialogue behavior but may lack breadth on academic knowledge tasks.

The paper's finding that "dataset suitability matters more than dataset size" reinforces this interpretation—a 9k sample dataset (OASST1) outperformed a 450k sample dataset (FLAN v2) on chatbot metrics precisely because of better task alignment, not data volume.

This suggests the community should be more explicit about what capabilities we're optimizing for: "Do we want to create models that do well on classroom highschool and colleague knowledge or do we want to do well on chatbot conversation ability?" The benchmarks we choose shape the models we build.
