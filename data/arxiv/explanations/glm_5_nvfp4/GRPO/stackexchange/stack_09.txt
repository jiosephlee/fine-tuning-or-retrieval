\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Understanding the data decontamination approach
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper describes their decontamination approach for removing benchmark data from training:

'text segment containing a 10-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed.'

For shorter benchmark texts (less than 10 grams but at least 3 grams), they use exact matching.

I have a few questions:
1. Why specifically 10-grams? Is there a principled reason for this threshold vs say 8-grams or 12-grams?
2. Does this approach risk removing too much legitimate math content that happens to contain similar sequences?
3. Is n-gram matching sufficient? Could there be paraphrased versions of benchmark problems that slip through?

It seems like a fairly simple heuristic - is this the standard approach for decontamination in LLM training?
Answer:
The decontamination approach described in the DeepSeekMath paper follows a fairly standard $n$-gram matching heuristic that has become common practice in LLM training. Let me address each of your questions:

**Why 10-grams specifically?**

The paper states they "follow [DeepSeek-Coder]" for this approach, indicating this is inherited from prior work rather than a novel design choice. While the paper doesn't provide a principled justification for the specific threshold of 10, the intuition is straightforward: longer $n$-grams are far less likely to occur by coincidence. A 10-gram match in mathematical text almost certainly indicates actual benchmark contamination rather than accidental overlap of common mathematical phrases. The choice represents a practical trade-off—shorter $n$-grams (like 4-grams) would catch more contamination but also generate many false positives from standard mathematical terminology, while longer $n$-grams might miss genuine contamination.

**Risk of removing legitimate content?**

This is a valid concern that the paper doesn't explicitly address. In practice, the risk is mitigated by the threshold choice—10-gram exact matches in mathematical prose are rare unless there's actual copying. Common mathematical expressions like "solve for $x$" or "therefore the answer is" won't trigger removal because they don't reach 10 consecutive words. That said, there's likely some minor collateral removal of legitimately useful content, which is an inherent trade-off in any decontamination strategy.

**Are paraphrased versions missed?**

Absolutely—this is a fundamental limitation of $n$-gram matching. A benchmark problem that has been reworded, had its numbers changed, or been translated will slip through undetected. The paper doesn't discuss this limitation, but it's important to understand that this approach catches only direct contamination, not semantic duplicates. More sophisticated approaches like embedding-based semantic similarity could catch paraphrases, but they introduce their own challenges: higher computational cost, potential false positives, and the risk of over-filtering mathematically similar but legitimately distinct problems.

**Is this the standard approach?**

Yes, $n$-gram based decontamination has become a de facto standard in the field. It's used precisely because it offers a reasonable balance—it's computationally efficient, deterministic, and catches the most egregious cases of contamination without requiring semantic understanding. The DeepSeekMath authors' approach of handling short benchmark texts (those under 10 grams but at least 3 grams) with exact matching is also sensible, ensuring complete coverage of benchmark content regardless of length.
