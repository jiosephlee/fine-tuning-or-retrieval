\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve Maj@K but not Pass@K?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper shows a fascinating result: reinforcement learning improves Maj@K (majority voting accuracy) but not Pass@K accuracy. They conclude that 'the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.'

I find this confusing. If RL doesn't improve the model's fundamental ability to solve problems (Pass@K), how does it make majority voting more effective (Maj@K)?

Does this mean RL is just 're-ranking' the outputs somehow? Or is it changing the distribution of correct answers to be more consistent? And what does 'robust output distribution' mean in this context?

If the model's actual problem-solving capability doesn't improve, what exactly IS improving?
Answer:
The key insight is that RL improves **consistency** of correct outputs rather than expanding the model's **capability frontier**.

Let me clarify what these metrics measure:

- **`$\text{Pass@}K$`**: Whether *at least one* of `$K$` sampled outputs is correct. This measures the model's fundamental problem-solving reach—if the model knows how to solve a problem at all, `$\text{Pass@}K$` captures that.
- **`$\text{Maj@}K$`**: Whether the *majority vote* across `$K$` samples yields the correct answer. This measures whether correct answers dominate the output distribution.

The paper's finding that RL improves `$\text{Maj@}K$` but not `$\text{Pass@}K$` tells us something subtle about what RL is doing. Consider a model that, for a given problem, produces outputs with the following distribution:

**Before RL**: `$40\%$` correct answer, `$30\%$` wrong answer A, `$30\%$` wrong answer B  
**After RL**: `$60\%$` correct answer, `$20\%$` wrong answer A, `$20\%$` wrong answer B

The `$\text{Pass@}K$` might remain unchanged because the model already "knew" the correct answer—there was always some probability of sampling it. However, `$\text{Maj@}K$` improves substantially because the correct answer now dominates the vote.

The authors describe this as RL making the output distribution "more robust." What does this mean? The model already possessed the correct reasoning patterns from SFT, but they weren't reliably favored. RL doesn't teach new reasoning; it amplifies and stabilizes existing correct reasoning while suppressing incorrect paths.

This relates to what the paper calls the "misalignment problem"—the SFT model has latent reasoning capabilities that aren't well-aligned with consistently producing correct outputs. RL addresses this alignment gap by boosting the probability of correct responses relative to incorrect ones.

The practical implication is important: if you want to improve `$\text{Maj@}K$` (single-query performance through voting), RL helps. But to improve `$\text{Pass@}K$` (the model's ability to solve problems it couldn't solve before), you'd need approaches that expand capability—such as the advanced sampling strategies or better reward model generalization the authors discuss in Section 5.2.3.

In essence, RL is doing something closer to "re-ranking" the model's own existing knowledge—making correct reasoning patterns more salient—rather than teaching fundamentally new problem-solving skills.
