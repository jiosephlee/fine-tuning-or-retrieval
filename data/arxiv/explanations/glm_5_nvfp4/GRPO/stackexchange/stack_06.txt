\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Understanding the gradient coefficient in the unified RL paradigm
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper presents a unified paradigm where the gradient can be written as:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{{\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \pi_{{rf}}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right)$$

They mention that different methods (SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO) differ in their 'Gradient Coefficient' $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$. 

I'm struggling to understand:
1. What specifically is the gradient coefficient for each method?
2. The paper says GRPO 'adjusts its gradient coefficient based on the reward value' while Online RFT 'uniformly reinforces all responses with correct answers.' What does this mean concretely?
3. Why does differential reinforcement/penalization matter?
Answer:
The unified paradigm in the DeepSeekMath paper provides a elegant framework for understanding how different training methods relate to each other. Let me address your questions systematically.

## The Gradient Coefficient for Each Method

The paper defines the gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}$ as what determines "the magnitude of the penalty or reinforcement for the data." Here's what each method uses:

**SFT**: The gradient coefficient is simply $GC_{SFT} = 1$ for all tokens in the training data. This is standard maximum likelihood training on human-selected demonstrations.

**RFT and Online RFT**: These methods use a binary gradient coefficient based on answer correctness. As stated in the paper, Online RFT "does not penalize incorrect responses and uniformly reinforces all responses with correct answers at the same level of intensity." The coefficient is effectively a constant positive value for correct responses and zero for incorrect ones (no negative signal).

**GRPO**: The gradient coefficient is the normalized advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ derived from group-relative rewards. For outcome supervision, $GC_{GRPO} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$, where $\mathbf{r}$ is the vector of rewards for all $G$ sampled outputs for a given question.

**PPO**: The gradient coefficient is the advantage function $A_t$ computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), based on rewards and a learned value function.

## What "Adjusting Based on Reward Value" vs "Uniform Reinforcement" Means

This distinction is crucial and represents a key insight of the paper. 

**Online RFT (uniform reinforcement)**: If you sample 4 responses where 2 are correct and 2 are incorrect, RFT would give equal positive gradient signal to both correct responses regardless of *how* correct they are, and zero signal to incorrect ones. It's binary: reinforce correct, ignore incorrect.

**GRPO (differential reinforcement)**: Using the same 4 responses, GRPO normalizes all rewards relative to the group. A response with a higher reward (e.g., more elegant solution) receives a stronger positive gradient signal. Critically, responses below the group mean receive *negative* gradient coefficients—they are actively penalized, not merely ignored. This creates a relative ranking effect within each group of samples.

## Why Differential Reinforcement Matters

The paper provides empirical evidence: "GRPO surpasses online RFT, thereby highlighting the efficiency of altering positive and negative gradient coefficients."

The intuition is that differential reinforcement provides richer learning signals. When a reward model can distinguish between a marginally correct solution and an excellent one, GRPO exploits this information. Furthermore, active penalization of low-quality responses (rather than mere ignorance) helps the model learn to avoid suboptimal reasoning patterns.

The paper also notes that "GRPO+PS shows superior performance compared to GRPO+OS, indicating the benefits of using fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients." This reinforces the principle: more granular, differentiated gradient coefficients lead to better learning outcomes in complex reasoning tasks.
