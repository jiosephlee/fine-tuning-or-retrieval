\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why sample 64 outputs per question in GRPO?
Question:
In the DeepSeekMath GRPO implementation, the paper mentions sampling $G = 64$ outputs per question for computing group-relative advantages.

I'm curious about this design choice:

1. Why 64 specifically? Is there a trade-off between:
   - Having enough samples for a reliable baseline estimate
   - Computational cost of sampling and scoring outputs

2. With 64 samples, the paper mentions computing normalized rewards: $\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$. Is this just standard normalization, or is there something special about how the variance reduction works here?

3. Would fewer samples (like 8 or 16) work reasonably well, or does the algorithm degrade significantly with smaller group sizes?
Answer:
The choice of $G = 64$ samples per question in GRPO isn't explicitly justified with ablation studies in the paper, but we can infer the reasoning from both the algorithm's design and practical considerations.

**Why 64?**

The paper notes that self-consistency over 64 samples achieves 60.9\% on MATH (up from 51.7\% for single samples), which suggests 64 is a meaningful number for capturing the diversity of reasoning paths in mathematical problems. This alignment between training and evaluation isn't coincidental—using the same group size helps ensure the training signal is calibrated to what you'd expect at inference time.

From a statistical perspective, 64 samples provide a reasonably stable estimate of the group mean and standard deviation. The advantage calculation:
$$\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$$

is standard z-score normalization, but what makes it special in GRPO is that it provides **group-relative advantages** rather than absolute ones. Since reward models are typically trained on comparison data (ranking outputs for the same question), this relative scoring naturally aligns with how the reward model "thinks" about quality.

**The variance reduction intuition:**

In PPO, the value function $V_\psi$ serves as a baseline to reduce variance in advantage estimates. GRPO replaces this learned baseline with the empirical group mean. With more samples, your baseline estimate becomes more accurate, reducing variance in your gradient estimates. However, this comes at computational cost—you're essentially running $G$ forward passes per question.

**Would fewer samples work?**

The paper doesn't ablate this directly, but the algorithm should theoretically work with smaller groups. The trade-off is:

- **Fewer samples (8-16)**: Higher variance in baseline estimates, potentially noisier gradients, but significantly cheaper computation
- **More samples**: Better variance reduction, but diminishing returns and quadratic-like scaling in compute

The key insight from the paper's unified paradigm (Section 5.2) is that GRPO's effectiveness comes from its gradient coefficient design—using the actual reward values to differentially reinforce/penalize outputs—rather than purely from the group size. The group-relative baseline enables this by providing meaningful normalization, but even smaller groups would preserve the core mechanism, just with potentially less stable training.
