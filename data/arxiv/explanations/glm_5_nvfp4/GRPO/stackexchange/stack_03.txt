\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why doesn't training on arXiv improve mathematical reasoning?
Question:
The DeepSeekMath paper claims that 'arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning,' which seems counterintuitive to me. After all, arXiv contains a wealth of mathematical content, theorems, and proofs.

The paper tested this with MathPile (85%+ arXiv content) and ArXiv-RedPajama corpora but found no notable improvements on benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, or MMLU-STEM.

What could explain this counterintuitive result? Is it:
- The format of the data (LaTeX)?
- The mismatch between proof writing and problem solving?
- The lack of educational/scaffolded content?

The paper mentions this conclusion has limitations, but I'm curious what the current understanding is about why arXiv doesn't seem to help with mathematical reasoning tasks.
Answer:
The DeepSeekMath paper presents a surprising empirical finding: despite arXiv's rich mathematical content, training on arXiv papers yields no notable improvements on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. While the paper doesn't provide a definitive explanation, several factors likely contribute to this counterintuitive result.

**The Nature of arXiv Content vs. Benchmark Tasks**

The paper tested on benchmarks like GSM8K (grade-school math), MATH (high school competition level), and MMLU-STEM. These require step-by-step problem-solving and computational reasoning. In contrast, arXiv papers primarily contain theorem statements, proofs, and advanced mathematical discourse. There's a fundamental mismatch between *reading and writing proofs* (arXiv's strength) and *solving computational problems* (what the benchmarks test). The paper notes this conclusion has limitations—they didn't test on tasks like theorem proving or informalization, where arXiv might prove more valuable.

**Format and Representation Issues**

ArXiv papers are predominantly LaTeX source files. The paper experimented with both MathPile (cleaned arXiv data) and ArXiv-RedPajama (with preambles, comments, and macros removed). Even with cleaning, the dense symbolic notation and formal proof structures differ substantially from the natural language problem-solving format required by benchmarks. Models trained on arXiv may learn to parse LaTeX and understand theorem statements, but this doesn't necessarily translate to the multi-step arithmetic and algebraic reasoning needed for GSM8K or MATH.

**Contrast with Successful Data Sources**

The paper's key finding is that carefully filtered Common Crawl web pages (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) significantly outperformed arXiv. Web pages with mathematical content likely include educational materials, tutorials, forum discussions (e.g., Math StackExchange), and worked examples—content that's more pedagogically aligned with problem-solving. This suggests that *explanatory, scaffolded content* is more valuable for developing reasoning capabilities than raw research output.

**Scale and Combination Effects**

The paper acknowledges they didn't test whether arXiv helps at larger model scales or when combined with other data types. It's possible arXiv provides latent benefits—developing deeper mathematical understanding—that don't manifest on the specific benchmarks tested, or that require larger model capacity to utilize effectively.

In summary, the mismatch between the *type* of mathematical reasoning in arXiv papers (theorem-proving, formal proofs) and what benchmarks like GSM8K/MATH measure (computational problem-solving), combined with format differences and the lack of pedagogical structure, likely explains why arXiv training didn't help. The finding underscores that not all mathematical data is equally valuable—it matters *what kind* of mathematical content models are trained on.
