\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 8: # Analysis: Why Reinforcement Learning Works

The effectiveness of reinforcement learning (RL) in improving mathematical reasoning capabilities of language models presents a fascinating phenomenon that warrants careful analysis. While the empirical results demonstrate clear performance improvements—DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieving 88.2% on GSM8K and 51.7% on MATH, surpassing its instruction-tuned predecessor—understanding the underlying mechanisms reveals important insights about what RL actually improves and where its limitations lie. This chapter examines the nature of RL's effectiveness, distinguishing between genuine capability enhancement and output distribution refinement, and explores promising directions for future research.

# Pass@K vs. Maj@K Analysis: What RL Actually Improves

To understand what reinforcement learning fundamentally changes in a language model's behavior, researchers employ two complementary evaluation metrics: Pass@K and Maj@K. These metrics capture different aspects of model performance and, when analyzed together, reveal the true nature of RL's contribution to mathematical reasoning.

Pass@K measures whether at least one correct answer exists among the top K sampled outputs from the model. This metric evaluates the model's fundamental capability—its ability to generate correct solutions when given multiple attempts. A model with high Pass@K possesses strong underlying problem-solving abilities, as the correct solution exists within its distribution of possible outputs. If Pass@K improves after RL training, this indicates that the model has genuinely learned new reasoning patterns or strengthened existing ones.

Maj@K, or Majority@K, takes a different approach: it samples K outputs and selects the most frequent answer (majority vote) as the final prediction. This metric measures the robustness and consistency of the model's output distribution. A model with high Maj@K produces consistent correct answers across multiple samples, suggesting that the correct answer is the dominant mode in its output distribution. Improvement in Maj@K indicates that the model has learned to make its correct outputs more likely relative to incorrect alternatives.

The empirical findings from the DeepSeekMath research reveal a striking pattern: reinforcement learning enhances Maj@K performance but does not significantly improve Pass@K accuracy. This observation carries profound implications for understanding what RL accomplishes. The improvement in Maj@K without corresponding gains in Pass@K indicates that RL does not primarily enhance the model's fundamental problem-solving capabilities. Instead, RL operates by reorganizing the output distribution—boosting the probability of correct responses that already existed within the model's repertoire while suppressing incorrect alternatives.

Consider what this means in practical terms. Before RL training, the instruction-tuned model already possessed the capability to generate correct solutions; these correct answers existed somewhere in its output distribution. However, the model might have assigned similar or greater probability mass to incorrect answers, leading to inconsistency when sampling. After RL training, the correct answer becomes more concentrated in the distribution—when sampling multiple times, the correct answer appears more frequently and thus wins majority votes more reliably.

This distinction between capability enhancement and distribution sharpening parallels concepts in other domains. Consider a student who knows the correct answer but lacks confidence, sometimes giving correct and sometimes incorrect responses to the same question. Drilling and practice (analogous to RL) doesn't teach new material but rather reinforces the correct response until it becomes the dominant answer. Similarly, RL fine-tuning appears to reinforce correct reasoning pathways that the model already possessed, making them more accessible and consistent.

# The Misalignment Problem in SFT Models

The observation that RL primarily improves output distribution robustness rather than fundamental capabilities points to an underlying issue in supervised fine-tuning (SFT): the misalignment problem. Understanding this problem provides essential context for why RL proves effective as a post-SFT refinement technique.

In supervised fine-tuning, a pre-trained model is trained on human-curated examples of question-answer pairs, typically incorporating chain-of-thought reasoning that demonstrates step-by-step problem solving. This process teaches the model to generate responses that mimic the format and content of the training examples. However, SFT does not explicitly train the model to prefer correct answers over incorrect ones when both are syntactically plausible. The model learns to produce reasoning steps that look mathematically sound, but it does not necessarily learn to assign higher probability to genuinely correct reasoning paths.

This creates a misalignment between what the model has learned to produce and what constitutes a correct solution. The model may generate reasoning that follows learned patterns—writing equations, performing calculations, and reaching conclusions—but the final answer might still be incorrect due to various errors accumulated throughout the reasoning chain. Importantly, incorrect answers can arise from many different reasoning errors, creating multiple modes of failure in the output distribution.

The misalignment problem manifests in the output distribution of SFT models. After training on mathematical instruction data, the model's distribution contains both correct and incorrect answers. The correct answer might be one of several high-probability outputs, competing with various incorrect alternatives that also appear plausible given the training data patterns. This competition between correct and incorrect outputs explains why SFT models can achieve reasonable performance with few-shot prompting but exhibit inconsistency when sampled multiple times.

Research has identified that the reasoning performance of SFT models can be improved through preference alignment strategies. These strategies explicitly teach the model to prefer certain outputs over others, addressing the misalignment between surface-level correctness and genuine accuracy. Reinforcement learning, along with other methods like Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and their variants, can be understood as different approaches to this alignment problem.

The misalignment problem also explains why RL training on a subset of instruction tuning data can improve performance across benchmarks not seen during RL. The RL process doesn't teach new mathematical concepts or reasoning patterns; rather, it refines the model's preference toward correct reasoning pathways that transfer across different problem types. The fundamental reasoning capabilities come from pre-training and SFT, while RL realigns the output distribution to make correct answers more dominant.

# Understanding Output Distribution Robustness

The concept of output distribution robustness captures how reinforcement learning improves mathematical reasoning performance. Robustness, in this context, refers to the stability and consistency of correct outputs when sampling from the model multiple times. A robust output distribution concentrates probability mass on correct answers, making them the dominant responses under various sampling conditions.

To formalize this intuition, consider a model faced with a mathematical problem. Before RL training, the model's conditional probability distribution over possible next tokens—given the problem and any partial solution—might assign significant probability mass to several paths, only some of which lead to correct answers. The model might have learned various reasoning patterns from training data, some correct and some leading to errors. When sampling, the model might follow any of these learned patterns, resulting in inconsistent performance.

Reinforcement learning modifies this distribution through the policy optimization process. Using reward signals that indicate correctness (either from outcome-based rewards or process-based rewards), RL increases the probability of tokens and reasoning paths that lead to correct answers while decreasing the probability of paths leading to incorrect answers. The GRPO algorithm achieves this through its advantage estimation mechanism: outputs with positive normalized advantages receive reinforcement (their probabilities increase), while outputs with negative advantages receive penalties (their probabilities decrease).

The normalization process in GRPO is particularly important for understanding distribution robustness. By computing advantages relative to the group average—subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation—the algorithm compares each output against others sampled for the same question. This relative comparison means that even among correct answers, some might receive higher advantages than others if they have higher raw reward scores. Similarly, incorrect answers are not uniformly penalized; their penalties scale with how much worse they are compared to the group average.

This relative weighting mechanism produces a sharpening effect on the output distribution. Correct answers that are clearly superior (perhaps having cleaner reasoning or being easier to verify) receive stronger reinforcement. Incorrect answers that are clearly wrong receive stronger penalties. Over many training iterations, this process concentrates probability mass on the most robust correct reasoning paths while dispersing mass away from incorrect alternatives.

The empirical observation that RL improves Maj@K performance validates this mechanism. When sampling K outputs, majority voting works correctly when the dominant mode in the distribution corresponds to the correct answer. RL training pushes the distribution toward this configuration. The Pass@K result—showing that RL doesn't significantly improve the existence of correct answers within K samples—confirms that the model already possessed correct reasoning paths; RL merely made them more accessible and dominant.

# Limitations of Current RL Approaches

While reinforcement learning demonstrates effectiveness in improving mathematical reasoning, careful analysis reveals several important limitations that constrain its potential. Understanding these limitations is crucial for setting realistic expectations and guiding future research directions.

The first limitation concerns the scope of improvement. Since RL primarily redistributes probability mass rather than generating new capabilities, the upper bound of RL improvement is determined by what already exists in the model's distribution. If a problem type is genuinely outside the model's capability—meaning no correct answer exists among possible outputs—RL cannot help. This explains why RL's effectiveness diminishes for problems that fundamentally exceed the model's training, such as geometry problems involving triangles and ellipses, which DeepSeekMath struggles with due to potential data selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning.

A second limitation stems from the data source for RL training. In the DeepSeekMath experiments, RL uses only questions from the instruction tuning stage with naive nucleus sampling to generate outputs. This restricted data source limits the diversity of exploration. The model reinforces its performance on familiar problem types but may not discover new reasoning strategies or generalize to substantially different problems. The finding that RL improves Maj@K but not Pass@K suggests this limitation—the model becomes more consistent on known problem types but doesn't expand its capability frontier.

The reliability of reward signals presents a third significant limitation. Current RL algorithms fully trust the reward signal to determine reinforcement and penalties. However, reward signals, whether from rule-based correctness checks or learned reward models, can be noisy and unreliable. The PRM800K dataset, which provides process supervision annotations created by well-trained human annotators, still contains approximately 20% incorrect annotations. When the reward signal itself is wrong, RL may reinforce incorrect behaviors or penalize correct ones, leading to suboptimal or even harmful training outcomes.

The limitation becomes more severe for complex mathematical tasks where determining correctness is itself challenging. For competition-level problems, even verifying whether a solution is correct can require significant expertise. Reward models trained on limited data may generalize poorly to out-of-distribution problems or reasoning paths not well-represented in their training data. This creates a feedback loop where unreliable rewards produce unreliable policy updates, potentially leading to reward hacking—where the model learns to maximize reward signals without genuinely improving its reasoning.

Another limitation concerns process supervision versus outcome supervision. While process supervision—providing rewards at each reasoning step—offers more fine-grained guidance, it requires accurate step-level annotations that are expensive to obtain and may still contain errors. The quality of process reward models fundamentally constrains the effectiveness of process-supervised RL. Current approaches to building process reward models may not capture all the nuances of valid mathematical reasoning, particularly for creative or non-standard solution paths.

# Future Directions for Data Source: Tree-Search and Advanced Sampling

The limitations of current RL approaches point toward promising research directions. One particularly fruitful avenue involves improving the data source—the questions and sampled outputs used for RL training. Currently, most approaches use naive sampling from instruction tuning data, but advanced sampling strategies could significantly enhance RL effectiveness.

Tree-search methods represent a compelling direction for future exploration. Rather than sampling independent outputs from the model, tree-search explores the space of possible reasoning paths systematically, building a search tree where nodes represent partial solutions and edges represent reasoning steps. Methods inspired by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) or other tree-search algorithms could identify promising reasoning paths more efficiently than random sampling. By exploring multiple branches and backtracking from dead ends, tree-search could find correct solutions that might be missed by independent sampling, effectively improving the Pass@K frontier that current RL approaches don't address.

The integration of tree-search with RL creates synergistic possibilities. Tree-search can generate higher-quality training data by focusing computational resources on promising reasoning paths. The discovered paths can then be used for RL training, where the model learns to prefer the reasoning strategies that tree-search identified as successful. This combination could address the limitation that RL only reshapes existing distributions; by introducing tree-search discovery, the training data itself could contain novel correct solutions that expand the model's capability.

Advanced decoding strategies beyond tree-search also warrant investigation. Techniques like best-of-n sampling, where multiple candidates are generated and the best is selected according to a verifier, could improve the quality of outputs used for RL training. Speculative decoding and other efficient inference techniques could reduce the computational cost of exploration, enabling broader search across the output space. The efficiency of inference directly impacts exploration effectiveness—faster generation allows more samples, more diverse exploration, and better coverage of the solution space.

Out-of-distribution question prompts represent another dimension for data source improvement. Current RL training often uses only questions from the instruction tuning stage, which may not adequately represent the diversity of mathematical problems encountered in deployment. Training on a broader distribution of questions could improve generalization and reduce overfitting to specific problem formats. Combining diverse question sources with advanced sampling strategies could create richer training data that pushes beyond the current limitations of RL.

# Future Directions for Algorithms: Robustness to Noisy Rewards

Current reinforcement learning algorithms assume that reward signals reliably indicate correctness. This assumption does not hold in practice—reward models make errors, human annotations contain mistakes, and rule-based verification may not capture all nuances of mathematical reasoning. Developing algorithms robust to noisy rewards represents a crucial direction for improving RL effectiveness.

The challenge of noisy rewards manifests in several ways. A reward model might incorrectly assign high rewards to outputs that appear correct but contain subtle errors, leading the policy model to reinforce flawed reasoning patterns. Conversely, genuinely correct but unconventional solutions might receive low rewards if they differ from patterns in the reward model's training data. These errors compound over training, potentially leading to convergence to suboptimal policies that maximize unreliable reward signals rather than genuine correctness.

Weak-to-strong alignment methods offer a promising approach to this challenge. The key insight is that imperfect reward signals can still provide useful guidance if the learning algorithm appropriately handles uncertainty. Rather than fully trusting every reward signal, robust algorithms could weight updates by the reliability of the signal—reinforcing more strongly when the reward signal is confident and scaling back when uncertainty is high. This approach mirrors how humans learn from potentially unreliable feedback, maintaining skepticism while still benefiting from available guidance.

Another direction involves developing algorithms that explicitly model reward uncertainty. Rather than treating rewards as fixed values, algorithms could maintain distributions over possible true rewards, reflecting uncertainty about the signal's reliability. Policy updates would then account for this uncertainty, being more conservative when reward signals are uncertain and more aggressive when signals are confident and consistent. This probabilistic treatment of rewards could prevent overfitting to noisy signals while still capturing useful signal.

The relationship between gradient coefficients and reward reliability deserves further investigation. In the unified paradigm for understanding different training methods, the gradient coefficient determines how strongly each token's probability is adjusted based on the reward signal. Current methods compute this coefficient directly from the reward, but future methods could modulate the coefficient based on estimated reliability. Tokens in outputs with high-reliability rewards would receive stronger updates, while tokens in outputs with uncertain rewards would receive weaker updates, reducing the impact of potentially incorrect signals.

Ensemble methods offer another potential approach. Rather than relying on a single reward model, training could use multiple independent reward models and aggregate their predictions. Disagreement among models could signal uncertainty, while agreement could indicate reliability. This ensemble approach could provide more robust reward estimates and explicit uncertainty quantification, enabling more sophisticated handling of noisy rewards.

# Future Directions for Reward Functions: Generalization and Uncertainty

Reward functions serve as the foundation for RL training signals, and their quality fundamentally constrains what RL can achieve. Three interconnected directions for improving reward functions emerge from analyzing current limitations: enhancing generalization, reflecting uncertainty, and building better process reward models.

Generalization ability is crucial because reward models must evaluate outputs that differ from their training data. During RL training, the policy model evolves, producing outputs that shift away from the initial distribution. The reward model must generalize to these shifted outputs, accurately assessing their correctness even when they differ from patterns seen during reward model training. Without sufficient generalization, RL may merely stabilize the output distribution around patterns the reward model recognizes, rather than improving fundamental reasoning capabilities that transfer to new problems.

The challenge of generalization is particularly acute for mathematical reasoning. Correct mathematical solutions can follow vastly different paths—using different solution methods, notation conventions, or problem decompositions. A reward model trained on limited solution diversity might fail to recognize valid alternative approaches, penalizing correct solutions that don't match expected patterns. Future work on reward functions must explicitly address this diversity, ensuring that reward models recognize correctness across multiple valid reasoning approaches.

Reflecting uncertainty represents a second critical direction. Current reward models typically output point estimates of quality, providing no indication of confidence. Yet the reliability of rewards varies significantly across different inputs and outputs. A reward model might be highly confident in assessing standard solutions to familiar problem types but uncertain about novel approaches or unfamiliar problem domains. Providing uncertainty estimates alongside reward predictions would enable downstream learning algorithms to appropriately weight the signal.

Uncertainty quantification could serve as a bridge between weak reward models and robust learning algorithms. When a reward model indicates high uncertainty, the learning algorithm could reduce reliance on that signal, preventing potentially harmful updates. When uncertainty is low, the algorithm could confidently update the policy. This selective use of reward signals, guided by uncertainty, could significantly improve the sample efficiency and final performance of RL training.

Building high-quality process reward models constitutes the third direction. Process reward models evaluate reasoning step-by-step, providing fine-grained signals that guide policy updates more precisely than outcome-only rewards. However, creating process reward models is challenging—annotating the correctness of individual reasoning steps requires substantial expertise, and disagreement among annotators can be high for nuanced reasoning. Developing methods to efficiently build process reward models, potentially using automated verification, synthetic data, or active learning strategies, could unlock more effective process-supervised RL.

The interplay between these three directions suggests an integrated approach to reward function development. Future reward models might combine strong generalization through diverse training data, explicit uncertainty quantification through calibrated predictions, and fine-grained process evaluation through efficient annotation methods. Such comprehensive reward functions would provide more reliable and informative signals, enabling RL approaches to push beyond current limitations and potentially improve fundamental reasoning capabilities rather than merely output distribution robustness.
