\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: # Math Pre-Training: DeepSeekMath-Base

# Model Architecture and Initialization from DeepSeek-Coder

The foundation of DeepSeekMath-Base lies in a strategic decision regarding model initialization. Rather than beginning mathematical pre-training from a general-purpose language model, the researchers made the deliberate choice to initialize from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a code-pre-trained model. This decision was motivated by empirical observations that starting from a code-trained model yields superior results compared to initializing from a general large language model. The model architecture itself comprises 7 billion parameters, placing it in the category of moderately sized language models that remain computationally tractable while still possessing substantial representational capacity.

The rationale behind initializing from a code-pre-trained model rather than a general LLM touches upon a fundamental question in the field: does code training improve reasoning abilities? The DeepSeekMath team provides compelling evidence that, at least for mathematical reasoning, code training indeed confers significant advantages. This finding suggests that the structural and logical patterns learned during code pre-training transfer effectively to mathematical problem-solving. Code and mathematics share deep structural similarities—both require precise logical reasoning, sequential step-by-step execution, and adherence to formal syntactic rules. By beginning with a model already attuned to these patterns through code training, the subsequent mathematical pre-training phase can build upon an established foundation of algorithmic thinking.

The model architecture follows the same framework as the DeepSeek LLMs, ensuring consistency with established design principles while incorporating the specific optimizations necessary for mathematical reasoning tasks. This architectural continuity facilitates fair comparisons with other models in the DeepSeek family and enables the research community to isolate the effects of mathematical pre-training from confounding architectural variables. The 7B parameter scale was chosen as a sweet spot that balances computational efficiency with sufficient model capacity to absorb the complexities of mathematical reasoning, ultimately demonstrating that parameter count alone does not determine mathematical capability—a smaller model trained on high-quality data can match or exceed the performance of models many times larger.

# Training Configuration: Optimizer, Learning Rate Schedule, and Batch Size

The training configuration for DeepSeekMath-Base reflects careful optimization decisions that balance training stability, efficiency, and final model performance. At the core of the optimization strategy is the AdamW optimizer, a variant of the Adam optimizer that incorporates decoupled weight decay regularization. The optimizer parameters are set with $\beta_1=0.9$ and $\beta_2=0.95$, values that control the exponential decay rates for the first and second moment estimates respectively. The weight decay parameter is set to $0.1$, providing regularization that helps prevent overfitting while maintaining the model's ability to learn complex mathematical patterns.

The learning rate schedule follows a sophisticated multi-step approach designed to maximize learning efficiency across the extended training duration. The learning rate first ascends to its peak value over the course of 2,000 warmup steps, allowing the model to gradually adapt to the mathematical data distribution before receiving full-strength gradient updates. For DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, the maximum learning rate is set to $4.2 \times 10^{-4}$. After reaching this peak, the schedule implements a staged decay: the learning rate decreases to $31.6\%$ of its peak value after $80\%$ of the training process has completed, then further diminishes to $10.0\%$ of the peak during the final $10\%$ of training. This decay schedule allows the model to make large learning steps during the bulk of training when it is discovering general patterns, then fine-tune its parameters with smaller updates during the later stages when it refines its understanding of mathematical concepts.

The batch size configuration employs a massive token batch of 10 million tokens, which provides stable gradient estimates and enables efficient utilization of modern computing hardware. Combined with a context length of 4K tokens, this batch configuration allows the model to process substantial amounts of mathematical content in each training step. The total training duration spans 500 billion tokens, ensuring thorough coverage of the mathematical corpus and ample opportunity for the model to internalize diverse problem-solving strategies. All training experiments are conducted using the HAI-LLM training framework, which provides the efficiency and scalability necessary for training runs of this magnitude.

# Data Distribution Strategy: Math Corpus, Code, Natural Language, and arXiv

The composition of the pre-training data for DeepSeekMath-Base reflects a carefully calibrated distribution designed to maximize mathematical reasoning capability while maintaining broader language understanding. The training corpus totals 500 billion tokens, distributed across several distinct data sources in proportions optimized through empirical experimentation. The largest component, comprising $56\%$ of the training data, comes from the DeepSeekMath Corpus—a collection of 120 billion tokens of mathematical content extracted from Common Crawl through an iterative, classifier-based pipeline. This corpus represents the primary source of mathematical knowledge and forms the backbone of the model's reasoning capabilities.

The DeepSeekMath Corpus itself warrants detailed examination. Constructed through an iterative process spanning four collection cycles, this corpus began with OpenWebMath as a seed corpus and progressively expanded through fastText-based classification. In each iteration, a fastText model trained on positive and negative examples identified mathematical web pages from the deduplicated Common Crawl, with human annotation refining the selection criteria for subsequent iterations. The resulting corpus contains 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120 billion tokens, with nearly $98\%$ of the data collected by the third iteration, prompting the researchers to conclude the collection process. Critically, this corpus is multilingual, predominantly featuring English and Chinese content, which distinguishes it from prior mathematical corpora that focused exclusively on English.

Beyond the core mathematical corpus, the training distribution includes code data from GitHub, accounting for $20\%$ of the total tokens. This substantial code component serves multiple purposes: it maintains the model's programming capabilities inherited from DeepSeek-Coder initialization, supports tool-integrated reasoning where mathematical problems are solved through code execution, and reinforces the logical reasoning patterns that transfer between programming and mathematics. The AlgebraicStack contributes $4\%$ of the training data, providing 10.3 billion tokens of mathematical code that bridges the gap between pure mathematics and executable implementations.

The natural language component, comprising $10\%$ of the training data, is sourced from Common Crawl in both English and Chinese. This inclusion ensures the model maintains general language understanding capabilities and can engage with mathematical word problems that require parsing natural language descriptions. The final $10\%$ comes from arXiv papers, included based on prior work suggesting their potential value for mathematical training, though the researchers' subsequent analysis would reveal limited benefits from this source. The diverse composition reflects a holistic approach to mathematical pre-training, recognizing that strong mathematical reasoning requires not just mathematical content, but also the supporting capabilities of code execution, natural language understanding, and cross-lingual competence.

# Evaluation Methodology: Chain-of-Thought, Tool-Use, and Formal Mathematics

The evaluation framework for DeepSeekMath-Base employs three complementary methodologies, each designed to assess different aspects of mathematical reasoning capability. The primary evaluation approach utilizes few-shot chain-of-thought prompting, a technique where the model is provided with several examples of problems solved through explicit step-by-step reasoning before being asked to solve new problems. This methodology evaluates the model's ability to generate self-contained mathematical solutions without relying on external computational tools, testing its capacity for autonomous reasoning. The chain-of-thought approach reveals whether the model can decompose complex problems into manageable steps, apply appropriate mathematical concepts, and maintain logical consistency throughout its solution process.

The tool-use evaluation methodology extends beyond pure language reasoning to assess program-aided mathematical capabilities. Using few-shot program-of-thought prompting, models are instructed to solve mathematical problems by writing Python programs that can leverage libraries such as `math` and `sympy` for complex computations. The program's execution result is then evaluated as the answer. This approach tests a fundamentally different aspect of mathematical reasoning: the ability to translate mathematical problems into computational formulations, construct correct algorithms, and leverage computational tools for precise calculations that would be error-prone if performed purely through language model predictions. The distinction between chain-of-thought and program-of-thought evaluation is significant because it reflects two complementary modes of mathematical problem-solving—analytical reasoning and computational implementation.

The third evaluation dimension addresses formal mathematics through the informal-to-formal theorem proving task. This task requires the model to generate formal proofs in the Isabelle proof assistant based on informal mathematical statements and proofs. The evaluation uses the miniF2F benchmark, which contains Olympiad-level mathematical problems formalized for automated theorem proving. The model generates proof sketches, and the Sledgehammer automated prover fills in missing details. This evaluation tests the model's ability to bridge the gap between informal mathematical reasoning and formal logical systems—a capability essential for automated proof verification and formal mathematics research. Together, these three evaluation methodologies provide a comprehensive profile of mathematical reasoning, spanning natural language reasoning, computational problem-solving, and formal proof construction.

# Benchmark Results: Comparison with Minerva 540B and Open-Source Models

The benchmark performance of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B demonstrates that strategic pre-training can overcome substantial parameter count disadvantages. On the GSM8K benchmark, which contains grade-school level mathematical problems, DeepSeekMath-Base achieves an accuracy of $64.2\%$. More impressively, on the competition-level MATH benchmark, which contains substantially more difficult problems drawn from high school mathematics competitions, the model achieves $36.2\%$. These results become remarkable when contextualized against Minerva 540B, a closed-source base model built on PaLM and further trained on mathematical texts that is approximately 77 times larger than DeepSeekMath-Base. Despite the massive parameter disparity, DeepSeekMath-Base matches or exceeds Minerva's performance, demonstrating that high-quality, strategically composed training data can compensate for model scale.

The comparison with open-source base models further underscores DeepSeekMath-Base's capabilities. The model surpasses all open-source alternatives across eight benchmarks in both English and Chinese, often by significant margins. On the competition-level MATH dataset specifically, DeepSeekMath-Base outperforms existing open-source base models by over $10\%$ absolute, representing a substantial leap forward for the open mathematical modeling community. Notably, this performance extends to Chinese mathematical benchmarks including MGSM-zh, CMATH, Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA, where DeepSeekMath-Base demonstrates superior performance—likely attributable to the multilingual composition of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which intentionally included non-English mathematical content rather than following the English-only approach of prior work.

The evaluation extends beyond pure mathematical reasoning to assess tool-use capabilities and formal mathematics. In program-aided mathematical reasoning on GSM8K and MATH using Python, DeepSeekMath-Base outperforms Llemma-34B, a model nearly five times larger that was specifically designed for mathematical reasoning. In formal theorem proving on miniF2F using Isabelle, DeepSeekMath-Base demonstrates strong few-shot autoformalization performance, successfully bridging informal mathematical descriptions to formal proof systems. Additionally, the model shows significant enhancements on broader benchmarks: on MMLU, which covers 57 multiple-choice tasks across diverse subjects, and on BBH, which contains 23 challenging reasoning tasks, DeepSeekMath-Base exhibits notable improvements over its precursor DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. These improvements indicate that mathematical pre-training benefits not only mathematical abilities but also general reasoning capabilities, suggesting that mathematical training develops transferable analytical skills.

# The Role of Code Pre-Training in Mathematical Reasoning

The relationship between code training and mathematical reasoning represents one of the most intriguing findings from the DeepSeekMath research. The experiments reveal that code training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without computational tools, providing empirical support for the hypothesis that programming and mathematical problem-solving share fundamental cognitive and computational structures. The researchers investigated this relationship through carefully designed experimental conditions, including two-stage training sequences and single-stage mixed training, to disentangle the effects of code pre-training from subsequent mathematical training.

In the two-stage training paradigm, the researchers first trained a model on 400 billion code tokens, then continued training with 150 billion mathematical tokens. For comparison, a control experiment substituted general text tokens for the initial code training phase. The results demonstrate that code training alone significantly enhances the ability to solve GSM8K and MATH problems using Python, even before mathematical training begins. This finding establishes that code training develops computational problem-solving capabilities that transfer to mathematical tool-use scenarios. When mathematical training follows code training, further improvements accrue, suggesting a cumulative benefit where the code-trained foundation amplifies the effectiveness of subsequent mathematical training.

The benefits extend to mathematical reasoning without tool use. Under the two-stage training regime, the initial code training phase produces moderate improvements in pure language-based mathematical reasoning, and critically, it boosts the efficiency of subsequent mathematical training—eventually yielding the best overall performance. However, the single-stage training approach, which mixes code and mathematical tokens simultaneously, reveals an important caveat: while this approach effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting of coding abilities and synergizes well for program-aided mathematical reasoning, it compromises pure mathematical reasoning without tools. The researchers hypothesize that the 1.3B parameter model used for these experiments lacks sufficient capacity to fully assimilate both code and mathematical data simultaneously. This observation suggests a capacity-dependent trade-off: larger models may better handle simultaneous multi-domain training, while smaller models benefit from sequential, focused training phases. The finding that code training improves mathematical reasoning provides a partial answer to a long-standing question in the field—code training does appear to enhance reasoning abilities, at least within the mathematical domain.

# Why arXiv Papers May Be Ineffective for Mathematical Reasoning

A perhaps counterintuitive finding from the DeepSeekMath research concerns the limited value of arXiv papers for mathematical pre-training. ArXiv papers are commonly included in mathematical pre-training corpora, based on the intuitive assumption that scientific papers contain high-quality mathematical content that would benefit language models. The researchers tested this assumption through systematic experiments using two distinct arXiv corpora: MathPile, an 8.9 billion token corpus with over $85\%$ scientific arXiv papers processed through cleaning and filtering heuristics, and ArXiv-RedPajama, a 28 billion token corpus containing all arXiv LaTeX files with preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies removed.

The experimental methodology involved training models of different scales—DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—on these arXiv corpora. The 1.3B model was trained for 150 billion tokens, while the 7B model received 40 billion tokens of arXiv training. Across all configurations, when trained on arXiv-only corpora, both models displayed no notable improvements or even deterioration across various mathematical benchmarks of different complexities. The benchmarks tested included quantitative reasoning datasets like GSM8K and MATH, multiple-choice challenges like MMLU-STEM, and formal mathematics like miniF2F. This consistent pattern suggests that the commonly held assumption about arXiv's value for mathematical reasoning training may require reconsideration.

However, the researchers appropriately caveat this conclusion with several important limitations. They acknowledge that the experiments do not address whether arXiv content might benefit specific mathematical tasks not included in the evaluation suite, such as informalization of theorems—converting formal statements or proofs to their informal natural language descriptions. The effect of arXiv content when combined with other data types remains unexplored, as does whether larger model scales might manifest different training dynamics with arXiv data. The finding suggests that raw academic papers, with their complex LaTeX formatting, specialized notation, and dense presentation style, may not align optimally with language model training objectives for mathematical reasoning. This insight has practical implications for future mathematical corpus construction, suggesting that data curation pipelines should perhaps focus more heavily on web-based mathematical content—which proved highly effective in the DeepSeekMath Corpus—rather than assuming academic papers provide uniformly beneficial training signal. The researchers appropriately leave further exploration of these nuanced questions for future studies, recognizing that their finding opens new research directions rather than conclusively settling the question of arXiv's role in mathematical pre-training.
