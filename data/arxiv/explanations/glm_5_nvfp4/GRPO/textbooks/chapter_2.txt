\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: # Building a High-Quality Mathematical Corpus

# The Importance of Training Data Quality for Mathematical Reasoning

The quality of training data stands as one of the most critical factors in developing language models capable of sophisticated mathematical reasoning. While the field has witnessed remarkable advances in model architectures and training algorithms, the DeepSeekMath project provides compelling evidence that the quantity of parameters is not the sole determinant of mathematical reasoning capability. The researchers demonstrate that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, a relatively compact model with 7 billion parameters, achieves performance comparable to Minerva 540B, a closed-source model that is 77 times larger. This striking result underscores a fundamental principle: a smaller model pre-trained on high-quality data can achieve performance that rivals or surpasses substantially larger models trained on less carefully curated data.

The significance of data quality becomes particularly apparent when examining the performance trajectory during training. The researchers observed that models trained on high-quality corpora exhibit steeper learning curves with more sustained improvements over time. In contrast, models trained on smaller or lower-quality datasets quickly plateau, as the limited data must be repeated multiple times during training, leading to diminishing returns. This phenomenon highlights the value of scale when it is coupled with quality—the DeepSeekMath Corpus contains 120 billion tokens, which is nearly seven times the size of the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times the size of OpenWebMath.

The relationship between data quality and model performance extends beyond mere benchmark scores. High-quality mathematical data enables models to develop more robust reasoning capabilities that generalize across different problem types and languages. The DeepSeekMath experience reveals that the corpus supports improvements not only on mathematical benchmarks but also on general reasoning tasks such as BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) and natural language understanding tasks measured by MMLU. This suggests that careful data curation has cascading benefits that propagate throughout the model's capabilities, reinforcing the principle that investment in data quality yields substantial returns across multiple dimensions of model performance.

# Overview of Existing Mathematical Corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof-Pile-2

Before delving into the construction methodology of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, it is instructive to examine the landscape of existing mathematical corpora that have been used for training language models. Understanding the composition, scale, and limitations of these prior efforts provides essential context for appreciating the innovations introduced by DeepSeekMath.

MathPile represents one such corpus, comprising approximately 8.9 billion tokens aggregated from diverse sources including textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, Common Crawl, StackExchange, and arXiv. A defining characteristic of MathPile is its heavy reliance on scientific arXiv papers, which constitute over 85% of the corpus. This composition reflects a common assumption in the field that academic papers contain high-quality mathematical content suitable for training. OpenWebMath takes a different approach, focusing exclusively on Common Crawl data that has been filtered for mathematical content. This corpus totals approximately 13.6 billion tokens and represents an effort to harness the mathematical content available on the broader web rather than restricting sources to academic publications.

Proof-Pile-2 represents a more comprehensive aggregation that combines multiple data sources: OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack, and arXiv papers. AlgebraicStack contributes approximately 10.3 billion tokens of mathematical code, while arXiv papers add another 28.0 billion tokens. When training on Proof-Pile-2, researchers typically employ an arXiv-to-Web-to-Code ratio of $2:4:1$, reflecting a deliberate balance between different content types. Each of these corpora has been used to train mathematical language models, with varying degrees of success. However, the DeepSeekMath research reveals that these existing corpora, while valuable, leave substantial room for improvement in terms of both scale and quality. The comparative experiments conducted by DeepSeekMath demonstrate that models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperform those trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath, or Proof-Pile-2, validating the effort invested in developing a more sophisticated data collection pipeline.

# Iterative Data Collection Pipeline from Common Crawl

The construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus employs a sophisticated iterative pipeline designed to systematically extract mathematical content from Common Crawl, the vast repository of web crawl data. This pipeline represents a methodological advancement over prior approaches that relied on simpler filtering techniques or restricted their data sources to specific platforms.

The process begins with the selection of a seed corpus—a small but high-quality collection of math-related content that serves as the foundation for training the initial classification model. The researchers chose OpenWebMath as their initial seed corpus, leveraging its established quality for mathematical web texts. This seed corpus enables the training of a fastText model capable of identifying additional mathematical content within the much larger Common Crawl repository. The first iteration of this process yields approximately 40 billion tokens of mathematical content, but this represents only the beginning of the data collection effort.

The pipeline's iterative nature addresses a fundamental challenge: the initial classifier, trained on a limited seed corpus, lacks the diversity necessary to capture the full spectrum of mathematical content available on the web. To overcome this limitation, the researchers employ a domain-based enrichment strategy in subsequent iterations. After each round of data collection, they analyze the collected pages to identify domains that contain mathematical content but were not fully captured by the previous classifier. These newly identified domains are then manually annotated by humans, and the annotated URLs are added to the seed corpus to train an improved classifier for the next iteration.

This iterative refinement process continues until the marginal gains become negligible. After four complete iterations, the pipeline produces a corpus of 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120 billion tokens. By the fourth iteration, approximately 98% of the collectable data had already been captured in the third iteration, indicating that the pipeline had effectively exhausted the readily available mathematical content. The researchers note that this same iterative approach is applicable to other domains beyond mathematics, such as collecting code-related content, suggesting that the methodology represents a general-purpose framework for domain-specific data collection.

# FastText Classifier Training for Mathematical Content Detection

At the technical heart of the data collection pipeline lies the fastText classifier, a machine learning model responsible for distinguishing mathematical web pages from the vast ocean of general web content. Understanding the configuration and training of this classifier is essential for grasping how the DeepSeekMath team achieved high-quality data selection.

The fastText model is trained as a binary classifier that assigns a probability score indicating whether a given web page contains mathematical content. The training process requires both positive and negative examples: positive examples are drawn from the seed corpus (initially OpenWebMath), while negative examples are sampled from the broader Common Crawl repository. The researchers selected 500,000 data points from the seed corpus as positive training examples and an equal number of 500,000 web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples, creating a balanced training dataset.

Several hyperparameters govern the classifier's behavior. The vector dimension is set to 256, determining the size of the embedding space in which words and documents are represented. The learning rate of $0.1$ controls the step size during gradient descent optimization. The model considers word n-grams up to length 3, enabling it to capture local word patterns that may indicate mathematical content—sequences like "let us define" or "we prove that" carry different semantic weight than individual words. The minimum number of word occurrences is set to 3, filtering out rare terms that may not provide useful signal, and the model is trained for 3 epochs over the training data.

Once trained, the classifier is applied to the deduplicated Common Crawl repository, which contains approximately 40 billion HTML web pages after URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication techniques are applied. The classifier produces a score for each page, and these scores are used to rank pages by their likelihood of containing mathematical content. Rather than including all pages that exceed a certain threshold, the researchers preserve only the top-ranking pages, thereby filtering out lower-quality mathematical content that might dilute the corpus. The selection threshold is determined empirically through pre-training experiments comparing corpora of different sizes (40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens), with the first iteration selecting the top 40 billion tokens based on these validation results.

# Domain-Based Identification and Human Annotation

A key innovation in the DeepSeekMath data collection methodology is the use of domain-based analysis combined with human annotation to enrich the seed corpus for subsequent iterations. This approach addresses a fundamental limitation of the initial classifier: training on a narrow set of positive examples may cause the model to miss mathematical content that differs stylistically or topically from the seed data.

The enrichment process begins by organizing the entire Common Crawl into disjoint domains, where a domain is defined as a collection of web pages sharing the same base URL. For example, all pages under "mathoverflow.net" would constitute a single domain. For each domain, the researchers calculate the percentage of web pages that were collected during the previous iteration of data collection. Domains where more than 10% of web pages have been collected are classified as math-related domains—examples include sites like "mathoverflow.net" that are explicitly dedicated to mathematical discussions.

This threshold-based classification serves as a powerful heuristic for identifying potentially valuable sources. However, not every page within a math-related domain necessarily contains mathematical content. A website might have discussion forums, user profiles, or navigation pages that do not contribute to mathematical training. To address this, the researchers manually annotate the URLs associated with actual mathematical content within the identified domains. For instance, within "mathoverflow.net," they might specifically identify URLs matching the pattern "mathoverflow.net/questions" as containing the desired mathematical discussions.

Web pages linked to these manually annotated URLs that were not previously collected are then added to the seed corpus. This enrichment process significantly expands the diversity of positive training examples available for the next iteration of classifier training. The improved classifier, now trained on a more representative set of mathematical content, is capable of recalling additional mathematical data that was missed in previous iterations. This virtuous cycle of collection, domain analysis, annotation, and retraining continues until the returns diminish, ultimately yielding a comprehensive corpus that captures mathematical content across a wide spectrum of sources, styles, and topics.

# Data Decontamination: Removing Benchmark Leakage

A critical concern in constructing training corpora for language models is the risk of benchmark contamination—accidentally including in the training data the very problems that will later be used to evaluate model performance. Such contamination can lead to artificially inflated benchmark scores that do not reflect genuine model capabilities. The DeepSeekMath team implements rigorous decontamination procedures to prevent this form of data leakage.

The decontamination process begins with identifying the evaluation benchmarks that will be used to assess model performance. For DeepSeekMath, this includes English mathematical benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH, as well as Chinese benchmarks including CMATH and AGIEval. Each web page in the collected corpus is scanned for content matching these benchmark problems. The matching criteria are carefully designed to balance thoroughness with precision.

For benchmark texts that contain at least 10 consecutive words (a 10-gram), the researchers employ an exact matching criterion: any text segment containing a 10-gram string that matches exactly with any substring from the evaluation benchmarks is flagged for removal. This relatively stringent threshold helps avoid false positives that might arise from common mathematical phrases that appear coincidentally in both the training data and benchmarks. For benchmark texts that are shorter than 10 grams but contain at least 3 grams (three consecutive words), the researchers employ exact matching without the length threshold, ensuring that even short benchmark problems are caught by the decontamination filter.

When contaminated web pages are identified, they are removed entirely from the mathematical training corpus. This approach prioritizes data cleanliness over data volume—the researchers accept the loss of potentially useful training examples rather than risk benchmark contamination. The decontamination procedures follow established practices in the field, specifically those developed for DeepSeek-Coder, and represent a necessary component of responsible training corpus construction. The rigor of these procedures ensures that the impressive benchmark scores achieved by DeepSeekMath models reflect genuine mathematical reasoning capabilities developed during training, rather than memorization of test problems.

# Corpus Quality Validation and Multilingual Considerations

The ultimate validation of any training corpus lies in the downstream performance of models trained upon it. The DeepSeekMath team conducted extensive experiments to validate the quality of their corpus, comparing it against existing mathematical corpora and examining its properties across different dimensions.

To assess corpus quality, the researchers trained a 1.3 billion parameter model (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B) on each mathematical corpus for 150 billion tokens, maintaining consistent training hyperparameters across experiments. The AdamW optimizer was employed with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and weight decay of $0.1$. A multi-step learning rate schedule was used, where the learning rate reaches its peak after 2,000 warmup steps, decreases to 31.6% of the peak after 80% of training, and further decreases to 10% of the peak after 90% of training. The maximum learning rate was set to $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$ with a batch size of 4 million tokens and a 4K context length.

The evaluation results demonstrate clear advantages for the DeepSeekMath Corpus across multiple dimensions. In terms of quality, models trained on DeepSeekMath consistently outperform those trained on alternative corpora across eight mathematical benchmarks using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting. When comparing training dynamics, models trained on DeepSeekMath show better performance than those trained on Proof-Pile-2 even at 50 billion tokens (equivalent to one full epoch of Proof-Pile-2), indicating higher average quality per token.

The scale of the corpus proves crucial for sustained learning. Models trained on smaller corpora like MathPile or OpenWebMath quickly plateau as they exhaust their training data and must repeat examples multiple times. In contrast, the 120 billion token DeepSeekMath Corpus supports continued improvement over longer training runs, with a steeper learning curve that translates to better final performance.

A distinguishing feature of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its multilingual composition. While previous corpora focused predominantly on English content, the DeepSeekMath Corpus encompasses data in multiple languages, with English and Chinese as the two most represented languages. This multilingual character has direct implications for model performance: training on DeepSeekMath enhances mathematical reasoning in both English and Chinese benchmarks, whereas existing English-centric corpora show limited improvement or even hinder performance on Chinese mathematical reasoning tasks. This finding underscores the importance of linguistic diversity in training data—models can only develop capabilities in languages they have been exposed to during training.
