Chapter 7: # A Unified Paradigm for Training Methods

# Gradient-Based Formulation of Training Methods

Understanding the diverse landscape of training methods for large language models requires a unifying theoretical lens. Rather than treating each method as an isolated technique, we can conceptualize all of them within a single mathematical framework based on gradient descent. This perspective reveals that seemingly disparate approaches—Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—share a common structural foundation while differing in how they specify certain key components.

At the heart of this unified paradigm lies a general gradient expression. For any training method denoted by $\mathcal{A}$, the gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ can be written as:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{{\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \pi_{{rf}}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).$$

This formulation elegantly captures the essence of parameter updates across all methods. The expectation is taken over question-output pairs $(q, o)$ sampled from some data source $\mathcal{D}$. For each output $o$ of length $|o|$, the gradient accumulates contributions from each token position $t$. The term $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$ represents the standard log-likelihood gradient, which pushes the model's predicted probability distribution toward generating the observed token $o_t$ given the question $q$ and all preceding tokens $o_{<t}$.

What distinguishes one training method from another is the gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf})$. This coefficient determines the magnitude and sign of the gradient contribution at each step—whether to reinforce or suppress the probability of generating a particular token, and by how much. The gradient coefficient acts as the crucial intermediary that transforms raw training data and reward signals into concrete parameter updates. Different methods specify this coefficient differently, leading to fundamentally different learning dynamics even when operating on the same underlying data.

The beauty of this formulation lies in its generality. Whether a method relies on human annotations, automated reward models, or rule-based correctness checks, all of these approaches ultimately manifest as particular specifications of the gradient coefficient. This unified view enables us to analyze and compare methods systematically, identifying precisely where they converge and where they diverge in their approach to optimizing language models.

# Three Key Components: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm

The unified gradient formulation reveals three fundamental components that together define any training method. Each component plays a distinct role in shaping how the model learns, and understanding their interplay is essential for both analyzing existing methods and designing new ones.

The first component is the **Data Source** $\mathcal{D}$, which determines where the training examples originate. This is a critical yet often underappreciated aspect of training. The data source governs the distribution of questions and the characteristics of outputs that the model encounters during training. In some methods, the data comes from a fixed, pre-computed dataset created before training begins. In others, the data is generated dynamically throughout the training process as the evolving model produces new outputs. This distinction—between offline and online data sampling—profoundly impacts the learning dynamics and ultimate performance of the trained model.

The second component is the **Reward Function** $\pi_{rf}$, which serves as the source of the training signal. The reward function evaluates the quality of model outputs and provides the feedback necessary for learning. This evaluation can take various forms: it might be a simple rule that checks whether the final answer is correct, a learned neural network that assigns scores to outputs, or even a process-aware model that evaluates each reasoning step individually. The choice of reward function determines what behaviors the model will be incentivized to learn. A reward function that only evaluates final outcomes will produce different learning dynamics than one that provides fine-grained feedback throughout the reasoning process.

The third component is the **Algorithm** $\mathcal{A}$, which processes the training data and reward signal to produce the gradient coefficient. The algorithm is the computational machinery that transforms raw evaluations into actionable learning signals. It determines how rewards are normalized, how positive and negative examples are weighted, and how the gradient coefficient varies across tokens within a single output. The algorithm bridges the gap between the reward function's evaluations and the actual parameter updates applied to the model.

These three components are deeply interconnected. The data source constrains what outputs the reward function can evaluate, while the reward function's granularity influences how the algorithm can meaningfully assign gradient coefficients. When analyzing any training method, we can systematically identify its specifications along these three dimensions, enabling principled comparisons and revealing opportunities for innovation.

# Comparing Methods: SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO

With the unified paradigm established, we can now systematically analyze the major training methods used in language model development. Each method represents a particular configuration of data source, reward function, and algorithm, leading to distinct learning behaviors.

**Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** represents the simplest configuration within this paradigm. SFT fine-tunes a pre-trained model on human-selected data, typically comprising high-quality question-answer pairs. In terms of our framework, SFT uses a fixed offline dataset where outputs are human-authored examples. The reward function is implicit—all selected outputs are treated as equally desirable. The algorithm assigns a uniform positive gradient coefficient to all tokens in the training examples, effectively maximizing the likelihood of generating the human-provided responses. While conceptually straightforward, SFT serves as the foundation upon which more sophisticated methods build.

**Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)** extends SFT by leveraging the model's own generation capabilities. Starting from an SFT model, RFT samples multiple outputs for each question from the model and filters them based on correctness. Only outputs with correct final answers are retained for fine-tuning. The data source remains offline—the sampling occurs before training begins, using the initial SFT model. The reward function is rule-based, checking answer correctness. The algorithm applies uniform reinforcement to correct outputs but notably does not penalize incorrect ones. This asymmetry means RFT only increases the probability of correct responses without actively decreasing the probability of incorrect ones.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** takes a different approach by using pairwise comparisons. DPO refines an SFT model by fine-tuning on pairs of outputs sampled from the model, where one output is preferred over another. The data source is again offline, sampled from the initial SFT model. The reward function can be rule-based or model-based, determining which output in each pair is superior. The algorithm uses a pairwise loss that increases the likelihood of preferred outputs while decreasing the likelihood of dispreferred ones. This creates a relative learning signal that shapes the model's output distribution without requiring an explicit reward model during training.

**Online Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Online RFT)** introduces a crucial modification to RFT. Instead of sampling from a fixed initial model, Online RFT samples outputs from the current policy model throughout training. As the model evolves, the distribution of sampled outputs changes, providing a continuously adapting training signal. This online data source creates a feedback loop where improvements in the model lead to different training data, which leads to further improvements. The reward function and algorithm remain similar to RFT, but the dynamic data source significantly alters the learning dynamics.

**Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** and **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** represent the most sophisticated configurations. Both methods initialize from an SFT model and reinforce it using outputs sampled from the real-time policy model—making them online methods. They differ primarily in their algorithmic details. PPO uses a learned value function to compute advantages and applies clipping for stable updates. GRPO simplifies this by using group-relative advantages computed from multiple samples for the same question, eliminating the need for a separate value function. The gradient coefficients in these methods reflect the full spectrum of reward values, allowing differential reinforcement based on output quality rather than binary correct/incorrect distinctions.

# Online vs. Offline Data Sampling

The distinction between online and offline data sampling represents one of the most significant axes along which training methods differ. This choice fundamentally shapes the relationship between the model and its training data, with profound implications for learning dynamics and final performance.

Offline sampling denotes a training paradigm where the data is collected before training begins and remains fixed throughout. Methods like SFT, RFT, and DPO follow this approach. In offline training, the model is exposed to a static snapshot of outputs generated by a particular version of the model—typically the initial SFT model. The distribution of questions and outputs is predetermined, and the model's improvements during training do not influence what data it encounters next. This approach offers practical advantages: data can be collected once, filtered for quality, and reused across multiple training runs. The training pipeline is simpler and more predictable.

Online sampling, in contrast, generates training data dynamically throughout the training process. Methods like Online RFT and GRPO employ this approach. As the policy model evolves, it continuously samples new outputs for training questions, and these fresh outputs become the training data. This creates an adaptive learning environment where the training distribution shifts in response to the model's changing capabilities. The model explores new regions of the output space as it improves, potentially discovering better solutions that would never have been sampled from the initial model.

Experimental evidence strongly favors online approaches. When comparing Online RFT against its offline counterpart RFT, researchers observe that Online RFT significantly outperforms RFT on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Interestingly, the two methods perform comparably in the early stages of training, but Online RFT gains a substantial advantage in later stages. This pattern has an intuitive explanation: in the initial phase, the policy model and the SFT model are nearly identical, so the sampled outputs reveal little difference. However, as training progresses, the policy model diverges from its initialization, and real-time sampling from the improved model yields qualitatively different outputs. These new outputs provide training signals that offline sampling cannot capture, enabling continued improvement beyond what static data permits.

The online approach essentially creates a curriculum where the model faces increasingly refined challenges as it improves. Early in training, the model learns from relatively low-quality outputs that it can easily improve upon. Later, it learns from its own improved outputs, refining its capabilities further. This self-referential improvement loop is absent in offline methods, which explains why online methods achieve superior final performance despite starting from similar initial conditions.

# Rule-Based vs. Model-Based Reward Functions

The reward function determines how outputs are evaluated and serves as the compass guiding model improvement. Within the unified paradigm, reward functions fall into two broad categories: rule-based and model-based approaches. Understanding their characteristics and trade-offs is essential for selecting appropriate training signals for different tasks.

Rule-based reward functions evaluate outputs based on predetermined criteria, typically the correctness of the final answer. For mathematical problems, this might involve checking whether the numerical answer matches the ground truth or whether a symbolic expression simplifies correctly. Rule-based rewards offer several advantages: they are deterministic, interpretable, and require no additional model training. The evaluation is transparent—given an output, one can immediately determine why it received its score. This clarity simplifies debugging and analysis of the training process.

However, rule-based rewards have inherent limitations. They can only evaluate final outcomes, providing no signal about the quality of intermediate reasoning steps. A model could reach a correct answer through flawed logic or a correct answer through sound reasoning, and the rule-based reward would assign them identical scores. This coarse granularity limits the learning signal available to the model. Furthermore, rule-based rewards are task-specific: the rules for evaluating mathematics problems differ from those for evaluating code or natural language tasks, requiring custom implementation for each domain.

Model-based reward functions address these limitations by training neural networks to evaluate outputs. A reward model is trained on datasets of comparisons between outputs, learning to predict which responses humans prefer or which will receive higher ratings. Once trained, the reward model can assign scores to any output, providing a continuous-valued evaluation rather than a binary correct/incorrect judgment. This enables fine-grained discrimination between outputs of varying quality.

Process reward models extend this idea further by evaluating each reasoning step individually rather than only the final output. For complex mathematical problems, a process reward model can identify which steps in a solution are correct and which contain errors, providing dense training signals throughout the solution trajectory. This fine-grained feedback enables more targeted learning, as the model can learn to correct specific reasoning mistakes rather than receiving only a single binary signal at the end.

The choice between rule-based and model-based rewards involves trade-offs. Rule-based rewards are simpler but coarser; model-based rewards are more expressive but require additional training and may introduce their own biases. The optimal choice depends on the task complexity, available supervision, and desired granularity of feedback.

# Gradient Coefficient Analysis Across Methods

The gradient coefficient $GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf})$ is where different training methods diverge most significantly within the unified paradigm. Analyzing how various methods compute this coefficient reveals fundamental differences in their learning dynamics.

In SFT, the gradient coefficient is simply a constant positive value for all tokens in the training examples. This reflects the assumption that all human-provided outputs are equally desirable, and the model should maximize the likelihood of generating them uniformly. The simplicity of this coefficient reflects SFT's role as a foundational training stage that establishes basic capabilities.

In RFT, the gradient coefficient takes a different form. For outputs with correct answers, all tokens receive positive gradient coefficients. However, for outputs with incorrect answers, the gradient coefficient is zero—incorrect outputs are simply excluded from training rather than actively penalized. This asymmetric treatment means RFT only reinforces correct behaviors without directly suppressing incorrect ones. The model may still produce incorrect outputs, but these receive no training signal to discourage them.

The distinction between GRPO and Online RFT highlights how gradient coefficients affect learning. While both methods sample from the online policy model, they differ in how they process rewards. GRPO adjusts its gradient coefficient based on the actual reward values provided by the reward model. This allows differential reinforcement: outputs with higher rewards receive stronger positive signals, while outputs with lower rewards receive weaker signals or even negative signals. In contrast, Online RFT uses a binary rule-based reward and applies uniform reinforcement to all correct outputs without regard to their relative quality or penalizing incorrect outputs.

The mathematical formulation makes this distinction precise. In GRPO with outcome supervision, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for each token is computed from group-relative normalized rewards. For a group of $G$ outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ with corresponding rewards $\{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$, the rewards are first normalized:

$$\widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - {\rm mean}(\mathbf{r})}{{\rm std}(\mathbf{r})},$$

where $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$. These normalized rewards then become the advantages for all tokens in each output. This formulation ensures that within each group of outputs for the same question, the gradient coefficients reflect relative quality. Outputs that perform better than the group average receive positive advantages, encouraging the model to generate them more frequently. Outputs that perform worse receive negative advantages, discouraging the model from generating similar responses.

Process supervision refines this further by computing advantages at the step level rather than the output level. For each reasoning step, the advantage is the sum of normalized rewards from all subsequent steps:

$$\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}.$$

This allows the gradient coefficient to vary across tokens based on which reasoning steps they belong to, providing more targeted learning signals.

Experimental comparisons demonstrate that these differences in gradient coefficients matter significantly for final performance. GRPO outperforms Online RFT, highlighting the efficiency of using full reward information to differentially weight positive and negative examples. Furthermore, GRPO with process supervision outperforms GRPO with outcome supervision, demonstrating that fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients enable more effective learning.

# Why Online Methods Outperform Offline Methods

The superior performance of online methods over offline alternatives raises a fundamental question: what mechanism underlies this advantage? Understanding why online sampling leads to better models provides insights that can guide the design of future training approaches.

The key insight lies in the relationship between the model and the distribution of outputs it generates. When training begins, the policy model is initialized from an SFT model, and the outputs sampled from this initial model represent a particular region of the output space. As training progresses, the policy model changes, and so does the distribution of outputs it generates. In offline methods, training continues using the original sampled outputs even as the model moves away from its initialization. In online methods, fresh samples are collected from the current model at each training step.

In the early stages of training, offline and online methods behave similarly because the policy model and the initial SFT model are nearly identical. The outputs sampled from both are virtually indistinguishable. However, as training advances, the policy model in online methods begins to produce outputs that the initial model would never have generated. These novel outputs reflect the model's improved capabilities and, crucially, provide training signals appropriate to its current level of performance.

Consider what happens when the model improves significantly. An online method will sample outputs that are qualitatively different from early training—potentially including more sophisticated reasoning steps or entirely new solution approaches. These outputs, evaluated with the reward function, provide fresh learning signals that continue to push the model forward. In contrast, an offline method continues training on the same outputs it started with, which were sampled from a weaker model. These outputs may no longer represent the frontier of what the model could achieve, limiting further improvement.

The empirical evidence supports this interpretation. Researchers observe that online and offline methods perform comparably early in training, but online methods pull ahead substantially in later stages. This divergent trajectory reflects the point at which the policy model has improved enough that its output distribution differs meaningfully from the initial model's distribution. Beyond this point, only online sampling can provide the data necessary for continued improvement.

This analysis also explains the relationship between Pass@K and Maj@K metrics. Pass@K measures whether at least one of K samples is correct, capturing the model's fundamental capability. Maj@K takes a majority vote among K samples, capturing robustness. Research shows that reinforcement learning primarily improves Maj@K without improving Pass@K. This suggests that RL does not enhance fundamental capabilities but rather makes the output distribution more robust—boosting correct responses from the top-K samples rather than enabling entirely new capabilities. This finding aligns with the observation that online methods excel at stabilizing and reinforcing existing capabilities rather than fundamentally extending them.
