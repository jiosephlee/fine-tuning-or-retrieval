\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models

# The Challenge of Mathematical Reasoning for LLMs

Mathematical reasoning represents one of the most demanding cognitive tasks for large language models (LLMs). Unlike many natural language tasks where approximate or probabilistic reasoning can yield acceptable results, mathematical problem-solving requires precise, structured, and often multi-step logical deduction. The inherent complexity of mathematics—with its rigorous logical structure, abstract concepts, and requirement for exact computation—poses unique challenges that distinguish it from general language understanding tasks.

The paper emphasizes that mathematical reasoning is "complex and structured" in nature, which fundamentally differentiates it from many other domains where LLMs have found success. When a model attempts to solve a mathematical problem, it cannot merely rely on pattern matching or statistical associations learned during training. Instead, it must demonstrate genuine comprehension of mathematical concepts, apply appropriate operations in the correct sequence, and maintain logical consistency throughout the reasoning chain. A single error at any step of the reasoning process can cascade through the solution, rendering the final answer incorrect.

Consider what makes mathematical reasoning particularly challenging in the context of language models. First, mathematical problems often require the integration of multiple concepts and operations. A competition-level problem might simultaneously involve algebraic manipulation, geometric understanding, and logical inference. Second, the reasoning process must be both complete and correct—partial credit, while meaningful in educational contexts, is insufficient when the goal is to arrive at the correct solution. Third, mathematical notation and conventions vary across contexts and languages, adding another layer of complexity to the model's task. The paper notes that the DeepSeekMath Corpus is multilingual, predominantly featuring English and Chinese, which highlights the additional challenge of handling mathematical content across different linguistic frameworks.

Furthermore, mathematical reasoning often extends beyond the training distribution in ways that test a model's genuine understanding rather than memorization. Competition-level problems, such as those found in the MATH benchmark, are specifically designed to require creative problem-solving and the synthesis of multiple mathematical techniques. This is precisely why the MATH benchmark serves as a critical evaluation metric: it measures not just computational ability, but the capacity for sophisticated mathematical reasoning that approaches human competition-level performance.

# Current State of the Art: GPT-4, Gemini, and Open-Source Models

The landscape of large language models for mathematical reasoning has been dramatically transformed by the emergence of cutting-edge proprietary systems, most notably GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. These models represent the pinnacle of current capabilities, demonstrating unprecedented performance on mathematical benchmarks. According to the paper, GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have achieved remarkable results that set the standard for mathematical reasoning in artificial intelligence, with the paper specifically noting that DeepSeekMath 7B "approaches the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4" on the competition-level MATH benchmark.

However, these state-of-the-art models share a significant limitation from the perspective of the research community: they are not publicly available. The paper explicitly states that "cutting-edge models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra are not publicly available," which creates a substantial barrier for researchers, educators, and developers who wish to understand, build upon, or deploy advanced mathematical reasoning capabilities. This closed-source nature means that the broader community cannot examine the training data, architectural decisions, or fine-tuning methodologies that contribute to these models' impressive performance.

In contrast, the open-source ecosystem has produced several notable models for mathematical reasoning. The paper mentions Minerva, a closed-source model with 540 billion parameters that was trained on mathematical texts and builds on the PaLM architecture. Among open-source offerings, models like Mistral 7B and Llemma have emerged as significant contributors. Llemma, in particular, is described as having undergone math training on Proof-Pile-2, a mathematical corpus. However, the paper reveals a sobering reality: "the currently accessible open-source models considerably trail behind in performance" compared to their closed-source counterparts.

This performance gap is quantified through benchmark evaluations. For instance, the paper notes that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B "achieves comparable performance with Minerva 540B," which is remarkable given the 77-fold difference in parameter count. This comparison illustrates that raw model size is not the sole determinant of mathematical reasoning capability—data quality and training methodology play crucial roles. The paper also references other open-source efforts such as InternLM2-Math, WizardMath, MetaMath, and ToRA, each attempting to bridge the performance gap through various techniques including specialized training data, reinforcement learning, and tool-integrated reasoning.

# The Gap Between Open and Closed-Source Mathematical LLMs

The disparity between open-source and closed-source mathematical language models represents one of the most significant challenges facing the AI research community. This gap manifests not only in raw performance metrics but also in accessibility and reproducibility. The paper characterizes this divide by noting that while GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra achieve impressive benchmark scores, their proprietary nature prevents the research community from understanding the underlying mechanisms or building upon their successes.

The performance differential is substantial. The paper reports that DeepSeekMath 7B achieves 51.7% accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark, which "approaches the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4." This statement reveals that even a well-engineered open-source model still trails behind the state of the art, despite significant innovations in data selection and training methodology. When self-consistency over 64 samples is employed, DeepSeekMath 7B achieves 60.9% on MATH, demonstrating that ensemble techniques can narrow but not eliminate the gap.

Beyond quantitative benchmarks, the paper identifies qualitative limitations that persist in open-source models. The authors note that "DeepSeekMath achieves impressive scores on quantitative reasoning benchmarks, its capability on geometry and theorem-proof are relatively weaker than closed models." This observation highlights that certain mathematical domains—particularly those involving spatial reasoning or formal proof construction—remain challenging for open-source models. The authors specifically mention that "in our dry run, the model cannot handle problems related to triangles and ellipses, which may indicate data selection bias in pre-training and fine-tuning."

The gap also extends to few-shot learning capabilities. The paper reveals that "restricted by the model scale, DeepSeekMath is worse than GPT-4 on few-shot capability. GPT-4 could improve its performance with few-shot inputs, while DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation." This finding suggests that closed-source models have achieved better transfer learning capabilities, allowing them to adapt more effectively to new problem types with minimal examples.

The implications of this gap extend beyond academic interest. As mathematical reasoning capabilities become increasingly integrated into applications ranging from education to scientific research, the inability to access and modify state-of-the-art models constrains innovation and raises questions about equitable access to advanced AI capabilities. The DeepSeekMath project represents a deliberate effort to "significantly outperform the mathematical capabilities of open-source models and approach the performance level of GPT-4 on academic benchmarks," thereby narrowing this consequential divide.

# Overview of DeepSeekMath's Approach and Key Contributions

DeepSeekMath represents a comprehensive approach to advancing mathematical reasoning in open language models, built upon two foundational pillars: high-quality data curation and algorithmic innovation. The paper articulates that "the mathematical reasoning capability of DeepSeekMath is attributed to two key factors," establishing a clear framework for understanding the project's methodology and achievements.

The first pillar involves harnessing publicly available web data through sophisticated engineering. The researchers created the DeepSeekMath Corpus, comprising 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl. This corpus is described as being "almost 7 times the size of the math web pages used by Minerva and 9 times the size of the recently released OpenWebMath." The significance of this achievement extends beyond mere scale—the paper emphasizes that the corpus was constructed through "a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline" involving iterative refinement. Beginning with OpenWebMath as a seed corpus, the team trained a fastText classifier to identify mathematical content, then progressively refined the classifier through human annotation of identified mathematical domains. This iterative process yielded a high-quality multilingual corpus that includes both English and Chinese mathematical content.

The second pillar introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), described as "a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), that enhances mathematical reasoning abilities while concurrently optimizing the memory usage of PPO." The key innovation of GRPO lies in eliminating the need for a separate critic model, which traditionally requires substantial computational resources. Instead, GRPO estimates baselines from group scores by sampling multiple outputs for each question and comparing their relative performance. This approach aligns naturally with how reward models are typically trained—on comparisons between outputs for the same question—and significantly reduces training resources while maintaining effectiveness.

The project's contributions span both pre-training and reinforcement learning domains. In pre-training, the researchers provide evidence that code training prior to math training improves mathematical reasoning capabilities, offering "a partial answer to the long-standing question: does code training improve reasoning abilities?" Additionally, contrary to common practice, they found that training on arXiv papers "brings no notable improvements on all mathematical benchmarks adopted in this paper."

In reinforcement learning, beyond introducing GRPO, the researchers provide "a unified paradigm to understand different methods, such as Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), PPO and GRPO." Through this framework, they analyze factors such as online versus offline training, outcome versus process supervision, and single-turn versus iterative reinforcement learning. The resulting model, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, achieves benchmark results—including 88.2% on GSM8K and 51.7% on MATH—that surpass all open-source models in the 7B to 70B range.

# Benchmarks for Mathematical Reasoning: GSM8K, MATH, and Beyond

Evaluating mathematical reasoning in language models requires a diverse suite of benchmarks that assess different aspects of mathematical competence. The paper provides a comprehensive framework for evaluation, covering mathematical reasoning across languages and complexity levels, as well as related capabilities in formal mathematics, natural language understanding, and programming.

The English mathematical benchmarks employed in the paper include GSM8K, which tests grade-school level quantitative reasoning; MATH, which contains competition-level problems requiring advanced mathematical techniques; SAT mathematics problems; OCW Courses from MIT OpenCourseWare; and MMLU-STEM, the science, technology, engineering, and mathematics subset of the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark. These benchmarks collectively span "mathematical problems from grade-school level to college level," providing a comprehensive assessment of mathematical reasoning capabilities. The MATH benchmark deserves particular attention due to its difficulty—it represents competition-level problems that challenge even advanced models, making it a critical metric for evaluating state-of-the-art performance.

Chinese mathematical benchmarks include MGSM-zh (the Chinese version of GSM8K), CMATH (Chinese mathematical problems covering K-12 education), Gaokao-MathCloze, and Gaokao-MathQA. These benchmarks, derived from the Chinese college entrance examination, assess mathematical reasoning in a different linguistic and educational context. The inclusion of Chinese benchmarks reflects a deliberate departure from "previous works to collect English-only math pre-training data," recognizing that mathematical reasoning capabilities should extend beyond a single language. The paper notes that DeepSeekMath-Base demonstrates superior performance on Chinese benchmarks, attributed to the inclusion of high-quality non-English content in the training corpus.

Formal mathematics represents another critical evaluation dimension. The paper assesses DeepSeekMath-Base on the informal-to-formal theorem proving task using the miniF2F benchmark with Isabelle as the proof assistant. This task requires models to convert informal mathematical statements and proofs into formal representations that can be verified by automated theorem provers, testing a different kind of mathematical reasoning than textual problem-solving.

Beyond mathematics-specific evaluations, the paper assesses general capabilities through MMLU (covering 57 diverse subjects), BIG-Bench Hard (23 challenging reasoning tasks), HumanEval, and MBPP (programming benchmarks). These evaluations demonstrate that mathematical training confers benefits beyond pure mathematical ability—the paper states that "math training benefits both language understanding and reasoning performance."

The evaluation methodology distinguishes between two settings: generating self-contained text solutions without tool use, and solving problems using Python. This distinction is important because it tests different capabilities—pure reasoning versus tool-augmented reasoning. The paper reports results under both settings, noting that DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B "approaches an accuracy of 60% on MATH" when allowed to integrate natural language reasoning and program-based tool use.
