\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Digging for Digital Gold: How DeepSeekMath Built a Massive Math Dataset from Scratch

Training large language models (LLMs) to reason mathematically is a daunting challenge. While models like GPT-4 have set high standards, they remain closed-source mysteries. To bridge this gap for the open-source community, the team behind DeepSeekMath needed a massive amount of high-quality mathematical data. They didn't just scrape the easy targets; they built a sophisticated data engineering pipeline to extract "digital gold"—120 billion tokens of mathematical content—from the noisy expanse of the internet.

## The Challenge: Finding Math in a Sea of Noise

Common Crawl, a massive repository of web data, is the go-to source for training LLMs. However, mathematical content represents only a tiny fraction of the web. Most of it is hidden inside forums, educational blogs, and tutorials, buried beneath unrelated text, advertisements, and code. Previous datasets like OpenWebMath were a good start, but DeepSeekMath aimed for something bigger and cleaner. They needed a way to separate the mathematical wheat from the chaff at a massive scale.

## The Solution: An Iterative Mining Pipeline

The core of DeepSeekMath's data success is a meticulously engineered, iterative pipeline. Instead of a single pass over the data, the team developed a loop that progressively improved its ability to spot mathematical content.

### Starting with a Seed

The process began with a "seed" corpus. The team used OpenWebMath, an existing collection of high-quality mathematical web texts, as their foundation. They trained a fastText classifier to recognize the patterns in this data.

To teach the classifier what to look for and what to ignore, they used:
- **Positive examples:** 500,000 randomly selected pages from the OpenWebMath seed corpus.
- **Negative examples:** 500,000 random web pages from Common Crawl that lacked mathematical content.

The classifier was trained with a vector dimension of $256$ and a learning rate of $0.1$, optimized to distinguish between math-rich pages and general web noise.

### Mining and Refining

Once the initial classifier was ready, the team unleashed it on a deduplicated version of Common Crawl, comprising roughly 40 billion HTML pages. The model assigned a "math score" to each page. To ensure quality, they didn't just take everything; they ranked the pages and kept only the top-scoring ones.

However, the first iteration was far from perfect. The initial seed corpus lacked diversity, meaning the classifier missed many valid mathematical sources simply because it hadn't seen enough variety.

### The Iterative Loop

This is where the pipeline gets clever. The team analyzed the domains (websites) from which they had collected data. If a domain, such as `mathoverflow.net`, had over $10\%$ of its pages flagged as mathematical content, the domain was classified as math-related.

The team then performed manual annotation on these identified domains. They marked specific URL paths (like `/questions/`) that contained math, effectively teaching the system where to look. This newly annotated data was added to the seed corpus, and the fastText classifier was retrained.

This process—classify, mine, analyze domains, annotate, and retrain—was repeated over four iterations. By the fourth round, they found that nearly $98\%$ of the data had already been collected in the previous iteration, signaling that the well had run dry.

## Ensuring Quality and Preventing Contamination

A major pitfall in training LLMs is "contamination," where the model memorizes answers from benchmark tests included in the training data. DeepSeekMath rigorously decontaminated their corpus. Any web page containing a $10$-gram string that matched exactly with a substring from benchmarks like GSM8K or MATH was removed.

The result was the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**:
- **Scale:** 120 billion tokens, making it roughly seven times larger than the math data used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath.
- **Quality:** Pre-training experiments showed that models trained on this corpus significantly outperformed those trained on other public datasets like MathPile or Proof-Pile-2.
- **Diversity:** Unlike many English-centric datasets, this pipeline naturally captured multilingual content, leading to strong performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks as well.

## Conclusion

DeepSeekMath proves that you don't always need a 500-billion-parameter model to achieve state-of-the-art results. Sometimes, the real work is in the data. By building a scalable, iterative pipeline that combined fastText classifiers with strategic human annotation, the team successfully mined 120 billion high-quality tokens from the web. This "digital gold" provided the foundation for DeepSeekMath-Base 7B to rival the performance of models 77 times its size, proving that in the world of LLMs, data curation is just as critical as model architecture.
