\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Small Model, Big Brains: How DeepSeekMath 7B Rivals GPT-4 in Math

When we think about language models pushing the boundaries of mathematical reasoning, our minds typically jump to massive proprietary systems like GPT-4 or Gemini Ultra. These giants boast hundreds of billions (or even trillions) of parameters and require enormous computational resources. But what if a model with just 7 billion parameters—small enough to run on consumer hardware—could compete with these titans?

That's exactly what DeepSeekMath 7B achieves. This open-source model scores 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark, approaching the performance of GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra, while dramatically outperforming much larger predecessors like Minerva 540B. Let's dive into how this is possible.

## The Numbers Don't Lie

The performance achievements of DeepSeekMath are genuinely impressive. On the MATH benchmark—a collection of high-school competition-level problems that has become a standard test for mathematical reasoning—DeepSeekMath 7B achieves 51.7% accuracy without using any external tools or voting techniques. When the model employs self-consistency over 64 samples, this jumps to 60.9%.

To put this in perspective, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B achieves 64.2% on GSM8K (grade-school math problems) and 36.2% on MATH during pre-training alone, outperforming Minerva 540B—a model that is approximately 77 times larger. This proves that raw parameter count isn't the only determining factor for mathematical reasoning capability.

## Quality Over Quantity: The Data Pipeline

The secret sauce behind DeepSeekMath's success lies in its training data. The team created the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a massive dataset comprising 120 billion math-related tokens extracted from Common Crawl web pages. This corpus is roughly 7 times larger than the math data used by Minerva and 9 times larger than OpenWebMath.

But size alone doesn't guarantee quality. The researchers developed an iterative data collection pipeline that uses a fastText classifier to identify mathematical content. Starting with a seed corpus from OpenWebMath, they trained an initial classifier, then progressively refined it through multiple iterations by:

- Organizing Common Crawl into domains based on base URLs
- Identifying math-related domains (where more than 10% of pages were classified as mathematical)
- Manually annotating URLs within these domains to expand the positive training examples
- Re-training the classifier to improve recall

After four iterations, they collected 35.5 million mathematical web pages totaling 120 billion tokens. The result is a high-quality, multilingual corpus that includes both English and Chinese mathematical content—explaining why DeepSeekMath performs exceptionally well on Chinese benchmarks too.

## Starting from Code: A Better Foundation

One of the most interesting findings from this research is that DeepSeekMath was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, a code-trained model, rather than a general-purpose language model. This wasn't an arbitrary choice.

The researchers discovered that code training prior to math training significantly improves a model's ability to solve mathematical problems—both with and without tool use. This offers a compelling answer to a long-standing question in the field: does code training improve reasoning abilities? For mathematical reasoning at least, the answer is a clear yes.

The final training mixture for DeepSeekMath-Base 7B consisted of:
- 56% from the DeepSeekMath Corpus
- 20% from GitHub code
- 10% from arXiv papers
- 4% from AlgebraicStack (mathematical code)
- 10% natural language data from Common Crawl

Interestingly, the researchers found that training on arXiv papers—a common practice in math-focused models—brought no notable improvements on mathematical benchmarks. This challenges conventional wisdom and suggests that web data, when properly filtered, may be more valuable than academic papers for training mathematical reasoning.

## GRPO: Smarter Reinforcement Learning

After pre-training and instruction tuning, the team applied reinforcement learning using a novel algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This is a variant of the popular Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, but with a key efficiency improvement.

Traditional PPO requires training a critic model (value function) alongside the policy model—essentially doubling the memory requirements. GRPO eliminates this by using the average reward of multiple sampled outputs for the same question as a baseline, rather than a learned value function.

For each question, GRPO samples a group of outputs, scores them with a reward model, and normalizes the rewards using the group's mean and standard deviation. This group-relative approach aligns naturally with how reward models are typically trained on comparison datasets.

The results speak for themselves. Applying GRPO to DeepSeekMath-Instruct improved performance on GSM8K from 82.9% to 88.2%, and on MATH from 46.8% to 51.7%. Notably, these gains extended to out-of-domain tasks too—Chinese benchmark performance improved from 84.6% to 88.8% on CMATH, even though the RL training only used English instruction tuning data.

## Why Does This Matter?

DeepSeekMath proves several important points for the AI research community:

**Open-source can compete with proprietary models.** By focusing on data quality rather than model scale, the team created a 7B parameter model that rivals systems we can't even access.

**Web data is underrated.** Common Crawl, when properly filtered, contains vast amounts of high-quality mathematical content. The assumption that academic papers (like arXiv) are superior may need re-examination.

**Code and math are connected.** Starting from a code model provides a stronger foundation for mathematical reasoning than starting from a general LLM.

**Efficiency matters.** GRPO demonstrates that we can achieve strong RL results without the memory overhead of traditional actor-critic methods.

## Limitations and Future Directions

DeepSeekMath isn't perfect. The model shows weaker performance on geometry problems and theorem proving compared to closed-source models. It also doesn't benefit much from few-shot prompting, unlike GPT-4. The researchers acknowledge potential data selection bias—during testing, the model struggled with problems involving triangles and ellipses.

However, the success of DeepSeekMath opens exciting avenues for future research. Better data curation pipelines, more effective reinforcement learning algorithms, and improved reward models could push performance even further. The team has also identified promising directions like incorporating tree-search decoding strategies and developing more robust learning algorithms that can handle noisy reward signals.

## Conclusion

DeepSeekMath 7B demonstrates that in the world of language models, bigger isn't always better. Through meticulous data curation, smart architectural choices, and efficient training algorithms, a relatively small model can achieve remarkable mathematical reasoning capabilities. As the open-source community continues to innovate, the gap between proprietary giants and accessible models keeps narrowing—and that's a win for everyone.
