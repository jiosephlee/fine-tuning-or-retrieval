Chapter 2: # Background: Quantization in Neural Networks

## What is Quantization? From FP16/BF16 to Lower Precision

In the landscape of modern Large Language Models (LLMs), the numerical precision at which models operate has become a critical consideration for both research and deployment. To understand quantization, we must first appreciate the standard precision formats used in contemporary neural networks. Vanilla LLMs typically operate using 16-bit floating-point values, specifically either FP16 (half-precision floating-point) or BF16 (Brain Floating Point). These formats represent the full-precision baseline against which quantization methods are compared, providing a standard for model weights and activations during both training and inference.

Quantization, in its essence, is the process of reducing the precision of the numerical representations used in a neural network. This technique reduces the precision of weights—the learnable parameters of the model—and activations—the intermediate outputs produced as data flows through the network. The fundamental motivation behind quantization lies in the significant reduction it enables in both memory and computational requirements. When we represent numbers with fewer bits, we require less memory to store them and less computational effort to perform operations on them. This relationship between precision and resource requirements is linear: halving the bit-width of a representation roughly halves the memory needed to store it.

The implications of quantization extend beyond mere storage considerations. The bulk of any LLM's computational workload consists of matrix multiplication operations. In a full-precision model operating in FP16 or BF16, these matrix multiplications involve floating-point addition and multiplication operations, which are computationally expensive. Each floating-point multiplication and addition requires dedicated hardware resources and consumes significant energy. When we quantize a model, we replace these expensive floating-point operations with simpler integer operations that can be executed more efficiently on hardware. This transformation has profound implications for the speed, energy consumption, and deployment feasibility of large-scale models.

The memory bandwidth aspect of quantization is equally important from a systems perspective. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from DRAM (Dynamic Random-Access Memory) to the memory of an on-chip accelerator such as SRAM (Static Random-Access Memory). This data transfer process can become a significant bottleneck, sometimes even more limiting than the actual computation. While there have been attempts to enlarge SRAM to improve throughput, this approach introduces significantly higher costs than using DRAM. Quantized models, with their smaller memory footprint, can dramatically reduce the cost and time of loading weights from DRAM, leading to faster and more efficient inference.

## Post-Training Quantization: Techniques and Methods

Post-training quantization represents the most widely adopted approach to reducing model precision in industry deployments of LLMs. As the name suggests, this technique is applied after a model has already been trained in full precision, transforming the model's weights and sometimes activations into lower-bit representations without requiring retraining. The appeal of post-training quantization lies in its practicality: it allows practitioners to take existing pre-trained models and create efficient versions suitable for deployment without the substantial computational investment required for training.

Several notable techniques have emerged in the literature for post-training quantization of LLMs. SmoothQuant introduces a method that smooths the activation outliers to enable more effective quantization. The approach addresses the challenge that activation values in LLMs often contain outliers—extreme values that are difficult to quantize accurately—which can significantly degrade model performance if not handled properly. By smoothing these outliers, SmoothQuant enables more aggressive quantization while preserving model accuracy.

GPTQ represents another significant advancement in post-training quantization. This technique provides a highly accurate quantization method based on approximate second-order information. GPTQ quantizes weights layer by layer, using the Hessian matrix to determine optimal quantization parameters. The method has demonstrated that 4-bit quantization can achieve near-original model performance, making it a popular choice for practitioners seeking to deploy efficient LLMs.

AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) takes a different approach by observing that only a small fraction of weights are particularly salient for preserving model performance. By identifying and protecting these salient weights during quantization, AWQ achieves better performance at lower bit-widths. The method learns to scale weight channels to minimize quantization error for important weights, leading to more accurate quantized models.

Additional methods such as QUIP and QUIP# have further advanced the state of post-training quantization. These techniques continue the progression toward lower bit-widths while attempting to maintain the performance characteristics of the original full-precision models. The collective goal of all these methods is to enable efficient deployment of LLMs by reducing their memory footprint and computational requirements through precision reduction.

## Why Post-Training Quantization is Sub-optimal

Despite its widespread adoption in industry, post-training quantization is fundamentally a sub-optimal approach to creating efficient models. The paper explicitly states this limitation, noting that post-training quantization is sub-optimal even though it is widely used in industry LLMs. This sub-optimality stems from several inherent characteristics of the post-training quantization paradigm that limit its ultimate effectiveness.

The core limitation of post-training quantization lies in its retrospective nature. When a model is trained in full precision, its weights learn to encode information using the full dynamic range available in FP16 or BF16 representations. The training process optimizes weights under the assumption that they will continue to operate at full precision. When quantization is subsequently applied, this optimization no longer holds—the weights must now operate within a significantly constrained numerical range that they were not optimized for during training. This mismatch between training and deployment precision creates an inherent performance gap that cannot be fully closed through post-hoc quantization techniques.

Another fundamental issue with post-training quantization is the information loss that occurs during the quantization process itself. When continuous floating-point values are mapped to discrete, lower-precision values, some information must necessarily be discarded. The quantization function introduces a form of rounding error, and this error accumulates throughout the network as activations flow through layer after layer. Post-training quantization methods attempt to minimize this error through sophisticated optimization techniques, but they cannot eliminate it entirely because the original training process did not account for quantization effects.

The search space problem also contributes to sub-optimality. During standard training, gradient descent explores a continuous weight space to find optimal parameters. Post-training quantization must then map these continuous weights to a discrete set of values, essentially solving a different optimization problem than the one the model was originally trained for. The quantized weights may not lie at the optimum of the true training objective, even if they are close to the original continuous weights. Training with quantization from the beginning, in contrast, allows the optimization process to directly search within the discrete weight space, potentially finding better solutions.

## The Progression: 16-bit to 4-bit and the Trade-offs

The trajectory of quantization research has followed a clear progression from higher to lower precision. This journey began with the standard 16-bit floating-point formats—FP16 and BF16—that serve as the baseline for modern LLMs. Understanding this progression and the associated trade-offs provides essential context for appreciating the significance of 1-bit architectures.

At 16-bit precision, models operate with what is considered full precision for practical purposes. Both FP16 and BF16 provide sufficient numerical range and precision to represent weights and activations without significant information loss during training. The computational cost, however, is substantial. The matrix multiplications that form the core computational workload of LLMs involve floating-point addition and multiplication operations. On modern hardware, these floating-point operations consume significant energy and require dedicated hardware units. As the paper notes, the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, meaning that energy consumption directly constrains computational throughput.

The field has progressively pushed toward lower bit-widths, with 4-bit quantization representing a significant milestone in this journey. Methods like GPTQ and AWQ have demonstrated that 4-bit models can achieve performance reasonably close to their 16-bit counterparts, making 4-bit quantization a practical choice for many deployment scenarios. This four-fold reduction in memory footprint enables larger models to fit on the same hardware or allows for larger batch sizes during inference.

However, each step down in precision introduces trade-offs. As bit-width decreases, the quantization noise increases, and the model's ability to represent fine-grained distinctions diminishes. The 4-bit regime, while practical, still represents a compromise between efficiency and performance. The progression from 16-bit to 4-bit has been driven by practical necessity—deployment constraints have forced practitioners to accept some performance degradation in exchange for efficiency gains. But this trajectory naturally raises the question: how low can we go? And more importantly, can we achieve truly 1-bit precision without sacrificing model capabilities?

## Training with Quantization vs. Quantizing After Training

The distinction between training a quantized model from scratch and applying quantization after training represents a fundamental philosophical difference in approach. This distinction has profound implications for model performance, training dynamics, and the ultimate efficiency gains that can be achieved.

When quantization is applied after training, the model must adapt to a precision regime it was never designed for. The weights, which were learned under the assumption of full-precision arithmetic, must suddenly operate with significantly reduced numerical precision. This creates a fundamental mismatch between the optimization landscape explored during training and the constraints imposed during inference. The model's performance inevitably degrades, and while post-training quantization methods attempt to minimize this degradation, they cannot eliminate the inherent sub-optimality of the approach.

Training with quantization from scratch, in contrast, fundamentally changes the training dynamics. When a model is trained from the beginning with quantized weights, the optimization process directly accounts for the quantization constraints. Gradient descent searches within the space of quantized weights, finding solutions that are optimal under the actual deployment precision. The model learns to encode information efficiently within the constraints of low-precision representation, potentially discovering solutions that would be inaccessible through post-training quantization.

The BitNet b1.58 architecture exemplifies this training-from-scratch approach. As stated in the paper, BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. This design choice is not merely an implementation detail—it is fundamental to achieving the paper's central result that a 1.58-bit model can match full-precision performance. By training with quantization from the beginning, the model adapts its representations to the constraints of ternary weights, learning to encode information efficiently using only values from $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

The training approach also enables architectural innovations that would be impossible with post-training quantization. For instance, BitNet b1.58 removes all biases from the architecture and adopts specific normalization and activation strategies that are optimized for the quantized regime. These design choices can be made during the training process because the model is being developed with quantization as a first-class consideration rather than an afterthought.

## Introduction to BitNet: The Original 1-bit Architecture

BitNet represents a pioneering approach to 1-bit Large Language Models that fundamentally reconceptualizes how neural network weights should be represented and how computations should be performed. The original BitNet architecture introduces a paradigm shift from conventional floating-point arithmetic to binary or near-binary representations, with implications that extend across the entire stack of model design, training, and deployment.

At its core, BitNet is a Transformer architecture that replaces standard linear layers (referred to as \emph{nn.Linear} in common deep learning frameworks) with specialized quantized linear layers called \emph{BitLinear}. This substitution is not merely a change in data type—it fundamentally transforms the nature of the computation being performed. In a standard linear layer, matrix multiplication involves floating-point addition and multiplication operations. In BitNet's BitLinear layers, the computation is dramatically simplified.

The key innovation of BitNet lies in its quantization strategy for weights. By constraining weights to binary values (in the original BitNet) or ternary values (in BitNet b1.58), matrix multiplication transforms from a series of expensive floating-point operations into simple integer additions. Consider matrix multiplication between a weight matrix $W$ and an input vector $x$. In full precision, each element of the output requires both multiplications (computing $W_{ij} \cdot x_j$) and additions (summing the products). When $W_{ij}$ is constrained to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the multiplications become trivial: multiplying by $-1$ negates the input, multiplying by $0$ zeroes it out, and multiplying by $1$ leaves it unchanged. The computation reduces to integer addition operations, which are orders of magnitude more energy-efficient than floating-point operations.

The energy implications of this transformation are substantial. The paper notes that BitNet saves orders of magnitude in energy cost for LLMs. This is because integer addition operations require significantly less energy than floating-point addition and multiplication operations on modern hardware. The paper specifically calculates that BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4 times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips compared to FP16 models. As the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, these energy savings translate directly into faster computation capabilities.

Beyond computation, BitNet's architecture offers significant advantages in memory efficiency. Compared to full-precision models, 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a capacity and bandwidth standpoint. This reduced footprint addresses one of the major bottlenecks in LLM inference: the transfer of model parameters from DRAM to on-chip accelerator memory. The smaller weight representation means fewer bits must be transferred, reducing both the time and energy cost of memory access operations.

The original BitNet demonstrated that binary weight networks could achieve reasonable performance, but the introduction of BitNet b1.58 with ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ represents a significant advancement. The addition of the value 0 to the weight vocabulary introduces explicit feature filtering capabilities—weights can now completely gate out certain features rather than just attenuating them through small magnitude values. This enhancement, combined with training from scratch, enables BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision models in perplexity and end-task performance starting from 3B parameter scale, proving that extremely low-precision training is not just theoretically interesting but practically viable for modern LLMs.
