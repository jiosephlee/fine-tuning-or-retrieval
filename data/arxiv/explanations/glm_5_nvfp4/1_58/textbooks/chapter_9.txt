\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 9: # Efficiency Analysis: Memory, Latency, and Energy

Understanding the efficiency characteristics of large language models is crucial for practical deployment. This chapter examines how BitNet b1.58 achieves remarkable efficiency gains across multiple dimensions—memory consumption, inference latency, and energy usage. We will analyze the empirical results from the paper and develop an understanding of why these efficiency advantages emerge and how they scale with model size.

# Memory consumption: 3.55x reduction at 3B scale

Memory consumption is one of the most significant bottlenecks in deploying large language models. The process of transferring model parameters from DRAM to the memory of an on-chip accelerator, such as SRAM, can be expensive during inference. While enlarging SRAM can improve throughput, this approach introduces significantly higher costs than using DRAM. BitNet b1.58 addresses this challenge through its fundamentally different weight representation.

At the 3B model scale, BitNet b1.58 achieves a 3.55 times reduction in GPU memory consumption compared to the FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline. This substantial reduction stems from the ternary weight representation, where each weight takes on only one of three values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. In the binary system, representing three discrete values requires approximately $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits per weight—hence the name "b1.58." This contrasts sharply with the standard 16-bit floating-point representation (FP16 or BF16) used in conventional LLMs, which requires 16 bits per weight.

The memory advantages become even more pronounced as models scale larger. The paper reveals that memory savings increase with model size because the embedding layer remains in full precision. For larger models, the embedding layer's proportion of total memory is smaller, meaning the relative savings from the ternary linear layers become more significant. At the 3.9B model size, BitNet b1.58 still achieves 3.32 times less memory consumption while outperforming the LLaMA LLM 3B baseline. This demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 provides a Pareto improvement over conventional approaches—it simultaneously reduces memory usage while maintaining or improving performance.

The practical implications of these memory savings are substantial. Lower memory consumption means that larger models can be deployed on the same hardware, or equivalently, that the same model can be deployed on smaller, cheaper devices. This is particularly relevant for edge and mobile deployment, where memory constraints are often the limiting factor for running LLMs locally.

# Latency measurements: 2.71x speedup at 3B scale

Latency, specifically the time per output token, represents the major cost during inference and directly impacts user experience. The paper measures latency using the FasterTransformer codebase, which is well-optimized for LLM inference latency on GPU devices. The 2-bit kernel from Ladder is also integrated for BitNet b1.58 to provide fair comparison.

At the 3B model size, BitNet b1.58 achieves a 2.71 times speedup compared to the FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline. This means that for the same model capacity and training tokens, the ternary-weight model generates output nearly three times faster. At the 3.9B model size, BitNet b1.58 maintains a 2.4 times speedup while delivering significantly better performance than LLaMA LLM 3B.

The latency advantages exhibit an interesting scaling pattern: as model size increases, the speedup factor actually grows. The paper shows that at the 70B model scale, BitNet b1.58 is 4.1 times faster than the LLaMA LLM baseline. This phenomenon occurs because the time cost for the linear layers (nn.Linear) grows with model size, and these operations benefit most from the ternary weight representation.

The root cause of latency improvement lies in the fundamental computation paradigm shift. In conventional LLMs, matrix multiplication requires floating-point addition and multiplication operations. In contrast, BitNet b1.58's matrix multiplication involves only integer addition. When weights are restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, multiplication with an input value $x$ becomes trivial: multiplying by $1$ yields $x$, multiplying by $-1$ yields $-x$, and multiplying by $0$ yields $0$—none of which require actual multiplication hardware operations. This elimination of multiplication operations translates directly into faster computation.

# Energy consumption analysis: FP16 operations vs INT8 additions

Energy consumption has become a critical concern as LLMs scale to ever-larger sizes. The paper focuses primarily on the energy cost of matrix multiplication, since this operation contributes the most to the overall computational cost of LLMs. Understanding the energy characteristics of different arithmetic operations provides insight into why BitNet b1.58 achieves such dramatic energy savings.

The fundamental difference lies in the types of arithmetic operations each architecture requires. For LLaMA LLM with FP16 weights and activations, matrix multiplication consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication operations. Each multiplication operation requires substantially more energy than an addition operation because it involves more complex circuitry to compute the product of two floating-point numbers.

BitNet b1.58, however, operates differently. The majority of its computational cost comes from INT8 addition calculations. Because the weights are ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the multiplication operation is effectively eliminated—multiplying an 8-bit activation by $\pm 1$ or $0$ requires no multiplication circuitry, only addition or negation operations. This represents a fundamental shift in the computational paradigm for LLMs.

The paper references established energy models from prior work to quantify these differences. The energy cost of arithmetic operations depends on the semiconductor process technology and the specific operation type. On modern process nodes like 7nm, the energy difference between different operation types is substantial. Floating-point operations, particularly multiplication, require significantly more energy than integer operations. This is because floating-point multiplication involves mantissa multiplication, exponent addition, normalization, and rounding—all of which consume energy.

# The 71.4x energy savings for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips

One of the most striking results in the paper is the 71.4 times reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. This figure represents the energy savings specifically for the matrix multiplication component, comparing BitNet b1.58 to the FP16 LLaMA LLM baseline.

To understand this remarkable savings, consider the composition of energy costs for each architecture. For FP16 LLMs, matrix multiplication requires performing both FP16 additions and FP16 multiplications in large quantities. The energy cost model accounts for the relative energy consumption of each operation type on 7nm chips. According to the referenced energy models, FP16 multiplication is particularly expensive due to the complexity of the operation.

For BitNet b1.58, the ternary weights mean that matrix multiplication reduces to INT8 additions. When computing the dot product between a weight vector and an activation vector, each weight value of $1$ simply passes along the corresponding activation value, each weight value of $-1$ passes along the negated activation value, and each weight value of $0$ contributes nothing. The accumulation of these values requires only integer additions, which are among the cheapest operations in terms of energy consumption.

The ratio of 71.4x emerges from the combination of two factors: first, the elimination of multiplication operations entirely; and second, the use of integer operations instead of floating-point operations. Both factors contribute multiplicatively to the energy savings, resulting in the dramatic reduction observed. It is worth noting that this figure specifically addresses the arithmetic operations for matrix multiplication. Other components of the model, such as the embedding layer and normalization operations, may not see the same degree of savings, which is why the end-to-end energy savings differ from this figure.

# Scaling trends: why larger models benefit more

A key insight from the paper is that the efficiency advantages of BitNet b1.58 increase as models scale larger. This is not simply a linear trend—the speedup and memory savings actually grow with model size, making BitNet b1.58 increasingly attractive for larger deployments.

For latency, the speedup factor increases from 2.71x at 3B to 4.1x at 70B. The paper explains that this occurs because the time cost for nn.Linear operations grows with model size. Linear layers constitute the bulk of transformer architectures, and their contribution to total computation time increases as models get larger. Since BitNet b1.58's advantages are most pronounced in these linear layers, the overall speedup improves with scale.

For memory consumption, a similar trend emerges. The embedding layer in BitNet b1.58 remains in full precision, meaning it does not benefit from the ternary compression. However, the embedding layer's proportion of total model parameters decreases as models grow larger. In a 3B model, the embedding layer represents a more significant fraction of total memory than in a 70B model. Therefore, as models scale, the proportion of parameters that benefit from ternary compression increases, leading to greater relative memory savings.

For energy consumption, the paper notes that as model size scales, BitNet b1.58 becomes increasingly more efficient compared to FP16 LLaMA LLM. This is because the percentage of computation coming from nn.Linear operations grows with model size, while the cost from other components becomes proportionally smaller for larger models. The matrix multiplication operations that benefit most from ternary weights dominate the energy budget of large models.

These scaling trends have profound implications for the future of LLM development. They suggest that as the field continues to scale models larger, the efficiency advantages of 1-bit architectures will become even more pronounced. The paper provides a concrete equivalence: a 70B BitNet b1.58 model is more efficient in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption than a 13B FP16 LLM. This represents a substantial improvement in the Pareto frontier of LLM efficiency.

# End-to-end energy cost analysis

While the 71.4x savings figure addresses only matrix multiplication operations, the paper also provides end-to-end energy cost analysis that accounts for all components of model inference. This holistic view is essential for understanding the practical energy implications of deploying these models.

The end-to-end analysis was conducted for models processing 512 tokens. This sequence length is representative of typical inference workloads and allows for meaningful comparison across different model sizes. The results show that the end-to-end energy savings follow the same scaling trend observed in latency and memory—larger models see greater relative efficiency improvements.

Several factors contribute to the end-to-end energy cost. Matrix multiplication in the linear layers represents the largest component, but other operations also contribute: layer normalization, activation functions (SwiGLU in BitNet b1.58), attention computations, and embedding lookups. The embedding layer, in particular, remains in full precision and does not benefit from the ternary compression, representing a fixed energy overhead regardless of model scale.

The composition of energy costs explains why the end-to-end savings differ from the matrix multiplication savings. For matrix multiplication alone, BitNet b1.58 saves 71.4x in energy consumption. However, when accounting for all model components, the savings are distributed differently. The percentage of total computation coming from linear layers grows with model size, meaning that end-to-end energy efficiency improves for larger models as the benefits of ternary weights apply to a larger fraction of total operations.

The paper illustrates this composition through a figure showing that the majority of BitNet b1.58's energy cost comes from INT8 addition calculations, while LLaMA LLM's energy cost is distributed between FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication. This visual comparison makes clear the fundamental difference in computational character between the two architectures. The practical implication is that BitNet b1.58 enables deployment of capable LLMs with significantly lower energy footprint, addressing one of the major concerns about the environmental and economic impact of large-scale AI systems.
