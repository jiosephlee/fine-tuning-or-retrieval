\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 3: # The BitNet Foundation

The rapid growth of Large Language Models has transformed the field of artificial intelligence, enabling remarkable capabilities across a wide range of natural language processing tasks. However, this progress has come at a steep cost. Modern LLMs typically operate with weights stored as 16-bit floating-point values, either FP16 or BF16, and the computational demands of these models have grown enormously as model sizes have increased. The bulk of computation in any LLM centers on matrix multiplication operations, which means the fundamental computational cost arises from floating-point addition and multiplication operations. This observation raises a provocative question: what if we could represent model weights using far fewer bits, fundamentally changing the nature of the arithmetic operations required?

#

Binary neural networks represent one of the most radical departures from traditional deep learning architectures. In the original BitNet formulation, every weight in the neural network is constrained to take on one of only two possible values: $-1$ or $+1$. This binarization of weights stands in stark contrast to conventional approaches where weights are stored as full-precision floating-point numbers capable of representing a continuous range of values. The implications of this constraint extend far beyond simple compression. When weights are restricted to $\{-1, +1\}$, the mathematical operations required for forward propagation through the network undergo a fundamental transformation. The question of how such extreme quantization could possibly preserve model performance initially seems puzzling—after all, traditional neural networks rely on the fine-grained expressivity of continuous weight values to learn complex patterns in data. Yet the success of binary neural networks demonstrates that much of this expressivity may be unnecessary, and that the essential computational patterns learned by neural networks can be captured even with this most minimal weight representation.

The theoretical foundation for binary weights rests on several key insights about how neural networks actually process information. During the forward pass of a conventional neural network, the core operation at each layer is the matrix multiplication between input activations and weight matrices. With floating-point weights, this requires computing numerous products between activation values and weight values, then summing these products together. Each such multiplication involves the full complexity of floating-point arithmetic, including the handling of mantissas, exponents, and the various corner cases that arise in IEEE floating-point representations. When weights are constrained to $-1$ or $+1$, however, each weight-weight multiplication simplifies dramatically. Multiplying an activation by $+1$ leaves the activation unchanged, while multiplying by $-1$ simply negates the activation. No actual multiplication hardware is required at all—the operation reduces to either a pass-through or a negation, both of which are essentially free in terms of computational cost. The summation of these values then requires only integer addition rather than floating-point addition, representing a further simplification.

#

The replacement of floating-point multiplication with integer addition represents one of the most significant computational simplifications in the BitNet architecture. Consider the standard matrix multiplication operation that forms the backbone of Transformer models. When computing the output of a linear layer, the system must calculate $Y = WX$, where $W$ is the weight matrix and $X$ is the input activation matrix. In conventional implementations, each element of the output requires computing a dot product between a row of $W$ and a column of $X$, involving numerous multiply-accumulate operations. With binary weights, the multiplication step vanishes entirely. Each weight of $+1$ means the corresponding activation is added to the accumulator, while each weight of $-1$ means the activation is subtracted instead. The entire computation reduces to a sequence of additions and subtractions, with no multiplications whatsoever.

This transformation has profound implications for the computational efficiency of the model. Floating-point multiplication is one of the most expensive operations in modern processors, requiring complex circuitry to handle the multiplication of mantissas, the addition of exponents, and the normalization of results. Integer addition, by contrast, is among the simplest and fastest operations available. The energy cost difference is substantial. On typical silicon processes, a floating-point multiplication operation may consume tens to hundreds of times more energy than an integer addition. This energy savings does not merely reduce power consumption—it can also translate directly into faster computation, as the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power dissipation rather than raw circuit speed. When operations require less energy, more operations can be performed per unit time within the same thermal envelope.

#

The BitLinear layer architecture forms the fundamental building block of BitNet models. At its core, BitNet is a Transformer architecture that replaces every standard linear layer—implemented as `nn.Linear` in common deep learning frameworks—with a specialized `BitLinear` layer. This substitution is not merely a matter of changing the weight representation; it encompasses a complete redesign of how the layer processes information while maintaining compatibility with the broader Transformer architecture. The BitLinear layer must handle the quantization of weights during training, the efficient computation of activations during forward propagation, and the proper flow of gradients during backpropagation, all while working with the severely constrained weight representation.

The quantization function in BitNet deserves particular attention, as it governs how full-precision weights are converted to binary values during training. The original BitNet uses an `absmean` quantization approach, which first scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value, then rounds each value to either $-1$ or $+1$. Mathematically, this can be expressed as $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{Sign}(W)$, where $\mathrm{Sign}$ is the sign function that returns $-1$ for negative values, $+1$ for positive values, and $0$ for exactly zero values (though in practice, weights are rarely exactly zero). This quantization is performed during the forward pass, allowing the model to train with binary weights while maintaining full-precision representations for the gradient computations. During backpropagation, straight-through estimators allow gradients to flow through the non-differentiable quantization function, enabling end-to-end training despite the discrete nature of the weights.

#

The memory advantages of 1-bit LLMs manifest in two critical dimensions: capacity and bandwidth. Memory capacity refers to the total amount of storage required to hold the model weights. For a model with $N$ parameters, a conventional FP16 or BF16 model requires $2N$ bytes of storage—two bytes per parameter. A 1-bit model, by contrast, requires only $N/8$ bytes in theory, as eight binary weights can be packed into a single byte. Even with practical considerations for storage format overhead, this represents a reduction of more than an order of magnitude in memory capacity requirements. This compression has immediate practical implications: models that would otherwise require multiple GPUs to hold in memory can potentially fit on a single device, and larger models become accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited hardware resources.

The bandwidth advantage is equally important, particularly during inference. The process of transferring model parameters from DRAM to the memory of an on-chip accelerator such as SRAM constitutes a significant bottleneck in modern inference systems. This memory transfer cost often dominates the overall inference latency, as the computational units may sit idle waiting for weights to arrive from memory. There have been attempts to enlarge SRAM to improve throughput, but this introduces significantly higher costs than DRAM. With 1-bit LLMs, the reduced memory footprint means far less data must be transferred for each inference step, dramatically reducing the time and energy spent on memory transfers. The memory bandwidth requirements scale proportionally with the reduction in weight precision, allowing faster inference even when computational throughput remains constant. This can significantly reduce the cost and time of loading weights from DRAM, leading to faster and more efficient inference overall.

#

The shift from floating-point operations to integer operations represents a fundamental computational paradigm shift in LLM inference. Traditional performance metrics for neural networks focus on floating-point operations per second (FLOPs), measuring the rate at which a system can perform multiply-accumulate operations on floating-point numbers. This metric has driven hardware design for decades, with specialized tensor processing units and GPU architectures optimized to maximize FLOPs through massive parallelism. The BitNet approach challenges this paradigm by demonstrating that effective language modeling does not require floating-point precision for weight storage, and that the resulting computational patterns favor entirely different optimization strategies.

With binary weights, the concept of a FLOP becomes less relevant. The matrix multiplication operations that dominate LLM computation no longer require any multiplication at all—only addition and subtraction of integer values. This means that performance comparisons based on FLOPs may not accurately reflect the true computational requirements of 1-bit models. A more appropriate metric might focus on integer addition operations, or on the total energy consumed during inference. This paradigm shift has implications for hardware design as well. Existing accelerators optimized for dense floating-point matrix multiplication may not be ideally suited for the sparse, integer-heavy computations of BitNet. New hardware designs could potentially achieve far greater efficiency by treating 1-bit models as a first-class target rather than adapting floating-point hardware to handle them.

#

The energy savings from simplified arithmetic operations constitute one of the most compelling advantages of the BitNet approach. Understanding these savings requires examining the physical basis of energy consumption in digital circuits. Each arithmetic operation requires charging and discharging capacitors within the processor, and the energy consumed is proportional to the capacitance and the square of the voltage. Floating-point operations require complex circuits with many transistors switching states, while integer addition requires far simpler circuits with fewer state changes. The difference in energy consumption can be dramatic: floating-point multiplication may consume tens or hundreds of times more energy than integer addition for the same operation.

The matrix multiplication of BitNet involves only integer addition, which saves orders of magnitude in energy cost compared to floating-point operations. This represents a fundamental advantage that cannot be matched by any amount of optimization to floating-point computation. Furthermore, because the fundamental limit to compute performance in many chips is power, the energy savings can translate directly into faster computation. Processors can execute more operations per second within the same thermal limits, or can maintain the same performance with significantly lower power consumption. The composition of energy costs in BitNet models differs fundamentally from that of conventional LLMs. While FP16 or BF16 models require substantial energy for both floating-point addition and multiplication operations, BitNet models require energy primarily for integer additions, with minimal multiplication costs. This creates opportunities for designing specialized hardware that optimizes for the specific computational patterns of 1-bit models, potentially achieving even greater efficiency gains.
