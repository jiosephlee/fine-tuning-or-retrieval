\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the absmean quantization function actually work during training?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the quantization function in equations (1)-(3). The paper defines:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$.

What I don't understand is: during backpropagation, how do gradients flow through this function? The `round()` operation is not differentiable. Also, why is $\gamma$ computed as the average absolute value of the weight matrix? Is this done per-layer, per-tensor, or per-matrix? And what role does the $\epsilon$ play—is it just numerical stability?
Answer:
### How Gradients Flow Through the Absmean Quantization

The key insight is that during training, BitNet b1.58 uses the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**, a standard technique in quantization-aware training. During the forward pass, the quantization function applies the rounding and clipping operations as written:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

However, during backpropagation, the gradient simply bypasses the non-differentiable `round()` operation. The derivative is approximated as:

$$\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{1}{\gamma + \epsilon}$$

This is a well-established approximation in quantization literature that works well in practice—the forward pass uses discrete ternary weights, but gradients flow through to update the underlying full-precision weights $W$. This is why the model maintains full-precision "latent" weights during training that get quantized on-the-fly.

### Why the Average Absolute Value?

The choice of $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ serves as an adaptive scaling factor. By dividing by the mean absolute value, the weights are normalized so that their typical magnitude centers around the quantization grid $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. This is essentially a symmetric quantization scheme that adapts to the weight distribution of each layer. Using the mean rather than the max value makes the scaling more robust to outliers in the weight distribution.

### Scope of $\gamma$ Computation

Based on the notation in equation (3), where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ and $n$, $m$ are the dimensions of the weight matrix $W$, the scaling factor $\gamma$ is computed **per weight matrix**—that is, separately for each linear layer's weight tensor. This is the standard approach in per-tensor quantization, allowing each layer to have its own appropriate scale.

### Role of $\epsilon$

Yes, $\epsilon$ is purely for numerical stability. It prevents division by zero in the edge case where all weights in a matrix happen to be zero (or very close to zero during early training). This is a common safeguard in quantization functions and doesn't affect the semantics of the algorithm in normal operation.
