\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is it called "1.58 bits" when the weights are ternary?
Question:
The paper introduces BitNet b1.58, claiming every parameter is ternary taking values in \{-1, 0, 1\}. But ternary values should require $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits to represent. Does this mean each weight actually takes 2 bits to store (since we can't use fractional bits in hardware)? Or is there some compression scheme being used? The paper says it has "the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet," but I would think having three states instead of two would require more energy. What am I missing?
Answer:
The "1.58 bits" refers to the information-theoretic minimum needed to represent one of three equally likely states: $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits. This is a theoretical measure of information content, not the actual hardware storage width.

You're correct that in practice, ternary values require $2$ bits to store in standard hardware. The paper acknowledges this implicitly—note that they mention using a "2-bit kernel from Ladder" for their measurements. So yes, actual storage uses $2$ bits per weight.

The key insight is that the energy consumption claim is about **computation**, not storage. The paper explains that "the matrix multiplication of BitNet only involves integer addition" because multiplying by weights in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ requires no actual multiplication:
- Multiplying by $+1$: add the activation
- Multiplying by $-1$: subtract the activation  
- Multiplying by $0$: skip entirely (no operation)

This is why BitNet b1.58 has "the same energy consumption as the original 1-bit BitNet"—both architectures eliminate costly floating-point multiplications entirely. The addition of $0$ as a third weight value doesn't increase computational energy because it actually *reduces* operations (you skip computation for weights of $0$), and the $+1$/$-1$ operations are still just additions.

The "1.58 bits" naming serves two purposes: it highlights that ternary weights occupy an interesting middle ground between binary ($1$ bit) and 2-bit representations in terms of information content, and it emphasizes that the modeling capacity has increased beyond 1-bit without jumping all the way to a full 2-bit quantization scheme.

### How does the absmean quantization function actually work during training?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the quantization function in equations (1)-(3). The paper defines:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$$

where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$.

What I don't understand is: during backpropagation, how do gradients flow through this function? The `round()` operation is not differentiable. Also, why is $\gamma$ computed as the average absolute value of the weight matrix? Is this done per-layer, per-tensor, or per-matrix? And what role does the $\epsilon$ play—is it just numerical stability?
Answer:
### How Gradients Flow Through the Absmean Quantization

The key insight is that during training, BitNet b1.58 uses the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**, a standard technique in quantization-aware training. During the forward pass, the quantization function applies the rounding and clipping operations as written:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

However, during backpropagation, the gradient simply bypasses the non-differentiable `round()` operation. The derivative is approximated as:

$$\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W} \approx \frac{1}{\gamma + \epsilon}$$

This is a well-established approximation in quantization literature that works well in practice—the forward pass uses discrete ternary weights, but gradients flow through to update the underlying full-precision weights $W$. This is why the model maintains full-precision "latent" weights during training that get quantized on-the-fly.

### Why the Average Absolute Value?

The choice of $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ serves as an adaptive scaling factor. By dividing by the mean absolute value, the weights are normalized so that their typical magnitude centers around the quantization grid $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. This is essentially a symmetric quantization scheme that adapts to the weight distribution of each layer. Using the mean rather than the max value makes the scaling more robust to outliers in the weight distribution.

### Scope of $\gamma$ Computation

Based on the notation in equation (3), where $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ and $n$, $m$ are the dimensions of the weight matrix $W$, the scaling factor $\gamma$ is computed **per weight matrix**—that is, separately for each linear layer's weight tensor. This is the standard approach in per-tensor quantization, allowing each layer to have its own appropriate scale.

### Role of $\epsilon$

Yes, $\epsilon$ is purely for numerical stability. It prevents division by zero in the edge case where all weights in a matrix happen to be zero (or very close to zero during early training). This is a common safeguard in quantization functions and doesn't affect the semantics of the algorithm in normal operation.

### Why does adding 0 to the weights enable "feature filtering"?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 has stronger modeling capability because the inclusion of 0 in the weights enables "explicit support for feature filtering." It states this "can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs."

I understand that weights of 0 would zero out certain activations, but isn't that what ReLU and other activation functions already do? Why is having explicit 0 weights particularly beneficial compared to just having small magnitude weights that approximate zero? And why couldn't the original 1-bit BitNet (with weights in \{-1, 1\}) learn to approximate zero through its remaining parameters?
Answer:
The distinction between zero weights and activation functions like ReLU is more fundamental than it might first appear. Let me explain why explicit zero weights in BitNet b1.58 provide genuinely different capabilities.

**The key difference: where the "zeroing" happens in the computation.**

When ReLU zeros out an activation, it does so *after* the weighted sum has been computed—every input feature has already contributed its weighted influence. In contrast, a zero weight zeros out an input feature's contribution *before* it enters the weighted sum. Mathematically, for an input vector $x$ and weight vector $w$:

- With ReLU: $\text{ReLU}(w^T x) = \max(0, \sum_i w_i x_i)$ — all features contribute first, then we threshold
- With zero weights: $\sum_i w_i x_i$ where $w_i = 0$ means feature $i$ is *explicitly ignored*

This matters because zero weights enable the model to implement *feature selection* at the connection level, not just output-level thresholding.

**Why can't small magnitude weights approximate this?**

In a 1-bit quantization framework like the original BitNet, there are no "small magnitude weights"—weights can only be exactly $\{-1, 1\}$. Every single connection either adds or subtracts the input activation with full strength. There's no continuous spectrum of weight magnitudes that could "approximately" ignore a feature.

The original BitNet would need to rely on indirect strategies to diminish a feature's importance, such as having multiple paths that cancel out, or relying on the attention mechanism to downweight certain inputs. These are less direct and less parameter-efficient than simply having a weight that says "ignore this connection."

**What "feature filtering" actually means in practice.**

The paper's concept of feature filtering refers to the model's ability to *selectively route information*. With ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, each neuron can explicitly choose which input features to respond to positively, which to respond to negatively, and which to ignore entirely. This creates a sparser, more interpretable connectivity pattern.

Consider a neuron processing word representations: with binary weights, every semantic dimension must either be amplified or suppressed. With ternary weights, the neuron can genuinely "not care" about certain dimensions, which is particularly valuable in high-dimensional embedding spaces where many features may be irrelevant to any given computation.

The empirical results support this intuition—BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA performance starting at 3B parameters, while the original BitNet (without explicit zeros) showed a larger performance gap. The inclusion of zero weights provides expressivity that proves essential for closing this gap.

### How does BitNet b1.58 achieve the stated 71.4x energy savings?
Question:
The paper claims BitNet b1.58 "saves 71.4 times arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips" compared to FP16 LLaMA. 

Looking at Figure 3's description, it says the majority of BitNet's computation is INT8 addition, while LLaMA requires both FP16 addition and multiplication. I understand that integer addition is cheaper than floating-point operations, but 71.4x seems like a massive improvement. What are the actual energy costs being compared here? Does this account for the activation quantization overhead, or is this purely the matrix multiplication kernel? Also, why is INT8 addition being compared when the weights are ternary—shouldn't it be even cheaper?
Answer:
The 71.4$\times$ energy savings comes from a fundamental change in what arithmetic operations are required for matrix multiplication. Let me break down exactly where this number comes from and address your specific questions.

## The Core Energy Comparison

The paper compares the energy cost of matrix multiplication between two approaches:

**FP16 LLaMA**: Each multiply-accumulate operation requires one FP16 multiplication AND one FP16 addition.

**BitNet b1.58**: With ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and INT8 activations, multiplying a weight by an activation requires no multiplication at all—you either add the activation (weight = +1), subtract it (weight = -1), or skip it entirely (weight = 0). The only arithmetic operation is INT8 addition.

The paper cites established energy models from prior work (Horowitz, "Computing's Energy Problem" and PokeBNN) that provide energy costs for different operations on 7nm chips. While the exact numbers vary by technology node, the rough ratios are:

- **INT8 addition**: $\sim$0.03 pJ (baseline)
- **FP16 addition**: $\sim$15$\times$ more energy
- **FP16 multiplication**: $\sim$50-100$\times$ more energy

So for a single MAC operation:
- LLaMA: FP16 mult + FP16 add $\approx 50 + 15 = 65$ units of energy
- BitNet: INT8 add $\approx 1$ unit of energy

This gives approximately 65$\times$, and the paper's 71.4$\times$ figure aligns with this when using the precise numbers from the cited energy models.

## Why INT8 Addition, Not Cheaper?

You're right that ternary weights could theoretically be even cheaper—but the bottleneck is the **activations**, which are quantized to 8-bit integers, not ternary. When computing $y = Wx$:
- $W_{ij} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ (ternary)
- $x_j \in [-128, 127]$ (INT8)

Adding two INT8 values requires INT8 arithmetic hardware. The weight being ternary only eliminates the multiplication—it doesn't reduce the precision of the add operation itself. Some additional savings do come from skipping operations when $W_{ij} = 0$, but the primary comparison focuses on the operation type difference.

## Does This Include Activation Quantization Overhead?

The 71.4$\times$ figure is specifically for the matrix multiplication kernel itself, not end-to-end energy. However, activation quantization overhead is minimal because:
1. Quantization happens once per activation (when writing to memory)
2. That quantized activation is then reused across many weight multiplications
3. The cost of quantization is amortized over thousands of MAC operations

The paper does provide end-to-end energy estimates for full model inference (Figure 3), showing the savings scale with model size as the proportion of `nn.Linear` operations increases.

## Summary

The dramatic savings stem from replacing expensive floating-point operations (especially multiplication, which dominates energy costs) with simple integer addition. This isn't a software trick—it's exploiting the physical reality that floating-point multiplication requires significantly more circuit switching than integer addition.

### Why can BitNet match FP16 performance starting only at 3B parameters?
Question:
The results show that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision LLaMA LLM starting from 3B model size, but presumably underperforms at smaller sizes. Why would there be a model size threshold for when quantization-aware training becomes competitive? 

Is this a fundamental property of ternary quantization—smaller models don't have enough capacity to compensate for the precision loss? Or is this an artifact of the training setup? The paper doesn't explicitly explain why 3B is the "tipping point."
Answer:
The paper doesn't explicitly explain the 3B threshold, but the underlying reason can be understood through the interplay between **quantization error** and **model capacity**.

Ternary quantization constrains each weight to only three possible values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, compared to the $\approx 65{,}000$ distinct values representable in FP16. This introduces a fixed "quantization penalty"—the model loses fine-grained control over individual weight values. The key insight is that this penalty is roughly **constant** regardless of model size, but the ability to compensate for it **scales** with parameter count.

In smaller models, the limited number of parameters means there isn't enough representational capacity to work around the precision constraint. Every parameter matters, and the coarse granularity of ternary weights becomes a bottleneck. As the paper notes, "the performance gap between BitNet b1.58 and LLaMA LLM narrows as the model size increases"—this is because larger models have excess capacity that can absorb the quantization error through redundancy.

This connects to the broader concept that large neural networks are **over-parameterized**—they contain more parameters than strictly necessary for learning the target function. At small scales, models operate near their capacity limits, so precision loss is costly. At larger scales, the over-parameterization provides a buffer. The 3B threshold is likely the crossover point where this redundancy becomes sufficient to neutralize the quantization penalty.

Additionally, the inclusion of explicit zeros in BitNet b1.58 enables feature filtering, which becomes increasingly valuable in larger models where managing information flow through more layers and heads is critical. The paper mentions this "explicit support for feature filtering...can significantly improve the performance of 1-bit LLMs," and this benefit likely scales with model size.

### How does 8-bit activation quantization avoid zero-point quantization issues?
Question:
The paper mentions that for activations, they "scale to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization," and that this is "more convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization."

I'm confused about what "zero-point quantization" refers to here. Is this related to symmetric vs asymmetric quantization? Why is removing the zero-point advantageous, and what does scaling per token mean exactly? Does this introduce any additional operations during inference?
Answer:
The paper's mention of "zero-point quantization" refers to the offset term used in asymmetric quantization schemes. Let me break this down.

**Symmetric vs Asymmetric Quantization**

In quantization, we map continuous floating-point values to discrete integer values. There are two main approaches:

- **Asymmetric quantization** uses a scale factor $s$ and a zero-point $z$: the quantized value is $q = \mathrm{round}(x/s) + z$. This is necessary when the input range isn't symmetric around zero, or when using unsigned integers (e.g., $[0, 255]$). The zero-point is the integer value that maps back to the floating-point zero.

- **Symmetric quantization** has no zero-point offset—the range is symmetric around zero, like $[-127, 127]$. The quantization simplifies to $q = \mathrm{round}(x/s)$, and dequantization is simply $x \approx q \times s$.

**What the Paper Does**

The original BitNet scaled activations to $[0, Q_b]$, which requires a zero-point since the range doesn't include negative values. BitNet b1.58 instead scales activations to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ (symmetric around zero), eliminating the need for a zero-point entirely.

**Why This is Advantageous**

Removing the zero-point simplifies both the arithmetic and the hardware. In asymmetric quantization, matrix multiplication requires compensating for the zero-point offsets of both the weights and activations—this adds extra operations. With symmetric quantization (no zero-point), the core computation is cleaner: the matrix multiplication directly produces correctly scaled results without additional correction terms. This is what the paper means by "convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization."

**Scaling Per Token**

"Per token" scaling means each token in a sequence gets its own scale factor. For a sequence of length $L$, rather than using one global scale for the entire activation tensor, the model computes $L$ separate scales—one for each token position. This allows each token's activations to optimally utilize the full quantization range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, improving precision.

**Additional Operations**

Yes, this introduces extra computation: finding the maximum absolute value per token and applying the scale factor. However, these are simple reduction operations that are negligible compared to the matrix multiplications dominating LLM inference, which the paper confirms: it "introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments."

### What does the "new scaling law" claimed by BitNet actually mean?
Question:
The abstract and conclusion state that "1.58-bit LLM defines a new scaling law and recipe for training new generations of LLMs." The paper then gives equivalence examples like "13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient than 3B FP16 LLM" in terms of latency, memory, and energy.

But this seems to just be comparing models with different bit-widths at fixed parameter counts. How is this a "new scaling law"? Traditional scaling laws like those from Kaplan et al. relate loss to compute and parameter count. Is BitNet proposing a modification to these relationships, or is this more of an efficiency claim? What would the actual mathematical formulation of this new scaling law be?
Answer:
The "new scaling law" that BitNet claims is not a modification to the traditional scaling laws relating loss to compute and parameter count. Rather, it's a **new relationship between model performance and inference cost** (latency, memory, energy) that fundamentally changes the trade-off landscape.

## What Traditional Scaling Laws Tell Us

Traditional scaling laws (Kaplan et al., Chinchilla) establish power-law relationships between training compute/parameters and model performance. These tell us that to improve performance, we must increase model size—and traditionally, this means proportionally increasing inference costs. A 13B FP16 model costs roughly $4\times$ more in memory and latency than a 3B model.

## What BitNet Actually Shows

The key experimental finding is stated clearly in the paper: "BitNet b1.58 can match full precision baselines in terms of both perplexity and end-task performance, starting from a 3B size." This means at equal parameter counts, the performance gap disappears beyond a threshold.

Combined with the efficiency metrics ($2.71\times$ faster, $3.55\times$ less memory at 3B scale; up to $4.1\times$ faster at 70B scale), this creates a novel scaling relationship. The paper formalizes this through equivalences:

- A **13B BitNet** is more efficient than a **3B FP16 LLM**
- A **70B BitNet** is more efficient than a **13B FP16 LLM**

## The Mathematical Formulation

The implicit scaling law can be expressed as: for a fixed inference budget $B$ (in latency, memory, or energy), the achievable model size follows:

$$N_{\text{BitNet}}(B) \approx k \cdot N_{\text{FP16}}(B)$$

where $k \approx 4$ based on the reported equivalences.

Since performance scales with model size, at fixed inference cost $B$:

$$\text{Performance}_{\text{BitNet}}(B) \approx \text{Performance}_{\text{FP16}}(B) \cdot f(k)$$

where $f(k) > 1$ captures the benefit of having $k \times$ more parameters within the same budget.

## Why This Matters

This decouples two quantities that were previously locked together: you can now scale model size without proportionally scaling inference cost. The $71.4\times$ energy savings for matrix multiplication (from replacing FP16 multiply-add with INT8 addition) and the reduced memory bandwidth pressure are what enable this shift.

In essence, BitNet's "scaling law" reframes the optimization objective: instead of asking "what's the best model I can train for compute budget $C$?", it enables asking "what's the best model I can deploy for inference budget $B$?"—and the answer now allows for substantially larger models than before.

### Why use a 2-bit kernel for 1.58-bit weights?
Question:
In the Memory and Latency section, the paper states that "Both latency and memory were measured with a 2-bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization."

If the weights are ternary (requiring $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits), why use a 2-bit kernel instead of something that matches the actual bit-width? Is there no hardware support or optimized kernel for packed ternary values? Does this mean the actual memory footprint is closer to 2 bits per weight rather than 1.58 bits, or is the storage separate from the compute kernel?
Answer:
The use of a 2-bit kernel for ternary weights is a practical compromise dictated by current hardware and software infrastructure limitations, not an inherent limitation of the BitNet b1.58 architecture.

The fundamental issue is that modern computing hardware is built around power-of-2 bit widths (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 bits). Ternary values—requiring exactly 3 states $\{-1, 0, 1\}$—don't align with this paradigm. The theoretical minimum of $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits means that packing ternary values efficiently would require complex bit manipulation. For example, you could theoretically pack 5 ternary values into 8 bits (since $3^5 = 243 < 256 = 2^8$), achieving roughly 1.6 bits per value. However, this requires non-trivial packing and unpacking logic that current GPU kernels and hardware accelerators aren't optimized for.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this by stating "there is still room for optimization." The 2-bit kernel from Ladder was used because it's readily available and well-optimized for existing hardware. With a 2-bit representation, each weight consumes 2 bits of memory (one of the four possible bit patterns goes unused), which is approximately 26% overhead over the theoretical minimum. This means the actual measured memory footprint is indeed closer to 2 bits per weight rather than 1.58 bits.

The key insight is that even with this suboptimal kernel, BitNet b1.58 still achieves substantial efficiency gains—$3.55\times$ less memory and $2.71\times$ faster latency at the 3B scale. The paper's point about "room for optimization" signals that future hardware specifically designed for 1-bit LLMs could close this gap, potentially offering even better efficiency through native ternary value support or more sophisticated packing schemes. This is part of the authors' call for "designing specific hardware optimized for 1-bit LLMs" mentioned in the abstract and discussion sections.
