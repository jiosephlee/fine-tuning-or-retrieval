# The Geometry of Finetuning: Why Orthogonality Matters

Large foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion are powerful, but training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. As a result, the ability to efficiently adapt these massive models to new downstream tasks has become a critical area of research. The dominant paradigm for years has been additive finetuning—taking a pretrained weight matrix and adding a small update to it. However, recent research suggests that looking at neural networks through the lens of geometry reveals a more stable path forward.

A key insight driving recent advancements, specifically Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), is that the knowledge within a neural network is encoded not just in the weights themselves, but in the geometric relationships between neurons.

## The Geometry of Neurons

To understand the motivation behind OFT, we first need to look at a layer in a neural network as a collection of vectors. In a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, each column can be viewed as a neuron. These neurons exist in a high-dimensional space, and their configuration defines the model's functionality.

The "semantic knowledge" learned during pretraining is largely captured by the relative positions of these neurons—their pairwise angles. If you imagine the neurons as a constellation of stars, the shape of the constellation (the angles between the stars) represents the knowledge. If you distort these angles, you risk erasing the valuable information the model learned during pretraining.

Standard finetuning methods often modify weights by adding a low-rank matrix (like in LoRA) or by directly updating weights with a small learning rate. While effective, these additive approaches can inadvertently distort the geometric structure. They change the lengths and orientations of neuron vectors, potentially altering those critical pairwise angles.

## Rotation Over Addition

This is where Orthogonal Finetuning takes a different stand. Instead of adding a delta to the weights, OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation.

Mathematically, for a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT updates it to $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix. An orthogonal matrix represents a rotation (or reflection). When you rotate a constellation of stars, the individual stars move, but the shape of the constellation remains intact. The distances between stars and the angles between them are preserved.

By ensuring that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, OFT provably preserves the pairwise angles between neurons throughout the finetuning process. This allows the model to adapt to new tasks while strictly maintaining the relational knowledge from pretraining.

## Stability Through Spectral Preservation

Beyond preserving geometry, the orthogonal approach offers significant benefits for training stability. This can be understood through spectral analysis.

When we decompose a weight matrix using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), we get $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. The singular values (the diagonal elements of $\bm{\Sigma}$) determine the spectral norm of the matrix, which is crucial for training dynamics.

Additive methods change these singular values, which can lead to spectral explosions or vanishing gradients if not carefully managed. In contrast, orthogonal matrices have a singular value of 1. Multiplying $\bm{W}^0$ by $\bm{R}$ results in $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. The singular values—and thus the spectral norm—remain untouched. This preservation of spectral properties contributes to the superior generalization and training stability observed in OFT compared to additive methods like LoRA.

## The Efficiency Challenge

If orthogonal finetuning is so effective at preserving knowledge, why hasn't it been the standard for all model adaptation? The answer lies in parameter efficiency.

A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. For modern models where dimensionality $d$ can be in the thousands, this is computationally impractical. Early versions of OFT used a block-diagonal structure to reduce parameters, but this forced an artificial independence between groups of dimensions, limiting the model's expressiveness.

This efficiency-expressivity trade-off is the focus of the paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization." The authors propose Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a method that uses butterfly factorization—inspired by the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm. BOFT composes a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse matrices.

This approach manages to achieve the best of both worlds: it retains the geometric preservation and stability benefits of OFT while reducing the parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. It allows practitioners to rotate the weight space efficiently, ensuring that the "constellation" of neurons stays perfectly intact even as the model learns new tasks.



# From Fast Fourier Transforms to Better AI: The Butterfly Effect

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized machine learning, but their sheer size makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. As a result, the ability to efficiently adapt these pretrained models to new tasks—known as finetuning—has become a critical area of research. While methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) are popular, a technique called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has shown promise for better stability and generalization. However, OFT comes with a heavy computational price tag.

A recent paper introduces a novel method called **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, which draws unexpected inspiration from a classic algorithm in signal processing: the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). By borrowing the "butterfly" structure from FFT, BOFT drastically reduces the parameter count from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ without sacrificing the model's expressivity.

## The Parameter Efficiency Problem in OFT

To understand the innovation, we first need to look at the problem it solves. OFT operates on a principled idea: to preserve the knowledge learned during pretraining, we should maintain the geometric relationships between neurons. Specifically, it ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons remain constant during finetuning.

To achieve this, OFT applies an orthogonal matrix transformation to the pretrained weights. If the weight matrix is $\bm{W}^0$, OFT transforms it to $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix. The catch is that a standard dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. For modern models where $d$ can be in the thousands, this is far too many parameters to train efficiently.

Previous solutions, such as block-diagonal OFT, reduced this by dividing the matrix into smaller blocks. While this lowers the parameter count, it introduces a problematic assumption: it treats different dimensions of a neuron as independent groups, effectively limiting how information can flow between dimensions. This led researchers to ask: *Can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix efficiently?*

## An Information Transmission Perspective

The authors tackle this problem by reimagining matrix multiplication as an information transmission problem. Imagine a grid of nodes where information must flow from one side to the other. Constructing a dense matrix is equivalent to ensuring that every input node can send information to every output node.

If we use a single dense matrix, we need $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ connections (edges). However, if we factorize that matrix into a product of sparse matrices, we can route information through intermediate steps. The key insight is that we can achieve full connectivity—where every node eventually talks to every other node—using a structured sequence of sparse connections.

This is where the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm enters the picture.

## The Butterfly Effect

The FFT algorithm revolutionized signal processing by reducing the complexity of computing the Discrete Fourier Transform from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$. It achieves this through a recursive "butterfly" structure, which allows global changes in the frequency domain to be computed via local, structured operations.

The authors realized that the topology of the butterfly network is perfect for the information transmission problem in OFT. In a butterfly network, nodes are connected in a specific pattern of recursive stages. This structure ensures that information propagates globally across the dimensions with a minimal number of edges.

BOFT applies this by factorizing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of sparse orthogonal matrices:
$$ \bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)} $$
Here, each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a "butterfly component"—a block-diagonal matrix composed of small orthogonal blocks. By chaining these sparse components together, BOFT constructs a dense orthogonal transformation.

## From $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$

The mathematical elegance of the butterfly structure lies in its efficiency. In BOFT, we can construct a dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ effective trainable parameters.

*   **Original OFT:** Requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a dense matrix.
*   **Block-diagonal OFT:** Reduces parameters but sacrifices expressivity by isolating dimension groups.
*   **BOFT:** Achieves the density of the original OFT but with the parameter efficiency of the butterfly structure.

The method essentially trades computation time for parameter space. By representing the transformation as a product of matrices, the model performs sequential multiplications during training, but the number of values it needs to learn is significantly lower.

## Why This Matters

Beyond the impressive reduction in parameters, the butterfly structure offers theoretical advantages. Unlike the block-diagonal approach used in earlier OFT versions, the butterfly structure can perfectly recover many classic linear transforms, such as the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Hadamard transform. This implies that BOFT provides a much richer hypothesis class for finetuning.

Furthermore, because the underlying structure is composed of orthogonal matrices, BOFT preserves the spectral properties of the pretrained weights. This ensures training stability and helps prevent the model from deviating too far from its pretrained knowledge.

By bridging concepts from classic algorithm design (FFT) with modern deep learning finetuning, BOFT offers a compelling path forward. It allows researchers and practitioners to adapt massive models with significantly fewer trainable parameters, proving that sometimes, old algorithms are the key to solving new problems.



# Outperforming LoRA: A New Benchmark for Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

## Introduction

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have transformed what's possible in AI, but their massive scale creates a practical problem: training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive. With models like GPT-3 containing around 175 billion parameters, efficiently adapting these pretrained models to new tasks has become a critical research area. For a while, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) has been the industry standard for parameter-efficient finetuning. However, a new method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is challenging that status quo, offering a fundamentally different approach that preserves more of what makes pretrained models powerful.

## The Current Standard: How LoRA Works

To understand the significance of BOFT, let's first examine how LoRA operates. LoRA's key insight is elegant: instead of updating all parameters in a weight matrix, it adds a low-rank matrix update. If $\bm{W}^0$ represents your pretrained weight matrix, LoRA updates it as:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$$

where $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are low-rank matrices. This reduces trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(dr)$, where $r$ is the rank and $d$ is the dimension.

While LoRA is effective, it has fundamental limitations. Its additive structure doesn't preserve the relational geometry within weight matrices—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons. This matters because these geometric relationships encode semantic knowledge from pretraining, and disrupting them can lead to training instability and poorer generalization.

## A Different Approach: Orthogonal Finetuning

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a fundamentally different route. Instead of adding a low-rank update, OFT multiplies pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$$

where $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This orthogonal transformation provably preserves pairwise angles between neurons, maintaining the semantic structure learned during pretraining.

The original OFT, however, had a parameter efficiency problem. A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—no better than full finetuning. The authors addressed this by using block-diagonal matrices, but this introduced new problems: it created artificial groupings of neuron dimensions and couldn't approximate classic linear transforms like the Fourier transform.

## The Innovation: Butterfly Factorization

This is where BOFT makes its key contribution. The insight is deceptively simple yet powerful: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix from a product of sparse orthogonal matrices?

The researchers approached this through an **information transmission framework**. Imagine a grid where information must flow from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes. Each sparse matrix in a factorization represents connections between adjacent levels of this grid. For the final matrix to be dense, every source must have a path to every receiver.

The breakthrough came from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm. Its butterfly structure achieves exactly this goal: it enables information from every source to reach every receiver using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, far fewer than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ needed for dense connections.

In BOFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed as:

$$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component. The notation BOFT($m$,$b$) specifies the number of factors $m$ and block size $b$. When $m = 1$, BOFT reduces to the original block-diagonal OFT; when $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, it produces a dense orthogonal matrix with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters.

## Key Differences Between BOFT and LoRA

The two methods differ in fundamental ways that matter for practical performance:

**Update Mechanism.** LoRA uses additive updates ($\bm{W}^0 + \Delta\bm{W}$), while BOFT uses multiplicative updates ($\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$). This multiplicative approach ensures the transformation preserves the pretrained weight matrix's structure.

**Spectral Properties.** BOFT preserves the spectral norm of pretrained weights. Through singular value decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, applying an orthogonal matrix gives $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. The singular values—and crucially, the spectral norm—remain unchanged. This preservation has been shown to significantly benefit training stability and generalization.

**Expressivity.** The butterfly structure can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms, including the Discrete Fourier Transform and Hadamard transform. The block-diagonal structure in original OFT cannot approximate any of these. BOFT therefore introduces a beneficial inductive bias aligned with well-studied mathematical structures.

**Information Flow.** The information transmission view reveals that BOFT ensures every input dimension can influence every output dimension through multiple interconnected paths, rather than isolating dimension groups as block-diagonal approaches do.

## Empirical Evidence: BOFT in Action

The researchers validated BOFT across diverse tasks and model architectures. Simulation studies showed that BOFT achieves impressive expressivity with fewer parameters. For example, BOFT(9,2) achieves better expressivity than BOFT(1,16)—equivalent to block-diagonal OFT—while using far fewer parameters. The paper formally proves that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.

Across all tested domains, BOFT outperformed current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin. The researchers applied BOFT to large vision transformers for computer vision tasks, large language models for NLP, and text-to-image diffusion models. This represents the first time orthogonal finetuning has been successfully applied beyond controllable text-to-image generation, demonstrating BOFT's potential as a generic finetuning framework.

## Practical Considerations for Implementation

For those looking to adopt BOFT, the paper provides important implementation details:

**Identity Initialization.** BOFT initializes all butterfly components as identity matrices, ensuring training starts from the exact pretrained model. This prevents the finetuned model from deviating too far from its pretrained foundation too quickly.

**Multiplicative Dropout.** Standard dropout doesn't work with multiplicative updates, so the researchers designed a novel multiplicative dropout. This randomly replaces selected butterfly components and diagonal blocks with identity matrices to prevent overfitting.

**Zero Inference Latency.** After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained weights. This means there's no additional computational overhead during inference—a crucial advantage for production deployments.

## Conclusion

LoRA has served the machine learning community well, but BOFT presents a mathematically principled alternative that preserves more of what makes pretrained models powerful. By reimagining parameter-efficient finetuning through orthogonal transformations and butterfly factorization, it achieves superior parameter efficiency while maintaining the geometric structure of pretrained weights.

The information transmission framework introduced in the paper offers a new lens for thinking about finetuning—one that could inspire future work drawing from signal processing and computer networking. As foundation models continue to grow, methods like BOFT that balance efficiency, expressivity, and generalization will become increasingly vital for the field.



# Visualizing Neural Networks as Information Highways

Large foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion are technological marvels, but adapting them to new tasks is a growing challenge. Training them from scratch is prohibitively expensive, so researchers rely on parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT). One powerful technique is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which preserves the "knowledge" stored in pretrained weights by applying orthogonal transformations. However, OFT has a bottleneck: it requires a massive number of trainable parameters to create these transformations. To solve this, a recent paper introduces a fascinating new perspective, viewing matrix construction as an information transmission problem.

## The Information Transmission Framework

At the heart of the paper is a novel framework for visualizing how matrices interact. Imagine you want to create a dense orthogonal matrix—essentially a complex transformation that mixes all inputs with all outputs. Traditionally, constructing a $d$-dimensional dense matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This is computationally heavy.

The authors propose viewing this matrix construction as a graph. Specifically, they frame it as an information transmission problem on a grid. In this view, generating a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ by multiplying a sequence of sparse matrices $\bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$ corresponds to transmitting information across a grid of nodes.

Imagine a grid with $d$ rows and $m+1$ columns. The goal is to get a piece of information from any node in the first column to every node in the last column. If we treat the non-zero elements in our matrices as "edges" connecting nodes between adjacent columns, the problem changes. We are no longer just optimizing abstract weights; we are designing a network topology that ensures dense connectivity.

## The Cost of Isolation: Block-Diagonal Approaches

Previous versions of OFT tried to save parameters by using a block-diagonal structure. In our network analogy, this is akin to dividing the grid into isolated neighborhoods. Information can flow freely within a small block of nodes, but there are no roads connecting different blocks.

While this saves parameters, it introduces a problematic inductive bias. It assumes that dimensions of a neuron can be neatly partitioned into independent groups. This limits the expressivity of the model, as it cannot easily approximate classic linear transforms that require global mixing, like the discrete Fourier transform.

## The Butterfly Effect

To bridge the gap between parameter efficiency and dense connectivity, the authors draw inspiration from an unexpected source: the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT relies on a "butterfly" structure to exchange information efficiently.

In a butterfly network, nodes are connected in a specific pattern that allows information to traverse the entire network in $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ steps. Instead of building direct roads between every city (dense matrix) or isolating cities into districts (block-diagonal), the butterfly structure builds a sophisticated highway system. It allows information to flow from any source to any destination efficiently, requiring only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges (parameters) rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

## Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Applying this insight, the authors propose Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT parameterizes the orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices, each corresponding to a stage in the butterfly network.

The mathematical elegance here is striking. The forward pass becomes:

$$
\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}
$$

Here, $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight and $\bm{R}$ is the orthogonal matrix constructed from butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$.

By structuring the matrix this way, BOFT achieves the best of both worlds:
*   **Parameter Efficiency:** It uses significantly fewer parameters than a dense OFT matrix and is often more efficient than block-diagonal OFT with similar expressivity.
*   **Expressivity:** Unlike block-diagonal structures, the butterfly network can recover classic linear transforms like the Fourier transform and Hadamard transform.
*   **Stability:** Because the transformation is orthogonal, it preserves the spectral norm of the original weights, leading to stable training.

## Why This Matters

This graphical perspective transforms a purely algebraic problem into a topological one. It provides a clear set of desiderata: we want dense connectivity (every node can talk to everyone) with minimum free edges (fewest possible parameters).

BOFT doesn't just save memory; it offers a better interpolation between the pretrained model and the finetuned task. By constraining the finetuning to a structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT implicitly regularizes the model, preventing overfitting while still allowing the network to learn complex adaptations.

The paper validates this approach across large vision transformers, language models, and diffusion models. By visualizing neural finetuning as information flow, the authors have opened up a new avenue for designing efficient algorithms—borrowing wisdom from signal processing and computer networking to solve deep learning's scaling problems.
