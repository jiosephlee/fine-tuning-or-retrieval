# Introduction to Foundation Model Adaptation

# The Era of Large Foundation Models

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a dramatic transformation with the emergence of large foundation models. Models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, showcasing the power of training on massive datasets and scaling up model parameters. These foundation models serve as powerful starting points that can be adapted to a wide variety of downstream tasks, representing a paradigm shift in how we approach machine learning problems.

This revolution in capability, however, has come with a significant cost. The relationship between model performance and model size has become increasingly apparent. For instance, GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters, and this is just one example among many increasingly large models. The rapid increase in the performance of such models is paired with a dramatic increase in the number of parameters, making these models what we now call "ubiquitous" foundation models. They are becoming the backbone of modern AI applications, serving as the base upon which specialized systems are built.

The scale of these models fundamentally changes the research and deployment landscape. When models were smaller, the standard practice was to train them from scratch for each new task. With foundation models, this approach has become prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and financial investment required to train a model like GPT-3 or Stable Diffusion from scratch place such endeavors beyond the reach of most researchers and practitioners. This reality has necessitated a fundamental rethinking of how we approach the development of AI systems, shifting the focus from training models from scratch to adapting existing pretrained models to new tasks.

# The Challenge of Adaptation: Training Costs vs. Downstream Tasks

The prohibitive cost of training foundation models from scratch presents a fundamental challenge: how can we leverage the power of these models for specific downstream tasks without the enormous expense of full retraining? This question has become central to modern machine learning research and practice. Broad progress in the field now depends critically on the ability to adapt existing foundation models to downstream tasks efficiently.

The challenge is multifaceted. On one hand, foundation models encode vast amounts of knowledge from their pretraining on massive datasets. This knowledge includes linguistic understanding, visual representations, and complex patterns that would be extremely costly to learn anew. On the other hand, downstream tasks often have specific requirements that differ from the general pretraining objectives. The task of adapting a general-purpose model to a specialized application while preserving its pretrained knowledge forms the core challenge of efficient model adaptation.

The fundamental principle behind successful model adaptation is ensuring that the pretrained model and the adapted model remain similar based on certain measurements. This similarity constraint serves to preserve the pretraining knowledge. For example, many current model finetuning methods employ a small learning rate, an approach that encourages a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned model states. While this heuristic approach can be effective, it lacks a principled theoretical foundation. Given the structured nature of weight matrices in neural networks, more principled approaches seek to preserve the relational information encoded in these matrices—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons. This insight has led to novel adaptation frameworks that provide stronger theoretical guarantees about knowledge preservation during the adaptation process.

# Overview of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) Methods

The field of parameter-efficient finetuning has emerged as a vibrant area of research, driven by the necessity to adapt large foundation models without the prohibitive costs of full model training. The core idea behind PEFT methods is straightforward yet powerful: instead of updating all parameters of a massive model, we update only a small subset or introduce a small number of new parameters while keeping the majority of the pretrained model frozen.

Among the many PEFT methods that have been proposed, reparameterization-based methods are particularly relevant to the study of orthogonal finetuning. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) represents one of the most influential approaches in this category. LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices, achieving promising performance on natural language tasks while requiring significantly fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning. In LoRA, the rank of the added matrices is typically set to a constant value, and several subsequent methods have proposed dynamically adjusting this rank for different layers to better allocate the parameter budget.

The popularity of low-rank weight reparameterization stems from its simplicity and effectiveness. This approach has gained widespread adoption across various applications and model types. However, reparameterization-based methods extend beyond low-rank approaches. Some methods have drawn inspiration from how hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization, leading to orthogonal finetuning approaches that offer an alternative paradigm to low-rank adaptation. The key insight is that orthogonal transformations can provide stronger generalization and more stable training than additive low-rank updates, though this often comes at the cost of more trainable parameters.

The landscape of PEFT methods continues to evolve, with researchers exploring various strategies to balance parameter efficiency with model expressivity. The fundamental trade-off in all these methods remains the same: how can we achieve strong performance on downstream tasks while modifying as few parameters as possible? This question drives ongoing research and has led to diverse approaches, each with its own strengths and limitations.

# Model Finetuning vs. Adapter Tuning vs. Prompt Tuning

There are primarily three approaches to performing efficient task adaptation, each with distinct characteristics and trade-offs. Understanding these three paradigms is essential for appreciating where orthogonal finetuning fits within the broader landscape of adaptation methods.

Model finetuning represents the most straightforward approach. In this paradigm, the model architecture remains unchanged, and a subset of the model parameters is updated during the finetuning process. This approach distinguishes itself through its simplicity and power, and importantly, it introduces no inference latency. Because the model structure is preserved and only parameter values change, the finetuned model operates at the same speed as the original pretrained model during deployment. This lack of inference overhead makes model finetuning particularly attractive for production systems where latency matters.

Adapter tuning takes a different approach. Rather than modifying the original model parameters, this method adds additional trainable parameters to the original model. These adapter modules are inserted into the pretrained model and trained for the downstream task while the original parameters remain frozen. This approach offers modularity—different adapters can be trained for different tasks and swapped as needed—but comes at the cost of increased model complexity and potential inference latency due to the added adapter layers.

Prompt tuning represents yet another paradigm. In this approach, additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. These tokens are learned to guide the model toward the desired behavior for the downstream task. Prompt tuning is particularly elegant because it leaves the model parameters entirely untouched, but it may require careful design and can be sensitive to initialization and hyperparameter choices.

Among these three approaches, model finetuning stands out for its combination of simplicity, effectiveness, and lack of inference overhead. The methods discussed in this book, including Orthogonal Finetuning and Orthogonal Butterfly, fall within the model finetuning paradigm. They update the model through multiplicative transformations applied to the weight matrices, preserving the efficiency advantages of model finetuning while introducing principled constraints that help preserve pretrained knowledge.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

Orthogonal Finetuning represents a principled approach to model adaptation that draws on theoretical insights about how knowledge is encoded in neural network weights. The fundamental insight behind OFT is that the pairwise angles between neurons in a layer encode meaningful relational information that should be preserved during finetuning. This insight connects to research showing that hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization in neural networks.

Concretely, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ represent the input and output vectors, respectively. This multiplicative update contrasts with LoRA's additive approach of updating weights with a low-rank matrix. The key mathematical property is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles: when neurons (column vectors of the weight matrix) are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, the angles between them remain unchanged.

To achieve a proper starting point for finetuning, OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. This zero initialization ensures that the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model state, preventing large deviations at the start of training. To guarantee that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the finetuning process, OFT employs Cayley parameterization. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization ensures that $\bm{R}$ is always orthogonal regardless of how the trainable parameters in $\bm{Q}$ are updated.

The motivation for applying orthogonal transformation to finetune the weight matrix is deeply rooted in the goal of preserving semantic knowledge from pretraining. By maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons, OFT provably preserves the relational structure encoded in the weight matrices. This principled approach has demonstrated strong generalizability and convergence for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, showing that the theoretical benefits translate to practical improvements in model adaptation.

# The Trade-off in OFT: Parameter Efficiency vs. Expressivity

Despite the strong theoretical foundation and empirical performance of Orthogonal Finetuning, it faces a significant practical challenge: the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which can be prohibitive when adapting large models where weight matrices have thousands or tens of thousands of dimensions.

To address this parameter efficiency concern, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and the block dimensions satisfy $br=d$. This block-diagonal approach reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, where $b$ is the block size. The smaller the block size $b$, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes.

However, this parameter efficiency comes at a substantial price. The block-diagonal structure introduces an assumption that the dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ groups, with dimensions in different groups transformed independently using different orthogonal matrices. This fixed sparsity pattern creates several problems. First, it reduces expressiveness: the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms. Second, the independent transformation of blocks may introduce undesirable inductive biases, as there is no principled reason to divide neuron dimensions into groups based on their indices. Third, the question of how to find a good sparsity pattern remains largely unexplored—why should dimensions 1 through $b$ form one group, dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$ form another, and so on?

This trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity creates a fundamental tension in OFT. A block-diagonal OFT with block size $b=d$ generates a dense orthogonal matrix with full expressivity but requires many parameters. Reducing the block size improves parameter efficiency but sacrifices the ability to model complex transformations that involve interactions across different dimension groups. The goal of achieving a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining parameter efficiency seems infeasible at first glance, since generating such a matrix would naively require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This apparent impossibility raises a natural question: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency? The answer, as we will explore in subsequent chapters, lies in composing dense orthogonal matrices from multiple factorized sparse matrices through the elegant structure of butterfly factorization.

# Theoretical Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning

# Preserving Relational Information: Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The fundamental challenge in finetuning large foundation models lies in preserving the valuable knowledge acquired during pretraining while adapting the model to new downstream tasks. When a model is pretrained on massive datasets, it learns rich representations encoded in the relationships between neurons across its layers. These relationships, particularly the geometric arrangements of neurons in the weight space, capture essential semantic information that enables the model's remarkable generalization capabilities. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) emerges from a principled consideration of how to preserve these relationships during adaptation.

At the heart of OFT's design philosophy is the insight that the relational information between neurons—specifically, the pairwise angles between them—encodes crucial semantic knowledge. Consider a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ in a pretrained model, where each column vector represents a neuron. The angle between any two neurons $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$ is determined by their dot product relative to their magnitudes: $\cos\theta_{ij} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|}$. These pairwise angles define the relative positioning of neurons in the high-dimensional weight space, forming a geometric structure that reflects the semantic relationships learned during pretraining.

The motivation for preserving these pairwise angles stems from research connecting hyperspherical energy to model generalization. When neurons maintain their angular relationships, the semantic knowledge encoded in their relative orientations remains intact. This is in contrast to approaches that modify the weight matrices without explicit constraints on the geometric structure, which risk disrupting the carefully learned representations. The key insight is that by transforming all neurons in the same layer with an identical orthogonal transformation, the pairwise angles between neurons are mathematically guaranteed to remain unchanged throughout the finetuning process.

To understand why orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, consider what happens when an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is applied to the weight matrix. The transformed neurons become $\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j$. The angle between the transformed neurons satisfies: $\cos\theta'_{ij} = \frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j)}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \cos\theta_{ij}$, where the second equality uses the defining property of orthogonal matrices that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ and that orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms. This mathematical guarantee ensures that the semantic knowledge encoded in the geometric structure of the pretrained weights is preserved regardless of how the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ changes during finetuning.

# Mathematical Formulation of OFT

Having established the motivation for orthogonal transformations, we now turn to the precise mathematical formulation of Orthogonal Finetuning. The core idea is deceptively simple yet powerful: instead of directly modifying the pretrained weight matrix through additive updates (as in methods like LoRA), OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation that preserves the geometric structure while enabling adaptation to downstream tasks.

Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In the original forward pass, given an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the output is computed as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the output vector. OFT modifies this forward pass by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms the weight matrix before the computation. The modified forward pass becomes: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. This formulation ensures that every neuron (column vector) in the weight matrix is transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, thereby guaranteeing the preservation of pairwise angles.

The distinction between OFT's multiplicative approach and LoRA's additive approach is fundamental. LoRA, which has gained tremendous popularity in parameter-efficient finetuning, updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, where $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ with rank $r \ll \min(d, n)$. While this additive approach achieves parameter efficiency through the low-rank structure, it does not explicitly preserve the geometric structure of the pretrained weights. The additive perturbation can arbitrarily modify the pairwise angles between neurons, potentially disrupting the semantic knowledge encoded in the weight geometry.

In contrast, OFT's multiplicative approach treats the adaptation problem as finding an appropriate rotation (or reflection) of the neuron space. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can rotate all neurons together while maintaining their relative positions. This is analogous to rotating an entire geometric structure as a rigid body—no matter how you rotate it, the internal angular relationships remain unchanged. The adaptability comes from the ability to choose which rotation is most suitable for the downstream task, while the guarantee of angle preservation ensures that the pretrained knowledge is not corrupted.

# Cayley Transform for Orthogonal Parameterization

A critical challenge in implementing OFT is ensuring that the learned matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire finetuning process. Simply parameterizing $\bm{R}$ directly as a $d \times d$ matrix and applying standard gradient descent would not guarantee orthogonality. Even if $\bm{R}$ were initialized as an orthogonal matrix, gradient updates would quickly break the orthogonality constraint. This necessitates a parameterization scheme that inherently constrains the parameter space to the set of orthogonal matrices.

The solution employed by OFT is the Cayley transform, a classical mathematical construction that provides a smooth mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices. A skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ satisfies $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, meaning all diagonal entries are zero (since $Q_{ii} = -Q_{ii}$ implies $Q_{ii} = 0$) and the off-diagonal entries come in pairs with opposite signs (since $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$). The Cayley transform defines the orthogonal matrix as: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. This parameterization guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal for any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ for which the inverse exists.

To verify that the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix, observe that: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = [(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^\top)^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}^\top)][(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}]$. Using the skew-symmetry property $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, this becomes: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Since $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ commute (their product in either order equals $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$), we have: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$. This proves that the Cayley transform produces orthogonal matrices.

The practical advantage of the Cayley parameterization is that it reduces the optimization problem from learning $d^2$ parameters (for a dense $d \times d$ matrix) to learning only $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ parameters (for a skew-symmetric matrix). However, this is still quadratic in $d$, which remains prohibitively expensive for large models. Additionally, the Cayley transform provides a natural way to achieve zero initialization: by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as a zero matrix, the resulting orthogonal matrix becomes $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, the identity matrix. This ensures that the finetuning process starts exactly from the pretrained model, preventing sudden deviations from the pretrained weights at the start of training.

# The High Dimensionality Problem of Orthogonal Matrices

Despite the elegant theoretical properties of orthogonal finetuning, a practical obstacle emerges when scaling to modern foundation models: the high dimensionality of weight matrices leads to a prohibitive number of trainable parameters. A dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters even with efficient parameterizations like the Cayley transform. For context, consider that large language models have hidden dimensions $d$ ranging from thousands to tens of thousands, making direct orthogonal parameterization impractical.

To illustrate the severity of this problem, consider a single linear layer in a model with dimension $d = 4096$ (a common size for models like GPT-3's smaller variants). A dense orthogonal matrix of this size would require approximately $\frac{d(d-1)}{2} \approx 8.4$ million trainable parameters using the Cayley parameterization. A typical transformer layer contains multiple such linear transformations (attention projections, feed-forward networks), and modern foundation models have dozens to hundreds of layers. The total parameter count for orthogonal finetuning would quickly explode, defeating the purpose of parameter-efficient adaptation.

This problem highlights a fundamental tension in the design of parameter-efficient finetuning methods. On one hand, we desire the strong theoretical guarantees provided by orthogonal transformations: preservation of geometric structure, spectral norm stability, and principled weight updates. On the other hand, we need methods that scale gracefully to models with billions of parameters, where even a single layer's orthogonal matrix would be impractically large. This tension motivates the search for structured parameterizations that can reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the benefits of orthogonal finetuning.

The natural question that arises is whether there exist parameterizations of orthogonal matrices that require far fewer than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters while still providing sufficient expressivity for downstream task adaptation. The answer lies in introducing structure into the orthogonal matrix itself—a strategy that trades off some expressivity for significant gains in parameter efficiency. The challenge is to identify structures that reduce the parameter count without introducing undesirable biases that would harm the model's adaptation capability.

# Block-Diagonal OFT: Structure and Limitations

The original OFT paper introduces a block-diagonal structure to address the high dimensionality problem. The key insight is that instead of learning a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, we can decompose it into a block-diagonal form: $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a smaller orthogonal matrix and $b \cdot r = d$. This structure significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, where $b$ is the block size.

To understand the parameter savings, consider a $d \times d$ matrix with block-diagonal structure of block size $b$. The total number of trainable parameters is $r \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d}{b} \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2} \approx \frac{bd}{2}$ for large $b$. When $b \ll d$, this represents a dramatic reduction in parameters. For example, with $d = 4096$ and $b = 16$, the block-diagonal OFT requires approximately $32,768$ parameters, compared to $8.4$ million for the dense case—a reduction of over 99%.

The block-diagonal structure can be understood as dividing the $d$ dimensions of each neuron into $r$ groups and applying independent orthogonal transformations to each group. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ transforms dimensions $(i-1) \cdot b + 1$ through $i \cdot b$ while leaving other dimensions unchanged within that block's scope. The overall transformation is then the composition of these independent block transformations. When $b = d$ (a single block), block-diagonal OFT reduces to the original dense OFT. When $b = 1$, the orthogonal matrix collapses to the identity (with possible sign flips), offering no adaptation capability. The block size $b$ thus controls a trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity.

However, the block-diagonal structure introduces significant limitations. Most critically, it cannot generate a dense orthogonal matrix regardless of the parameterization used for individual blocks. The sparsity pattern is fixed: entries that connect different blocks are always zero, meaning information cannot flow between dimensions in different groups. This severely limits the expressiveness of the transformation. Furthermore, the block-diagonal structure makes it impossible to approximate many classic linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform or discrete cosine transform, which require dense connectivity between all dimensions.

# Inductive Biases in Block-Diagonal Structures

The block-diagonal structure, while achieving parameter efficiency, introduces an implicit inductive bias that may not align with the structure of the adaptation problem. This bias manifests in several concerning ways that can limit the effectiveness of finetuning.

The most significant inductive bias is the assumption that dimensions of a neuron should be grouped by their indices. The block-diagonal structure implicitly assumes that dimensions $1$ through $b$ form a meaningful group, dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$ form another group, and so on. However, this grouping is entirely arbitrary—it makes no semantic sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron based on their positional indices. In learned representations, dimensions do not have inherent groupings that align with this block structure; features are distributed across dimensions in complex, entangled ways that may not respect arbitrary index boundaries.

This arbitrary grouping leads to another problem: dimensions in different groups are transformed independently, meaning the method cannot learn transformations that mix information across the block boundaries. Consider a scenario where two semantically related features happen to fall in different blocks due to their indices—the block-diagonal structure would prevent the model from learning to transform these features in a coordinated way. This separation can hinder adaptation when downstream tasks require global transformations of the neuron space.

The fixed sparsity pattern also reduces expressiveness. The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix has a specific structure—diagonal blocks that are dense, off-diagonal blocks that are all zeros—that cannot be changed during training. This means the hypothesis class of possible transformations is severely constrained. As a concrete example, consider trying to approximate a rotation matrix that mixes all dimensions evenly (like those used in principal component analysis). The block-diagonal structure fundamentally cannot represent such transformations, regardless of how many parameters are allocated to each block.

An additional concern is that the choice of block size $b$ becomes a critical hyperparameter that is difficult to set optimally. Too small a block size yields parameter efficiency but poor expressivity; too large a block size improves expressivity but increases parameters. The paper notes that "how to find a good sparsity pattern remains a mystery"—there is no principled way to determine what block structure would be appropriate for a given downstream task. This stands in contrast to the butterfly structure proposed in BOFT, which provides a more principled approach to constructing dense orthogonal matrices with logarithmic parameter complexity while introducing inductive biases that align with classical fast linear transforms.

# An Information Transmission Framework

## Matrix Factorization as Information Transmission

The quest for parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning leads us to a fundamental question: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without sacrificing parameter efficiency? At first glance, this might seem infeasible, since a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix typically requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. However, the key insight lies in composing a dense orthogonal matrix through the product of multiple sparse matrices. To develop intuition for this approach and to establish a principled framework for designing such factorizations, we introduce an information transmission perspective that reframes the problem of generating dense orthogonal matrices.

Consider the task of generating a dense matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ through the product of $m$ square matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. We can interpret this matrix factorization as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. The motivation stems from a simple yet powerful observation: a $d$-dimensional dense square matrix represents dense connectivity from $d$ nodes to another $d$ nodes. When we examine the individual elements of matrix $\bm{R}$, each element carries semantic meaning in terms of information flow. Specifically, if the element $R_{ij}$ equals zero, this indicates that information from the $j$-th node cannot flow to the $i$-th node. Conversely, if $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, information transmission between these nodes becomes possible.

This interpretation allows us to view the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$ as a sequential process of information exchange. Rather than allowing information to flow directly from source to destination in a single hop, we route it through intermediate stages, with each stage governed by a factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$. The information flows following $\bm{B}_1$ first and $\bm{B}_m$ last, creating a cascade of transformations that ultimately determines which source-destination pairs remain connected. This framework transforms the abstract algebraic problem of matrix factorization into an intuitive graphical problem of network connectivity, enabling us to leverage insights from graph theory and computer networking to design efficient factorizations.

## Grid-Structured Graph Representation of Matrix Multiplication

To make the information transmission framework concrete, we construct a formal correspondence between matrix factorization and graph structure. The graph is the result of unrolling the matrix multiplication, creating a layered grid structure with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where $d$ is the matrix dimension and $m$ is the number of factor matrices. Each layer of nodes corresponds to a stage in the matrix multiplication process.

Within this grid-structured graph, each factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$ serves as the connectivity matrix from the $i$-th level nodes to the $(i+1)$-th level nodes. More specifically, the $(j_1, j_2)$ element of $\bm{B}_i$ encodes the presence or absence of a directed edge from the $j_2$-th node in the $i$-th level to the $j_1$-th node in the $(i+1)$-th level. A zero element indicates no edge exists, while a non-zero element indicates an active connection. This one-to-one correspondence between matrix entries and graph edges provides a powerful visualization tool for understanding how sparsity patterns affect information flow.

As a concrete illustration, consider the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_5 \bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$ for a matrix of dimension $d = 4$. For the final composed matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every node in the first level must be able to transmit information to every node in the sixth level. However, examining intermediate products reveals how information propagates through the network. For instance, considering only $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$, we might find that information from node 1 cannot reach node 3, corresponding to a zero element at position $(3, 1)$ in this intermediate product. This demonstrates that the sparsity pattern of any intermediate product can be directly read from the graph connectivity between the corresponding levels.

The general principle emerges clearly: for a matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ to be dense, the $m$ factorization matrices must correspond to a set of directed edges on a $d \times (m+1)$ grid where each directed edge connects only two nodes between adjacent levels. The constraint that edges span only adjacent levels reflects the sequential nature of matrix multiplication—information cannot skip levels. The connectivity requirement demands that information from every node in the first level can reach every node in the $(m+1)$-th level through some path.

## Desiderata for Efficient Factorization: Dense Connectivity and Minimum Free Edges

The information transmission framework provides clear criteria for designing efficient orthogonal matrix factorizations. To achieve our goal of composing a dense orthogonal matrix using multiple sparse orthogonal matrices with minimal trainable parameters, we establish two fundamental desiderata.

The first desideratum is **dense connectivity**: every node in the first level must have at least one path to every node in the last level. This requirement ensures that the final composed matrix $\bm{R}$ has no zero entries—it is truly dense. Dense connectivity guarantees that every source node can influence every destination node, preserving the full representational capacity we seek. Without dense connectivity, the composed matrix would contain zeros, limiting its expressiveness and potentially introducing undesirable inductive biases.

The second desideratum is **minimum free edges**: the total number of edges should be as small as possible under the orthogonality constraint. This criterion directly addresses parameter efficiency, as each edge beyond those mandated by orthogonality represents a trainable parameter. Minimizing free edges while maintaining dense connectivity captures the essence of efficient factorization—achieving maximal information transmission with minimal parameter cost.

The orthogonality constraint introduces subtle but important restrictions on edge patterns. For each factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be orthogonal (and thus full-rank), we require $d$ edges forming a bijection between all nodes in level $i$ and all nodes in level $(i+1)$. This means the number of edges between adjacent levels must be at least $d$. These $d$ edges are necessary for orthogonality and should not be counted as trainable parameters because their values are constrained—for a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix with exactly $d$ non-zero entries, these entries can only be $\pm 1$, leaving no degrees of freedom for learning.

Beyond this minimal set, orthogonality creates additional dependencies among edges that complicate the counting. For example, in a $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at position $(1,1)$ forces a zero at position $(2,2)$—one missing edge implies another missing edge due to orthogonality constraints. Conversely, adding certain edges may necessitate adding others to maintain orthogonality. These dependencies mean that simply counting edges does not directly translate to counting trainable parameters, but the framework still provides valuable intuition about the relative efficiency of different factorization patterns.

To appreciate the potential efficiency gains, consider that a naive dense connection between two levels requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges, yielding $d^2 - d$ trainable edges for $d = 4$, this equals 12 edges. However, a carefully designed factorization can achieve the same dense connectivity with fewer edges. The example factorization presented in the paper takes only 10 edges in total, fewer than a single dense orthogonal matrix. This demonstrates that matrix factorization can genuinely reduce parameter requirements while maintaining expressivity.

## Analyzing Block-Diagonal Sparsity via Information Flow

The information transmission framework illuminates the limitations of the block-diagonal structure employed in the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method. Block-diagonal OFT parameterizes the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br = d$. This structure reduces the number of trainable parameters by decomposing the high-dimensional orthogonal transformation into independent low-dimensional transformations.

Viewing this structure through the lens of information transmission reveals its fundamental limitation: the block-diagonal structure cannot construct a dense matrix $\bm{R}$. Consider what this means in graphical terms. The block-diagonal sparsity pattern partitions the $d$ nodes into $r$ disjoint groups, with each group containing $b$ nodes. Information can only flow within each group—nodes in different groups remain isolated from each other. This partitioning means that source nodes in one group cannot send information to destination nodes in another group, resulting in large zero blocks in the final composed matrix.

The block-diagonal structure introduces a strong assumption: the dimensions of a neuron (represented as a column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$) are divided into $r$ groups, and dimensions in different groups are transformed independently using different orthogonal matrices. Despite the empirical effectiveness of this approach, this assumption lacks justification. There is no principled reason to divide the dimensions of a neuron into groups based purely on their indices—this arbitrary partitioning may not reflect any meaningful structure in the underlying problem.

Moreover, the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix reduces expressiveness and cannot approximate classic linear transforms. Many important transformations, such as discrete Fourier transforms or discrete cosine transforms, require information exchange across all dimensions—transformations that the block-diagonal structure fundamentally cannot represent. The question of how to find a good sparsity pattern thus remains a mystery, motivating the search for alternative structures that can achieve dense connectivity more naturally.

## The Need for Dense Orthogonal Matrices

The limitations of block-diagonal sparsity motivate our pursuit of dense orthogonal matrices. Dense matrices are desirable for several compelling reasons that extend beyond mere parameter counting.

First, dense orthogonal matrices eliminate the arbitrary partitioning assumption inherent in block-diagonal structures. When dimensions are grouped arbitrarily, we implicitly assert that certain dimensions share more computational affinity than others—a claim that lacks theoretical justification. Dense matrices allow all dimensions to interact, preserving the full relational structure of the original weight matrix without imposing artificial constraints.

Second, dense orthogonal matrices possess greater expressiveness. Theorem 1 establishes that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size, meaning the butterfly matrix represents a more structured subset of the orthogonal group compared to the block-diagonal structure. This expressiveness is not merely theoretical—butterfly structures can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms including the discrete Fourier transform, Hadamard transform, and discrete sine/cosine transform. The block-diagonal structure cannot recover any of these transforms, highlighting a fundamental gap in representational capacity.

Third, dense matrices enable smoother interpolation between different transformations. BOFT uses the butterfly structure to construct a smoother interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). This interpolation property is crucial for finetuning, where we want to balance preserving pretrained knowledge (staying close to identity) with adapting to new tasks (expressivity). The block-diagonal structure, with its hard partitioning, creates discontinuities in this interpolation.

The challenge, of course, is achieving density without sacrificing parameter efficiency. A naive dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, defeating the purpose of parameter-efficient finetuning. This apparent dilemma leads us to the key insight: we can achieve density through clever factorization rather than direct parameterization.

## Trading Computation Time for Parameter Space

The resolution to the density-efficiency trade-off lies in a fundamental insight: the number of trainable parameters can be reduced by trading computation time for space. This trade-off is foundational in computer science, appearing in contexts from algorithm design to hardware architecture. Here, we apply it to matrix parameterization.

The key observation is that representing an orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse matrices shifts the computational burden. Instead of storing a dense matrix with $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, we store multiple sparse matrices, each with $\mathcal{O}(d)$ parameters. However, this compact storage comes with a computational cost: the matrix multiplications must be performed repeatedly during each training iteration. During forward propagation, we must multiply through the sequence of sparse matrices to obtain the effective transformation.

This trade-off is highly favorable for finetuning scenarios. Training time is abundant—models undergo many iterations during finetuning. Memory, on the other hand, is the critical constraint: we want to adapt large models while keeping trainable parameters minimal. By accepting more computation per forward pass, we dramatically reduce storage requirements from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ when using butterfly factorization.

The information transmission framework helps us understand why this trade-off works. A single dense orthogonal matrix represents a "direct" information transmission scheme—all possible source-destination connections are explicitly stored. This is like having a complete graph with all $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges present. Factorization into sparse matrices instead represents an "indirect" routing scheme—information takes multiple hops through intermediate nodes to reach its destination. Each hop requires only local connectivity (sparse matrices), but the composition of multiple hops achieves global connectivity (dense matrix).

The butterfly structure, borrowed from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, represents an optimal design for this routing problem. The butterfly network achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges, far fewer than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required for direct connectivity. For example, when $d = 4$, a naive dense orthogonal matrix requires 12 trainable parameters beyond the mandatory edges, while a butterfly network achieves the same connectivity with only 8 edges. As dimensionality increases, this advantage grows: for large $d$, the butterfly structure offers exponential savings in parameters while maintaining the ability to construct dense matrices.

Importantly, this computational overhead exists only during training. After finetuning, the learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model, eliminating any additional inference latency. The product of sparse matrices yields a single dense matrix that replaces the original weight transformation, ensuring that the efficiency gains during training do not impose costs during deployment.

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Methodology

# Inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

The development of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) draws profound inspiration from one of the most celebrated algorithms in computer science: the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Understanding this connection is essential for grasping why butterfly structures provide an elegant solution to the parameter efficiency problem in orthogonal finetuning.

The Cooley-Tukey algorithm, introduced in 1965, revolutionized signal processing by enabling efficient computation of the discrete Fourier transform. At its core, the algorithm exploits a recursive decomposition strategy: a $d$-point discrete Fourier transform can be recursively reduced to two $\frac{d}{2}$-point discrete Fourier transforms. This divide-and-conquer approach dramatically reduces the computational complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, making Fourier analysis practical for large-scale applications.

The recursive decomposition in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm induces a distinctive topological structure known as the butterfly graph. This name derives from the visual appearance of the computation pattern, where pairs of values are combined in a manner that resembles the wings of a butterfly. The butterfly structure has a remarkable property that directly addresses the challenge faced in orthogonal finetuning: it enables efficient information transmission across all nodes in a network.

To appreciate this connection, consider the fundamental property of Fourier transforms: a local change in the frequency domain causes a global change in the spatial domain. This property is conceptually analogous to the information transmission problem in generating dense orthogonal matrices. When we seek to construct a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, we need every node in the input space to potentially influence every node in the output space. In other words, information from any source node must be able to reach any destination node.

The butterfly network topology achieves exactly this goal with remarkable efficiency. While a naive dense connection between two levels of $d$ nodes each would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges, the butterfly structure accomplishes full connectivity using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. This efficiency stems from the hierarchical, recursive nature of the butterfly graph, where information propagates through multiple stages, with each stage enabling certain pairings of nodes to exchange information.

The paper illustrates this efficiency with a concrete example. A particular factorization shown in Figure 2 of the paper requires $10$ edges to achieve dense connectivity for $d = 4$ nodes, while the butterfly network accomplishes the same connectivity using only $8$ edges. This demonstrates that the butterfly structure is not merely a theoretical curiosity but provides a genuinely more efficient routing of information.

Beyond its computational efficiency, the butterfly structure has established itself as a popular computer network topology for efficient information exchange. This practical application in networking further validates the butterfly structure's suitability for parameter-efficient matrix construction. The shared characteristic between the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT and the butterfly structure is sparsity—both introduce data sparsity into orthogonal matrices to reduce the effective number of trainable parameters. However, the butterfly structure provides a fundamentally different inductive bias that proves more suitable for the task at hand.

# Defining Butterfly Factors and Butterfly Components

The construction of butterfly matrices proceeds through a hierarchical definition that builds from elementary building blocks called butterfly factors to larger composite structures. Understanding this hierarchy is crucial for implementing BOFT effectively.

We begin by defining the butterfly factor, which serves as the fundamental building block. For a dimension $k$ where $k \geq 2$ is a power of $2$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as a block matrix of the following form:

$$\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$$

Here, each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ for $i = 1, 2, 3, 4$ is a vector, and $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_i)$ denotes a diagonal matrix with the elements of $\bm{d}_i$ along its diagonal. The butterfly factor thus has a distinctive sparse structure where non-zero elements appear only in four diagonal blocks, each of size $\frac{k}{2} \times \frac{k}{2}$. This structure is significantly sparser than a full dense matrix, yet retains enough flexibility to enable meaningful transformations.

The butterfly factor can be understood as performing a specific type of mixing between pairs of dimensions. Each diagonal element in $\bm{d}_1$ pairs with a corresponding element in $\bm{d}_2$ to process two dimensions together, and similarly for $\bm{d}_3$ and $\bm{d}_4$. This pairing mechanism is what enables information exchange across dimensions.

Building upon butterfly factors, we introduce the butterfly component. The butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is defined as a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ where each diagonal block is a butterfly factor:

$$\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}\big(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\big)$$

The butterfly component thus places $\frac{d}{k}$ butterfly factors along the diagonal, each operating on a disjoint subset of $k$ dimensions. The total number of non-zero elements in a butterfly component scales as $\mathcal{O}(d)$ when $k$ is fixed, making it highly parameter-efficient.

The paper further generalizes these definitions by introducing block butterfly components. In the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, each entry in the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ (for all $i$) becomes a $b \times b$ matrix rather than a scalar. This generalization allows for more expressive transformations within each position of the butterfly factor. The block butterfly component with $b > 1$ provides additional flexibility while maintaining the overall sparse structure that enables parameter efficiency.

# Constructing Dense Matrices via Butterfly Factorization

The true power of butterfly structures emerges when we compose multiple butterfly components through multiplication. This composition, known as butterfly factorization, enables the construction of dense matrices from sparse factors.

Let $d = 2^N$ be a power of 2. The $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is defined recursively:

$$\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
\end{bmatrix}= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$$

Here, $\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})$ and $\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})$ are two independent $\frac{d}{2}$-dimensional butterfly matrices. The recursive definition reveals that a $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix can be decomposed as the product of $N = \log_2 d$ butterfly components, each operating at a different scale.

The butterfly components in this factorization have a hierarchical structure. The first component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ has the smallest block size of 2, meaning it contains $\frac{d}{2}$ butterfly factors, each mixing pairs of adjacent dimensions. The next component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,4)$ has block size 4, mixing dimensions that are 4 apart. This pattern continues, with each subsequent component mixing dimensions at increasingly larger scales, until the final component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$ operates on all dimensions at once.

This hierarchical mixing pattern is precisely what enables the construction of dense matrices. Consider a single butterfly component with block size $k$. It only allows information to flow between dimensions within each block of size $k$—dimensions in different blocks remain isolated. However, when we multiply multiple butterfly components with different block sizes, information propagates across increasingly larger groups of dimensions. After multiplying all $N$ components, information from any dimension can reach any other dimension through the cascade of mixing operations.

To make this concrete, the information transmission view provides valuable intuition. The butterfly factorization $\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ corresponds to a graph with $d \times (N+1)$ nodes arranged in a grid. Each butterfly component represents the edges connecting adjacent levels of the grid. The specific sparsity pattern of each component ensures that after passing through all $N$ stages, information from every node in the first level can reach every node in the last level—achieving dense connectivity with sparse edges.

# Ensuring Orthogonality in Butterfly Structures

For butterfly matrices to be suitable for orthogonal finetuning, each component must satisfy the orthogonality constraint. This section explains how to construct orthogonal butterfly matrices from orthogonal butterfly factors.

An orthogonal matrix satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. The key insight is that if each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ is orthogonal, then their product is also orthogonal. This follows from the fundamental property that the product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal: if $\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}$ are orthogonal, then $(\bm{AB})^\top(\bm{AB}) = \bm{B}^\top\bm{A}^\top\bm{AB} = \bm{B}^\top\bm{I}\bm{B} = \bm{B}^\top\bm{B} = \bm{I}$.

The challenge thus reduces to ensuring that each butterfly component is orthogonal. We begin with the simplest case: the butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ with block size 2. This component consists of $\frac{d}{2}$ butterfly factors, each of size $2 \times 2$. For a single $2 \times 2$ butterfly factor to be orthogonal, it must satisfy the orthogonality condition. The paper proposes using Cayley parameterization to guarantee orthogonality. The Cayley transform maps a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$) to an orthogonal matrix:

$$\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$$

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, this simplifies to parameterizing a rotation matrix, which is inherently orthogonal. Each $2 \times 2$ block in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ can thus be parameterized as an orthogonal matrix using Cayley transform or equivalently as a 2-dimensional rotation.

For butterfly components with larger block sizes $k > 2$, the paper provides an elegant solution based on permutation. The non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$. This means that by appropriately rearranging rows and columns, any butterfly component can be transformed into a block-diagonal structure with $2 \times 2$ blocks. Since the orthogonality constraint is preserved under permutation, all butterfly components can be parameterized as orthogonal matrices using the same approach.

For the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ with block size $b$, each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix is parameterized to be orthogonal. The orthogonality of these larger blocks is again ensured through Cayley parameterization applied to skew-symmetric matrices of appropriate dimension. The paper notes that the non-zero pattern of other block butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ for $k > 2$ are block-wise permutations of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, allowing them to be similarly transformed into orthogonal matrices.

# BOFT Formulation and Notation: The (m, b) Parameters

Having established the mathematical foundations, we now present the complete BOFT formulation and explain the notation used to specify different BOFT configurations.

The forward pass in BOFT modifies the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ by multiplying it with an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ constructed from butterfly components:

$$\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}$$

where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output vector. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is constructed as:

$$\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

subject to the constraint that each butterfly component is orthogonal:

$$\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top=\bm{I}_d$$

for all $j \in [1, m]$. Here, $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is a shorthand notation for $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m-i+1})$, representing a block butterfly component with block size $2^{m-i+1}$.

The notation BOFT($m$,$b$) specifies the configuration with two key parameters. The parameter $b$ represents the block size used in the butterfly structure, where $b \geq 2$. The parameter $m$ represents the number of butterfly components multiplied together. Different combinations of $m$ and $b$ yield different trade-offs between expressivity and parameter efficiency.

Several special cases deserve attention. When $m = 1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. This is because with only one butterfly component, there is no multi-scale mixing—only independent transformations within each block of size $b$. When $b = d$ (the full dimension), BOFT($1$,$d$) further reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix.

At the other extreme, when $m = \log \frac{2d}{b}$, BOFT uses the full butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ as the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, yielding a dense orthogonal matrix. This configuration maximizes the mixing across dimensions while maintaining parameter efficiency. When $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT achieves its most parameter-efficient dense configuration, using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters to represent a dense orthogonal matrix.

# Complexity Analysis: Reducing Parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$

One of BOFT's most significant advantages is its dramatic reduction in the number of trainable parameters compared to full orthogonal matrices. This section provides a detailed complexity analysis to quantify these savings.

Consider finetuning a linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In BOFT($m$,$b$), each butterfly component contains $\frac{d}{2^{m-i+1}}$ blocks, and each block is a $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal matrix. An orthogonal matrix of size $2b \times 2b$ can be parameterized using $b(2b-1)$ free parameters (since a skew-symmetric matrix of size $2b$ has $\frac{2b(2b-1)}{2} = b(2b-1)$ independent entries).

However, the paper uses a slightly different counting that accounts for the effective trainable parameters. For BOFT($m$,$b$), the number of effective trainable parameters is given by:

$$\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$$

This formula captures the key insight: the parameter count scales linearly with both the dimension $d$ and the number of components $m$, but only grows sublinearly with the block size $b$.

To appreciate the magnitude of this reduction, compare with alternative approaches. The original OFT with a full dense orthogonal matrix uses $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, as a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ degrees of freedom. The block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$ uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters, as it requires $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks, each with $\mathcal{O}(b^2)$ parameters, giving a total of $\mathcal{O}(\frac{d}{b} \cdot b^2) = \mathcal{O}(bd)$.

When we use the butterfly matrix configuration with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT achieves the remarkable complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This represents a substantial improvement over the quadratic $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ complexity of the original OFT, while still enabling the construction of dense orthogonal matrices.

The paper provides empirical validation of this theoretical advantage through approximation experiments. When approximating random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$, BOFT($9$,$2$) achieves better approximation quality than BOFT($1$,$16$) (block-diagonal OFT with block size 16) while using far fewer parameters. Similarly, BOFT($6$,$4$) achieves approximation error comparable to BOFT($2$,$16$) but with significantly fewer parameters. These results confirm that smaller $b$ combined with larger $m$ generally yields better parameter efficiency.

# Implementation Details: Identity Initialization and Multiplicative Dropout

Practical implementation of BOFT requires careful attention to initialization and regularization strategies. This section details two key implementation techniques that ensure stable training and prevent overfitting.

Identity initialization is a critical component of BOFT that ensures the finetuning process begins from the exact pretrained model. The rationale is straightforward: finetuning methods should start with minimal deviation from the pretrained model to preserve the knowledge encoded in the pretrained weights. For BOFT, this is achieved by initializing all butterfly components as identity matrices. Specifically, in the Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ is initialized to zero, which yields $\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. This initialization strategy parallels the zero initialization used in LoRA for the low-rank weight updates, ensuring that at the start of finetuning, the model outputs exactly match the pretrained model outputs.

Multiplicative dropout is a regularization technique specifically designed for BOFT's multiplicative weight update structure. Standard dropout, which randomly zeros elements in additive updates, is not directly applicable to BOFT because the weight update is multiplicative rather than additive. To address this, the paper proposes a multiplicative dropout strategy that exploits the structure of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$.

Since $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, and these components can be easily permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, multiplicative dropout operates at two levels. First, it randomly selects $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components. Second, within each selected component, it randomly selects $p_2$ percent of the diagonal blocks. These selected blocks are then replaced with identity matrices. This multiplicative dropout effectively regularizes the learned orthogonal transformation by randomly "turning off" certain mixing operations during training, encouraging the model to learn more robust transformations that do not overly rely on any specific butterfly component or block.

Together, identity initialization and multiplicative dropout provide essential foundations for successful BOFT training, enabling stable convergence and preventing overfitting in downstream task adaptation.

# Theoretical Analysis and Properties of BOFT

Understanding why a method works is often as important as demonstrating that it works. In this chapter, we delve deep into the theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), examining its expressivity, spectral properties, and the inductive biases that emerge from its butterfly structure. These theoretical insights provide rigorous justification for the empirical effectiveness of BOFT and help us understand when and why it outperforms alternatives.

# Expressivity of BOFT: Approximating Random Orthogonal Matrices

A fundamental question when designing any parameter-efficient finetuning method is whether the restricted parameterization can still adequately express the transformations needed for downstream tasks. For BOFT, this question translates to: can a product of sparse orthogonal matrices approximate a general dense orthogonal matrix? To answer this, we must examine the approximation capabilities of the butterfly structure.

The expressivity of BOFT can be understood through the lens of approximating random dense orthogonal matrices. Consider the task of approximating a randomly generated orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ using BOFT with various configurations of the hyperparameters $m$ (the number of butterfly components) and $b$ (the block size). This simulation provides concrete evidence for the approximation power of the butterfly factorization.

In such experiments, one generates random dense orthogonal matrices using standard methods, such as the QR decomposition of random matrices with independent standard normal entries. The approximation error is then measured as the Frobenius norm difference between the target orthogonal matrix and the BOFT-represented matrix. The results reveal several important patterns that illuminate the expressivity of BOFT.

First, BOFT generally yields superior parameter efficiency compared to block-diagonal OFT. For instance, when comparing BOFT($9$,$2$) with BOFT($1$,$16$), the former achieves better approximation quality while using significantly fewer parameters. This is because BOFT($1$,$b$) corresponds exactly to the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, which cannot create dense connectivity between all dimensions. The butterfly structure, by contrast, enables information flow across all dimensions through its multi-layer factorization.

Second, the relationship between the hyperparameters and expressivity follows a clear pattern: BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and larger number of components $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient. For example, BOFT($6$,$4$) achieves similar approximation error to BOFT($2$,$16$) but with substantially fewer parameters. This makes intuitive sense when we consider that increasing $m$ adds more layers to the information transmission pathway, allowing for more complex transformations to be composed, while decreasing $b$ increases the granularity at which transformations can be applied.

The number of effective trainable parameters in BOFT($m$,$b$) is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ for finetuning a linear layer of size $d \times n$. When using the full butterfly matrix with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$, BOFT requires only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, compared to the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters needed for an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix. This exponential reduction in parameter count, while maintaining the ability to produce dense orthogonal matrices, is the key advantage of the butterfly factorization.

# Comparison of Expressivity: BOFT vs. Block-Diagonal OFT

The relationship between BOFT and the original OFT provides important theoretical insights. BOFT subsumes OFT as a special case: when $m = 1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. When $b = d$, BOFT reduces to the original OFT with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. This nested relationship allows us to precisely characterize when and why BOFT offers advantages.

A fundamental theoretical result establishes the expressivity advantage of BOFT over block-diagonal OFT. The butterfly matrix represents a more structured subset of the orthogonal group compared to the block-diagonal structure, which makes BOFT provably more expressive than OFT with the same block size. This is formalized in the following theorem:

**Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT):** BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size. For the butterfly matrix to approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$, we can multiply butterfly matrices with $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^{\top}_{1,2}(d)$, where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ for all $i$ and $j$ are butterfly matrices.

This theorem suggests a natural generalization of BOFT through what is called kaleidoscope hierarchy. The final orthogonal matrix can be generalized to $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}^{\top}_{l,2}(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}^{\top}_{1,2}(m_2,b_2)$, where $\bm{R}^{\top}_{i,j}(m,b)$ denotes the orthogonal matrix used in BOFT. When $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d-1$, this construction $\bm{R}^G$ can represent the entire orthogonal group.

However, an important caveat must be emphasized: better expressivity does not always translate to better finetuning performance. Full finetuning, despite having universal expressiveness, often yields unsatisfactory performance due to overfitting and the loss of pretrained knowledge. The trade-off between expressivity and regularity is the key to the generalizability of model finetuning. BOFT enlarges the finetuning parameter space with structural priors derived from the butterfly factorization, enabling a better balance between expressivity and regularity than either full finetuning or block-diagonal OFT.

# Spectral Properties and Training Stability

One of the most significant theoretical advantages of orthogonal finetuning methods, including both OFT and BOFT, lies in their spectral properties. Understanding these properties provides insight into why orthogonal transformations lead to more stable training and better generalization.

The key spectral property of orthogonal finetuning is that it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. To see this, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the pretrained weight matrix: $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values.

Both OFT and BOFT multiply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the left of the pretrained weights, obtaining the finetuned weights as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}$. Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal by construction, the product $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ remains orthogonal. Therefore, the singular values of the finetuned weight matrix are exactly the same as those of the pretrained weight matrix. In particular, the largest singular value, which equals the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, is perfectly preserved.

This preservation has profound implications for training stability and generalization. The spectral norm of weight matrices plays a crucial role in controlling the Lipschitz constant of neural network layers, which in turn affects gradient flow during training and the model's sensitivity to input perturbations. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, OFT and BOFT ensure that the pretrained model's carefully learned spectral properties are not disrupted.

The stability benefits of spectral norm preservation have been extensively studied in the literature on spectral normalization and regularization. Methods that control the spectral norm during training have been shown to improve both training dynamics and generalization performance. Orthogonal finetuning achieves this control naturally and exactly, without requiring additional regularization terms or normalization procedures that might interfere with the optimization process.

In contrast, methods like LoRA, which add a low-rank matrix to the pretrained weights, can potentially increase the spectral norm, especially if the added low-rank components have large singular values. While LoRA typically uses small initialization to mitigate this issue, the spectral norm is not guaranteed to stay bounded during finetuning. This theoretical distinction helps explain why orthogonal finetuning methods often exhibit more stable training behavior, particularly in challenging finetuning scenarios.

# Inductive Bias: Connection to Classic Linear Transforms (FFT, Hadamard)

The butterfly structure carries with it a rich inductive bias rooted in decades of research on fast linear transforms. Understanding this connection illuminates why BOFT is particularly well-suited for finetuning and provides theoretical grounding for its empirical success.

The butterfly structure is originally used in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm to perform the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). In Fourier transform, a local change in the frequency domain causes a global change in the spatial domain—every point in the input affects every point in the output. This property is conceptually similar to the information transmission problem in orthogonal finetuning, where every node in the first level must be able to transmit information to all nodes in the last level.

The butterfly structure along with permutations can perfectly recover many classic fast linear transforms, including the Fast Fourier Transform, the Hadamard transform, the discrete sine transform, and the discrete cosine transform. These transforms have been fundamental tools in signal processing, numerical computation, and data analysis for decades. Their ubiquity and effectiveness suggest that the butterfly structure captures something essential about how information should flow through linear transformations.

This connection to classic linear transforms introduces a beneficial inductive bias. Since $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT represents a structured subset of the orthogonal group, BOFT naturally constrains the hypothesis class during finetuning. The structured inductive bias induced by the butterfly factorization aligns with the structure underlying many natural signal processing tasks, potentially making it easier to find good solutions within the parameter space.

Importantly, the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT cannot recover any of these classic linear transforms. The block-diagonal structure imposes a hard partition on the dimensions, treating blocks independently and preventing the global information mixing that characterizes transforms like the FFT. This limitation provides a concrete theoretical advantage for the butterfly structure: BOFT can express transformations that have proven useful across numerous applications, while block-diagonal OFT cannot.

Furthermore, the sparse matrix factorization inherent in BOFT may introduce additional implicit inductive biases related to the structure of the optimization landscape. Sparse factorizations have been studied in the context of implicit regularization, where the structure of the parameterization influences which solutions are preferred during optimization, even without explicit regularization terms.

# Weight Interpolation in BOFT

An intriguing property of BOFT emerges when we consider how it interpolates between different orthogonal matrices during the finetuning process. Understanding this interpolation behavior provides insight into why BOFT achieves better generalization than alternative approaches.

BOFT uses the butterfly structure to construct a smoother interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). When BOFT is initialized, all butterfly components start as identity matrices, meaning the finetuned model exactly matches the pretrained model at initialization. As training progresses, the butterfly components gradually deviate from identity, producing increasingly complex orthogonal transformations.

The key insight is that the butterfly structure enables finding a smaller hypothesis class within the orthogonal group that is particularly well-suited for downstream tasks. This hypothesis class is parameterized by the product of sparse orthogonal matrices, where each factor is constrained to have a specific sparsity pattern determined by the butterfly structure. By traversing this hypothesis class during optimization, BOFT explores a region of the parameter space that balances flexibility with structure.

The interpolation property also relates to the expressivity-regularity trade-off. When $m$ is small and $b$ is large (approaching the block-diagonal case), BOFT has limited expressivity but strong regularity. As $m$ increases and $b$ decreases, BOFT becomes more expressive while still maintaining the structural constraints of the butterfly factorization. This smooth continuum allows practitioners to select the appropriate balance based on the complexity of the downstream task and the available parameter budget.

This interpolation behavior stands in contrast to the block-diagonal structure, which imposes a hard partition on dimensions and cannot smoothly transition between different levels of expressivity. The butterfly structure's ability to provide a gradual spectrum from identity to expressive orthogonal matrices makes it more adaptable to varying finetuning requirements.

# BOFT as Learning Bilinear Similarity

A final theoretical perspective on BOFT comes from interpreting orthogonal finetuning through the lens of bilinear similarity learning. This connection reveals deep relationships to classical machine learning concepts and provides another angle from which to understand BOFT's effectiveness.

BOFT can be written as learning a bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i \bm{R} \bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ is the $i$-th neuron (a column vector) of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, $\bm{R}$ is the learned orthogonal matrix, and $\bm{x}$ is the input vector. In this formulation, BOFT learns a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ that measures the compatibility between pretrained neurons and inputs, with the strong constraint that $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal.

This interpretation connects BOFT to two rich areas of machine learning research. First, it relates to distance metric learning, where the goal is to learn a transformation of the feature space that better reflects semantic similarity for a given task. In BOFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ transforms the pretrained feature space such that the similarity between neurons and inputs is adjusted for the downstream task, while preserving the geometric relationships (angles and distances) through orthogonality.

Second, the bilinear form interpretation connects BOFT to classical bilinear models and bilinear pooling methods in computer vision. Bilinear models capture pairwise interactions between features, and the constraint that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal can be seen as a form of regularization that prevents the learned interactions from distorting the pretrained representation space too severely.

The orthogonality constraint ensures that $\bm{R}$ preserves angles between vectors: $\langle \bm{R}\bm{x}, \bm{R}\bm{y} \rangle = \langle \bm{x}, \bm{y} \rangle$ for any vectors $\bm{x}$ and $\bm{y}$. This means that while BOFT learns to adjust the similarity between neurons and inputs, it does so without introducing arbitrary distortions to the geometry of the feature space. The pretrained knowledge encoded in the relative positions and angles of neurons is preserved, while their absolute orientations can be adjusted.

This bilinear perspective also helps explain why BOFT is particularly effective for finetuning large pretrained models. These models have already learned rich representations where geometric relationships between features carry semantic meaning. By constraining the finetuning transformation to be orthogonal, BOFT respects this pretrained geometry while still allowing for task-specific adaptation through the learned orthogonal transformation.

# Summary of the BOFT Framework

The BOFT framework represents a significant advancement in parameter-efficient finetuning for large foundation models. At its core, BOFT addresses a fundamental challenge: how to construct dense orthogonal matrices without the prohibitive parameter cost that typically accompanies them. The original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework established that preserving pairwise angles between neurons during finetuning helps maintain the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. This preservation is achieved by transforming weight matrices with orthogonal matrices, which provably maintain the angular relationships between neuron representations. However, the original OFT approach faced a critical tension between parameter efficiency and expressivity. Using a full dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a $d$-dimensional weight matrix, which becomes impractical for large models. The block-diagonal structure introduced to reduce parameters, while practical, imposes arbitrary constraints on how dimensions are grouped and transformed, potentially introducing undesirable inductive biases.

BOFT resolves this tension through a principled approach to matrix factorization. The key insight is that a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ can be composed as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. This factorization draws inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, where butterfly structures enable efficient computation of complex linear transforms. The butterfly factorization allows BOFT to generate a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, representing a dramatic improvement over the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement of a full dense matrix.

The information transmission framework provides an elegant theoretical lens for understanding why this factorization works. Viewing matrix multiplication as information flow through a grid-structured graph reveals that dense connectivity between input and output nodes can be achieved through carefully designed sparse connections across multiple layers. The butterfly structure satisfies two critical desiderata: it ensures dense connectivity where every source node can transmit information to every destination node, and it minimizes the number of edges required under orthogonality constraints. This graphical perspective not only explains BOFT's efficiency but also opens avenues for exploring alternative sparse factorization patterns.

In practice, BOFT modifies the forward pass of a pretrained linear layer from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is an orthogonal matrix parameterized by $m$ butterfly components, each with block size $b$. The notation BOFT$(m, b)$ encapsulates this parameterization, with special cases recovering earlier methods: BOFT$(1, b)$ reduces to block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$, and BOFT$(1, d)$ recovers the original unconstrained OFT. The framework provides smooth interpolation between the full orthogonal group (maximum expressivity) and identity matrices (maximum regularity), allowing practitioners to navigate this trade-off based on their specific needs.

Empirical validation across diverse domains demonstrates BOFT's versatility and effectiveness. The method has been successfully applied to large vision transformers for image classification, large language models for natural language understanding and generation, and text-to-image diffusion models for controllable generation. Across these tasks, BOFT consistently outperforms existing parameter-efficient finetuning methods, including the widely adopted LoRA approach. The superior performance validates the theoretical motivation that orthogonal transformations, when properly parameterized, offer better preservation of pretrained knowledge than additive low-rank updates.

# Limitations: Training Runtime Overhead

Despite its impressive parameter efficiency and strong empirical performance, BOFT is not without limitations. The most significant practical concern involves training runtime overhead. Because the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT is computed as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components—specifically, $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$—the forward pass requires sequential matrix multiplications. Each multiplication involves computing the product of sparse matrices, and while each individual sparse matrix is efficient to compute, the cumulative effect across multiple components introduces computational overhead during training.

This overhead is particularly noticeable when comparing BOFT to simpler finetuning approaches. Standard full finetuning requires no additional computation beyond the standard forward and backward passes. Even LoRA, which adds low-rank matrices to the pretrained weights, requires only simple matrix additions and multiplications with relatively small matrices. In contrast, BOFT requires multiplying through a chain of butterfly components, where each component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has a specific block-diagonal structure that must be applied sequentially. The multiplicative nature of the weight update means that gradients must propagate through this entire chain during backpropagation, adding computational steps not present in additive update methods.

The training runtime overhead scales with the number of butterfly components $m$ and the block size $b$. Larger values of $m$ (which provide better expressivity and denser final orthogonal matrices) require more sequential multiplications, directly increasing computation time. Similarly, larger block sizes $b$ increase the size of each orthogonal block within the butterfly components, requiring more computation per component. Practitioners must therefore balance their desired level of expressivity against training efficiency when selecting hyperparameters.

The paper acknowledges that improving BOFT's training runtime remains an open problem. Several potential directions could address this limitation. One approach involves developing more efficient implementations of butterfly matrix multiplications, potentially leveraging specialized hardware acceleration or optimized sparse matrix routines. Another direction explores whether certain butterfly components can be computed in parallel rather than sequentially, reducing the critical path length in the computation graph. Additionally, techniques from automatic differentiation could potentially identify computational shortcuts when computing gradients through the butterfly component chain.

It is important to contextualize this limitation appropriately. The training overhead affects only the finetuning phase, not the deployed model's inference. Once finetuning is complete, the learned orthogonal matrix can be merged directly into the pretrained weights, eliminating any computational overhead during deployment. Furthermore, the parameter efficiency of BOFT often allows for more aggressive exploration of hyperparameter configurations within a given parameter budget, potentially offsetting some of the runtime cost through faster iterations in the experimental cycle.

# Inference Efficiency: Merging Weights

One of BOFT's most practically significant advantages is its inference efficiency. Unlike some parameter-efficient finetuning methods that introduce additional computational overhead during inference, BOFT maintains the same inference latency as the original pretrained model. This efficiency stems from a fundamental property of multiplicative weight updates: they can be algebraically merged into the pretrained weights before deployment.

Consider the BOFT forward pass: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the frozen pretrained weight matrix and $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is the learned orthogonal matrix composed of butterfly components. During finetuning, this formulation must be explicitly computed, with the butterfly components multiplied together and then applied to $\bm{W}^0$. However, once finetuning is complete and the optimal $\bm{R}(m,b)$ has been learned, we can pre-compute the product $\bm{W}_{\text{finetuned}} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$ and store this merged weight matrix.

This merging process is straightforward: compute the full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ by multiplying all butterfly components, then multiply this matrix with the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. The resulting $\bm{W}_{\text{finetuned}}$ has exactly the same dimensions as $\bm{W}^0$, and inference proceeds exactly as it would with the original model: $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}_{\text{finetuned}})^\top \bm{x}$. No special inference code, no additional parameters to load, and no computational overhead whatsoever compared to the pretrained model.

This property stands in stark contrast to adapter-based methods, which insert additional trainable modules into the model architecture. While adapters achieve parameter efficiency, they increase the depth of the computational graph during inference, adding latency to every forward pass. Even methods like LoRA, which also permits weight merging through addition, introduce some overhead if the merged weights are not pre-computed or if multiple LoRA adaptations need to be swapped dynamically.

The ability to merge weights also has significant implications for deployment in resource-constrained environments. When serving multiple finetuned variants of a base model, BOFT allows each variant to be stored as its complete merged weight matrix. At serving time, the appropriate weights can be loaded without any architectural modifications or additional computational steps. This simplicity reduces engineering complexity and potential sources of errors in production systems.

Furthermore, weight merging enables interesting deployment strategies. Organizations can maintain a single base pretrained model and distribute only the relatively small BOFT parameters for specialized tasks. Users can then merge these parameters into their local copy of the base model, achieving the finetuned behavior without downloading full model weights. This approach dramatically reduces bandwidth requirements for model distribution while maintaining the full capabilities of the finetuned model.
