\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does OFT use multiplicative orthogonal matrices instead of additive updates like LoRA?
Question:
I'm reading the BOFT paper and trying to understand the fundamental difference between OFT and LoRA. The paper mentions that OFT reparameterizes the new weight matrix as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ (multiplicative) while LoRA uses an additive low-rank matrix update $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$. Why does OFT choose the multiplicative approach? What advantages does this provide over the additive approach in terms of preserving pretrained knowledge?
Answer:
The fundamental reason OFT uses multiplicative orthogonal matrices is to **provably preserve the relational structure** learned during pretraining—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons.

**The Key Insight: Preserving Neuron Geometry**

When we think about what a weight matrix encodes, the columns (neurons) have learned specific angular relationships during pretraining. These angles capture semantic relationships in the representation space. As the paper explains:

> "The motivation for applying orthogonal transformation to finetune the weight matrix is to preserve the pair-wise angles of neurons, such that the semantic knowledge from pretraining can be largely preserved."

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which means it performs a rotation or reflection. This operation preserves the angle between any two vectors: if $\theta$ is the angle between columns $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ of the pretrained weight matrix, then the angle between $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$ remains exactly $\theta$.

**Why Additive Updates Like LoRA Can't Guarantee This**

LoRA's additive update $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ has no mathematical guarantee about preserving neuron relationships. The low-rank perturbation can arbitrarily change the angles between neurons, potentially degrading the pretrained knowledge. While LoRA encourages small changes through initialization and learning rate, this is an *ad hoc* approach rather than a principled guarantee.

**Additional Advantages: Spectral Properties**

Beyond angle preservation, the multiplicative orthogonal approach has important spectral properties. Using SVD decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, when we apply an orthogonal transformation:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$$

The singular values $\bm{\Sigma}$ (and hence the spectral norm) remain unchanged. As the paper notes, this preservation "has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization."

**Trade-offs: Parameter Efficiency vs. Expressivity**

The main drawback of multiplicative orthogonal matrices is their parameter cost—a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This is precisely why the paper introduces BOFT: using butterfly factorization, a dense orthogonal matrix can be constructed with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while maintaining the geometric guarantees of orthogonal finetuning.

In summary, the multiplicative approach provides mathematical guarantees about preserving pretrained knowledge through angle preservation and spectral stability, while LoRA's additive approach relies on soft constraints without such guarantees.

### What does 'pairwise angles between neurons' mean in the context of OFT?
Question:
The paper states that OFT preserves 'the pairwise angles between neurons' through orthogonal transformation. I'm confused about what exactly this means. If we have a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, are the 'neurons' the column vectors? And how does multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserve angles between these columns? A mathematical explanation would help.
Answer:
The "neurons" in this context are indeed the column vectors of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. Each column $\bm{w}_i^0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$ corresponds to one output neuron, representing the weights connecting all $d$ input units to the $i$-th output unit.

The **pairwise angle** $\theta_{ij}$ between two neurons $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ is defined through the standard inner-product formula:
$$\cos(\theta_{ij}) = \frac{(\bm{w}_i^0)^\top \bm{w}_j^0}{\|\bm{w}_i^0\| \|\bm{w}_j^0\|}$$

Intuitively, this angle captures the relational geometry between neurons—whether they point in similar directions (small angle) or are orthogonal/independent (angle near $90^\circ$).

**Why orthogonal matrices preserve these angles:**

After applying the orthogonal transformation, the weight matrix becomes $\bm{W}' = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where each column is now $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$. The angle $\theta'_{ij}$ between transformed neurons satisfies:

$$\cos(\theta'_{ij}) = \frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0)}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\| \|\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0\|}$$

Since $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$), two key properties hold:

1. **Inner products are preserved**: $(\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0)^\top(\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0) = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0 = (\bm{w}_i^0)^\top\bm{w}_j^0$

2. **Norms are preserved**: $\|\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0\| = \sqrt{(\bm{w}_i^0)^\top\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0} = \|\bm{w}_i^0\|$

Substituting these into the formula gives $\cos(\theta'_{ij}) = \cos(\theta_{ij})$, meaning all pairwise angles remain unchanged.

This preservation matters because the relational structure between neurons encodes learned semantic knowledge from pretraining. By guaranteeing that angles are preserved, OFT ensures the finetuned model maintains this geometric structure, which is why the paper states this "enables the semantic knowledge from pretraining [to be] largely preserved."

### How does the information transmission framework relate to matrix factorization?
Question:
The paper introduces an 'information transmission view' where generating a dense matrix $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ is viewed as transmitting information on a grid with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. I'm struggling to understand the correspondence between matrix elements and edges in this graph. Specifically, the paper says 'If the element $R_{ij}$ is zero, then it indicates that information from the $j$-th node cannot flow to the $i$-th node.' Can someone explain this mapping more intuitively?
Answer:
The information transmission framework provides an elegant graphical interpretation of matrix factorization. The key insight is that matrix multiplication naturally corresponds to computing paths through a layered graph.

Consider factorizing $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ where each $\bm{B}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This creates a grid with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes: $d$ nodes at each of $m+1$ levels. Each factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents connectivity between two adjacent levels. Specifically, the element $(\bm{B}_i)_{j_1, j_2}$ corresponds to a directed edge from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$. A zero entry means no edge exists; a non-zero entry means information can traverse that edge.

The matrix product naturally computes connectivity through the entire network. When you compute $\bm{R}_{ij}$, you're essentially summing over all paths from node $j$ at level $1$ to node $i$ at level $m+1$. Each path contributes the product of edge weights along that path. If $\bm{R}_{ij} = 0$, no path connects source $j$ to destination $i$—information cannot flow. If $\bm{R}_{ij} \neq 0$, at least one valid path exists.

This explains why block-diagonal OFT cannot produce dense matrices: within each block, information is trapped and cannot propagate to nodes in other blocks. In contrast, butterfly factorization ensures every source node connects to every destination node through a structured topology with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges rather than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. The butterfly pattern achieves dense connectivity efficiently because each intermediate level enables a specific pattern of mixing that ultimately reaches all destinations.

### Why can't the block-diagonal structure in OFT recover classic linear transforms?
Question:
The paper states that 'the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix... cannot approximate classic linear transforms' while 'the butterfly structure can easily recover many classic linear transforms.' I understand that block-diagonal matrices apply transformations independently within blocks, but why exactly does this prevent them from representing transforms like the discrete Fourier transform? What is it about the butterfly structure that enables this recovery?
Answer:
The key insight lies in understanding how information flows through the matrix structure, which the paper elegantly explains through its information transmission framework.

## Why Block-Diagonal Cannot Recover Transforms Like DFT

A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix has the form $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \ldots, \bm{R}_r)$ where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal matrix operating on a subset of dimensions. The fundamental limitation is that **entries connecting different blocks are permanently zero**.

Consider what this means for transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). The DFT matrix $\bm{F}$ is completely dense—every frequency component depends on **every** input coordinate. Specifically, the $(k, j)$-th entry of the DFT matrix is $\omega^{kj}/\sqrt{d}$ where $\omega = e^{-2\pi i/d}$, which is non-zero for all $(k, j)$ pairs.

A block-diagonal structure fundamentally cannot represent such dense mixing because information from dimension $j$ can only affect dimensions within its assigned block. There's no mechanism for information to flow across block boundaries—dimensions in different groups are transformed completely independently.

## Why Butterfly Structure Succeeds

The butterfly structure, inspired by the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm, overcomes this by using a **product of sparse matrices with different sparsity patterns**:

$$\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is block-diagonal, but critically, **the block structure shifts between successive factors**. This is where the magic happens.

In the information transmission view from the paper, this creates a multi-layer graph where:
- Each factor corresponds to connections between adjacent layers
- The shifting block pattern ensures that over $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ layers, **every node eventually connects to every other node**

For example, at layer 1, dimension $j$ might only connect to dimensions $\{j, j+1\}$. But at layer 2, those dimensions connect to a different set, and so on. After $\log d$ layers, there exists a path from any source node to any destination node.

This is precisely how the Cooley-Tukey algorithm achieves the DFT in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ operations: it decomposes the transform into a sequence of smaller transforms that progressively mix information across larger spans of the input. The butterfly structure **naturally encodes this hierarchical mixing pattern**.

## Summary

The block-diagonal structure imposes permanent separation between dimension groups, making it impossible to achieve the dense, global mixing required by transforms like DFT. The butterfly structure achieves dense connectivity through sequential multiplication of matrices with different sparsity patterns—each multiplication "mixes" information a bit further, and after $\log d$ steps, all dimensions have potentially interacted with all others. This is why the paper emphasizes that "the butterfly structure can easily recover many classic linear transforms" while the block-diagonal structure "cannot approximate classic linear transforms."

### How do the parameters $m$ and $b$ in BOFT($m$,$b$) affect the expressivity-efficiency tradeoff?
Question:
BOFT is parameterized as BOFT($m$,$b$) where the paper mentions it takes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ trainable parameters. I see that when $m=1$, it reduces to block-diagonal OFT, and when $m=\log \frac{2d}{b}$, we get a dense orthogonal matrix. But I'm confused about how to choose these hyperparameters in practice. The paper says 'BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient' -- could someone explain the intuition behind this?
Answer:
The parameters $m$ and $b$ in $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ control a fundamental tradeoff between parameter efficiency and expressivity through the structure of the orthogonal matrix factorization.

## Understanding $m$ and $b$

The parameter $m$ represents the number of butterfly components (factorization depth), while $b$ represents the block size within each butterfly component. The total number of trainable parameters is $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ for a weight matrix of dimension $d$.

## Why Smaller $b$ and Larger $m$ is More Parameter-Efficient

The key insight comes from the information transmission framework introduced in the paper. Generating a dense orthogonal matrix requires information to flow from every node to every other node. The butterfly structure achieves this efficiently because:

**With smaller $b$**: Each butterfly factor operates on smaller blocks, creating finer-grained transformations. A $2 \times 2$ block ($b=2$) is the smallest possible, allowing the most granular mixing.

**With larger $m$**: More factorization layers provide more opportunities for information to propagate across the network. Each additional butterfly component enables information to "travel further" through the network topology.

The paper illustrates this with a concrete example: $\mathrm{BOFT}(6, 4)$ achieves similar approximation error to $\mathrm{BOFT}(2, 16)$ but uses only $9d$ parameters versus $15d$ parameters. This demonstrates that deeper factorization with smaller blocks uses parameters more efficiently than shallow factorization with larger blocks.

## The Expressivity Spectrum

$\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ interpolates smoothly between two extremes:

- **$m=1$**: Reduces to block-diagonal OFT, where each block is processed independently (limited expressivity, but strong regularization)
- **$m=\log \frac{2d}{b}$**: Produces a fully dense orthogonal matrix (maximum expressivity)

The butterfly structure can recover classic linear transforms like FFT and Hadamard transform, which the block-diagonal structure cannot approximate. This gives BOFT richer expressivity even with comparable parameter counts.

## Practical Guidance

The paper recommends favoring smaller $b$ and larger $m$ when parameter efficiency is critical. However, the optimal choice depends on the task—the structured inductive bias of the butterfly parameterization may benefit generalization on certain downstream tasks even when expressivity is not the primary concern.

### How does Cayley parameterization ensure orthogonality in BOFT?
Question:
The paper mentions using Cayley parameterization $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, to ensure the orthogonal matrix constraint. I understand that this guarantees orthogonality, but how exactly is this applied to the butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$? Are we parameterizing each $2 \times 2$ block separately with a $2 \times 2$ skew-symmetric matrix, or is there a larger skew-symmetric matrix involved?
Answer:
The Cayley parameterization is applied at the level of individual blocks within each butterfly component, not to the full matrix. Let me walk through exactly how this works.

For the basic butterfly case (block parameter $b = 1$), each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ is a block-diagonal matrix consisting of $\frac{d}{2}$ blocks, each of size $2 \times 2$. Each of these $2 \times 2$ blocks is parameterized independently using Cayley transform with a $2 \times 2$ skew-symmetric matrix:

$$\bm{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -q \\ q & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where $q$ is a single learnable parameter. Applying the Cayley transform $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$ yields a $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix, which is guaranteed to be orthogonal. This is why the paper mentions that this approach builds upon "many $2 \times 2$ orthogonal matrices."

For the block butterfly case BOFT($m$, $b$), the paper clarifies that this notation actually corresponds to using $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, meaning the block parameter is $\frac{b}{2}$. Each block in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d,2)$ then has size $2 \times \frac{b}{2} = b$ in each dimension, so each block is $b \times b$. Each such $b \times b$ block is parameterized via Cayley transform with a $b \times b$ skew-symmetric matrix, which has $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ independent trainable parameters.

Crucially, the paper notes that "the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$." This means that even for butterfly components with larger $k$ values (like $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,4)$, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,8)$, etc.), after an appropriate permutation of rows and columns, they exhibit the same block structure and can be parameterized similarly. This is why all $m$ butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ for $i \in [1,m]$ can be efficiently parameterized as orthogonal matrices.

This parameterization strategy explains the total parameter count given in the paper: BOFT($m$, $b$) uses $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters, which comes from $m$ butterfly components, each having $\frac{d}{b}$ blocks, with each $b \times b$ orthogonal block contributing $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ parameters from its skew-symmetric matrix.

### In BOFT, why does multiplying butterfly components preserve orthogonality?
Question:
The paper defines $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ and states that each butterfly component is orthogonal. I know that the product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, so this makes sense mathematically. But I'm confused about how each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is constructed to be orthogonal when the block sizes change (e.g., $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ vs $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,4)$). The paper mentions 'the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$' -- how does this permutation preserve orthogonality?
Answer:
The key insight is that the butterfly factor structure ensures that non-zero entries in any butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ naturally form independent $2 \times 2$ groups that can each be parameterized as orthogonal—exactly like the blocks in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$.

Let me walk through this concretely. A butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as:
$$\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}$$

For $k=4$, this gives a $4 \times 4$ matrix with non-zero pattern:
$$\begin{bmatrix}
\times & 0 & \times & 0 \\
0 & \times & 0 & \times \\
\times & 0 & \times & 0 \\
0 & \times & 0 & \times
\end{bmatrix}$$

Now here's the crucial observation: if you permute the rows and columns (swapping positions 2 and 3), this becomes:
$$\begin{bmatrix}
\times & \times & 0 & 0 \\
\times & \times & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \times & \times \\
0 & 0 & \times & \times
\end{bmatrix}$$

This is a block-diagonal matrix with $2 \times 2$ blocks! The same structure as $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$.

Since permutation matrices are orthogonal, we can write $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,4) = \bm{P}^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}' \bm{P}$ where $\bm{P}$ is a permutation matrix and $\tilde{\bm{B}}'$ is block-diagonal with $2 \times 2$ blocks. If we parameterize each $2 \times 2$ block in $\tilde{\bm{B}}'$ as orthogonal (using Cayley transform or 2D rotation), then $\tilde{\bm{B}}'$ is orthogonal. And since permutation matrices are orthogonal, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,4) = \bm{P}^\top \tilde{\bm{B}}' \bm{P}$ is also orthogonal—the product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal.

This pattern extends to any block size $k = 2^i$. The "block-wise permutation" for the generalized $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ works analogously: each $2b \times 2b$ submatrix within the larger blocks can be permuted into a form where the trainable parameters form independent orthogonal blocks.

Intuitively, the butterfly structure ensures that information only flows between carefully chosen pairs of coordinates at each step, and the permutation view reveals that these pairs are exactly the coordinate pairs that would interact in the simpler $k=2$ case—the indices are just shuffled by the permutation. This is why the paper can claim that "all butterfly components can be easily parameterized as orthogonal matrices" once we know how to handle the $k=2$ case.

### What is the relationship between BOFT($1$,$b$) and block-diagonal OFT?
Question:
The paper states that 'BOFT($1$,$b$) reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with the block size $b$.' I'm trying to verify this. If $m=1$, then $\bm{R}(1,b) = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^m) = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$. The paper defines $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ as 'a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ with block size $k$'. But I'm confused about the relationship between $b$ and the block size in the resulting block-diagonal OFT. Could someone walk through this reduction step by step?
Answer:
You're on the right track, but there's a key notational convention that resolves this. Let me walk through the reduction step by step.

The crucial detail is in how the paper defines the BOFT notation: "For convenience, we denote BOFT with $\bm{R}(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$."

This means $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ actually uses $\bm{R}(m, \frac{b}{2})$, not $\bm{R}(m, b)$. So when $m=1$:

$$\bm{R}(1, \tfrac{b}{2}) = \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}_{(d,1)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d, 2^{1-1+1}) = \tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d, 2)$$

Now, what is $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d, 2)$? The block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is defined such that "each entry in $\bm{d}_i,\forall i$ becomes a $b\times b$ matrix."

For $k=2$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(2)$ is a $2\times 2$ matrix where each entry is a scalar (since $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2} = \mathbb{R}^1$). With the block generalization $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d, 2)$, each scalar entry becomes a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix. Since there are 4 entries arranged in a $2\times 2$ grid, the resulting block butterfly factor has size:
$$2 \times \tfrac{b}{2} = b$$

So each block in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^{b/2}(d, 2)$ is a $b \times b$ matrix, and there are $\frac{d}{b}$ such blocks. This is precisely a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size $b$, which matches the definition of block-diagonal OFT.

To summarize the relationship between $b$ in $\mathrm{BOFT}(1, b)$ and the block size:
- $\mathrm{BOFT}(1, b)$ uses the superscript $\frac{b}{2}$ in the block butterfly component
- The butterfly factor has 4 entries (a $2\times 2$ structure)
- Each entry is a $\frac{b}{2} \times \frac{b}{2}$ matrix
- Effective block size: $2 \times \frac{b}{2} = b$

This confirms the paper's statement that "$\mathrm{BOFT}(1, b)$ reduces to the block-diagonal OFT with the block size $b$." The notation is designed so that the block size of the resulting orthogonal matrix matches the parameter $b$ in $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$.

### Why does multiplicative dropout work differently than standard dropout for BOFT?
Question:
The paper introduces 'multiplicative dropout' for BOFT, stating that 'conventional Dropout naturally works for LoRA, but not for BOFT due to our multiplicative weight update.' I understand that BOFT multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$, while LoRA adds a low-rank update. But why does this difference necessitate a different dropout strategy? Why can't we just apply dropout to the butterfly components' trainable parameters?
Answer:
The key difference stems from how additive and multiplicative updates interact with zero-valued dropout masks.

**In LoRA**, the weight update is additive: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$. When you apply standard dropout to the low-rank matrices $\bm{B}$ or $\bm{A}$, randomly setting entries to zero means certain "update contributions" are removed. Crucially, zeroing out an additive update leaves you with the pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$—exactly what you want during dropout. The operation $(\bm{W}^0 + \text{dropout}(\bm{B}\bm{A}))$ naturally interpolates between "applying the learned update" and "keeping the original."

**In BOFT**, the update is multiplicative: $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$. If you naively apply dropout to the trainable parameters within the butterfly components, you're zeroing entries of an orthogonal matrix. This is problematic for two reasons:

1. **Breaking orthogonality**: Orthogonal matrices have strict structural constraints—each row and column must be unit vectors. Randomly zeroing entries violates these constraints, potentially destabilizing training and losing the spectral preservation benefits that make OFT/BOFT effective.

2. **Semantically incorrect**: Zeroing entries in a multiplicative transformation doesn't mean "don't update"—it means "apply a different, potentially degenerate transformation." When a subset of $\bm{R}$'s entries becomes zero, the transformation no longer behaves like a gentle refinement of identity; instead, it may destroy information from $\bm{W}^0$.

The paper's multiplicative dropout addresses this by replacing selected butterfly components or diagonal blocks with **identity matrices** rather than zero masks. Since identity matrices are valid orthogonal matrices, this preserves the orthogonality constraint throughout dropout. Semantically, replacing a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ with $\bm{I}_d$ effectively says "don't apply this component's learned transformation"—which correctly removes the update while maintaining structural integrity.

This is analogous to how dropout works in recurrent networks or transformers where careful masking preserves temporal structure—here, multiplicative dropout preserves the geometric structure that makes BOFT effective.

### How does Theorem 1 about BOFT's expressivity relate to the kaleidoscope hierarchy?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size, and mentions that approximating all orthogonal matrices requires $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$. The paper then introduces a generalized form $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ and mentions 'kaleidoscope hierarchy'. I'm confused about two things: (1) What does it mean for BOFT to be 'more expressive' in practical finetuning terms? (2) If the generalized form can represent the entire orthogonal group, why doesn't the paper use this for maximum expressivity?
Answer:
**What does "more expressive" mean in practical finetuning terms?**

The phrase "more expressive" has a precise meaning in this context: for the same block size $b$, $\mathrm{BOFT}(m, b)$ can approximate a strictly larger subset of the orthogonal group than block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$.

Concretely, OFT with block size $b$ can only represent orthogonal matrices where each block operates independently—dimensions within different blocks never interact. BOFT, through butterfly factorization, enables information mixing across *all* dimensions through its cascade of sparse orthogonal matrices. In practical finetuning, this means BOFT can learn transformations that capture cross-dimensional dependencies in the weight matrix, which OFT fundamentally cannot. The experiments (Figure 2 in the paper) validate this: $\mathrm{BOFT}(9, 2)$ achieves lower approximation error with fewer parameters than OFT with block size 16.

**Why not use the kaleidoscope hierarchy for maximum expressivity?**

The paper explicitly addresses this right after Theorem 1: "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance in finetuning." Full expressivity—the ability to represent *any* orthogonal matrix—is not the goal. The key insight is that parameter-efficient finetuning works precisely *because* it constrains the hypothesis class.

Using the full kaleidoscope hierarchy $\bm{R}^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l)$ with $l = d-1$ would allow representing any orthogonal matrix, but this defeats the purpose: you would lose the structural regularization that prevents overfitting, and you would dramatically increase both parameter count and computational overhead. The paper emphasizes this trade-off: "BOFT enlarges the finetuning parameter space with structural priors, which enables us to find a better trade-off between expressivity and regularity."

The butterfly structure provides a useful inductive bias—it can efficiently represent many classic linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard) while remaining parameter-efficient. This structured expressivity is what makes BOFT generalize well in practice.

### What does 'trading computation time for space' mean in BOFT's parameterization?
Question:
The paper states that 'the number of trainable parameters can be reduced by trading computation time for space' and that 'Since we represent the orthogonal matrix with the multiplication of sparse matrices, the multiplication has to be performed repeatedly in each training iteration.' I understand that BOFT uses fewer parameters, but I'm confused about the computational overhead. How much slower is BOFT compared to block-diagonal OFT during training? And why does the paper say there's 'no additional inference latency' after finetuning?
Answer:
The phrase "trading computation time for space" refers to a fundamental design choice in BOFT's parameterization. Let me break this down.

**The Trade-off Explained**

In the original OFT, a dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Block-diagonal OFT reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters by restricting $\mathbf{R}$ to a block-diagonal structure, but this comes at a cost: the resulting matrix is no longer dense, limiting expressiveness.

BOFT takes a different approach. Instead of storing a single dense matrix, it factorizes the orthogonal matrix into a product of $m$ sparse matrices:
$$\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. The total parameter count becomes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters, which for the full butterfly setting ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) yields $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

The computational cost, however, increases during training. Rather than performing a single matrix-vector multiplication with a dense matrix, BOFT must sequentially multiply through $m$ sparse matrices. Each multiplication costs $\mathcal{O}(d)$, so the total cost per forward pass becomes $\mathcal{O}(md)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This is the essence of "trading computation time for space": we accept repeated sparse matrix multiplications during training in exchange for significantly fewer trainable parameters.

**Training Runtime Overhead**

The paper notes that "the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT." For BOFT($m$,$b$), the forward pass requires $m$ sequential matrix multiplications. Compared to block-diagonal OFT with the same block size $b$ (which is equivalent to BOFT($1$,$b$)), BOFT is roughly $m$ times slower per layer during training. However, this overhead is typically modest in practice since the butterfly operations are highly structured and can leverage efficient implementations.

**No Additional Inference Latency**

The key insight here is that once finetuning completes, the factorized form can be collapsed. Since $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is just a product of matrices, we can precompute:
$$\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \mathbf{R}(m,b) \cdot \mathbf{W}^0$$

This merges the learned transformation directly into the weight matrix. At inference time, the model uses $\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ as a single matrix, completely eliminating any factorization overhead. This mirrors LoRA's approach where low-rank updates can be merged into the base weights after training.

In summary, BOFT pays a modest computational cost during training (repeated sparse matrix multiplications) to achieve superior parameter efficiency, but this cost disappears at inference time through weight fusion.

### How does the spectral norm preservation of OFT/BOFT benefit training?
Question:
The paper mentions that 'Orthogonal finetuning generally yields better spectral property than LoRA, because it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix' via singular value decomposition. I see that if $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, then $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ preserves $\bm{\Sigma}$. But why is preserving the spectral norm beneficial for 'training stability and generalization'? And does LoRA fail to preserve this property?
Answer:
The spectral norm preservation of OFT/BOFT is indeed a key theoretical advantage. Let me explain why this matters and how LoRA differs.

**Why spectral norm preservation matters**

The spectral norm (largest singular value) of a weight matrix controls the Lipschitz constant of a linear layer—essentially, how much the layer can amplify input variations. When you preserve the spectral norm during finetuning, you maintain the "scaling behavior" that the pretrained model learned.

For training stability, this is crucial because the spectral norm directly affects gradient magnitudes during backpropagation. If the spectral norm grows unchecked, gradients can explode; if it shrinks dramatically, gradients can vanish. By preserving $\|\bm{W}^0\|_2$, orthogonal finetuning ensures that the scale of signal propagation through the network remains in a regime that was already validated during pretraining.

For generalization, there's substantial empirical and theoretical evidence (cited in the paper: Miyato et al. 2018, Yoshida & Miyato 2017) that smaller spectral norms are associated with smoother functions that generalize better. Since OFT/BOFT starts from the pretrained spectral norm and cannot increase it (multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves all singular values, not just the largest), the finetuned model stays within a well-conditioned hypothesis space.

**Why LoRA fails to preserve spectral norm**

LoRA uses an additive update: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, where $\bm{B}$ and $\bm{A}$ are learned low-rank matrices. The problem is immediate from the triangle inequality for spectral norms:

$$\left|\|\bm{W}^0\|_2 - \|\bm{B}\bm{A}\|_2\right| \leq \|\bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}\|_2 \leq \|\bm{W}^0\|_2 + \|\bm{B}\bm{A}\|_2$$

The spectral norm of the finetuned weights can either increase or decrease depending on what $\bm{B}\bm{A}$ learns. There's no built-in mechanism preventing the spectral norm from drifting to values that hurt stability or generalization.

**The mathematical elegance of orthogonal transformations**

Your observation about the SVD is correct and complete. Since $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$ and $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, we have $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = (\bm{R}\bm{U})\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. The key insight is that $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is still orthogonal, so the singular value matrix $\bm{\Sigma}$ is *unchanged*. Every singular value—not just the spectral norm—is preserved.

This is fundamentally different from LoRA's additive approach, which modifies the entire singular value structure in an uncontrolled way.
