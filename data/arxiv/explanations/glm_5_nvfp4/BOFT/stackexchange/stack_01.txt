\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does OFT use multiplicative orthogonal matrices instead of additive updates like LoRA?
Question:
I'm reading the BOFT paper and trying to understand the fundamental difference between OFT and LoRA. The paper mentions that OFT reparameterizes the new weight matrix as $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ (multiplicative) while LoRA uses an additive low-rank matrix update $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$. Why does OFT choose the multiplicative approach? What advantages does this provide over the additive approach in terms of preserving pretrained knowledge?
Answer:
The fundamental reason OFT uses multiplicative orthogonal matrices is to **provably preserve the relational structure** learned during pretraining—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons.

**The Key Insight: Preserving Neuron Geometry**

When we think about what a weight matrix encodes, the columns (neurons) have learned specific angular relationships during pretraining. These angles capture semantic relationships in the representation space. As the paper explains:

> "The motivation for applying orthogonal transformation to finetune the weight matrix is to preserve the pair-wise angles of neurons, such that the semantic knowledge from pretraining can be largely preserved."

An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$, which means it performs a rotation or reflection. This operation preserves the angle between any two vectors: if $\theta$ is the angle between columns $\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{w}_j^0$ of the pretrained weight matrix, then the angle between $\bm{R}\bm{w}_i^0$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}_j^0$ remains exactly $\theta$.

**Why Additive Updates Like LoRA Can't Guarantee This**

LoRA's additive update $\bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$ has no mathematical guarantee about preserving neuron relationships. The low-rank perturbation can arbitrarily change the angles between neurons, potentially degrading the pretrained knowledge. While LoRA encourages small changes through initialization and learning rate, this is an *ad hoc* approach rather than a principled guarantee.

**Additional Advantages: Spectral Properties**

Beyond angle preservation, the multiplicative orthogonal approach has important spectral properties. Using SVD decomposition $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, when we apply an orthogonal transformation:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$$

The singular values $\bm{\Sigma}$ (and hence the spectral norm) remain unchanged. As the paper notes, this preservation "has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalization."

**Trade-offs: Parameter Efficiency vs. Expressivity**

The main drawback of multiplicative orthogonal matrices is their parameter cost—a full $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This is precisely why the paper introduces BOFT: using butterfly factorization, a dense orthogonal matrix can be constructed with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while maintaining the geometric guarantees of orthogonal finetuning.

In summary, the multiplicative approach provides mathematical guarantees about preserving pretrained knowledge through angle preservation and spectral stability, while LoRA's additive approach relies on soft constraints without such guarantees.
