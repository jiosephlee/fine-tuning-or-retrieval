\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What does 'trading computation time for space' mean in BOFT's parameterization?
Question:
The paper states that 'the number of trainable parameters can be reduced by trading computation time for space' and that 'Since we represent the orthogonal matrix with the multiplication of sparse matrices, the multiplication has to be performed repeatedly in each training iteration.' I understand that BOFT uses fewer parameters, but I'm confused about the computational overhead. How much slower is BOFT compared to block-diagonal OFT during training? And why does the paper say there's 'no additional inference latency' after finetuning?
Answer:
The phrase "trading computation time for space" refers to a fundamental design choice in BOFT's parameterization. Let me break this down.

**The Trade-off Explained**

In the original OFT, a dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. Block-diagonal OFT reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters by restricting $\mathbf{R}$ to a block-diagonal structure, but this comes at a cost: the resulting matrix is no longer dense, limiting expressiveness.

BOFT takes a different approach. Instead of storing a single dense matrix, it factorizes the orthogonal matrix into a product of $m$ sparse matrices:
$$\mathbf{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

Each butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ has only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. The total parameter count becomes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters, which for the full butterfly setting ($m = \log d$, $b = 2$) yields $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters—a dramatic reduction from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$.

The computational cost, however, increases during training. Rather than performing a single matrix-vector multiplication with a dense matrix, BOFT must sequentially multiply through $m$ sparse matrices. Each multiplication costs $\mathcal{O}(d)$, so the total cost per forward pass becomes $\mathcal{O}(md)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(d)$. This is the essence of "trading computation time for space": we accept repeated sparse matrix multiplications during training in exchange for significantly fewer trainable parameters.

**Training Runtime Overhead**

The paper notes that "the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT." For BOFT($m$,$b$), the forward pass requires $m$ sequential matrix multiplications. Compared to block-diagonal OFT with the same block size $b$ (which is equivalent to BOFT($1$,$b$)), BOFT is roughly $m$ times slower per layer during training. However, this overhead is typically modest in practice since the butterfly operations are highly structured and can leverage efficient implementations.

**No Additional Inference Latency**

The key insight here is that once finetuning completes, the factorized form can be collapsed. Since $\mathbf{R}(m,b)$ is just a product of matrices, we can precompute:
$$\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}} = \mathbf{R}(m,b) \cdot \mathbf{W}^0$$

This merges the learned transformation directly into the weight matrix. At inference time, the model uses $\mathbf{W}^{\text{finetuned}}$ as a single matrix, completely eliminating any factorization overhead. This mirrors LoRA's approach where low-rank updates can be merged into the base weights after training.

In summary, BOFT pays a modest computational cost during training (repeated sparse matrix multiplications) to achieve superior parameter efficiency, but this cost disappears at inference time through weight fusion.
