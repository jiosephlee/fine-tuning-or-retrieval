\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does multiplicative dropout work differently than standard dropout for BOFT?
Question:
The paper introduces 'multiplicative dropout' for BOFT, stating that 'conventional Dropout naturally works for LoRA, but not for BOFT due to our multiplicative weight update.' I understand that BOFT multiplies the pretrained weights by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$, while LoRA adds a low-rank update. But why does this difference necessitate a different dropout strategy? Why can't we just apply dropout to the butterfly components' trainable parameters?
Answer:
The key difference stems from how additive and multiplicative updates interact with zero-valued dropout masks.

**In LoRA**, the weight update is additive: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$. When you apply standard dropout to the low-rank matrices $\bm{B}$ or $\bm{A}$, randomly setting entries to zero means certain "update contributions" are removed. Crucially, zeroing out an additive update leaves you with the pretrained weight $\bm{W}^0$—exactly what you want during dropout. The operation $(\bm{W}^0 + \text{dropout}(\bm{B}\bm{A}))$ naturally interpolates between "applying the learned update" and "keeping the original."

**In BOFT**, the update is multiplicative: $\bm{W} = \bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0$. If you naively apply dropout to the trainable parameters within the butterfly components, you're zeroing entries of an orthogonal matrix. This is problematic for two reasons:

1. **Breaking orthogonality**: Orthogonal matrices have strict structural constraints—each row and column must be unit vectors. Randomly zeroing entries violates these constraints, potentially destabilizing training and losing the spectral preservation benefits that make OFT/BOFT effective.

2. **Semantically incorrect**: Zeroing entries in a multiplicative transformation doesn't mean "don't update"—it means "apply a different, potentially degenerate transformation." When a subset of $\bm{R}$'s entries becomes zero, the transformation no longer behaves like a gentle refinement of identity; instead, it may destroy information from $\bm{W}^0$.

The paper's multiplicative dropout addresses this by replacing selected butterfly components or diagonal blocks with **identity matrices** rather than zero masks. Since identity matrices are valid orthogonal matrices, this preserves the orthogonality constraint throughout dropout. Semantically, replacing a butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ with $\bm{I}_d$ effectively says "don't apply this component's learned transformation"—which correctly removes the update while maintaining structural integrity.

This is analogous to how dropout works in recurrent networks or transformers where careful masking preserves temporal structure—here, multiplicative dropout preserves the geometric structure that makes BOFT effective.
