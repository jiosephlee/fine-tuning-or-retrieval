\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 6: # Empirical Evaluation Across Modalities

To validate the theoretical foundations of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) and demonstrate its practical utility as a parameter-efficient finetuning method, an extensive empirical study was conducted across three major domains: computer vision, natural language processing, and generative modeling. This comprehensive evaluation serves multiple purposes. First, it tests whether the theoretical advantages of butterfly factorization translate into real-world performance gains. Second, it examines the applicability of orthogonal finetuning beyond its original application to text-to-image diffusion models. Third, it provides a direct comparison with existing state-of-the-art methods, particularly LoRA and the original OFT, to quantify the improvements in parameter efficiency and generalization ability.

The empirical study is motivated by a fundamental question that arises from the theoretical analysis: Can a dense orthogonal matrix constructed through butterfly factorization actually outperform both dense orthogonal matrices (which require many parameters) and block-diagonal orthogonal matrices (which sacrifice expressivity)? The results across all three modalities provide a resounding affirmative answer, demonstrating that BOFT successfully achieves parameter efficiency without compromising—and often improving—model performance.

#

## Experimental Setup and Benchmarks

The experimental validation of BOFT was designed to cover a diverse range of downstream tasks and model architectures, ensuring that the conclusions drawn would be broadly applicable rather than specific to any single domain. The experimental design reflects a commitment to rigorous evaluation that spans different modalities, model scales, and task types.

For the computer vision experiments, large Vision Transformers (ViTs) were chosen as the primary model architecture. This choice is significant because ViTs represent the current state-of-the-art for many vision tasks and contain hundreds of millions of parameters, making parameter-efficient finetuning particularly valuable. The vision experiments encompass both image classification tasks, where the goal is to categorize images into predefined classes, and more complex vision tasks that require fine-grained understanding of visual content.

The natural language processing experiments focus on large language models (LLMs), which have become foundational to modern NLP. The rapid increase in LLM parameter counts—exemplified by models like GPT-3 with approximately 175 billion parameters—makes efficient adaptation crucial for practical deployment. The NLP experiments test BOFT's ability to adapt pretrained language models to various downstream language understanding and generation tasks.

The text-to-image diffusion model experiments extend the evaluation to generative AI, which has emerged as a major application area for foundation models. Models like Stable Diffusion demonstrate remarkable generalization ability but require efficient methods for controlled generation. This domain is particularly relevant because the original OFT was developed specifically for controlling text-to-image diffusion models, providing a direct point of comparison for BOFT.

Across all experiments, BOFT was compared against multiple baselines including full finetuning, LoRA, and the original OFT. The comparison metrics focused on both task performance (accuracy, quality metrics appropriate to each domain) and parameter efficiency (number of trainable parameters required to achieve a given performance level). This dual focus on performance and efficiency reflects the core motivation behind parameter-efficient finetuning: achieving strong downstream task performance while minimizing the computational and memory costs of adaptation.

#

## Adapting Large Vision Transformers (ViTs)

The application of BOFT to large Vision Transformers represents a significant expansion of orthogonal finetuning beyond its original scope. Prior to this work, OFT had been applied primarily to text-to-image diffusion models, leaving its effectiveness for general vision tasks an open question. The ViT experiments demonstrate that orthogonal finetuning, particularly with the butterfly structure, serves as a powerful generic finetuning method across modalities.

Vision Transformers process images by dividing them into patches and treating these patches as tokens, analogous to words in language models. This architectural choice means that the weight matrices in ViTs are often large and dense, making them ideal candidates for parameter-efficient finetuning methods. The orthogonal transformation approach of BOFT is particularly well-suited to ViTs because it preserves the pairwise angles between neurons—a property that helps maintain the relational structure learned during pretraining.

The experimental results show that BOFT achieves superior performance compared to both LoRA and the original block-diagonal OFT when adapting ViTs to downstream vision tasks. This advantage is particularly pronounced when considering the trade-off between performance and parameter count. BOFT can achieve equivalent or better performance than block-diagonal OFT while using significantly fewer trainable parameters, validating the theoretical prediction that butterfly factorization provides better parameter efficiency without sacrificing expressivity.

The comparison with LoRA is especially instructive. While LoRA uses a low-rank additive update to modify pretrained weights, BOFT uses a multiplicative orthogonal transformation. The experimental results demonstrate that the orthogonal approach often yields better generalization, particularly on tasks that require the model to maintain its pretrained knowledge while adapting to new domains. This finding aligns with the theoretical motivation for orthogonal finetuning: by preserving pairwise angles between neurons, BOFT maintains more of the pretrained model's semantic knowledge.

## Finetuning Large Language Models (LLMs)

The application of BOFT to large language models addresses one of the most pressing challenges in modern NLP: how to efficiently adapt massive pretrained models to specific tasks without retraining from scratch. With models like GPT-3 containing hundreds of billions of parameters, even storing gradients for backpropagation becomes prohibitively expensive, making parameter-efficient methods essential.

The LLM experiments test BOFT across a variety of natural language tasks, evaluating its ability to adapt language models for both understanding tasks (such as classification and question answering) and generation tasks. This comprehensive evaluation is important because different types of NLP tasks may have different requirements for finetuning methods.

The results demonstrate that BOFT successfully adapts LLMs with performance that surpasses existing state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin. This finding validates the extension of orthogonal finetuning to language tasks, demonstrating that the benefits of preserving neuron relationships through orthogonal transformations generalize beyond vision domains. The multiplicative nature of BOFT's weight updates proves effective for language models, where maintaining the pretrained representations while allowing task-specific adaptations is crucial for performance.

A key finding from the LLM experiments concerns training stability. Orthogonal finetuning methods, including both OFT and BOFT, demonstrate more consistent and stable training compared to low-rank methods like LoRA. This stability advantage becomes increasingly important as model scale increases, where training instability can lead to catastrophic forgetting or divergence. The spectral properties of orthogonal transformations—specifically their preservation of the spectral norm of pretrained weight matrices—contribute to this training stability.

The parameter efficiency gains in LLM finetuning are particularly significant given the scale of modern language models. BOFT achieves these gains while maintaining a dense, expressive orthogonal matrix rather than resorting to the restrictive block-diagonal structure. This means that LLMs can be adapted with fewer trainable parameters while preserving the ability to make complex transformations of the pretrained weights.

## Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The text-to-image diffusion model experiments represent a direct extension of the original OFT work, which was developed specifically for controlling generative models like Stable Diffusion. These experiments serve two purposes: validating that BOFT maintains the strong performance of OFT in this domain, and demonstrating that the improved parameter efficiency does not come at the cost of control quality.

Controlling text-to-image diffusion models involves adapting a pretrained generator to produce images according to specific constraints or styles while maintaining the model's general generation capability. This task is challenging because it requires fine-grained control over the generation process without catastrophic forgetting of the pretrained knowledge that enables high-quality image synthesis.

The experimental results confirm that BOFT successfully adapts text-to-image diffusion models with performance comparable to or better than the original OFT, while using significantly fewer trainable parameters. This finding is particularly important because it demonstrates that the butterfly structure, despite introducing additional sparsity constraints, does not limit the expressiveness needed for controlling complex generative processes.

The comparison with LoRA in this domain is especially relevant, as LoRA has become a popular method for finetuning diffusion models. The results show that BOFT's orthogonal approach provides stronger generalization and more stable training compared to LoRA's low-rank additive updates. This advantage manifests in more consistent generation quality and better preservation of the pretrained model's capabilities.

These experiments also highlight an important practical consideration: after finetuning, the orthogonal matrices learned by BOFT can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model weights. This means that during inference, there is no additional computational overhead compared to the original pretrained model—a property that BOFT shares with other parameter-efficient methods but which is crucial for practical deployment of generative models.

## Comparative Analysis with LoRA and Standard OFT

A systematic comparison of BOFT against both LoRA and standard OFT provides crucial insights into the relative merits of different parameter-efficient finetuning strategies. This comparison addresses fundamental questions about how the choice of weight update parameterization affects both performance and efficiency.

LoRA represents the low-rank approach to parameter efficiency, updating pretrained weights by adding a product of two low-rank matrices. This approach has gained widespread adoption due to its simplicity and effectiveness across many tasks. The experimental comparison shows that while LoRA performs well, BOFT's orthogonal multiplicative approach often achieves better generalization, particularly on tasks where preserving pretrained knowledge is important.

The comparison with standard OFT is particularly illuminating for understanding the benefits of butterfly factorization. Standard OFT with a dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters for a weight matrix of dimension $d$, which can be prohibitive for large models. Block-diagonal OFT reduces this to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters by partitioning the weight dimensions into independent blocks, but at the cost of expressivity—block-diagonal matrices cannot represent many useful transformations.

BOFT bridges this gap by using butterfly factorization to construct dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. The empirical results demonstrate that this approach captures the benefits of both extremes: BOFT achieves parameter counts competitive with block-diagonal OFT while maintaining expressiveness closer to that of dense OFT. Specifically, the experiments show that BOFT with smaller block size $b$ and larger number of factors $m$ is generally more parameter-efficient than block-diagonal OFT.

The expressivity comparison is quantified through experiments approximating random dense orthogonal matrices of size $512 \times 512$. These simulations reveal that BOFT with appropriate parameter settings can achieve better approximation of general orthogonal matrices than block-diagonal OFT while using fewer parameters. For example, BOFT with parameters set as $\text{BOFT}(9,2)$ achieves better expressiveness than $\text{BOFT}(1,16)$—which corresponds to block-diagonal OFT with block size 16—while using many fewer parameters.

The results also highlight the different inductive biases introduced by different parameterizations. LoRA's low-rank structure assumes that weight updates can be captured in a low-dimensional subspace, which may limit the complexity of possible adaptations. BOFT's butterfly structure, in contrast, can represent many classic linear transforms including discrete Fourier transforms and Hadamard transforms—transformations that are impossible for block-diagonal matrices to represent. This structural difference contributes to BOFT's superior performance on many downstream tasks.

## Ablation Studies on Butterfly Parameters

The ablation studies on butterfly parameters provide essential insights into how the structural choices in BOFT affect performance. These studies systematically vary the key hyperparameters—the number of butterfly factors $m$ and the block size $b$—to understand their influence on both parameter efficiency and model performance.

The number of factors $m$ controls the depth of the butterfly factorization, determining how many sparse matrices are multiplied to compose the final orthogonal matrix. As $m$ increases, the composed matrix becomes denser in the sense that information can flow between more pairs of dimensions. The ablation studies show that increasing $m$ generally improves expressivity while maintaining parameter efficiency, validating the theoretical prediction that butterfly factorization enables efficient construction of expressive transformations.

The block size $b$ controls the granularity of the butterfly components. When $b = 2$, each butterfly factor operates on pairs of dimensions, creating the finest-grained butterfly structure. Larger block sizes provide coarser-grained transformations but may be computationally more efficient for each individual factor. The studies show that smaller block sizes generally lead to better parameter efficiency, as the butterfly structure can route information more flexibly through the network.

A key finding from the ablation studies concerns the trade-off between parameter count and expressivity. The results demonstrate that BOFT with $m = \log d$ and $b = 2$ achieves the theoretical minimum of $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters while still producing a dense orthogonal matrix. However, the studies also reveal that intermediate settings of $m$ and $b$ can provide useful trade-offs for specific applications.

The ablation studies also examine the effect of multiplicative dropout, a regularization technique designed specifically for BOFT's multiplicative weight update structure. Traditional dropout, which randomly zeros elements of weight matrices, is not directly applicable to orthogonal matrices. Multiplicative dropout addresses this by randomly replacing blocks of butterfly components with identity matrices during training. The results demonstrate that this form of regularization effectively prevents overfitting while being compatible with the orthogonal constraint.

The identity initialization strategy, where all butterfly components are initialized as identity matrices, is also validated through ablation. This initialization ensures that training begins with the exact pretrained model, allowing gradual deviation as needed for the downstream task. The ablation studies confirm that this initialization contributes to stable training and good final performance, aligning with the broader principle that finetuning should start close to the pretrained model to preserve learned knowledge.
