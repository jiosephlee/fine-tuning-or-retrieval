Chapter 2: # Theoretical Foundations of Orthogonal Finetuning

# Preserving Relational Information: Pairwise Angles Between Neurons

The fundamental challenge in finetuning large foundation models lies in preserving the valuable knowledge acquired during pretraining while adapting the model to new downstream tasks. When a model is pretrained on massive datasets, it learns rich representations encoded in the relationships between neurons across its layers. These relationships, particularly the geometric arrangements of neurons in the weight space, capture essential semantic information that enables the model's remarkable generalization capabilities. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) emerges from a principled consideration of how to preserve these relationships during adaptation.

At the heart of OFT's design philosophy is the insight that the relational information between neurons—specifically, the pairwise angles between them—encodes crucial semantic knowledge. Consider a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ in a pretrained model, where each column vector represents a neuron. The angle between any two neurons $\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{w}^0_j$ is determined by their dot product relative to their magnitudes: $\cos\theta_{ij} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|}$. These pairwise angles define the relative positioning of neurons in the high-dimensional weight space, forming a geometric structure that reflects the semantic relationships learned during pretraining.

The motivation for preserving these pairwise angles stems from research connecting hyperspherical energy to model generalization. When neurons maintain their angular relationships, the semantic knowledge encoded in their relative orientations remains intact. This is in contrast to approaches that modify the weight matrices without explicit constraints on the geometric structure, which risk disrupting the carefully learned representations. The key insight is that by transforming all neurons in the same layer with an identical orthogonal transformation, the pairwise angles between neurons are mathematically guaranteed to remain unchanged throughout the finetuning process.

To understand why orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles, consider what happens when an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is applied to the weight matrix. The transformed neurons become $\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i$ and $\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j$. The angle between the transformed neurons satisfies: $\cos\theta'_{ij} = \frac{(\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i)^\top (\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j)}{\|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R}\bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \frac{(\bm{w}^0_i)^\top \bm{w}^0_j}{\|\bm{w}^0_i\| \|\bm{w}^0_j\|} = \cos\theta_{ij}$, where the second equality uses the defining property of orthogonal matrices that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ and that orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms. This mathematical guarantee ensures that the semantic knowledge encoded in the geometric structure of the pretrained weights is preserved regardless of how the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ changes during finetuning.

# Mathematical Formulation of OFT

Having established the motivation for orthogonal transformations, we now turn to the precise mathematical formulation of Orthogonal Finetuning. The core idea is deceptively simple yet powerful: instead of directly modifying the pretrained weight matrix through additive updates (as in methods like LoRA), OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation that preserves the geometric structure while enabling adaptation to downstream tasks.

Consider a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In the original forward pass, given an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the output is computed as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the output vector. OFT modifies this forward pass by introducing a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms the weight matrix before the computation. The modified forward pass becomes: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. This formulation ensures that every neuron (column vector) in the weight matrix is transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, thereby guaranteeing the preservation of pairwise angles.

The distinction between OFT's multiplicative approach and LoRA's additive approach is fundamental. LoRA, which has gained tremendous popularity in parameter-efficient finetuning, updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices: $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{B}\bm{A}$, where $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $\bm{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ with rank $r \ll \min(d, n)$. While this additive approach achieves parameter efficiency through the low-rank structure, it does not explicitly preserve the geometric structure of the pretrained weights. The additive perturbation can arbitrarily modify the pairwise angles between neurons, potentially disrupting the semantic knowledge encoded in the weight geometry.

In contrast, OFT's multiplicative approach treats the adaptation problem as finding an appropriate rotation (or reflection) of the neuron space. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can rotate all neurons together while maintaining their relative positions. This is analogous to rotating an entire geometric structure as a rigid body—no matter how you rotate it, the internal angular relationships remain unchanged. The adaptability comes from the ability to choose which rotation is most suitable for the downstream task, while the guarantee of angle preservation ensures that the pretrained knowledge is not corrupted.

# Cayley Transform for Orthogonal Parameterization

A critical challenge in implementing OFT is ensuring that the learned matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire finetuning process. Simply parameterizing $\bm{R}$ directly as a $d \times d$ matrix and applying standard gradient descent would not guarantee orthogonality. Even if $\bm{R}$ were initialized as an orthogonal matrix, gradient updates would quickly break the orthogonality constraint. This necessitates a parameterization scheme that inherently constrains the parameter space to the set of orthogonal matrices.

The solution employed by OFT is the Cayley transform, a classical mathematical construction that provides a smooth mapping from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal matrices. A skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ satisfies $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, meaning all diagonal entries are zero (since $Q_{ii} = -Q_{ii}$ implies $Q_{ii} = 0$) and the off-diagonal entries come in pairs with opposite signs (since $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$). The Cayley transform defines the orthogonal matrix as: $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. This parameterization guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal for any skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ for which the inverse exists.

To verify that the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix, observe that: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = [(\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^\top)^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q}^\top)][(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}]$. Using the skew-symmetry property $\bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q}$, this becomes: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Since $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})$ and $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ commute (their product in either order equals $\bm{I} - \bm{Q}^2$), we have: $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})^{-1}(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \bm{I}$. This proves that the Cayley transform produces orthogonal matrices.

The practical advantage of the Cayley parameterization is that it reduces the optimization problem from learning $d^2$ parameters (for a dense $d \times d$ matrix) to learning only $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ parameters (for a skew-symmetric matrix). However, this is still quadratic in $d$, which remains prohibitively expensive for large models. Additionally, the Cayley transform provides a natural way to achieve zero initialization: by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as a zero matrix, the resulting orthogonal matrix becomes $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{0})(\bm{I} - \bm{0})^{-1} = \bm{I}$, the identity matrix. This ensures that the finetuning process starts exactly from the pretrained model, preventing sudden deviations from the pretrained weights at the start of training.

# The High Dimensionality Problem of Orthogonal Matrices

Despite the elegant theoretical properties of orthogonal finetuning, a practical obstacle emerges when scaling to modern foundation models: the high dimensionality of weight matrices leads to a prohibitive number of trainable parameters. A dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters even with efficient parameterizations like the Cayley transform. For context, consider that large language models have hidden dimensions $d$ ranging from thousands to tens of thousands, making direct orthogonal parameterization impractical.

To illustrate the severity of this problem, consider a single linear layer in a model with dimension $d = 4096$ (a common size for models like GPT-3's smaller variants). A dense orthogonal matrix of this size would require approximately $\frac{d(d-1)}{2} \approx 8.4$ million trainable parameters using the Cayley parameterization. A typical transformer layer contains multiple such linear transformations (attention projections, feed-forward networks), and modern foundation models have dozens to hundreds of layers. The total parameter count for orthogonal finetuning would quickly explode, defeating the purpose of parameter-efficient adaptation.

This problem highlights a fundamental tension in the design of parameter-efficient finetuning methods. On one hand, we desire the strong theoretical guarantees provided by orthogonal transformations: preservation of geometric structure, spectral norm stability, and principled weight updates. On the other hand, we need methods that scale gracefully to models with billions of parameters, where even a single layer's orthogonal matrix would be impractically large. This tension motivates the search for structured parameterizations that can reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining the benefits of orthogonal finetuning.

The natural question that arises is whether there exist parameterizations of orthogonal matrices that require far fewer than $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters while still providing sufficient expressivity for downstream task adaptation. The answer lies in introducing structure into the orthogonal matrix itself—a strategy that trades off some expressivity for significant gains in parameter efficiency. The challenge is to identify structures that reduce the parameter count without introducing undesirable biases that would harm the model's adaptation capability.

# Block-Diagonal OFT: Structure and Limitations

The original OFT paper introduces a block-diagonal structure to address the high dimensionality problem. The key insight is that instead of learning a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, we can decompose it into a block-diagonal form: $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \cdots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a smaller orthogonal matrix and $b \cdot r = d$. This structure significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, where $b$ is the block size.

To understand the parameter savings, consider a $d \times d$ matrix with block-diagonal structure of block size $b$. The total number of trainable parameters is $r \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d}{b} \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{d(b-1)}{2} \approx \frac{bd}{2}$ for large $b$. When $b \ll d$, this represents a dramatic reduction in parameters. For example, with $d = 4096$ and $b = 16$, the block-diagonal OFT requires approximately $32,768$ parameters, compared to $8.4$ million for the dense case—a reduction of over 99%.

The block-diagonal structure can be understood as dividing the $d$ dimensions of each neuron into $r$ groups and applying independent orthogonal transformations to each group. Each block $\bm{R}_i$ transforms dimensions $(i-1) \cdot b + 1$ through $i \cdot b$ while leaving other dimensions unchanged within that block's scope. The overall transformation is then the composition of these independent block transformations. When $b = d$ (a single block), block-diagonal OFT reduces to the original dense OFT. When $b = 1$, the orthogonal matrix collapses to the identity (with possible sign flips), offering no adaptation capability. The block size $b$ thus controls a trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity.

However, the block-diagonal structure introduces significant limitations. Most critically, it cannot generate a dense orthogonal matrix regardless of the parameterization used for individual blocks. The sparsity pattern is fixed: entries that connect different blocks are always zero, meaning information cannot flow between dimensions in different groups. This severely limits the expressiveness of the transformation. Furthermore, the block-diagonal structure makes it impossible to approximate many classic linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier transform or discrete cosine transform, which require dense connectivity between all dimensions.

# Inductive Biases in Block-Diagonal Structures

The block-diagonal structure, while achieving parameter efficiency, introduces an implicit inductive bias that may not align with the structure of the adaptation problem. This bias manifests in several concerning ways that can limit the effectiveness of finetuning.

The most significant inductive bias is the assumption that dimensions of a neuron should be grouped by their indices. The block-diagonal structure implicitly assumes that dimensions $1$ through $b$ form a meaningful group, dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$ form another group, and so on. However, this grouping is entirely arbitrary—it makes no semantic sense to divide the dimensions of a neuron based on their positional indices. In learned representations, dimensions do not have inherent groupings that align with this block structure; features are distributed across dimensions in complex, entangled ways that may not respect arbitrary index boundaries.

This arbitrary grouping leads to another problem: dimensions in different groups are transformed independently, meaning the method cannot learn transformations that mix information across the block boundaries. Consider a scenario where two semantically related features happen to fall in different blocks due to their indices—the block-diagonal structure would prevent the model from learning to transform these features in a coordinated way. This separation can hinder adaptation when downstream tasks require global transformations of the neuron space.

The fixed sparsity pattern also reduces expressiveness. The block-diagonal orthogonal matrix has a specific structure—diagonal blocks that are dense, off-diagonal blocks that are all zeros—that cannot be changed during training. This means the hypothesis class of possible transformations is severely constrained. As a concrete example, consider trying to approximate a rotation matrix that mixes all dimensions evenly (like those used in principal component analysis). The block-diagonal structure fundamentally cannot represent such transformations, regardless of how many parameters are allocated to each block.

An additional concern is that the choice of block size $b$ becomes a critical hyperparameter that is difficult to set optimally. Too small a block size yields parameter efficiency but poor expressivity; too large a block size improves expressivity but increases parameters. The paper notes that "how to find a good sparsity pattern remains a mystery"—there is no principled way to determine what block structure would be appropriate for a given downstream task. This stands in contrast to the butterfly structure proposed in BOFT, which provides a more principled approach to constructing dense orthogonal matrices with logarithmic parameter complexity while introducing inductive biases that align with classical fast linear transforms.
