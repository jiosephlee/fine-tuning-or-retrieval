\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Foundation Model Adaptation

# The Era of Large Foundation Models

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a dramatic transformation with the emergence of large foundation models. Models such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have demonstrated remarkable generalization abilities, showcasing the power of training on massive datasets and scaling up model parameters. These foundation models serve as powerful starting points that can be adapted to a wide variety of downstream tasks, representing a paradigm shift in how we approach machine learning problems.

This revolution in capability, however, has come with a significant cost. The relationship between model performance and model size has become increasingly apparent. For instance, GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters, and this is just one example among many increasingly large models. The rapid increase in the performance of such models is paired with a dramatic increase in the number of parameters, making these models what we now call "ubiquitous" foundation models. They are becoming the backbone of modern AI applications, serving as the base upon which specialized systems are built.

The scale of these models fundamentally changes the research and deployment landscape. When models were smaller, the standard practice was to train them from scratch for each new task. With foundation models, this approach has become prohibitively expensive. The computational resources, time, and financial investment required to train a model like GPT-3 or Stable Diffusion from scratch place such endeavors beyond the reach of most researchers and practitioners. This reality has necessitated a fundamental rethinking of how we approach the development of AI systems, shifting the focus from training models from scratch to adapting existing pretrained models to new tasks.

# The Challenge of Adaptation: Training Costs vs. Downstream Tasks

The prohibitive cost of training foundation models from scratch presents a fundamental challenge: how can we leverage the power of these models for specific downstream tasks without the enormous expense of full retraining? This question has become central to modern machine learning research and practice. Broad progress in the field now depends critically on the ability to adapt existing foundation models to downstream tasks efficiently.

The challenge is multifaceted. On one hand, foundation models encode vast amounts of knowledge from their pretraining on massive datasets. This knowledge includes linguistic understanding, visual representations, and complex patterns that would be extremely costly to learn anew. On the other hand, downstream tasks often have specific requirements that differ from the general pretraining objectives. The task of adapting a general-purpose model to a specialized application while preserving its pretrained knowledge forms the core challenge of efficient model adaptation.

The fundamental principle behind successful model adaptation is ensuring that the pretrained model and the adapted model remain similar based on certain measurements. This similarity constraint serves to preserve the pretraining knowledge. For example, many current model finetuning methods employ a small learning rate, an approach that encourages a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned model states. While this heuristic approach can be effective, it lacks a principled theoretical foundation. Given the structured nature of weight matrices in neural networks, more principled approaches seek to preserve the relational information encoded in these matrices—specifically, the pairwise angles between neurons. This insight has led to novel adaptation frameworks that provide stronger theoretical guarantees about knowledge preservation during the adaptation process.

# Overview of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) Methods

The field of parameter-efficient finetuning has emerged as a vibrant area of research, driven by the necessity to adapt large foundation models without the prohibitive costs of full model training. The core idea behind PEFT methods is straightforward yet powerful: instead of updating all parameters of a massive model, we update only a small subset or introduce a small number of new parameters while keeping the majority of the pretrained model frozen.

Among the many PEFT methods that have been proposed, reparameterization-based methods are particularly relevant to the study of orthogonal finetuning. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) represents one of the most influential approaches in this category. LoRA updates the pretrained weight matrix by adding a product of two low-rank matrices, achieving promising performance on natural language tasks while requiring significantly fewer trainable parameters than full finetuning. In LoRA, the rank of the added matrices is typically set to a constant value, and several subsequent methods have proposed dynamically adjusting this rank for different layers to better allocate the parameter budget.

The popularity of low-rank weight reparameterization stems from its simplicity and effectiveness. This approach has gained widespread adoption across various applications and model types. However, reparameterization-based methods extend beyond low-rank approaches. Some methods have drawn inspiration from how hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization, leading to orthogonal finetuning approaches that offer an alternative paradigm to low-rank adaptation. The key insight is that orthogonal transformations can provide stronger generalization and more stable training than additive low-rank updates, though this often comes at the cost of more trainable parameters.

The landscape of PEFT methods continues to evolve, with researchers exploring various strategies to balance parameter efficiency with model expressivity. The fundamental trade-off in all these methods remains the same: how can we achieve strong performance on downstream tasks while modifying as few parameters as possible? This question drives ongoing research and has led to diverse approaches, each with its own strengths and limitations.

# Model Finetuning vs. Adapter Tuning vs. Prompt Tuning

There are primarily three approaches to performing efficient task adaptation, each with distinct characteristics and trade-offs. Understanding these three paradigms is essential for appreciating where orthogonal finetuning fits within the broader landscape of adaptation methods.

Model finetuning represents the most straightforward approach. In this paradigm, the model architecture remains unchanged, and a subset of the model parameters is updated during the finetuning process. This approach distinguishes itself through its simplicity and power, and importantly, it introduces no inference latency. Because the model structure is preserved and only parameter values change, the finetuned model operates at the same speed as the original pretrained model during deployment. This lack of inference overhead makes model finetuning particularly attractive for production systems where latency matters.

Adapter tuning takes a different approach. Rather than modifying the original model parameters, this method adds additional trainable parameters to the original model. These adapter modules are inserted into the pretrained model and trained for the downstream task while the original parameters remain frozen. This approach offers modularity—different adapters can be trained for different tasks and swapped as needed—but comes at the cost of increased model complexity and potential inference latency due to the added adapter layers.

Prompt tuning represents yet another paradigm. In this approach, additional trainable prefix tokens are attached to the input. These tokens are learned to guide the model toward the desired behavior for the downstream task. Prompt tuning is particularly elegant because it leaves the model parameters entirely untouched, but it may require careful design and can be sensitive to initialization and hyperparameter choices.

Among these three approaches, model finetuning stands out for its combination of simplicity, effectiveness, and lack of inference overhead. The methods discussed in this book, including Orthogonal Finetuning and Orthogonal Butterfly, fall within the model finetuning paradigm. They update the model through multiplicative transformations applied to the weight matrices, preserving the efficiency advantages of model finetuning while introducing principled constraints that help preserve pretrained knowledge.

# Introduction to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

Orthogonal Finetuning represents a principled approach to model adaptation that draws on theoretical insights about how knowledge is encoded in neural network weights. The fundamental insight behind OFT is that the pairwise angles between neurons in a layer encode meaningful relational information that should be preserved during finetuning. This insight connects to research showing that hyperspherical energy characterizes generalization in neural networks.

Concretely, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$. The forward pass is modified from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ represent the input and output vectors, respectively. This multiplicative update contrasts with LoRA's additive approach of updating weights with a low-rank matrix. The key mathematical property is that orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles: when neurons (column vectors of the weight matrix) are transformed by the same orthogonal matrix, the angles between them remain unchanged.

To achieve a proper starting point for finetuning, OFT initializes $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix. This zero initialization ensures that the finetuned model starts exactly at the pretrained model state, preventing large deviations at the start of training. To guarantee that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the finetuning process, OFT employs Cayley parameterization. Specifically, the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This parameterization ensures that $\bm{R}$ is always orthogonal regardless of how the trainable parameters in $\bm{Q}$ are updated.

The motivation for applying orthogonal transformation to finetune the weight matrix is deeply rooted in the goal of preserving semantic knowledge from pretraining. By maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons, OFT provably preserves the relational structure encoded in the weight matrices. This principled approach has demonstrated strong generalizability and convergence for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, showing that the theoretical benefits translate to practical improvements in model adaptation.

# The Trade-off in OFT: Parameter Efficiency vs. Expressivity

Despite the strong theoretical foundation and empirical performance of Orthogonal Finetuning, it faces a significant practical challenge: the number of trainable parameters can be quite large due to the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which can be prohibitive when adapting large models where weight matrices have thousands or tens of thousands of dimensions.

To address this parameter efficiency concern, OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and the block dimensions satisfy $br=d$. This block-diagonal approach reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$, where $b$ is the block size. The smaller the block size $b$, the more parameter-efficient OFT becomes.

However, this parameter efficiency comes at a substantial price. The block-diagonal structure introduces an assumption that the dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ groups, with dimensions in different groups transformed independently using different orthogonal matrices. This fixed sparsity pattern creates several problems. First, it reduces expressiveness: the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms. Second, the independent transformation of blocks may introduce undesirable inductive biases, as there is no principled reason to divide neuron dimensions into groups based on their indices. Third, the question of how to find a good sparsity pattern remains largely unexplored—why should dimensions 1 through $b$ form one group, dimensions $b+1$ through $2b$ form another, and so on?

This trade-off between parameter efficiency and expressivity creates a fundamental tension in OFT. A block-diagonal OFT with block size $b=d$ generates a dense orthogonal matrix with full expressivity but requires many parameters. Reducing the block size improves parameter efficiency but sacrifices the ability to model complex transformations that involve interactions across different dimension groups. The goal of achieving a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining parameter efficiency seems infeasible at first glance, since generating such a matrix would naively require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters. This apparent impossibility raises a natural question: can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix without losing parameter efficiency? The answer, as we will explore in subsequent chapters, lies in composing dense orthogonal matrices from multiple factorized sparse matrices through the elegant structure of butterfly factorization.
