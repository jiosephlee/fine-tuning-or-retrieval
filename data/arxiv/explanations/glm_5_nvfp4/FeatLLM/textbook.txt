# Introduction to LLM-Enhanced Tabular Learning

# The Rise of Large Language Models and Their Reasoning Capabilities

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed a remarkable transformation with the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, have demonstrated an impressive ability to generate suitable responses for previously unseen tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. This capability, often referred to as in-context learning, represents a paradigm shift in how we approach machine learning problems. By incorporating a task description and a few examples into prompts, LLMs can understand the context of a task and "attend" to important features and examples, showcasing their remarkable reasoning capabilities.

The power of LLMs stems from their rich prior knowledge accumulated during training on massive text datasets. This knowledge can substitute for the need for expert domain knowledge in many areas, playing a crucial role in the automation of diverse applications. For instance, LLMs have been successfully applied to dialogue analysis and program repair, demonstrating their versatility across different domains. The ability to tackle challenging and unseen reasoning problems positions LLMs as a transformative technology with immense potential for various machine learning applications, including the domain of tabular learning that forms the focus of this chapter.

A key aspect of LLM reasoning is their capacity for what researchers call "chain-of-thought" processing. When presented with a problem, LLMs can be guided through structured reasoning paths that mimic how an expert human might approach a task. This structured approach allows the models to filter out uninformative features and focus on more significant aspects of the problem. The emergent abilities of LLMs—their capacity to perform tasks that were not explicitly trained—have opened new possibilities for applying these models to domains that traditionally relied heavily on domain expertise and feature engineering.

# The Importance of Tabular Data in Real-World Machine Learning Applications

Tabular data—data organized in rows and columns—remains the dominant format in real-world machine learning applications. From financial risk assessment to healthcare diagnostics, from customer churn prediction to scientific research, tabular datasets form the backbone of decision-making systems across virtually every industry. The structured nature of tabular data, with its clearly defined features and target variables, makes it particularly suited for supervised learning tasks where the goal is to predict outcomes based on input characteristics.

The significance of tabular learning extends beyond its ubiquity. In many critical applications, the relationships between features and targets are governed by domain-specific knowledge that practitioners have accumulated over years of study. For example, in healthcare, the knowledge that older individuals face increased risk of certain diseases is invaluable for disease prediction tasks. Similarly, in finance, understanding the factors that influence credit risk or market behavior requires expertise that has traditionally been the domain of specialists. This is precisely where the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs becomes relevant—the extensive text corpora used to train these models contain vast amounts of information about relationships, causes, and effects that could potentially benefit tabular learning tasks.

However, despite its importance, tabular data presents unique challenges that distinguish it from other data modalities like images or text. Unlike the homogeneous structure of image pixels or word tokens, tabular features are heterogeneous—they may be numerical, categorical, or ordinal, each requiring different treatment. The features also have semantic meaning encoded in their names and definitions, which can provide valuable context for learning algorithms. This heterogeneity and semantic richness make tabular data both challenging to work with and uniquely positioned to benefit from the knowledge captured in language models.

# Challenges in Few-Shot Learning: Data Scarcity and Spurious Correlations

Few-shot learning—the ability to learn from a small number of labeled samples—has emerged as a critical capability in modern machine learning. The advancement of few-shot learning has enabled the extraction of generalizable representations from data, even with minimal labeling costs. While initially focusing on image data, few-shot learning has proven its strong generalizability across various data modalities including text, audio, and, more recently, tabular data. However, learning from limited samples introduces significant challenges that are particularly pronounced in the tabular domain.

The primary challenge in few-shot learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations. When the number of labeled samples is small relative to the number of features, there is an increased likelihood that the model will identify patterns that exist in the training data by chance rather than reflecting true underlying relationships. These spurious correlations lead to models that perform well on training data but fail to generalize to unseen samples. In the tabular setting, where datasets may contain dozens or hundreds of features, this problem is exacerbated—the ratio of samples to features becomes unfavorable, and the model has insufficient information to distinguish meaningful patterns from noise.

To address these challenges, recent research efforts have employed additional sources of information or introduced domain-specific prior knowledge to impart appropriate inductive biases for model training. Approaches have included utilizing large quantities of more readily-available unlabeled datasets with judicious augmentation for semi-supervised learning, or seeking good model initialization through task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training. Unsupervised objectives can involve masking or corrupting part of the data and then using a reconstruction objective, or employing data augmentation to apply contrastive learning objectives. However, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data makes it challenging to find universally applicable augmentations, and these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings. This limitation motivates the exploration of alternative approaches that can leverage external knowledge sources—such as that embedded in LLMs—to guide learning when data is scarce.

# Current Limitations of LLM-Based Tabular Learning: Inference Costs, Prompt Size Limits, and Training Constraints

The application of Large Language Models to tabular learning has generated significant interest, with researchers exploring methods to harness the rich prior knowledge encapsulated by these models. Current approaches typically propose serializing tabular data into text and feeding them into the input prompts of LLMs. By including tabular data, feature descriptions, and task descriptions in the input prompts, these methods can infer the relationships between tasks and features to perform predictions. Research has evolved to either specifically train models using parameter-efficient tuning techniques like LoRA or IA3, or employ in-context learning by adding few-shot example demonstrations to the prompts without training.

Despite the effectiveness of these methods in improving few-shot tabular learning, they suffer from fundamental limitations that pose challenges for real-world application. The first major constraint is computational cost: for end-to-end predictions, at least one LLM inference per sample is required, making it computationally expensive. This per-sample inference requirement becomes particularly problematic in industrial settings where low latency and high throughput are essential. Unlike traditional machine learning models that can make predictions efficiently once trained, LLM-based approaches require passing each new sample through the entire model, incurring significant time and computational resources.

A second critical limitation concerns accessibility and training constraints. Most current methods require fine-tuning the LLM for high accuracy, making them infeasible to apply to LLMs that do not come with accessible training codebases or tuning APIs. This is a prominent concern given that recently-proposed top-performance LLMs, such as GPT-4 and PaLM, only permit limited access via inference APIs. Practitioners cannot fine-tune these models even if they wanted to, limiting the applicability of approaches that depend on parameter updates. Furthermore, most approaches are not suitable with lengthy prompts—when the number of features in tabular data grows to the point where the serialized text length becomes long, the approach often turns infeasible or experiences a decline in performance, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as being "lost in the middle." These constraints pose fundamental challenges to the real-world application of LLM-based tabular learning models and motivate the search for alternative approaches.

# The Paradigm Shift: Treating LLMs as Feature Engineers Rather Than End-to-End Predictors

Given the limitations of current LLM-based tabular learning approaches, a fundamental rethinking of how we utilize these models is necessary. Rather than treating LLMs as end-to-end predictors—where each sample passes through the model to generate a prediction—we can assign them the role of feature engineers. This paradigm shift represents a more efficient way to utilize the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs while avoiding the computational and accessibility constraints of existing methods.

The key insight behind this approach is that LLMs can directly infer and generate rules that determine which feature conditions result in identifying each class. For instance, for the task of predicting a particular disease, the LLM can infer criteria such as "if the patient is over 65 years old AND has high blood pressure, then the risk of the disease is elevated." These criteria, or rules, can then be used to create new features that replace or augment existing ones in solving tasks. This approach shifts the capability of the LLM from prediction to feature generation, yielding a simpler downstream model—once the features are discovered, a complex machine learning model is no longer needed for inference, significantly improving inference latency.

This feature engineering perspective offers several conceptual advantages. First, it separates the knowledge extraction phase—which requires the LLM's sophisticated reasoning capabilities—from the prediction phase—which can be accomplished with a simple, efficient model. By leveraging the in-context learning ability of LLMs, it becomes possible to extract features without needing model training, simply by adding example demonstrations to the prompts. Second, by employing techniques from ensemble methods, like feature bagging, the challenge of excessive prompt size can be mitigated. When tabular data exceed the allowable input prompt size, bagging helps reduce it, ensuring robust performance. The paradigm thus addresses the key limitations of existing approaches while preserving the benefits of LLM-generated knowledge.

# Overview of FeatLLM Objectives: Efficiency, Interpretability, and Performance

The FeatLLM framework embodies this paradigm shift, introducing a novel in-context learning approach that employs LLMs as feature engineers to produce an input dataset that is optimally suited for tabular predictions. The framework is designed with three primary objectives in mind: computational efficiency, practical accessibility, and strong predictive performance.

The first objective, efficiency, is achieved by eliminating the need to send queries to the LLM for each sample at inference time. Unlike existing LLM-based approaches that require at least one LLM inference per prediction, FeatLLM uses the LLM only during the feature generation phase. The generated features are then used to infer class likelihood with a simple downstream machine learning model, such as linear regression, yielding high performance few-shot learning. At inference time, only this simple predictive model with the discovered features is used, resulting in computational costs comparable to conventional machine learning methods.

The second objective addresses accessibility constraints. FeatLLM merely requires API-level access to LLMs, making it applicable to the many top-performance models that only provide inference APIs without training access. By structuring the input prompt to guide the LLM through a two-step reasoning process—first understanding the problem and inferring relationships between features and targets, then deriving decisive rules—the framework extracts high-quality features without requiring any model fine-tuning. The framework also overcomes prompt size limitations through its ensemble approach with bagging, selecting subsets of features or instances for each trial to ensure robust performance even on datasets with numerous features.

The third objective is performance. Across numerous tabular datasets from a wide range of domains, FeatLLM generates high-quality rules that significantly—10% on average—outperform alternatives such as TabLLM and STUNT. The framework demonstrates that LLMs proficiently utilize both prior knowledge and the knowledge from the data, extracting features that are informative for the target task. Using just 4 shots, FeatLLM achieves performance comparable to conventional tabular baselines that require 32 shots, demonstrating remarkable data efficiency. By combining the prior knowledge embedded in LLMs with principled feature engineering and ensemble techniques, FeatLLM represents a significant advancement in few-shot tabular learning.

# Foundations: Few-Shot Learning and Language-Interfaced Models

# Overview of Few-Shot Learning Strategies

Few-shot learning represents a fundamental paradigm shift in machine learning, where the goal is to extract generalizable representations from data using only a small number of labeled samples. This capability is particularly valuable in real-world scenarios where labeling data requires substantial human effort, specialized domain expertise, or significant financial resources. The advancement of few-shot learning has enabled models to achieve strong generalization even with minimal labeling costs, a capability that was initially demonstrated on image data before being extended to text, audio, and more recently, tabular data.

However, learning from a small number of labeled samples presents inherent challenges. The primary risk is that models may learn spurious correlations—patterns that appear meaningful in the limited training data but do not generalize to unseen examples. With insufficient data to properly constrain the learning process, models can easily overfit to noise or irrelevant features, leading to poor performance on new samples. Consequently, recent research efforts have focused on introducing additional sources of information or incorporating domain-specific prior knowledge to impart appropriate inductive biases for model training.

One prominent strategy involves semi-supervised learning, which leverages large quantities of more readily-available unlabeled datasets alongside a small set of labeled examples. Methods such as SubTab and VIME employ judicious augmentation strategies to utilize unlabeled data effectively. The intuition behind this approach is that unlabeled data, while lacking explicit labels, still contains valuable information about the underlying structure and distribution of the data. By learning from this additional data, models can develop better representations that transfer to the labeled few-shot task.

Another approach seeks good model initialization through task-agnostic unsupervised pre-training. These unsupervised objectives can take various forms. Some methods involve masking or corrupting part of the data and then using a reconstruction objective to train the model. For instance, TabNet and MET employ such reconstruction-based approaches, where the model must learn to predict or reconstruct missing portions of the input. Other methods apply data augmentation to enable contrastive learning objectives, as seen in SCARF, which uses data augmentation to create pairs of similar and dissimilar examples for representation learning. However, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data—where features can be numerical, categorical, or text, each with different scales and semantics—makes it challenging to find universally applicable augmentations. Unfortunately, these strategies have not been notably effective in few-shot settings, as the augmentation strategies designed for other data modalities do not translate well to the tabular domain.

Transfer learning approaches have emerged as another promising direction for few-shot tabular learning. These methods train models on a broad range of tabular datasets unrelated to the target task and then apply transfer learning to the target task. The key insight is that knowledge about how to process and learn from tabular data can transfer across different domains and tasks. For example, TabPFN takes a unique approach by mixing different types of distributions to create synthetic data for pre-training a Bayesian neural network. After this pre-training phase, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task without requiring further training. The prior knowledge gained from various synthetic datasets has helped this approach surpass the performance of traditional tree-based methods, demonstrating the value of pre-training for few-shot settings.

# Language-Interfaced Tabular Learning

A promising approach to leverage prior knowledge for tabular learning involves the incorporation of large language models (LLMs). LLMs, trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, have demonstrated remarkable abilities to generalize to unseen tasks, proving their utility across various domains. The key insight behind language-interfaced tabular learning is that the rich prior knowledge encapsulated by LLMs—derived from their training on vast amounts of text—can be harnessed to improve predictions on tabular data.

Recent studies have proposed serializing tabular data into text and feeding them into the input prompts of LLMs. The fundamental challenge in this approach is that tabular data and language exist in fundamentally different formats. Tabular data consists of structured rows and columns with numerical and categorical values, while LLMs are designed to process natural language text. Serialization bridges this gap by converting tabular data into a text format that LLMs can understand.

The serialization process follows a structured format that has been adopted in previous studies. For a given sample with features $\mathbf{x}^i$ and label $\mathbf{y}^i$, along with feature names $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, the serialization can be expressed as:

$$\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\  \text{is}\  \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\  \mathbf{y}^i\ ”$$

This format presents each feature name followed by its value, creating a natural language description of the sample. For example, a patient record might be serialized as: "Age is 65. Blood pressure is 140/90. Cholesterol is high. Answer: High risk." By including tabular data, feature descriptions, and task descriptions in the input prompts, LLMs can infer the relationships between tasks and features to perform inference. The feature descriptions provide semantic meaning to otherwise abstract column names, helping the LLM understand what each feature represents in the real world.

# In-Context Learning versus Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

When applying LLMs to tabular learning tasks, two primary approaches have emerged: in-context learning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. These approaches represent fundamentally different ways of adapting LLMs to specific tasks, each with distinct advantages and limitations.

In-context learning operates by adding few-shot example demonstrations to the prompts without training the model. This approach leverages the emergent abilities of LLMs to understand and perform tasks based solely on examples provided in the prompt, without any modification to the model's parameters. The LLM reads the examples, understands the pattern, and applies this understanding to make predictions on new samples. This approach is appealing because it requires no model training, making it accessible even when model parameters are not available. However, a significant limitation is that each sample at inference time requires a separate query to the LLM, making predictions computationally expensive at scale. Additionally, in-context learning faces challenges when dealing with lengthy prompts—when the number of features in tabular data grows to the point where the serialized text length becomes long, the approach often becomes infeasible or experiences a decline in performance.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, on the other hand, modifies a small subset of the LLM's parameters to adapt it to the specific task. Techniques such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) and IA3 (Infused Adapter by Inhibiting and Amplifying Inner Activations) have been developed to enable efficient tuning without modifying the entire model. These methods add a small number of trainable parameters to the model while keeping the majority of pre-trained parameters frozen. This approach can achieve higher accuracy than in-context learning for specific tasks but introduces additional requirements. Most notably, it requires access to the model's training infrastructure, making it infeasible when working with LLMs that only permit limited access via inference APIs. This concern is particularly relevant given that recently-proposed top-performance LLMs often do not provide publicly available checkpoints or tuning APIs.

The choice between these approaches involves trade-offs between accessibility, computational cost, and performance. In-context learning requires no training but incurs high inference costs and may struggle with complex or high-dimensional data. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning can achieve better performance but requires model access and training infrastructure. Both approaches represent significant steps toward leveraging LLMs for tabular learning, but both also face fundamental challenges in real-world applications, including computational expense at inference time and limitations related to prompt size and model accessibility.

# Key Baselines in Few-Shot Tabular Learning

Understanding the landscape of existing methods is essential for appreciating the contributions of new approaches. Several key baselines have emerged in the literature, each representing different strategies for addressing few-shot tabular learning.

TabLLM represents one of the most prominent approaches in language-interfaced tabular learning. This method employs parameter-efficient tuning techniques, specifically IA3, to train the LLM with few-shot samples. The approach serializes tabular data into text, includes feature and task descriptions, and fine-tunes the language model to make predictions. However, TabLLM restricts the use of LLMs to only those with publicly available checkpoints, meaning that state-of-the-art commercial LLMs accessible only through APIs cannot be used with this approach. This limitation arises because fine-tuning requires access to the model's internal parameters and training infrastructure, which is not provided by API-only services.

TabPFN takes a fundamentally different approach based on Bayesian inference. Rather than leveraging language models, TabPFN mixes different types of distributions to create synthetic data for pre-training a Bayesian neural network. The pre-training phase exposes the model to a wide variety of potential data distributions, allowing it to develop general-purpose priors for tabular learning. After pre-training, inference is performed by inputting both training and test samples of the target task without requiring further training. The prior knowledge gained from various synthetic datasets has helped this approach surpass the performance of traditional tree-based approaches, demonstrating that well-designed pre-training can effectively address few-shot learning challenges.

STUNT addresses few-shot tabular learning through semi-supervised learning, assuming access to unlabeled datasets. By leveraging information from unlabeled samples, STUNT aims to improve model generalization beyond what would be possible with limited labeled data alone. However, the requirement for unlabeled datasets may be infeasible in many real-world applications where collecting any data, labeled or unlabeled, presents challenges.

TransTab approaches the problem through transfer learning across tabular datasets. This method trains transformer-based models to learn semantic relationships between columns through their names, not just the table values. The insight is that column names carry semantic meaning that can inform how features relate to predictions. For example, a column named "age" carries different implications than one named "blood_pressure," and understanding these semantic differences can improve learning. The knowledge extracted from various tables and columns facilitates zero-shot or few-shot inference for new tabular tasks, as the model has learned general patterns about how tabular data is structured and how different types of features typically relate to outcomes.

# Prior Knowledge in Large Language Models

The concept of prior knowledge in LLMs forms the theoretical foundation for their application in tabular learning. Large language models are trained on extensive and diverse text corpora, absorbing an enormous amount of information across countless domains. This training imbues LLMs with rich prior knowledge that can substitute for expert domain knowledge in many areas, playing a crucial role in the automation of diverse applications.

The prior knowledge encapsulated in LLMs derives from their exposure to vast amounts of text during training. This text includes scientific literature, medical records, financial reports, news articles, and countless other sources spanning virtually every domain of human knowledge. As a result, LLMs develop an understanding of relationships, patterns, and regularities that exist in the world. For example, an LLM may learn from medical literature that older individuals face increased risk of certain diseases, or from financial texts that certain indicators correlate with credit risk.

This prior knowledge becomes particularly valuable in domain-specific tasks where expert knowledge would otherwise be required. In healthcare applications, LLMs have demonstrated capabilities relevant to disease prediction and patient assessment. In financial applications, their knowledge about economic indicators and risk factors can inform credit decisions. The potential for LLMs to serve as repositories of domain knowledge means that practitioners can leverage this information without requiring deep expertise in every relevant domain.

The application of prior knowledge to tabular learning represents a significant innovation. Traditional machine learning models must learn all relationships from the training data alone, which becomes problematic when training samples are scarce. By contrast, LLMs bring pre-existing knowledge about how features typically relate to outcomes. When predicting a particular disease, for example, the LLM already understands that factors like age, lifestyle, and medical history are relevant, and it can focus attention on these features even without extensive training examples.

However, the application of LLM prior knowledge also raises important considerations about the source and quality of this knowledge. The prior knowledge embedded in LLMs reflects patterns present in their training data, which may include biases, inaccuracies, or outdated information. In domain-specific applications, particularly those involving sensitive decisions in healthcare or finance, the impact of these biases must be carefully considered. Future work analyzing the influence of societal biases and misinformation embedded in LLM prior knowledge will be essential for responsible deployment in real-world applications.

# The FeatLLM Methodology: An Architectural Overview

# Problem Formulation: Defining Datasets, Feature Names, and Labels for K-Shot Learning

To understand the FeatLLM framework, we must first establish the formal problem setting in which it operates. The framework addresses the challenge of tabular learning, a fundamental task in machine learning where data is organized in a structured format with rows representing individual samples and columns representing features or attributes. This stands in contrast to unstructured data like images or raw text, and tabular learning remains vital for many real-world applications in finance, healthcare, and business analytics.

Consider a tabular dataset comprising $N$ labeled samples, which we denote as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Each sample consists of an input feature vector $\mathbf{x}^i$ that is $d$-dimensional, meaning it contains $d$ distinct features or attributes. The label $\mathbf{y}^i$ represents the target variable we wish to predict, and in the classification setup that FeatLLM addresses, this label belongs to a predefined set of classes. The framework assumes a classification task where the goal is to assign each input sample to one of several possible categories.

What distinguishes tabular data from other data modalities, and what FeatLLM leverages, is the semantic meaning carried by feature names. The dataset comes with natural language feature names, denoted by $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, along with their definitions provided separately. For instance, in a medical dataset, feature names might include "age," "blood pressure," or "cholesterol level," each carrying semantic meaning that a language model can interpret and reason about. This semantic richness is crucial because it allows the LLM to apply its pre-existing knowledge about relationships between features and outcomes.

The k-shot learning paradigm introduces an additional constraint that makes the problem particularly challenging. Rather than having access to all $N$ labeled samples for training, only $k$ samples are randomly sampled, where $k < N$ and typically $k$ is quite small. This setting, known as few-shot learning, presents the risk of learning spurious correlations—patterns that appear meaningful in the small training set but do not generalize to the broader population. The constraint reflects real-world scenarios where obtaining labeled data is expensive, time-consuming, or requires expert annotation, such as in medical diagnosis or financial risk assessment.

In the experiments conducted by the authors, the value of $k$ ranges from as few as 2 to 32 shots, demonstrating the framework's capability to perform meaningful learning even with extremely limited supervision. This regime is where conventional machine learning methods often struggle, as they typically require substantial amounts of labeled data to learn accurate models without overfitting. The few-shot setting demands that the learning system incorporate prior knowledge or inductive biases that compensate for the scarcity of labeled examples.

# The Core Philosophy: Extracting Rules Instead of Direct Predictions

The central innovation of FeatLLM lies in reconceptualizing the role that large language models play in the prediction pipeline. Rather than treating LLMs as end-to-end predictors that directly output answers for each input sample, FeatLLM assigns them the role of feature engineers. This philosophical shift has profound implications for both the efficiency and interpretability of the resulting system.

When an LLM is asked to make a prediction directly, it must process each sample through its entire computational graph at inference time. For a tabular dataset with millions of samples, this means invoking the LLM millions of times, incurring substantial computational costs and latency. Moreover, this black-box approach provides little insight into why particular predictions are made, making it difficult to debug, validate, or trust the system's outputs. The LLM becomes an inscrutable oracle whose reasoning remains hidden.

FeatLLM takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of asking "what is the prediction for this sample?", it asks "what criteria determine whether a sample belongs to a particular class?" This shift from instance-level prediction to rule extraction represents a more sophisticated use of the LLM's capabilities. The LLM is tasked with understanding and articulating the underlying logic that connects features to outcomes. For example, when predicting heart disease, the LLM might generate rules such as "if age is greater than 60 AND blood pressure exceeds 140, then high risk" or "if cholesterol level is above 240 AND family history is positive, then high risk."

These rules, which the paper refers to as the "criteria" underlying predictions, serve as the foundation for feature engineering. By converting the LLM's knowledge into explicit, interpretable rules, FeatLLM creates a transparent representation of the reasoning process. Each rule can be examined, validated, and modified by domain experts, fostering trust and enabling collaboration between human experts and machine learning systems. This interpretability is particularly valuable in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where understanding the basis for predictions is essential for clinical decision-making.

The extraction of rules also enables a clean separation between the knowledge-intensive reasoning process and the statistical learning process. The LLM contributes its vast prior knowledge—gleaned from training on extensive text corpora spanning diverse domains—to propose plausible relationships between features and outcomes. A simple downstream machine learning model then learns the relative importance of these rules from the limited labeled data. This division of labor allows each component to do what it does best: the LLM provides domain knowledge and reasoning capabilities, while the statistical model provides data-driven calibration.

Furthermore, this approach addresses a fundamental limitation of LLM-based tabular learning methods. Current approaches require at least one LLM inference per sample for end-to-end predictions, making them computationally expensive for real-world deployment. By extracting rules once and reusing them for all samples, FeatLLM reduces the LLM's role to a preprocessing step, dramatically reducing inference costs while still benefiting from the LLM's knowledge.

# Pipeline Overview: From Prompting to Model Training

The FeatLLM pipeline consists of several interconnected stages that transform raw tabular data into accurate predictions. Understanding this end-to-end workflow is essential for appreciating how the framework achieves its goals of efficiency, interpretability, and strong performance in few-shot settings.

The first stage involves carefully designing prompts that guide the LLM toward extracting meaningful rules. The prompt comprises three main components: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction. The basic information description provides essential context for solving the problem, including a description of the task formulated as a question (such as "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure?"), descriptions of each feature including value types and optional definitions, and example demonstrations where few training samples are serialized into text along with their ground-truth labels.

The serialization of training samples follows a consistent format: $``\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\  \text{is}\  \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\  \mathbf{y}^i\ ''$. This format presents each feature name and its corresponding value in natural language, followed by the correct answer. For example, a sample might be serialized as "age is 65. blood_pressure is 150. cholesterol is 280. Answer: positive." This serialization enables the LLM to process tabular data using its natural language understanding capabilities.

The reasoning instruction guides the LLM through a structured two-step reasoning process. In the first step, the LLM is encouraged to infer causal relationships or tendencies between features and the target variable without referring to the example demonstrations. This step relies on the LLM's general knowledge and common sense, allowing it to fully extract prior knowledge about the problem domain. In the second step, the LLM uses both the information from the first step and the example demonstrations to deduce specific rules for each class. This structured approach prevents the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns and helps focus attention on more significant features.

The response instruction ensures that the LLM's output is structured in a way that facilitates automated parsing. The prompt specifies the format for responses and the structure each rule should follow, allowing the LLM to select appropriate formats based on feature types (numeric or categorical). Without such guidance, the LLM might produce rules in various formats that would be difficult to parse programmatically.

Once the LLM generates rules in natural language, the next stage involves parsing these rules into executable code. This is achieved by leveraging the LLM itself to generate Python code that implements the rules. The function name, input and output descriptions, and inferred rules are included in a prompt that asks the LLM to generate the corresponding code. This generated code is then executed using Python's $\texttt{exec()}$ function to perform the actual data transformation. This approach handles the challenge of parsing noisy text, as the LLM can understand semantic meaning despite syntactic variations in the rules.

The feature generation stage applies the parsed rules to create new binary features. For each class, binary features indicate whether a sample satisfies the rules associated with that class. If we denote the generated binary feature from sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ for class $k$ as $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class and $c$ is the number of classes, each feature takes a value of 1 if the sample satisfies the corresponding rule and 0 otherwise.

The final stage involves training a simple machine learning model on these binary features to estimate class probabilities. The framework uses a linear model without bias, where the probability for each class $\mathbf{p}^i$ is computed as follows:

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,$$

$$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}({[\text{logit}_{1}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]}).$$

Here, $\mathbf{w}_k$ represents trainable weights that signify the importance of each feature for predicting class $k$. By projecting weights into a positive subspace through the $\max$ operation, the framework ensures that features generated by the LLM do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning. The model is trained by optimizing cross-entropy loss using the few-shot training samples.

# Advantages of Decoupling Feature Engineering from the Inference Model

The architectural decision to separate feature engineering from the inference model yields several significant advantages that address fundamental challenges in applying LLMs to tabular learning tasks.

The most immediate benefit is the dramatic reduction in inference cost. Since the LLM is only consulted during the feature engineering phase, which occurs once before model training, the framework eliminates the need to send queries to the LLM for each sample at inference time. In contrast, approaches that use LLMs for end-to-end predictions must invoke the model for every single test sample, leading to substantial computational overhead and latency. FeatLLM's inference cost is comparable to conventional machine learning methods, making it practical for deployment in real-world systems with large datasets or real-time prediction requirements.

A second advantage concerns accessibility to state-of-the-art LLMs. Many of the most powerful recent LLMs, such as GPT-4 and PaLM, only permit limited access via inference APIs without providing access to training codebases or fine-tuning APIs. This restriction poses a fundamental challenge for approaches that require fine-tuning the LLM for high accuracy. FeatLLM circumvents this limitation by only requiring API-level access to the LLM for inference during the feature engineering phase. The framework is model-agnostic and can work with any LLM that can process prompts and generate text outputs.

The decoupled architecture also addresses the challenge of prompt size limitations. LLMs have finite context windows that limit the amount of text they can process in a single query. When tabular data has many features, the serialized text length can exceed these limits, rendering the approach infeasible or causing performance degradation. FeatLLM employs ensemble methods with feature bagging to mitigate this challenge. By selecting subsets of features for different ensemble members, the framework ensures that each individual prompt remains within acceptable size limits while still covering the full feature set across the ensemble.

The ensemble approach, inspired by techniques from ensemble methods, offers additional benefits for robustness. Even if the LLM generates incorrect rules in some trials, correct rules from other trials can compensate, enhancing overall prediction quality. This relates to the concept of self-consistency in LLMs—rules commonly inferred across multiple trials are more likely to be accurate. The framework sets a sufficiently high temperature for LLM inference and randomizes the order of few-shot demonstrations to introduce diversity in rule generation, ensuring that the ensemble captures a broad range of potentially useful rules.

Finally, the separation enables interpretability. Each generated rule can be examined as a human-readable statement about the relationship between features and outcomes. The learned weights indicate which rules the data suggests are most important. This transparency stands in stark contrast to end-to-end LLM predictions, where the reasoning process remains opaque. In domains where understanding the basis for predictions is critical for trust and accountability, this interpretability provides substantial value.

# Visualizing the Workflow: From Raw Tabular Samples to Binary Features

Understanding how raw tabular data transforms through the FeatLLM pipeline provides concrete insight into the framework's operation. Let us trace the journey of a single sample through this transformation process.

Consider a sample from a medical dataset attempting to predict chronic heart failure. The raw input might consist of numerical and categorical values: age equals 65, blood pressure equals 150, cholesterol equals 280, and so forth through all $d$ features. This sample, along with $k-1$ other labeled samples and the feature descriptions, enters the prompt construction phase.

The LLM receives this structured prompt and generates rules through its two-step reasoning process. For the positive class (chronic heart failure present), it might produce rules such as: "age is greater than 60," "blood pressure exceeds 140," or "cholesterol level is greater than 240 AND previous heart condition is present." For the negative class, it might generate complementary rules. Each rule represents a condition that the LLM believes distinguishes samples of that class.

The parsing stage converts these natural language rules into executable Python code. The LLM, when prompted with the rules and appropriate function signatures, generates code that checks each condition. For example, a rule "age is greater than 60" becomes a function that returns 1 if the age feature exceeds 60 and 0 otherwise. This automated code generation handles the variability in how the LLM might express rules, such as substituting mathematical symbols with phrases like "greater than."

With the parsed code, feature generation proceeds by applying each rule to each sample. The original sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ with its $d$ original features transforms into a new representation $\mathbf{z}_k^i$ containing $R$ binary features for each class $k$. If $R$ rules are extracted for each of $c$ classes, the total number of new binary features equals $R \times c$. Each binary feature indicates whether the sample satisfies a particular rule associated with a particular class.

This transformation from continuous and categorical original features to binary rule-based features fundamentally changes the nature of the learning problem. The downstream linear model no longer needs to discover complex relationships between features and outcomes; instead, it simply learns which rules, among those proposed by the LLM, are most predictive. The heavy lifting of reasoning about feature-target relationships has already been accomplished by the LLM's prior knowledge.

The ensemble approach adds another dimension to this workflow. Multiple independent trials of rule generation and model training occur, each potentially producing different rules due to the randomized prompt construction and LLM temperature settings. The final prediction aggregates the probability estimates from all ensemble members, combining diverse perspectives into a more robust prediction. This aggregation ensures that the system does not rely too heavily on any single set of rules, providing resilience against occasional poor rule generation.

The complete journey—from raw tabular samples with semantic feature names, through LLM-guided rule extraction, to binary features processed by a simple linear model—represents a thoughtful integration of large language model capabilities with traditional machine learning principles. Each stage builds upon the previous, transforming data in ways that preserve semantic meaning while enabling efficient computation.

# Engineering the Prompt: Extracting Knowledge from LLMs

The design of prompts for Large Language Models (LLMs) represents a critical intersection between human intent and machine reasoning. When we task an LLM with feature engineering for tabular learning, the prompt becomes more than a simple request—it becomes a structured mechanism for knowledge extraction. This chapter explores how FeatLLM approaches prompt engineering as a systematic process, transforming the LLM into an effective feature engineer capable of generating high-quality, interpretable rules from limited data.

The FeatLLM framework structures its input prompt into three distinct components, each serving a specific purpose in guiding the LLM toward accurate rule generation. This structured approach mimics how an expert human might approach a tabular learning task, breaking down complex reasoning into manageable steps. By understanding each component and its role in the overall process, we gain insight into how to effectively leverage LLMs for feature engineering tasks.

#

## Component 1: Basic Information Description

The foundation of any effective prompt lies in providing the LLM with sufficient context to understand the problem at hand. In FeatLLM, the basic information description serves as this foundational layer, equipping the LLM with essential information for solving the classification task. This component comprises three critical elements: the task description, feature descriptions, and example demonstrations.

The task description provides the LLM with a clear understanding of what problem it needs to solve. In FeatLLM, task descriptions are formulated as questions, which naturally prompts the LLM to reason toward an answer. For instance, when predicting medical outcomes, the task description might take the form: "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?" This question-based format serves a dual purpose: it clearly defines the prediction objective while also framing the problem in a way that encourages the LLM to identify the factors that influence the answer. The task description implicitly communicates what features should be examined and what kind of reasoning would be appropriate for the domain.

Feature descriptions provide the LLM with detailed information about each variable in the dataset. For each feature, the description indicates its value type and optionally includes its definition, providing semantic context that goes beyond mere column names. For categorical variables, examples of the possible categories can be provided, helping the LLM understand the range of values it might encounter. This level of detail is crucial because tabular data often contains features with names that may be ambiguous or domain-specific. By including definitions and value type information, the prompt ensures that the LLM can properly interpret each feature's meaning and significance. For example, a feature named "cp" in a heart disease dataset might be cryptic on its own, but when described as "chest pain type" with possible values of "typical angina," "atypical angina," "non-anginal pain," and "asymptomatic," the LLM can draw upon its medical knowledge to understand how this feature relates to cardiac outcomes.

### Data Serialization Techniques: Converting Tabular Rows into Natural Language

Example demonstrations constitute the third element of the basic information description, and they require careful serialization to transform tabular data into a format the LLM can process. Since LLMs operate on natural language text, tabular data must be converted into textual representations—a process known as serialization. FeatLLM follows serialization formats established in prior research, converting each data sample into a coherent natural language statement.

The serialization process follows a specific formula that combines feature names with their corresponding values. Given a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ with feature names $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, the serialized representation takes the form:

$$\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = ``f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ...\ f_d\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\ \mathbf{y}^i"$$

This serialization approach creates a natural language description where each feature name is paired with its value using the connecting phrase "is." For example, a patient record might be serialized as: "age is 45. sex is male. chest_pain_type is asymptomatic. resting_blood_pressure is 130. Answer: heart_disease." This format provides several advantages. First, it maintains semantic coherence, allowing the LLM to process the data as it would any other natural language text. Second, it explicitly associates feature names with values, ensuring the LLM understands which measurement corresponds to which attribute. Third, by including the answer (label) at the end, it establishes a clear connection between the input features and the target variable, enabling the LLM to learn from these examples during the reasoning process.

The choice of how many examples to include in the prompt relates to the few-shot learning paradigm. In FeatLLM, these examples serve as demonstrations from which the LLM can infer patterns and relationships. The serialization must balance comprehensiveness—providing enough information for the LLM to identify patterns—with conciseness, as prompt length constraints remain a practical consideration. When dealing with datasets containing many features, careful attention must be paid to serialization efficiency to ensure that relevant information can fit within the LLM's context window.

#

## Component 2: Reasoning Instructions and the Two-Step Inference Process

The second major component of the FeatLLM prompt design focuses on reasoning instructions. This component plays a pivotal role in guiding the LLM through a logical thought process, ensuring that the generated rules are grounded in meaningful relationships rather than superficial patterns. The reasoning instruction begins with an introductory sentence similar to the chain-of-thought approach, which has been shown to improve LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks by encouraging step-by-step problem decomposition.

What distinguishes FeatLLM's approach is the division of the LLM's inference process into two distinct steps. This structured reasoning approach serves a crucial purpose: it separates the LLM's prior knowledge application from its data-driven learning, allowing each source of information to contribute appropriately to rule generation. By explicitly structuring the reasoning process, FeatLLM guides the LLM toward more thoughtful and reliable feature engineering.

### Step 1: Inferring Causal Relationships Using Prior Knowledge Without Examples

The first step of the reasoning process encourages the LLM to infer causal relationships or tendencies between features and the task outcome without referring to example demonstrations. This step relies entirely on the LLM's general knowledge and common sense, allowing it to fully extract and apply its prior knowledge about the problem domain. By explicitly instructing the LLM to reason before examining examples, FeatLLM ensures that the model's inherent understanding of the domain contributes to rule formation.

Consider a medical diagnosis task. Before examining any patient data, the LLM might reason about factors that influence heart disease risk based on its training on medical literature. It could identify that factors such as advanced age, elevated blood pressure, high cholesterol, and certain chest pain types are generally associated with increased cardiac risk. This reasoning draws upon the vast corpus of medical information encoded in the LLM's parameters during pre-training. The LLM might articulate relationships like: "older individuals typically face higher cardiovascular risk" or "asymptomatic chest pain in diabetic patients often indicates silent ischemia."

This step is crucial because it allows domain knowledge to inform feature engineering before the model becomes influenced by the particularities of the limited examples. In few-shot learning scenarios, where only a handful of labeled examples are available, relying solely on these examples could lead to identifying spurious correlations that happen to exist in the small sample but do not reflect true relationships. By having the LLM first reason from prior knowledge, FeatLLM establishes a foundation of domain-appropriate relationships that can be refined—rather than determined—by the example demonstrations.

### Step 2: Deduce Decisive Rules Using Few-Shot Demonstrations

After establishing the prior knowledge foundation, the second step instructs the LLM to use both the example demonstrations and the information from the first step to deduce specific rules for each class. This step bridges general knowledge with specific data patterns, allowing the LLM to generate concrete, actionable rules that can be applied to new samples.

The reasoning instruction explicitly guides the LLM to examine the serialized examples and identify distinguishing characteristics between classes. For instance, given examples of patients with and without heart disease, the LLM might notice that patients with the positive label tend to have specific combinations of feature values. It then formulates rules that capture these distinguishing patterns. A rule might be: "IF age is greater than 60 AND chest_pain_type is asymptomatic, THEN predict high risk of heart disease."

This two-step reasoning process prevents the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns. When an LLM is presented with many features and only a few examples, there is a risk that random variations in the data could be mistaken for meaningful patterns. By first establishing what relationships should exist based on domain knowledge, the model is better equipped to focus on features that plausibly relate to the target variable. The LLM can filter out uninformative features during rule extraction, concentrating its attention on features that both its prior knowledge and the example data suggest are important.

To ensure consistent and useful output, FeatLLM sets a fixed number of rules—specifically, ten—to be extracted for each class. This constraint addresses potential issues with both underfitting (too few rules) and prompt bloat (too many rules). The fixed number also provides predictability in downstream processing, as the system knows exactly how many binary features to generate per class. Additionally, when formulating rules, the LLM is instructed to refer to the type of each feature from the basic information description, avoiding type mismatches such as applying numerical comparisons to categorical variables.

#

## Component 3: Response Instructions for Structured and Parsable Outputs

The third component of the prompt design addresses a practical challenge inherent in working with LLMs: how to transform their free-form text outputs into structured data that can be programmatically processed. Response instructions guide the LLM on how to structure its outputs, ensuring that the generated rules can be reliably parsed and converted into executable code.

The response instructions specify two formatting requirements: the overall format for the response and the structure each individual rule should follow. These instructions serve a critical function in bridging the gap between natural language generation and automated feature engineering. Without structured response instructions, LLMs might produce rules in varying formats, using different syntactic conventions, making automated parsing unreliable or impossible.

The instructions specify that responses should be organized by answer class, with rules for each class presented in a clear, consistent format. For each rule, the LLM is guided to use appropriate comparison operators and logical connectives based on the feature type. For numerical features, rules might employ operators such as greater than ($>$), less than ($<$), or equality ($=$). For categorical features, rules might specify membership in a particular category. The instructions also allow the LLM to combine multiple conditions using logical operators like AND or OR, enabling the expression of more complex decision boundaries.

Consider an example where the LLM generates rules for predicting heart disease. A properly formatted rule might appear as: "Rule 1: age > 50 AND sex = 'male' AND chest_pain_type IN ['typical angina', 'atypical angina']." This structured format clearly specifies the feature, the comparison operator, and the threshold or category, enabling straightforward parsing into executable code.

The importance of response formatting becomes apparent when considering the downstream processing of these rules. FeatLLM must convert natural language rules into program code that can be executed to generate binary features. Even with formatting constraints, LLM output can include noisy syntactic variations. For example, the LLM might substitute "$>$" with phrases like "greater than" or "exceeds." Such variations, while semantically equivalent, pose challenges for automated parsing. FeatLLM addresses this by leveraging the LLM itself as a parser: the rules are fed back into the LLM with instructions to generate equivalent Python code. The LLM's ability to understand semantic meaning despite syntactic variations makes it well-suited for this translation task. The generated code is then executed using Python's `exec()` function to perform the actual data transformation, converting each sample into binary features indicating whether each rule is satisfied.

#

## Mitigating Spurious Correlations Through Structured Reasoning

A fundamental challenge in few-shot learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations—patterns that appear in the limited training data but do not reflect true underlying relationships. With only a handful of examples, random variations can be mistaken for meaningful signals, leading to models that perform well on training data but fail to generalize. FeatLLM's structured reasoning approach directly addresses this challenge through multiple mechanisms working in concert.

The two-step reasoning process serves as the primary defense against spurious correlations. By first asking the LLM to reason about causal relationships based on prior knowledge, independently of the examples, the framework establishes which feature-target relationships are plausible from a domain perspective. This prior knowledge acts as a filter, preventing the LLM from becoming overly influenced by chance patterns in the few-shot examples. When the LLM subsequently examines the examples in Step 2, it does so with the context of what relationships make sense, rather than freely fitting to whatever patterns happen to exist in the small sample.

Empirical evidence supports the effectiveness of this approach. In experiments where spurious correlations were artificially introduced by attaching irrelevant columns from unrelated datasets, FeatLLM demonstrated remarkable robustness. When columns from the Adult dataset (containing features like occupation and work class) were randomly attached to the Heart dataset (predicting coronary artery disease), FeatLLM showed the least performance degradation compared to baseline methods. Analysis revealed that rules based on these noisy columns accounted for only 1-2% of the total generated rules, indicating that the LLM successfully identified them as irrelevant and excluded them from meaningful rule generation.

The ablation studies in the FeatLLM paper further illuminate the importance of structured reasoning. When the reasoning instruction's first step—which elicits prior knowledge—was removed, performance degraded significantly, particularly in low-shot settings. This finding underscores that the prior knowledge component becomes increasingly crucial when fewer examples are available. With only four shots, there is simply not enough data to reliably distinguish signal from noise; domain knowledge must fill this gap. Conversely, as the number of shots increases, the relative contribution of prior knowledge decreases, though it remains beneficial.

It is worth noting that FeatLLM's approach is not foolproof. The framework can learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships when columns from closely related datasets are attached—situations where prior knowledge might actually reinforce incorrect associations. This limitation highlights an important consideration: the quality of feature engineering depends not only on the reasoning process but also on the relevance and quality of the features provided. The LLM's prior knowledge, while generally beneficial, may occasionally reinforce biases or incorrect assumptions present in its training data.

The ensemble approach employed by FeatLLM provides an additional layer of protection against spurious correlations. By generating rules multiple times with different random seeds, demonstration orderings, and feature subsets, the framework reduces the impact of any single erroneous rule. Rules that appear consistently across multiple trials are more likely to capture genuine relationships, following the principle of self-consistency in LLM reasoning. This ensemble strategy transforms the potential weakness of any single LLM query into a strength through aggregation, producing more robust feature sets for downstream prediction.

# From Natural Language to Binary Features: Rule Parsing and Application

## The Challenge of Parsing Noisy LLM Outputs

When Large Language Models generate rules in natural language, these outputs are inherently flexible and varied. This flexibility, while useful for human comprehension, presents a significant technical challenge when we attempt to systematically apply these rules to tabular data. The core problem lies in the syntactic variations that LLMs introduce into their outputs. Even when given explicit formatting instructions, an LLM might substitute symbolic operators like "$>$" or "$<$" with their textual equivalents such as "greater than" or "less than." Similarly, logical operators might appear as "AND," "and," "&&," or even spelled out as "and also."

Consider a rule that the LLM generates for identifying high-risk credit applicants. In one trial, the model might output "income $<$ 30000 AND debt_ratio $>$ 0.5," while in another trial, the semantically equivalent rule might appear as "income is less than 30000 and debt_ratio exceeds 0.5." A human reader would immediately recognize these as expressing the same condition, but a traditional parser built on rigid pattern matching would struggle to handle such variability. The challenge is compounded by the fact that these variations are not merely cosmetic—they can affect the structure of the output in ways that break predetermined parsing templates.

This noise in LLM outputs stems from the fundamental nature of language models. LLMs are trained to generate human-like text, and human language is characterized by its flexibility and redundancy. Multiple phrasings can express identical meanings, and the LLM has learned to navigate this space of linguistic equivalence. While this property is valuable for general language tasks, it becomes a hurdle when we need to convert natural language rules into executable code that can be systematically applied to data.

The variability in outputs also extends to how the LLM structures its logical reasoning. It might combine multiple conditions using different logical connectives, nest conditions in various ways, or introduce parenthetical clarifications. Each of these variations requires the parsing system to be robust enough to handle edge cases that may not have been anticipated during system design. Building a rule-based parser with hard-coded patterns to handle all these variations would be impractical and brittle, as it would fail to generalize to new patterns that the LLM might produce.

## Leveraging the LLM to Write Its Own Parsing Code

Rather than attempting to build an increasingly complex parser to handle the noisy and variable outputs from the LLM, the FeatLLM framework adopts an elegant solution: it leverages the LLM itself to perform the parsing. This approach recognizes that the LLM, having generated the rules, is uniquely positioned to understand their semantic meaning. The same model that produced "greater than" instead of "$>$" can understand that these expressions are equivalent and translate them into appropriate code.

The key insight here is that LLMs possess two complementary capabilities. First, they excel at understanding semantic meaning even when syntactic expressions vary. The model that generated a rule with unconventional phrasing can still parse that rule correctly because it understands the underlying logical structure. Second, LLMs trained on code corpora have acquired the ability to generate functional program code when given clear instructions. By combining these capabilities, we can ask the LLM to translate its own natural language rules into executable Python code.

The process begins after the LLM has generated its rules. The framework constructs a new prompt that includes the function name, input and output descriptions, and the inferred rules themselves. This prompt asks the LLM to generate Python code that implements the logical conditions specified in the rules. The generated code takes a data sample as input and returns a binary indicator for whether each rule is satisfied.

Consider how this works in practice. Suppose the LLM has generated a rule stating that a patient is at high risk for heart disease if "age is greater than 60 and blood pressure exceeds 140." When we feed this rule back to the LLM with a request to generate parsing code, the model produces Python code that checks whether a given sample's age value is greater than 60 and whether its blood pressure value is greater than 140. The code handles any necessary type conversions and comparisons, abstracting away the specific phrasing used in the original rule.

The generated code is then executed using Python's built-in \texttt{exec()} function, which allows dynamic execution of code stored as strings. This enables the system to automatically transform new data samples based on the rules without requiring human intervention or manual coding. The entire pipeline—from rule generation to code generation to execution—is automated, making it feasible to apply this approach across multiple trials and datasets.

This approach to parsing exemplifies a broader principle in working with LLMs: when faced with challenges arising from model behavior, it is often more effective to leverage the model's strengths rather than to build external systems that fight against its tendencies. By asking the LLM to translate its own outputs, we sidestep the need to anticipate and handle every possible syntactic variation.

## Feature Generation: Transforming Samples into Binary Vectors

Once the parsing code has been generated, the next step is to apply this code to transform data samples into new feature representations. This transformation converts each sample from its original representation—typically a vector of continuous and categorical values—into a binary feature vector based on rule satisfaction.

For each class in the classification task, the LLM generates a set of rules that characterize when a sample belongs to that class. When these rules are applied to a sample, they produce a binary vector where each element indicates whether the sample satisfies a particular rule. If a sample meets the conditions specified in a rule, the corresponding feature is set to 1; otherwise, it is set to 0. This creates a sparse, interpretable representation of the sample based on how it aligns with the LLM's reasoning.

To formalize this process, consider a classification problem with $c$ classes. For each class $k$, the LLM generates $R$ rules based on the few-shot examples and its prior knowledge. Given a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$, the generated parsing code evaluates each of these $R$ rules against the sample's feature values. The result is a binary vector $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ for each class $k$, where each component $z_{k,j}^i$ indicates whether sample $i$ satisfies rule $j$ for class $k$.

This transformation has several important properties. First, it abstracts away the specific feature values and instead focuses on whether samples satisfy certain logical conditions. This can be particularly valuable when the relationship between features and labels is not linear or when interactions between features are important. Second, the binary representation is highly interpretable—each feature corresponds directly to a human-readable rule, making it possible to understand exactly why a particular classification decision was made.

The number of rules $R$ per class is a design choice that balances several factors. With too few rules, the representation may be too coarse to capture important distinctions between samples. With too many rules, the feature dimensionality becomes large relative to the number of training samples, potentially leading to overfitting in the low-shot regime. The FeatLLM framework uses $R = 10$ rules per class as a default, which provides sufficient representational capacity while remaining tractable for few-shot learning.

An important consideration is that not all rules generated by the LLM will be equally informative. Some rules may capture genuine relationships between features and labels, while others may reflect spurious correlations or irrelevant reasoning. The transformation to binary features preserves all rules equally initially, and the subsequent learning process must identify which rules are most useful for the task at hand.

## Inferring Class Likelihood: A Linear Model Approach

With the binary feature representation in hand, the next step is to use these features to predict class membership. While many approaches could be used, the FeatLLM framework employs a simple linear model for this purpose. This choice is deliberate—by using a model with low complexity, the framework maintains its efficiency and interpretability while still achieving strong predictive performance.

The rationale for a linear model becomes clear when we consider the nature of the binary features. Each feature directly represents whether a sample satisfies a particular rule, and each rule was designed by the LLM to be relevant for identifying members of a particular class. The question then becomes: how important is each rule for making accurate predictions? A linear model directly answers this question by learning a weight for each feature that quantifies its contribution to the prediction.

A naive approach would be to simply count how many rules each sample satisfies for each class—summing the binary features—and use these counts as scores. However, this approach assumes that all rules are equally important, which is unlikely to be true in practice. Some rules may be highly diagnostic, strongly indicating class membership when satisfied. Other rules may be weaker signals, providing only marginal evidence. The linear model allows us to learn these relative importances from the few-shot training data.

The simplicity of the linear model also aligns with the constraints of few-shot learning. With only a small number of labeled samples available for training, complex models with many parameters would be prone to overfitting, memorizing the training examples rather than learning generalizable patterns. A linear model, with its limited parameter count, provides appropriate inductive bias for the low-data regime. The model can still achieve high performance because the heavy lifting—identifying relevant features and their relationships to classes—has already been done by the LLM during rule generation.

Furthermore, using a linear model enables efficient inference. Once the model weights have been learned, predictions for new samples require only simple matrix operations: a dot product between the weights and the binary features, followed by a softmax transformation. This computational efficiency stands in stark contrast to approaches that require LLM inference for each sample, where each prediction requires a potentially expensive API call.

## Mathematical Formulation of the Linear Model

The mathematical formulation of the classification model is designed to be both simple and effective. Let $\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0, 1\}^R$ denote the binary feature vector generated from sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ for class $k$, where $R$ is the number of rules per class. For each class, we learn a weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^R$ that represents the importance of each rule for predicting membership in that class.

The first step in computing the class likelihood is to calculate a logit score for each class. This is done using a dot product between the weight vector and the binary feature vector:

$$\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i$$

The $\max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0)$ operation projects the weights onto the positive subspace, ensuring that all weights are non-negative. This projection is a crucial design choice with an important rationale: the rules were generated by the LLM to identify samples belonging to class $k$. If a sample satisfies a rule for class $k$, this should never decrease its likelihood of belonging to that class. By constraining weights to be non-negative, we ensure that the features generated by the LLM can only increase the class likelihood, never reduce it.

Without this constraint, the learning process might assign negative weights to some rules, effectively treating them as evidence against class membership. But this would contradict the semantic meaning of the rules, which were explicitly designed to identify members of each class. The positive weight projection maintains consistency between the LLM's rule generation and the downstream learning process.

After computing logits for each class, the final step is to convert these scores into proper probability distributions using the softmax function:

$$\mathbf{p}^i = \text{Softmax}\left([\text{logit}_{1}^i, \text{logit}_{2}^i, ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i]\right)$$

The softmax function normalizes the logit scores so that they sum to one and are all positive, yielding a valid probability distribution over the $c$ classes. For each sample, the predicted class is the one with the highest probability.

This formulation elegantly combines the LLM's knowledge—encoded in the rules—with data-driven learning—encoded in the weights. The LLM identifies potentially relevant features, and the learning process determines which of those features are actually useful for the specific dataset at hand.

## Training the Downstream Model: Optimizing Weights to Learn Rule Importance

The training process for the linear model involves learning the weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_k$ for each class using the few-shot training samples. The objective is to assign higher weights to rules that are more predictive of class membership and lower weights to rules that are less informative or potentially misleading.

The weights are learned by optimizing the cross-entropy loss, which measures the discrepancy between the predicted probability distribution and the true class labels. For a dataset with $N$ labeled samples, the cross-entropy loss is:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_{y^i}^i$$

where $y^i$ is the true class label for sample $i$ and $p_{y^i}^i$ is the predicted probability for that class. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to assign high probability to the correct class for each training sample.

The optimization is performed using standard gradient-based methods, such as the Adam optimizer. Given the small number of parameters—the weight vectors for each class—and the limited number of training samples, the optimization converges quickly. The FeatLLM framework uses a learning rate of 0.01 and trains for up to 200 epochs, selecting the optimal epoch using $k$-fold cross-validation.

An important empirical finding from the research is that the benefit of weight tuning increases with the number of training samples. When only a handful of samples are available, there is limited information to estimate rule importance accurately, and the simple sum of binary features may perform nearly as well as the weighted approach. However, as more labeled samples become available, the weighted model can leverage this additional information to learn more nuanced rule importances, leading to significant performance improvements.

The training process also provides a form of automatic filtering for irrelevant rules. Rules that have little relationship to the target label will tend to receive low weights during optimization. In practice, the researchers found that setting a threshold of 0.1 on the learned weights effectively filters out rules with little relevance to the data, with the majority of rules receiving weights above this threshold confirming their informativeness. This demonstrates that the LLM generates high-quality rules that are genuinely useful for the prediction task.

The combination of LLM-based rule generation and data-driven weight learning creates a powerful synergy. The LLM contributes its vast prior knowledge and reasoning capabilities, identifying features that a human expert might specify. The learning process then adapts these features to the specific characteristics of the dataset at hand, learning which rules are most important and effectively discarding irrelevant ones. This division of labor allows each component to do what it does best, resulting in a system that achieves strong performance in few-shot tabular learning scenarios.

# Optimization and Robustness: Ensembling with Bagging

In the FeatLLM framework, the process of extracting meaningful rules from large language models (LLMs) and converting them into predictive features is not performed as a single, deterministic operation. Instead, the framework employs a sophisticated ensemble methodology that repeatedly executes the rule extraction and inference process, combining the results to produce final predictions. This chapter explores the theoretical motivation and practical implementation of ensembling with bagging in FeatLLM, demonstrating how these techniques address fundamental challenges in few-shot learning and LLM-based tabular prediction.

## The Necessity of Ensembling in Few-Shot Regimes

Few-shot learning presents a unique set of challenges that distinguish it from conventional supervised learning. When the number of labeled samples available for training is severely limited—often just 4, 8, or 16 examples—there is an inherent risk of learning spurious correlations rather than genuine relationships between features and target variables. A single rule extraction process, no matter how carefully designed, may inadvertently emphasize irrelevant features or miss important patterns simply due to the idiosyncrasies of the particular few-shot examples that happened to be selected.

Ensembling provides a powerful mechanism to mitigate these risks. By executing the entire FeatLLM pipeline multiple times and aggregating the results, the framework can compensate for incorrect or suboptimal rules generated in individual trials. The fundamental insight is that while any single rule extraction might fail to capture the true underlying patterns, the collective wisdom of multiple independent attempts is far more likely to converge on accurate predictions. This principle aligns with the well-established observation in machine learning that combining multiple weak learners often yields a strong learner with superior generalization properties.

The necessity of ensembling in FeatLLM extends beyond mere performance improvement. In the context of few-shot tabular learning, where the model must make predictions based on extremely limited evidence, ensembling serves as a form of epistemic safeguard. It allows the framework to express uncertainty through the variance across trials and reduces overreliance on any single set of rules. The experimental results in the paper demonstrate this clearly: the ablation study reveals that removing the ensemble process results in consistent performance degradation across all experimental settings, underscoring the critical role this component plays in the framework's success.

## Introducing Randomness: Temperature Settings and Example Ordering

For ensembling to provide meaningful benefits, the individual trials must produce diverse outputs rather than identical or highly correlated results. FeatLLM introduces controlled randomness at multiple stages of the rule extraction process to ensure this diversity. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for appreciating how the ensemble achieves robustness.

The first source of randomness comes from the LLM's temperature parameter during inference. When generating rules, the LLM is configured with a temperature setting of 0.5, which determines the stochasticity of the token sampling process. A temperature of zero would result in deterministic, greedy decoding where the most probable token is always selected, producing identical outputs for identical inputs. By setting the temperature to a non-zero value, the model samples from the probability distribution over tokens, introducing variation in the generated rules. This means that even with identical prompts, different trials can produce different rule sets, capturing different aspects of the relationship between features and targets.

The second mechanism for introducing randomness involves the ordering of few-shot demonstrations within the prompt. Research has demonstrated that the order in which examples are presented to LLMs can significantly impact their outputs—a phenomenon sometimes referred to as "order sensitivity" or recency bias. FeatLLM leverages this observation by randomizing the order of the serialized training samples in each trial. For instance, if the training set contains examples labeled A, B, C, and D, one trial might present them in the order A, B, C, D, while another might use D, A, C, B. This seemingly simple modification leads the LLM to attend differently to the examples, potentially extracting different rules based on which examples receive greater emphasis in the reasoning process.

The combination of temperature-based sampling and order randomization ensures that the ensemble is composed of genuinely diverse rule sets. This diversity is crucial because it allows the ensemble to cover a broader hypothesis space. If all trials produced identical rules, ensembling would provide no benefit—the variance reduction that makes ensembles effective requires that individual models make different errors. By carefully engineering randomness into the process, FeatLLM ensures that incorrect rules generated in some trials can be compensated for by correct rules in others, leading to more robust overall predictions.

## Bagging Strategies: Sub-sampling Features and Instances

While introducing randomness through temperature and example ordering creates diversity, FeatLLM employs an additional technique from classical machine learning to further enhance the ensemble: bootstrap aggregating, or "bagging." This approach, originally popularized by methods like Random Forest, involves training each model in the ensemble on a different subset of the available data. FeatLLM adapts this concept to the unique constraints of LLM-based rule extraction.

The bagging strategy in FeatLLM operates on two dimensions: feature sub-sampling and instance sub-sampling. When dealing with datasets that have a large number of input features, the framework can select a random subset of features to include in each trial's prompt. This means that one trial might generate rules based on features $\{f_1, f_3, f_5, f_7\}$, while another trial uses features $\{f_2, f_4, f_6, f_8\}$. Each trial's rule set captures relationships involving the specific features it was given, and the ensemble combines these partial perspectives into a comprehensive view.

Similarly, when the number of available training samples grows, the framework can sub-sample instances. Rather than including all $N$ training examples in every prompt (which might exceed context window limits), each trial selects a random subset. For example, with 16 training samples available, a particular trial might use only samples $\{x^1, x^5, x^9, x^{13}\}$ to demonstrate patterns to the LLM. Different subsets lead to different induced rules, and the aggregation process filters out idiosyncratic patterns specific to particular subsets.

The power of this dual bagging approach lies in its ability to create an ensemble of "weak learners"—each individual trial may have incomplete information due to the sub-sampling, but the collective can achieve strong performance. A rule that captures a genuine relationship between features and targets is likely to be discovered across multiple trials regardless of which subset is used, while spurious correlations that appear only in specific subsets will be averaged out. This elegant mechanism allows FeatLLM to maintain robust performance even when individual trials have access to only partial information about the dataset.

## Overcoming Prompt Size Limitations: Handling Datasets with Large Numbers of Attributes

One of the most pressing practical challenges in applying LLMs to tabular data is the constraint imposed by prompt length limits. LLMs have finite context windows—maximum token counts they can process in a single input. When tabular datasets contain large numbers of features, and each feature requires a text description and example values, the serialized representation can easily exceed these limits. Datasets like Communities and Myocardial, which include over 100 attributes each, pose particular difficulties for naive approaches that attempt to include all features in a single prompt.

FeatLLM's bagging strategy provides an elegant solution to this problem. Rather than attempting to squeeze all features into a single prompt—an approach that may fail entirely or lead to degraded performance due to information loss—the framework distributes features across multiple trials. Each trial receives a manageable subset of features that fits comfortably within the context window. The LLM then generates rules based solely on the features provided, creating a partial feature transformation.

The key insight is that while a single trial might capture only a fraction of the potentially useful relationships, the ensemble can achieve comprehensive coverage. Features that are highly predictive will likely appear in multiple trials and generate consistent rules, while less important features may be represented in fewer trials or generate rules that receive low weights during the training phase. The experimental results demonstrate this effectiveness: FeatLLM achieves satisfactory performance on datasets with numerous features where other LLM-based approaches fail to produce predictions at all.

It is worth noting that adapting bagging to existing LLM-based approaches would be problematic. Methods that require end-to-end LLM inference for each sample would need to double their per-sample inference costs with each bagging iteration, leading to computational costs that grow linearly with the ensemble size. FeatLLM, by contrast, only uses the LLM during the rule extraction phase—once rules are generated and converted to features, inference uses only the simple linear model, keeping computational costs low regardless of ensemble size.

## Ensemble Prediction: Averaging Probabilities Across Multiple Trials

Once multiple trials have been executed, each producing its own set of rules and trained weights, FeatLLM must aggregate the predictions into a final output. The ensemble prediction mechanism is straightforward yet powerful: the framework averages the class probabilities computed by each trial's model.

Formally, given a collection of trained weights from $T$ trials, denoted as $\{\mathbf{w}[t]\}_{t=1}^T$, the prediction for a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ is computed by first obtaining the probability vector $\mathbf{p}^i[t]$ from each trial according to the inference equation:

$$\text{logit}_k^i[t] = \max(\mathbf{w}_k[t], 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i[t]$$

$$\mathbf{p}^i[t] = \text{Softmax}([\text{logit}_{1}^i[t], ..., \text{logit}_{c}^i[t]])$$

where $\mathbf{z}_k^i[t]$ represents the binary features generated from the rules extracted in trial $t$, and $\mathbf{w}_k[t]$ are the corresponding learned weights. The final prediction is then:

$$\mathbf{p}^i_{final} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbf{p}^i[t]$$

This averaging approach has several desirable properties. First, it naturally smooths predictions, reducing variance without introducing bias. Second, it allows different trials to contribute differently to different samples—if some trials produce high-quality rules for certain regions of the feature space while others excel elsewhere, the averaging process benefits from all their strengths. Third, it provides a form of uncertainty quantification: high variance across trials suggests the model is less certain about a particular prediction, while low variance indicates consistent agreement across rule sets.

The implementation uses $T = 20$ trials as the default setting. Hyperparameter analysis reveals that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, but beyond approximately 20 trials, the performance gains begin to converge. This suggests that 20 trials provide sufficient diversity to capture the meaningful rule variations while avoiding diminishing returns that would increase computational cost without corresponding benefit.

## The Concept of Self-Consistency in LLM Reasoning

The ensemble approach in FeatLLM connects to a broader concept in LLM research known as "self-consistency." This principle suggests that when an LLM is asked to reason about a problem multiple times, the answers that appear most consistently across trials are more likely to be correct. Self-consistency leverages the observation that LLMs, when operating with non-zero temperature, explore different reasoning paths, and the correct answer tends to be reachable through multiple valid paths while incorrect answers arise from specific errors or confusions.

In the context of FeatLLM, self-consistency manifests through the rules generated across trials. A rule that captures a genuine relationship—for instance, that elevated blood glucose levels are associated with diabetes—will likely appear in multiple trials regardless of which few-shot examples are shown or how they are ordered. The underlying prior knowledge in the LLM supports this relationship, so it emerges consistently. Conversely, a rule based on a spurious correlation present only in a specific subset of training examples might appear in one trial but not others.

The training phase further reinforces this self-consistency principle. During the weight optimization process, rules that consistently help predict the correct class across training samples will receive higher weights, while rules that appear in only a few trials or that don't correlate well with the target will receive lower weights or be filtered out entirely. Experimental analysis confirms that rules with learned weights below a threshold of 0.1 are automatically filtered during tuning, eliminating features with little relevance to the target.

This combination of ensemble diversity and self-consistency creates a powerful mechanism for extracting high-quality features from LLMs. The framework doesn't simply trust every rule the LLM generates—instead, it uses statistical aggregation to identify which rules are robust and generalizable. The paper's analysis of feature quality shows that the majority of rules generated by FeatLLM are relevant to the target attribute, and the tuning process further refines this set by downweighting uninformative rules. Through this elegant interplay of diversity generation and consistency filtering, FeatLLM transforms the noisy, sometimes unreliable outputs of LLMs into reliable predictive features.

A robust empirical evaluation requires datasets that span diverse domains, vary in complexity, and present different challenges for machine learning algorithms. The Solution-mix dataset is another synthetic task, predicting whether the concentration percentage of a mixture from four solutions exceeds a threshold of 0.5. These synthetic datasets are particularly valuable because they are mathematically constructed rather than sampled from real-world data, guaranteeing they could not have been memorized by the LLM during pre-training.

The inclusion of both standard benchmarks and novel datasets provides a more complete picture of model capabilities. Strong performance on standard datasets demonstrates practical applicability to common machine learning problems, while strong performance on novel datasets provides evidence that the model is genuinely learning from the few-shot examples rather than merely retrieving memorized information.

# Experimental Setup: K-Shot Learning and Evaluation Protocols

The experimental methodology follows established protocols for few-shot learning, where the goal is to achieve strong predictive performance given only a small number of labeled training examples. This setting reflects many real-world scenarios where labeled data is expensive or time-consuming to obtain, making it essential to learn efficiently from limited supervision.

In the $k$-shot learning framework, only $k$ labeled samples are randomly sampled from the full dataset to form the training set. The value of $k$ represents a crucial experimental parameter, with smaller values presenting more challenging learning scenarios. The experiments evaluate performance across multiple values of $k$, including 4, 8, 16, and 32 shots. These values span the range from extremely data-scarce regimes, where most traditional machine learning methods struggle, to settings where more conventional approaches become viable.

The evaluation uses the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) as the primary metric. AUC is particularly appropriate for few-shot learning evaluation because it measures the model's ability to rank positive instances higher than negative instances, regardless of the specific probability threshold chosen. This threshold-independent quality is important because few-shot learning settings often lack sufficient data to reliably calibrate probability thresholds. Furthermore, AUC ranges from 0 to 1, with 0.5 indicating random performance and 1.0 indicating perfect discrimination, making it easily interpretable across different datasets.

To ensure reliable performance estimates despite the inherent randomness in few-shot sample selection, each experiment is repeated three times with different random seeds governing the choice of training and test sets. The reported results include both the mean and standard deviation of AUC values across these repetitions, providing insight into both typical performance and variability.

For model selection and hyperparameter tuning, $k$-fold cross-validation is employed on the few-shot training set. The value of $k$ is set to either 2 or 4, ensuring that the training folds include at least one example of each class. This careful design prevents situations where cross-validation fails due to missing class labels in a fold, which could otherwise occur in extremely low-shot regimes.

The implementation uses GPT-3.5 (specifically the gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 variant) as the large language model backbone for FeatLLM. The LLM inference uses a temperature parameter of 0.5 to introduce controlled randomness in rule generation, facilitating diversity across ensemble members. The top-p value is set to the default value of 1 in the API. The number of ensemble trials is set to 20, meaning the entire rule generation and training process is repeated 20 times, with predictions averaged across all trials. The number of rules extracted per class is set to 10, providing a balance between having sufficient expressive power and maintaining reasonable prompt length.

For the downstream linear model that learns rule importance weights, training uses the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01 for 200 epochs. The optimal epoch is selected using $k$-fold cross-validation on the training data. The weights are constrained to be non-negative, implemented by taking the maximum of learned weights and zero before computing class logits.

# Baseline Methods: A Comprehensive Comparison Framework

To properly contextualize the performance of FeatLLM, the experimental evaluation includes nine baseline methods spanning three categories: conventional machine learning approaches, pre-trained and semi-supervised methods, and large language model-based approaches.

The first category includes three conventional tabular learning methods that represent standard practice in applied machine learning. Logistic Regression (LogReg) is a fundamental linear classification method that models the log-odds of class membership as a linear combination of features. Despite its simplicity, logistic regression often provides competitive performance on tabular data, particularly when features are informative and the relationship with the target is approximately linear. XGBoost is a gradient boosting framework that has become one of the most successful methods for tabular prediction, combining decision tree ensembles with regularization techniques to prevent overfitting. XGBoost typically requires more training data than simple linear models but can capture complex nonlinear relationships. Random Forest is another tree-based ensemble method that constructs multiple decision trees on bootstrapped samples and averages their predictions. These conventional methods provide an important reference point: if FeatLLM cannot outperform these standard approaches in few-shot regimes, it would indicate that the LLM-based feature engineering approach is not providing genuine value.

The second category includes methods that leverage unlabeled data or pre-training to improve few-shot performance. SCARF is a contrastive learning approach for tabular data that learns representations by contrasting augmented views of data points. By leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data, such methods aim to learn useful feature representations that transfer to the labeled few-shot task. STUNT is a more recent semi-supervised approach that generates synthetic training data through self-training techniques. However, these methods require access to substantial unlabeled datasets, which may not be feasible in many real-world applications. TabPFN represents a different approach to incorporating prior knowledge: it pre-trains a Bayesian neural network on synthetic data generated from diverse distribution types. After pre-training, TabPFN can perform inference on new tabular datasets without further training by conditioning on both training and test samples. The prior knowledge encoded in the pre-trained weights helps overcome the data scarcity problem.

The third category includes methods that directly leverage large language models for tabular prediction. In-context learning represents the most straightforward approach: few-shot training examples are serialized into text and included in the LLM prompt along with feature and task descriptions, and the LLM generates predictions directly without any training or fine-tuning. This method tests the raw in-context learning capability of LLMs on tabular data. TABLET enhances basic in-context learning by incorporating additional hints derived from external classifiers, providing rule-sets and prototypes to improve the quality of inference. TabLLM employs parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as IA3 to adapt the LLM to the specific tabular prediction task. By tuning a small number of parameters while keeping most of the model frozen, TabLLM aims to leverage both the LLM's prior knowledge and the information in the few-shot examples.

It is worth noting that the baseline methods differ in their requirements and constraints. Traditional methods like logistic regression and XGBoost can be applied to any tabular dataset but may overfit with very few examples. Methods like SCARF and STUNT require additional unlabeled data, which may not always be available. TabLLM requires access to the model weights for fine-tuning, which is not possible with API-only access to proprietary models. Methods that require passing each sample through the LLM at inference time face significant computational costs when scaling to large datasets. FeatLLM is designed to overcome these limitations: it requires only API-level access, does not need unlabeled data, and performs inference using a simple linear model without requiring LLM queries for each sample.

For hyperparameter tuning of traditional machine learning methods, Grid-search combined with $k$-fold cross-validation is used to determine optimal parameters. In-context learning and TABLET use GPT-3.5 as the backbone, while TabLLM uses T0, following the original implementation. The restriction to T0 for TabLLM arises because TabLLM requires access to model weights for fine-tuning, and GPT-3.5 is only accessible via API without weight access.

# Ablation Studies: Dissecting Component Contributions

To understand which elements of the FeatLLM framework are most critical to its success, researchers conducted systematic ablation studies that remove or modify individual components. These experiments reveal the relative importance of four key elements: the weight tuning process, the ensemble mechanism, feature descriptions, and the reasoning instructions.

The ablation studies were designed with careful attention to isolating each component's contribution. For the weight tuning ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Tuning}$," the researchers omitted the weight tuning process entirely. Instead of learning the importance weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ through optimization, they simply summed the binary features to compute the logit values. This modification tests whether the learned weights genuinely capture meaningful information about rule importance, or whether the rules themselves are sufficiently discriminative without weighting.

The ensemble ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Ensemble}$," removes the bagging and ensemble process, relying instead on a single trial of rule generation and prediction. This component is particularly interesting because it tests whether the robustness gains from aggregation are essential to the framework's success, or whether a single well-constructed set of rules can achieve comparable performance.

The feature description ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Description}$," removes the natural language descriptions of features from the input prompt. This tests the hypothesis that the LLM's prior knowledge about feature semantics—such as understanding what "blood pressure" or "cholesterol level" means—contributes meaningfully to rule quality. Without descriptions, the LLM must rely solely on feature names and example values, potentially missing important semantic relationships.

Finally, the reasoning instruction ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Reasoning}$," removes the first step of the two-step reasoning process where the LLM is encouraged to infer causal relationships between features and the task using its general knowledge. This tests whether explicit reasoning guidance helps the LLM generate better rules, or whether the model can adequately infer these relationships from the few-shot examples alone.

The results of these ablation studies reveal a nuanced picture of component contributions. In all cases, removing the ablated component results in performance degradation, confirming that each element plays a meaningful role in the framework's success. However, the magnitude of the impact varies significantly depending on the number of available training shots, revealing interesting interactions between component importance and data availability.

The weight tuning component shows an interesting pattern: its benefit increases when more training samples are available. This finding makes intuitive sense—when there is abundant data, the optimization process can more accurately estimate the importance weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ for each rule. With limited data, the weight estimates may be noisy or unreliable, making the simple summation approach more competitive. This suggests that the tuning process effectively learns which rules are most predictive, and this learning becomes more reliable as sample size increases.

In contrast, the feature descriptions and reasoning instructions show the opposite pattern: their benefits are most pronounced when the number of shots is small. This observation highlights a crucial insight about how FeatLLM leverages LLM prior knowledge. When training data is scarce, the model cannot rely on data-driven patterns to identify relevant features. In this regime, the LLM's prior knowledge—accessed through feature descriptions and explicit reasoning instructions—becomes essential for guiding the rule generation process toward meaningful relationships. As more data becomes available, the few-shot examples themselves provide sufficient signal for the LLM to identify relevant patterns, reducing the relative importance of explicit knowledge elicitation.

The ensemble approach shows consistent improvement across all settings, confirming its role as a fundamental component of the framework's robustness. The ensemble provides two distinct benefits: it aggregates across multiple rule sets to reduce variance, and it enables feature bagging to handle datasets with large numbers of features. Even when individual trials may generate suboptimal rules, the aggregation process ensures that consistently high-quality rules dominate the final predictions.

A critical consideration for practical deployment is the computational cost of different methods. FeatLLM was designed with efficiency in mind, specifically addressing the limitation that existing LLM-based tabular learning methods require at least one LLM inference per sample during deployment. The experimental comparison of training and inference times provides concrete evidence of these efficiency gains.

The efficiency advantages of FeatLLM become even more significant when considering real-world deployment scenarios. FeatLLM's architecture—generating rules once and applying them efficiently—aligns with practical constraints on inference latency and cost.

# Quality Analysis of Generated Rules

Beyond computational efficiency, understanding the quality of the rules generated by FeatLLM provides insight into why the framework succeeds. Researchers conducted a systematic analysis to quantify how informative the generated rules are for solving the target classification task.

The quality analysis uses AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) as the metric for evaluating individual rules. For each generated binary feature, the AUROC is computed between that feature and the target labels. An AUROC of 0.5 indicates that the feature provides no discriminative information—it performs at random chance level. An AUROC above 0.5 indicates that the feature provides useful signal for predicting the target class.

The analysis computes the ratio of useful features as the proportion of rules with AUROC exceeding 0.5. This ratio provides a direct measure of how many of the LLM-generated rules are actually relevant to the classification task, as opposed to being random or irrelevant conditions.

The results show that the majority of rules generated by FeatLLM are informative for the target task. Before the weight tuning process, a substantial fraction of rules already demonstrate AUROC values above 0.5, indicating that the LLM successfully identifies conditions that correlate with the target variable. This finding validates the core hypothesis of FeatLLM: that LLMs can serve as effective feature engineers by leveraging their prior knowledge to generate relevant rules.

The weight tuning process provides an additional quality improvement by automatically filtering out less relevant rules. During optimization, rules that have little predictive value receive low weights, effectively reducing their influence on the final predictions. The analysis uses a threshold of 0.1 on the learned weights to identify rules that are effectively discarded by the model. After this filtering, the proportion of useful features increases, as rules with low relevance are down-weighted or removed from consideration.

This automatic filtering mechanism is particularly valuable because it requires no manual intervention. The LLM may occasionally generate rules that are syntactically valid but semantically weak, particularly when the relationship between features and targets is subtle or counterintuitive. The weight tuning process provides a data-driven mechanism to identify and suppress these less useful rules, ensuring that the final model relies primarily on the most informative features.

The quality analysis also reveals that the rule generation process benefits from the LLM's ability to incorporate domain knowledge. For datasets with semantically meaningful features—such as medical or financial attributes—the LLM generates rules that align with domain expertise. This alignment suggests that the LLM is not merely memorizing patterns from the few-shot examples, but actively applying its pretrained knowledge about relationships between concepts.

# Robustness to Spurious Correlations

A critical challenge in few-shot learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations—patterns that appear in the training data but do not reflect true underlying relationships. With limited samples, machine learning models may latch onto coincidental patterns that fail to generalize. FeatLLM's approach to rule generation offers potential protection against this failure mode, which researchers investigated through controlled experiments with artificial spurious correlations.

The experimental design uses the Heart dataset as the base task, predicting coronary artery disease. Researchers then progressively attached columns from the Adult dataset—completely unrelated to heart disease prediction—to create increasingly noisy input spaces. The Adult dataset columns (such as employment status, education level, and capital gains) have no causal relationship to heart disease, so any model that relies on these columns is learning a spurious correlation.

As the number of irrelevant columns increases, all methods experience some performance degradation. However, FeatLLM shows markedly less degradation compared to baseline methods. This robustness stems from the framework's approach to rule generation. When the LLM is prompted to generate rules based on the task description and its prior knowledge, it naturally focuses on features that are semantically relevant to the task. The LLM's understanding of medical concepts helps it recognize that employment status or capital gains are unlikely to be relevant for heart disease prediction.

Quantitative analysis confirms this intuition: rules based on the noisy columns account for only 1-2% of all generated rules. This remarkably low percentage demonstrates that the LLM effectively filters out irrelevant features during rule generation, before the downstream model even sees the data.

The comparison with baseline methods is instructive. Conventional machine learning methods like logistic regression and random forests have no built-in mechanism to distinguish relevant from irrelevant features—they treat all input columns equally and learn weights based purely on correlation. When spurious correlations appear in the few-shot training data, these methods may assign substantial weight to irrelevant features. Even sophisticated methods like TabLLM, which fine-tunes LLMs on the tabular data, show more vulnerability to spurious correlations because the fine-tuning process can cause the model to overfit to patterns in the limited training samples.

However, the experiments also reveal an important caveat: FeatLLM is not immune to spurious correlations in all cases. When columns from a closely related dataset are attached—such as adding columns from another medical dataset—the LLM may incorrectly conclude that these features are relevant based on their semantic similarity to legitimate medical features. This observation highlights that the framework's robustness comes from semantic filtering, not from a fundamental ability to detect causality. When irrelevant features appear semantically relevant, the LLM may generate rules that inadvertently incorporate spurious patterns.

This limitation points to an important consideration for practitioners: while FeatLLM provides substantial protection against clearly irrelevant features, careful feature selection remains valuable. The framework works best when the feature set contains features that are plausibly related to the task, allowing the LLM's semantic reasoning to focus on meaningful relationships.

# Hyper-Parameter Analysis: Rules and Ensemble Size

FeatLLM introduces two primary hyper-parameters that control the behavior of the framework: the number of ensembles and the number of rules extracted per class during each LLM query. Understanding how these parameters affect performance is essential for practical deployment and provides insight into the framework's mechanics.

The number of ensembles controls how many independent trials of rule generation and weight learning are performed. Each trial uses a different random seed for LLM inference (controlled by the temperature parameter) and different random orderings of few-shot demonstrations. The final prediction averages the probability estimates from all ensemble members.

Experimental results show that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, following a pattern familiar from ensemble learning literature. With a single ensemble member, performance varies substantially depending on the randomness of the LLM output. As more members are added, the variance decreases and performance stabilizes. However, this improvement follows a pattern of diminishing returns: beyond approximately 20 ensemble members, the performance gains begin to converge. This convergence point represents a sweet spot where additional computational cost yields minimal accuracy benefit.

The number of rules per class controls how many conditions the LLM generates for each class during a single query. This parameter creates a trade-off between information content and dimensionality. More rules provide the model with more potential features, increasing the chance of capturing meaningful patterns. However, more rules also increase the dimensionality of the feature space, potentially leading to overfitting when training data is limited.

Experimental analysis reveals that the optimal number of rules hovers around 10 per class. This number balances the competing considerations: it provides sufficient diversity of features to capture relevant patterns, while remaining manageable for the downstream linear model even in few-shot regimes. The analysis shows no statistically significant difference across a range of values, but 10 rules provides a reasonable default that works well across diverse datasets.

An additional consideration for the rules parameter is prompt length. More rules require longer LLM outputs, which increases both generation time and the risk of format errors. The fixed rule count also interacts with the response format instructions, which guide the LLM to structure its output in a parseable format. Too many rules may exceed the LLM's capacity to maintain consistent formatting, leading to parsing failures.

The interaction between these two hyper-parameters is also noteworthy. With larger ensembles, each individual trial can use fewer rules while still achieving comprehensive coverage of potential patterns. The ensemble process compensates for incomplete coverage in any single trial by aggregating across diverse rule sets. This observation suggests that practitioners facing computational constraints might prefer larger ensembles with fewer rules per trial, rather than fewer trials with more rules.

Together, these hyper-parameter analyses provide practical guidance for deployment while illuminating the mechanisms underlying FeatLLM's success. The framework benefits from both diversity (through ensembles) and appropriate complexity (through controlled rule counts), combining these elements to achieve robust few-shot learning performance.

# Conclusion and Future Horizons

The intersection of large language models and tabular learning represents a fascinating frontier in machine learning research, and the FeatLLM framework marks a significant step forward in this domain. Throughout this chapter, we have explored how FeatLLM reimagines the role of LLMs in tabular learning—not as end-to-end predictors, but as sophisticated feature engineers capable of extracting meaningful rules from limited data. This concluding chapter synthesizes the key contributions of this work, examines its broader implications for real-world applications, addresses important limitations and potential risks, and charts promising directions for future research.

## Summary of Contributions: Bridging LLMs and Tabular Learning via Feature Engineering

The central contribution of FeatLLM lies in its novel conceptualization of how LLMs can be leveraged for tabular learning tasks. Rather than treating LLMs as black-box predictors that must process each sample individually during inference, FeatLLM assigns them the role of feature engineers. This paradigm shift is both elegant and practical. The LLM is tasked with understanding the underlying criteria that determine class membership—for instance, in a medical diagnosis task, the LLM infers rules about which feature conditions indicate the presence of a particular disease. These rules are then parsed into executable code that transforms raw input features into binary indicators, which are subsequently used by a simple linear model for prediction.

This approach yields several important advantages over existing methods. First, by decoupling feature generation from prediction, FeatLLM eliminates the computational burden of querying the LLM for each new sample at inference time. Once the rules have been extracted and the linear model has been trained, predictions can be made rapidly without any further interaction with the LLM. This represents a substantial improvement in inference latency compared to methods like TabLLM, in-context learning, and TABLET, all of which require at least one LLM inference per sample. Second, FeatLLM requires only API-level access to LLMs, making it compatible with state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 that do not provide access to training codebases or fine-tuning APIs. This is a crucial practical consideration, as many of the most capable LLMs available today are only accessible through inference APIs. Third, through the use of feature bagging and ensemble methods borrowed from traditional machine learning, FeatLLM effectively addresses the challenge of prompt size limitations. When tabular datasets contain numerous features, the serialized text length can exceed the LLM's context window or lead to degraded performance; bagging allows the framework to work with subsets of features and samples, with the ensemble aggregating results across multiple trials.

The empirical results presented in the paper demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach across 13 diverse tabular datasets spanning multiple domains. FeatLLM consistently outperformed alternative methods, achieving an average improvement of 10% over baselines like TabLLM and STUNT. Remarkably, with only 4 training examples, FeatLLM achieved performance comparable to conventional machine learning methods that required 32 examples. This dramatic improvement in data efficiency underscores the value of leveraging LLM prior knowledge in low-shot regimes.

## Broader Impact: Applications in High-Cost Labeling Domains

The practical implications of FeatLLM extend far beyond academic benchmarks. The framework is particularly well-suited for real-world scenarios where labeled data is scarce and expensive to obtain. Finance and healthcare represent two domains where these constraints are especially acute. In healthcare, for example, labeling medical records often requires expert annotation by physicians, whose time is both valuable and limited. A disease prediction task might require careful review of patient histories, test results, and clinical notes to determine whether a particular condition is present. The cost of such labeling can be prohibitive, limiting the amount of training data available for conventional machine learning approaches.

FeatLLM addresses this challenge by supplementing limited labeled data with the rich prior knowledge embedded in LLMs. Because LLMs are trained on extensive text corpora that include medical literature, clinical guidelines, and health-related discussions, they possess substantial domain knowledge that can be extracted through appropriate prompting. When presented with a medical prediction task, the LLM can draw upon its knowledge about risk factors, symptom patterns, and disease progression to generate meaningful features. For instance, in predicting myocardial infarction complications, the LLM might generate rules related to patient age, blood pressure thresholds, or specific biomarker levels—reflecting medical knowledge that would otherwise require explicit encoding by domain experts.

Similarly, in finance, applications such as credit risk assessment, fraud detection, and investment prediction often face labeling challenges. Historical data may be limited, or labels may require retrospective analysis by financial experts. The ability to achieve strong predictive performance with minimal labeled samples can significantly reduce the cost and time required to deploy machine learning systems in these contexts.

The broader impact statement in the paper emphasizes that FeatLLM's contribution to data efficiency helps mitigate the problem of overfitting in low-sample regimes. By incorporating prior knowledge from LLMs, the framework provides an inductive bias that guides learning toward meaningful patterns rather than spurious correlations in the limited training data. This has important implications for practitioners across industries who face data scarcity but need reliable predictive models.

## Potential Risks: Societal Biases and Misinformation

While the incorporation of LLM prior knowledge offers substantial benefits, it also introduces potential risks that must be carefully considered. Large language models, trained on vast corpora of internet text, inevitably absorb the biases present in their training data. These biases can manifest in various ways—from demographic biases related to race, gender, and socioeconomic status to cultural biases reflecting the dominant perspectives in the training corpus. When LLMs are used to generate features for predictive tasks, these biases can be encoded directly into the learned representations.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this concern in its broader impact statement. The prior knowledge extracted from LLMs "directly affects the predictions," potentially "dominating over the observed or collected labeled data samples." This creates a situation where predictions may reflect not only the patterns in the training data but also the preconceptions and biases embedded in the LLM's prior knowledge. In sensitive applications like healthcare or criminal justice, such biases could lead to disparate outcomes across different demographic groups or perpetuate existing societal inequities.

Consider, for example, a healthcare prediction task where the LLM generates rules based on medical knowledge that historically underrepresented certain populations in clinical research. The resulting features might be less informative or even misleading for patients from these populations, leading to inferior predictive performance. Similarly, in financial applications, historical biases in lending practices might be reflected in the LLM's prior knowledge, potentially generating features that perpetuate discriminatory patterns.

The issue of misinformation presents another significant risk. LLMs can generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information—a phenomenon often referred to as "hallucination." In the context of feature engineering, this could manifest as rules that appear semantically meaningful but are not actually predictive of the target variable, or worse, rules based on incorrect causal reasoning. The paper's ablation studies reveal that FeatLLM can indeed "learn spurious correlations and misunderstand causal relationships between task and features if columns from closely related data are randomly attached." This highlights the importance of careful validation of generated features, particularly in high-stakes applications.

Addressing these risks requires ongoing research into bias detection and mitigation in LLM-generated features, as well as transparency about the sources of prior knowledge that influence predictions. Practitioners must be vigilant about evaluating whether generated features reflect genuine predictive signals or encoded biases.

## Limitations: Focus on Low-Shot Regimes and Specific Feature Types

Despite its impressive performance, FeatLLM has several limitations that constrain its applicability. The framework is explicitly designed for and evaluated on low-shot learning regimes—scenarios where only a handful of labeled examples are available. The paper's experiments use $k$-shot settings with $k$ ranging from 2 to 32, reflecting the intended use case of data-scarce environments. While this focus is appropriate given the framework's strengths in leveraging prior knowledge, it raises questions about performance in data-rich settings. The paper explicitly states that "FeatLLM is designed to focus on low-shot learning regime only," acknowledging this scope limitation.

The linear model trained on generated features may not capture complex nonlinear relationships that become exploitable with larger datasets. As the number of training samples increases, more sophisticated models like gradient boosting or neural networks trained directly on raw features might eventually match or exceed FeatLLM's performance. The value of LLM prior knowledge is greatest when labeled data is minimal; as more data becomes available, the information extracted from the data itself may supersede prior knowledge in importance.

Another limitation concerns the types of features generated. FeatLLM focuses on rule-based features—binary indicators of whether certain conditions are satisfied. While this approach yields interpretable features and works well for many tabular tasks, it may not capture all relevant feature transformations. For example, features based on arithmetic combinations of input columns, polynomial transformations, or more complex logical structures might be useful in some applications but are not explicitly explored in the current framework. The generated rules are constrained to specific formats based on feature types, which may limit expressiveness in some scenarios.

Additionally, the reliance on a fixed number of rules per class (set to 10 in the experiments) represents a design choice that balances prompt length against feature expressiveness. The hyperparameter analysis shows that while 10 rules performed well, there was no statistically significant difference across various numbers of rules. However, this one-size-fits-all approach may not be optimal for all tasks, particularly those with complex decision boundaries or those involving many relevant features.

## Future Directions: Expanding Scope and Enhancing Capabilities

The conclusion of the paper outlines an ambitious agenda for future research that addresses many of the current limitations while expanding the framework's capabilities. Three primary directions emerge: extending to larger datasets, exploring diverse feature types, and enhancing interpretability.

The first direction involves expanding FeatLLM's capabilities "for datasets with a larger number of samples." This would require rethinking the balance between prior knowledge and data-derived information. In low-shot regimes, prior knowledge from the LLM dominates the feature generation process. As the number of training samples increases, the framework could be adapted to give greater weight to patterns discovered directly in the data. One potential approach might involve using the LLM to generate a broader space of candidate features, then using data-driven selection methods to identify the most informative features for larger datasets. Alternatively, hybrid approaches could combine LLM-generated features with features learned through traditional feature engineering or representation learning methods.

The second direction focuses on "exploring various feature types beyond rules." While rule-based features offer interpretability and computational efficiency, other feature transformations might capture complementary information. For example, the LLM could be prompted to generate mathematical transformations (such as ratios, products, or polynomial terms), threshold-based features with learned cutoffs, or even text-based features derived from combinations of categorical variables. The framework could also be extended to generate features for regression tasks, time-series prediction, or multi-label classification, broadening its applicability across machine learning tasks.

The third direction emphasizes "bringing interpretability" to the framework. While FeatLLM already offers a degree of interpretability through its use of explicit rules and a simple linear model, there is room for deeper analysis. Understanding which rules contribute most to predictions, how rules interact across different ensemble members, and why certain rules are generated by the LLM could provide valuable insights. Such interpretability would enable practitioners to validate that generated features align with domain knowledge, identify potential biases or spurious correlations, and explain predictions to stakeholders. This is particularly important in regulated domains like healthcare and finance, where model interpretability is often a legal or ethical requirement.

Beyond these three directions, the paper's broader impact statement suggests another important area for future work: systematic analysis of "the impact of the societal biases and misinformation embedded in the prior knowledge of LLMs." Developing methods to detect, quantify, and mitigate biases in LLM-generated features would enhance the responsible deployment of FeatLLM in real-world applications. This might involve fairness constraints on the linear model, diversity requirements in generated rules, or post-hoc analysis of feature impacts across demographic groups.

The journey from traditional tabular learning to LLM-enhanced feature engineering represents a significant evolution in how we approach data-scarce prediction tasks. FeatLLM demonstrates that the rich knowledge encoded in large language models can be effectively extracted and operationalized for tabular learning, achieving impressive performance while maintaining computational efficiency. As research continues along the directions outlined above, the integration of LLMs with traditional machine learning is poised to yield even more powerful and responsible tools for practitioners across domains.
