\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why does the two-step reasoning process help filter out uninformative features?
Question:
In Section 3.1, the paper describes a two-step reasoning instruction where Step 1 infers causal relationships without looking at examples, and Step 2 uses examples to deduce rules. The paper claims this 'prevents the model from identifying spurious correlations in irrelevant columns.'

I don't quite follow why separating these steps helps. If the LLM has prior knowledge about which features are relevant, why can't it just generate rules directly? And how does looking at examples first lead to spurious correlations?
Answer:
The two-step reasoning process works by establishing a "prior filter" before the model ever sees the data samples. Here's why this matters:

**The problem with looking at examples first:** In few-shot settings (e.g., 4-16 samples), there's a high risk that random coincidences in those few examples appear meaningful. If the LLM examines examples immediately, it might latch onto spurious patterns—for instance, noticing that 3 out of 4 patients with heart disease in your examples happen to share an unrelated feature (like a particular occupation or zip code). Without first consulting its prior knowledge about what features are *plausibly* relevant to heart disease, the model has no basis to reject this as noise.

**How Step 1 helps:** By first asking the LLM to infer causal relationships based solely on task and feature descriptions—*without* seeing any examples—the model must rely entirely on its world knowledge. For a heart disease prediction task, it might reason: "Age, blood pressure, and cholesterol are known risk factors for heart disease, while occupation is not typically causally related." This step extracts the LLM's prior beliefs about which features are mechanistically relevant.

**How Step 2 uses this:** When the LLM then examines examples to derive specific rules, it's already primed to focus on features identified as causally relevant. The spurious correlation in that zip code column won't be encoded into rules because Step 1 already established it's not a meaningful feature for this task.

The paper provides empirical support in Section 4.2: when irrelevant columns from the Adult dataset were added to the Heart dataset, FeatLLM showed the least performance degradation among baselines, with rules based on noisy columns accounting for only 1-2\% of total rules generated. The ablation study also confirms that removing Step 1 (the "$\textsf{-Reasoning}$" condition) causes significant performance drops, especially in low-shot regimes where spurious correlations are most likely to mislead.
