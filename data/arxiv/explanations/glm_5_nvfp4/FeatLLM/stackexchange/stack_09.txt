\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why does the ablation show feature descriptions are more important with fewer shots?
Question:
In the ablation study (Section 4.2), removing feature descriptions causes performance degradation, and this effect is 'high when the number of shot is small.' The paper says this suggests 'efficient utilization of the prior knowledge of LLM becomes crucial.'

I'm confused about this reasoning. If we have fewer shots, shouldn't we rely more on the few examples we do have, rather than feature descriptions? Why would feature descriptions (prior knowledge) become more important when we have less data?
Answer:
The intuition you're suggesting actually works in the opposite direction. When you have fewer shots, the examples themselves are often insufficient to learn meaningful patterns—this is precisely why prior knowledge becomes *more* critical, not less.

Let me explain the reasoning:

**With few shots, data is unreliable.** When you only have 4 or 8 examples, those samples might be unrepresentative, noisy, or contain spurious correlations. Learning purely from such limited data risks overfitting to noise rather than discovering true signal. The paper explicitly notes this challenge in the Related Work section: "Learning from a small number of labeled samples presents the risk of learning spurious correlations."

**Feature descriptions activate the LLM's prior knowledge.** The feature descriptions tell the LLM what each column means semantically (e.g., that a column represents "age" or "blood pressure"). Without these descriptions, the LLM sees only abstract column names and cannot apply its domain knowledge. With descriptions, the LLM can leverage its pre-training—which includes knowledge like "older patients are at higher risk for certain diseases"—to generate meaningful rules.

**This aligns with the paper's reasoning structure.** Notice that in Section 3.1, the prompt design separates the reasoning into two steps: first, inferring causal relationships "without referring to example demonstrations, relying instead on general knowledge," and only then incorporating the few-shot examples. This design ensures prior knowledge guides the process before the sparse data is considered.

**The broader principle:** This connects to the bias-variance tradeoff in machine learning. With limited data (high variance), you need stronger inductive biases—or prior knowledge—to constrain the hypothesis space and prevent overfitting. Feature descriptions provide exactly this: they inject domain knowledge that guides the model toward plausible hypotheses rather than letting it learn arbitrary patterns from a handful of examples.

So the ablation result confirms that FeatLLM's strength lies in combining LLM prior knowledge with data-efficient learning, and this combination is most valuable precisely when the data signal is weakest.
