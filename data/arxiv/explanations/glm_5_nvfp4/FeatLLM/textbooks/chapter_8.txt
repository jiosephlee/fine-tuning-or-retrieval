\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 8: # Deep Dive: Analytical Insights and Robustness Checks

Having established the strong empirical performance of FeatLLM across diverse tabular datasets, we now turn our attention to understanding the mechanisms underlying its success. This chapter provides a comprehensive analysis of why FeatLLM works effectively, examining each component's contribution, computational efficiency, and robustness properties. Through systematic ablation studies, runtime comparisons, and quality assessments, we gain valuable insights into the framework's behavior that inform both theoretical understanding and practical deployment.

# Ablation Studies: Dissecting Component Contributions

To understand which elements of the FeatLLM framework are most critical to its success, researchers conducted systematic ablation studies that remove or modify individual components. These experiments reveal the relative importance of four key elements: the weight tuning process, the ensemble mechanism, feature descriptions, and the reasoning instructions.

The ablation studies were designed with careful attention to isolating each component's contribution. For the weight tuning ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Tuning}$," the researchers omitted the weight tuning process entirely. Instead of learning the importance weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ through optimization, they simply summed the binary features to compute the logit values. This modification tests whether the learned weights genuinely capture meaningful information about rule importance, or whether the rules themselves are sufficiently discriminative without weighting.

The ensemble ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Ensemble}$," removes the bagging and ensemble process, relying instead on a single trial of rule generation and prediction. This component is particularly interesting because it tests whether the robustness gains from aggregation are essential to the framework's success, or whether a single well-constructed set of rules can achieve comparable performance.

The feature description ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Description}$," removes the natural language descriptions of features from the input prompt. This tests the hypothesis that the LLM's prior knowledge about feature semantics—such as understanding what "blood pressure" or "cholesterol level" means—contributes meaningfully to rule quality. Without descriptions, the LLM must rely solely on feature names and example values, potentially missing important semantic relationships.

Finally, the reasoning instruction ablation, denoted as "$\text{-Reasoning}$," removes the first step of the two-step reasoning process where the LLM is encouraged to infer causal relationships between features and the task using its general knowledge. This tests whether explicit reasoning guidance helps the LLM generate better rules, or whether the model can adequately infer these relationships from the few-shot examples alone.

The results of these ablation studies reveal a nuanced picture of component contributions. In all cases, removing the ablated component results in performance degradation, confirming that each element plays a meaningful role in the framework's success. However, the magnitude of the impact varies significantly depending on the number of available training shots, revealing interesting interactions between component importance and data availability.

The weight tuning component shows an interesting pattern: its benefit increases when more training samples are available. This finding makes intuitive sense—when there is abundant data, the optimization process can more accurately estimate the importance weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ for each rule. With limited data, the weight estimates may be noisy or unreliable, making the simple summation approach more competitive. This suggests that the tuning process effectively learns which rules are most predictive, and this learning becomes more reliable as sample size increases.

In contrast, the feature descriptions and reasoning instructions show the opposite pattern: their benefits are most pronounced when the number of shots is small. This observation highlights a crucial insight about how FeatLLM leverages LLM prior knowledge. When training data is scarce, the model cannot rely on data-driven patterns to identify relevant features. In this regime, the LLM's prior knowledge—accessed through feature descriptions and explicit reasoning instructions—becomes essential for guiding the rule generation process toward meaningful relationships. As more data becomes available, the few-shot examples themselves provide sufficient signal for the LLM to identify relevant patterns, reducing the relative importance of explicit knowledge elicitation.

The ensemble approach shows consistent improvement across all settings, confirming its role as a fundamental component of the framework's robustness. The ensemble provides two distinct benefits: it aggregates across multiple rule sets to reduce variance, and it enables feature bagging to handle datasets with large numbers of features. Even when individual trials may generate suboptimal rules, the aggregation process ensures that consistently high-quality rules dominate the final predictions.

# Training and Inference Time Comparison

A critical consideration for practical deployment is the computational cost of different methods. FeatLLM was designed with efficiency in mind, specifically addressing the limitation that existing LLM-based tabular learning methods require at least one LLM inference per sample during deployment. The experimental comparison of training and inference times provides concrete evidence of these efficiency gains.

The experiments were conducted on the Adult dataset with 16 training shots, using consistent hardware configurations where possible. For most methods, a single A100 GPU was used. TabLLM, which requires model parallelism due to its fine-tuning approach, used four A100 GPUs. This difference in hardware requirements already hints at the substantial computational burden of fine-tuning approaches compared to FeatLLM's simpler architecture.

The inference time comparison reveals a stark contrast between FeatLLM and other LLM-based approaches. FeatLLM demonstrates relatively low inference time, comparable to conventional machine learning methods like logistic regression and random forests. This efficiency stems from the framework's design: once the rules are generated and weights are learned, prediction requires only computing which rules each sample satisfies and applying the learned weights. No LLM queries are needed during inference.

In contrast, methods like In-context learning, TABLET, and TabLLM require passing each test sample through the LLM to generate predictions. This requirement creates a substantial computational bottleneck, particularly when deploying at scale. The per-sample LLM inference cost multiplies with the number of test samples, making these methods impractical for applications requiring real-time predictions or processing large datasets.

The training time comparison shows that FeatLLM requires approximately 30 API queries to the LLM during the training phase. This number comes from the ensemble configuration: with 20 ensemble members, each requiring one rule generation query, plus some overhead for parsing and validation. Importantly, these queries can be parallelized, further reducing wall-clock training time. Compared to methods like TabLLM that require fine-tuning the entire language model, this query-based approach imposes minimal computational burden.

The efficiency advantages of FeatLLM become even more significant when considering real-world deployment scenarios. In industrial applications, where models may need to process thousands or millions of predictions, the per-sample inference cost of conventional LLM methods becomes prohibitively expensive. FeatLLM's architecture—generating rules once and applying them efficiently—aligns with practical constraints on inference latency and cost.

# Quality Analysis of Generated Rules

Beyond computational efficiency, understanding the quality of the rules generated by FeatLLM provides insight into why the framework succeeds. Researchers conducted a systematic analysis to quantify how informative the generated rules are for solving the target classification task.

The quality analysis uses AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) as the metric for evaluating individual rules. For each generated binary feature, the AUROC is computed between that feature and the target labels. An AUROC of 0.5 indicates that the feature provides no discriminative information—it performs at random chance level. An AUROC above 0.5 indicates that the feature provides useful signal for predicting the target class.

The analysis computes the ratio of useful features as the proportion of rules with AUROC exceeding 0.5. This ratio provides a direct measure of how many of the LLM-generated rules are actually relevant to the classification task, as opposed to being random or irrelevant conditions.

The results show that the majority of rules generated by FeatLLM are informative for the target task. Before the weight tuning process, a substantial fraction of rules already demonstrate AUROC values above 0.5, indicating that the LLM successfully identifies conditions that correlate with the target variable. This finding validates the core hypothesis of FeatLLM: that LLMs can serve as effective feature engineers by leveraging their prior knowledge to generate relevant rules.

The weight tuning process provides an additional quality improvement by automatically filtering out less relevant rules. During optimization, rules that have little predictive value receive low weights, effectively reducing their influence on the final predictions. The analysis uses a threshold of 0.1 on the learned weights to identify rules that are effectively discarded by the model. After this filtering, the proportion of useful features increases, as rules with low relevance are down-weighted or removed from consideration.

This automatic filtering mechanism is particularly valuable because it requires no manual intervention. The LLM may occasionally generate rules that are syntactically valid but semantically weak, particularly when the relationship between features and targets is subtle or counterintuitive. The weight tuning process provides a data-driven mechanism to identify and suppress these less useful rules, ensuring that the final model relies primarily on the most informative features.

The quality analysis also reveals that the rule generation process benefits from the LLM's ability to incorporate domain knowledge. For datasets with semantically meaningful features—such as medical or financial attributes—the LLM generates rules that align with domain expertise. This alignment suggests that the LLM is not merely memorizing patterns from the few-shot examples, but actively applying its pretrained knowledge about relationships between concepts.

# Robustness to Spurious Correlations

A critical challenge in few-shot learning is the risk of learning spurious correlations—patterns that appear in the training data but do not reflect true underlying relationships. With limited samples, machine learning models may latch onto coincidental patterns that fail to generalize. FeatLLM's approach to rule generation offers potential protection against this failure mode, which researchers investigated through controlled experiments with artificial spurious correlations.

The experimental design uses the Heart dataset as the base task, predicting coronary artery disease. Researchers then progressively attached columns from the Adult dataset—completely unrelated to heart disease prediction—to create increasingly noisy input spaces. The Adult dataset columns (such as employment status, education level, and capital gains) have no causal relationship to heart disease, so any model that relies on these columns is learning a spurious correlation.

As the number of irrelevant columns increases, all methods experience some performance degradation. However, FeatLLM shows markedly less degradation compared to baseline methods. This robustness stems from the framework's approach to rule generation. When the LLM is prompted to generate rules based on the task description and its prior knowledge, it naturally focuses on features that are semantically relevant to the task. The LLM's understanding of medical concepts helps it recognize that employment status or capital gains are unlikely to be relevant for heart disease prediction.

Quantitative analysis confirms this intuition: rules based on the noisy columns account for only 1-2% of all generated rules. This remarkably low percentage demonstrates that the LLM effectively filters out irrelevant features during rule generation, before the downstream model even sees the data.

The comparison with baseline methods is instructive. Conventional machine learning methods like logistic regression and random forests have no built-in mechanism to distinguish relevant from irrelevant features—they treat all input columns equally and learn weights based purely on correlation. When spurious correlations appear in the few-shot training data, these methods may assign substantial weight to irrelevant features. Even sophisticated methods like TabLLM, which fine-tunes LLMs on the tabular data, show more vulnerability to spurious correlations because the fine-tuning process can cause the model to overfit to patterns in the limited training samples.

However, the experiments also reveal an important caveat: FeatLLM is not immune to spurious correlations in all cases. When columns from a closely related dataset are attached—such as adding columns from another medical dataset—the LLM may incorrectly conclude that these features are relevant based on their semantic similarity to legitimate medical features. This observation highlights that the framework's robustness comes from semantic filtering, not from a fundamental ability to detect causality. When irrelevant features appear semantically relevant, the LLM may generate rules that inadvertently incorporate spurious patterns.

This limitation points to an important consideration for practitioners: while FeatLLM provides substantial protection against clearly irrelevant features, careful feature selection remains valuable. The framework works best when the feature set contains features that are plausibly related to the task, allowing the LLM's semantic reasoning to focus on meaningful relationships.

# Hyper-Parameter Analysis: Rules and Ensemble Size

FeatLLM introduces two primary hyper-parameters that control the behavior of the framework: the number of ensembles and the number of rules extracted per class during each LLM query. Understanding how these parameters affect performance is essential for practical deployment and provides insight into the framework's mechanics.

The number of ensembles controls how many independent trials of rule generation and weight learning are performed. Each trial uses a different random seed for LLM inference (controlled by the temperature parameter) and different random orderings of few-shot demonstrations. The final prediction averages the probability estimates from all ensemble members.

Experimental results show that performance improves as the number of ensembles increases, following a pattern familiar from ensemble learning literature. With a single ensemble member, performance varies substantially depending on the randomness of the LLM output. As more members are added, the variance decreases and performance stabilizes. However, this improvement follows a pattern of diminishing returns: beyond approximately 20 ensemble members, the performance gains begin to converge. This convergence point represents a sweet spot where additional computational cost yields minimal accuracy benefit.

The number of rules per class controls how many conditions the LLM generates for each class during a single query. This parameter creates a trade-off between information content and dimensionality. More rules provide the model with more potential features, increasing the chance of capturing meaningful patterns. However, more rules also increase the dimensionality of the feature space, potentially leading to overfitting when training data is limited.

Experimental analysis reveals that the optimal number of rules hovers around 10 per class. This number balances the competing considerations: it provides sufficient diversity of features to capture relevant patterns, while remaining manageable for the downstream linear model even in few-shot regimes. The analysis shows no statistically significant difference across a range of values, but 10 rules provides a reasonable default that works well across diverse datasets.

An additional consideration for the rules parameter is prompt length. More rules require longer LLM outputs, which increases both generation time and the risk of format errors. The fixed rule count also interacts with the response format instructions, which guide the LLM to structure its output in a parseable format. Too many rules may exceed the LLM's capacity to maintain consistent formatting, leading to parsing failures.

The interaction between these two hyper-parameters is also noteworthy. With larger ensembles, each individual trial can use fewer rules while still achieving comprehensive coverage of potential patterns. The ensemble process compensates for incomplete coverage in any single trial by aggregating across diverse rule sets. This observation suggests that practitioners facing computational constraints might prefer larger ensembles with fewer rules per trial, rather than fewer trials with more rules.

Together, these hyper-parameter analyses provide practical guidance for deployment while illuminating the mechanisms underlying FeatLLM's success. The framework benefits from both diversity (through ensembles) and appropriate complexity (through controlled rule counts), combining these elements to achieve robust few-shot learning performance.
